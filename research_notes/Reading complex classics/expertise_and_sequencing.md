# Reading Expertise and Sequencing the Classics

Scope note: Most of the expertise evidence comes from **students** (high-school, undergraduate, PhD students used as "experts"), and usually from small think-aloud samples. Almost none of it studies adult self-learners. Program evidence on sequencing is mostly stated rationale. I found no outcome studies comparing chronological and difficulty-ordered sequencing.

## (a) What distinguishes expert from novice literary readers, and how does expertise develop?

### Takeaway
Expert literary readers differ in **what they notice and what they do with it**, not in basic decoding. They bring genre and tradition knowledge that tells them where to look: oppositions, juxtapositions, unusual word choices, allusions. They treat difficulty and ambiguity as places where meaning is likely. They build a second, interpretive layer on top of the plot, and they enjoy the text more. These are learnable, knowledge-dependent conventions (Rabinowitz's "rules of notice", Culler's "literary competence"). Explicit instruction in them measurably increases interpretive inferences, at least in students.

### Cited Findings
- **Peskin (1998), "Constructing meaning when reading poetry: An expert-novice study," *Cognition and Instruction* 16, 235–263.**
  - Design: English PhD students (experts) were compared with undergraduates and advanced high-school students (novices). Each thought aloud while reading Marvell's "On a Drop of Dew," presented without the author's name. — [OnFiction research bulletin by Peskin](https://www.onfiction.ca/2008/11/research-bulletin-by-joan-peskin.html)
  - Experts used genre and period knowledge to direct attention. One expert saw "Manna," predicted a religious meaning and placed the poem in the Metaphysical tradition ("imagined Donne's name on it"). Her "deep structure of knowledge provided a frame of reference that directed her attention." — [OnFiction](https://www.onfiction.ca/2008/11/research-bulletin-by-joan-peskin.html)
  - Experts looked for meaning at binary oppositions, juxtapositions and dialectic, and spent extra time there. A novice noticed the same oppositions, found they "jumbl[ed] things," disliked them and ignored those lines. Novices understand oppositions in general (for example in folktales) but do not use them explicitly to build poetic meaning. — [OnFiction](https://www.onfiction.ca/2008/11/research-bulletin-by-joan-peskin.html)
  - **Pleasure rises with expertise.** The PhD students found the poems more pleasing and commented more often on delight in specific images. In Peskin's separate developmental study (*Discourse Processes*), Grade 12 students enjoyed poems more and responded more emotionally than Grade 8 students. — [OnFiction](https://www.onfiction.ca/2008/11/research-bulletin-by-joan-peskin.html)
  - Peskin and Hanauer's book *A Life with Poetry: The development of poetic literacy* (2023, John Benjamins, Linguistic Approaches to Literature 41) extends this into a developmental account. — [Benjamins catalog](https://benjamins.com/catalog/lal.41)
- **Zeitz (1994), "Expert-novice differences in memory, abstraction, and reasoning in the domain of literature."**
  - Literary experts beat novices on gist-level recall, on extracting interpretations and on the breadth of aspects they addressed. This held for literary texts but **not for a scientific text**, so the expertise is domain-specific.
  - Experts' arguments had greater hierarchical depth, and they made more complex comparisons between texts.
  - Zeitz proposes that experts build a layered representation: a novice-like base plus a richer derived, abstract layer. Literature is framed as an "ill-structured domain" whose expertise still resembles expertise in well-structured domains.
  - Sources: [Dialnet listing (pp. 277–312)](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/ejemplar/64701); [eScholarship version](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/54h4s99w). The journal is generally cited as *Cognition and Instruction* 12(4). I could not confirm the venue from the fetched page, because the eScholarship page returned empty.
- **Rules of notice and signification (Rabinowitz 1987), tested experimentally by McCarthy & Goldman (2019).** Rules of notice are stylistic cues such as juxtapositions and unexpected word choices that direct attention to likely interpretive meaning. Rules of signification are common literary themes.
  - McCarthy & Goldman derived the relevant rules from expert think-alouds on a short story ("The Elephant").
  - Teaching the rules increased attention to specific language, which in turn produced more interpretive inferences.
  - A related UIC dissertation found that combined instruction (notice plus signification) produced more interpretive behaviour than rules of notice alone.
  - These are small, lab-based studies with students. — [Search summary of UIC theses](https://indigo.uic.edu/articles/thesis/The_Role_of_Prior_Knowledge_in_Interpretive_Inferences_and_Reasoning/10854842); [UIC thesis on variation in literary reasoning](https://indigo.uic.edu/articles/thesis/Variations_in_Approach_to_Literary_Reasoning_Among_High_School_Students/10936472)
- **Project READI** (Goldman et al.) was a multi-institution effort to build instruction for "reading for understanding" in literary analysis, history and science for adolescents (grades 6–12). It produced an RCT report (2017). A case study from the project found that different interpretive approaches can each be an acceptable way of "doing" literary reading. — [UIC LSRI project page](https://lsri.uic.edu/our-research/lsri-research-projects-2/reading-for-understanding-across-grades-6-12-evidence-based-argumentation-for-disciplinary-learning/); [READI RCT report](https://readingapprenticeship.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Project-READI_RCT-Report-27_2017.pdf); [Lee & Goldman-related SSOL article](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/ssol.1.1.05lee)
- **Literary reading is a distinct processing mode (Zwaan 1991, *Poetics* 20:139–156).**
  - The same texts were framed either as literature or as news. The literary framing produced **slower reading and stronger surface-structure memory** (exact wording).
  - Zwaan (1994, *JEP: LMC*) adds that the literary framing gave **poorer situation-model memory**. Readers "differentially allocate their processing resources according to their expectations about the genre."
  - Sources: [Zwaan 1991 PDF](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/Cognitive/Readings/Zwaan_1991.pdf); [Genre Across Borders entry for Zwaan 1994](https://genreacrossborders.uwaterloo.ca/biblio/effect-genre-expectations-text-comprehension)
- **Miall & Kuiken (1994), "Foregrounding, defamiliarization, and affect."**
  - Across four studies, foregrounded passages (stylistically deviant ones) drew longer reading times and higher ratings for strikingness and affect.
  - "Response to foregrounding appeared to be independent of literary competence or experience."
  - So **basic sensitivity to style is not an expert skill**. What expertise adds is the interpretive use of what is noticed (compare Peskin's novice, who noticed the oppositions but discarded them).
  - Sources: [Miall & Kuiken 1994](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Miall_Kuiken_1994.htm); [Foregrounding (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foregrounding)
  - Dixon et al. (1993) reported that experienced readers appreciate a story more on a second reading. Kuijpers & Hakemulder's replications did not reproduce this; they attributed the gain mainly to better understanding of the story on rereading. — [Foregrounding (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foregrounding)
- **Expert and novice studies extend to other forms.**
  - Professional poets read Russian poetry with eye-movement patterns different from novices'. — [Reading Russian poetry: An expert–novice study (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11185421/)
  - Van Rees, Larsen & Viehoff (1995) edited a *Poetics* special issue on experts and novice readers of literary fiction. — [Tilburg record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/experts-and-novice-readers-of-literary-fiction-an-introduction/)

### Inferences
- The specific expert skills, from the cited findings:
  1. Genre, period and tradition knowledge that sets up expectations.
  2. Knowing *where* to look (rules of notice: oppositions, repetitions, anomalies, titles, openings and endings).
  3. Treating ambiguity and difficulty as signals rather than errors.
  4. Building an abstract thematic layer above the plot.
  5. Comparing across texts, which is how allusion gets recognised.
- These are mostly **knowledge and conventions**, not general reading ability. Zeitz's null result on scientific text supports this. So a reading path should deliberately **build the knowledge the hard book assumes**: earlier works it alludes to, and the conventions it subverts. Raw "reading level" is not the main thing to build.
- Novices *notice* stylistic oddity just as much (Miall & Kuiken) but abandon it as noise (Peskin). App scaffolding that says "this is strange on purpose; here is why that matters" targets exactly the novice gap.
- Enjoyment grows with expertise (Peskin). Ramping up gradually is therefore also a motivation strategy, not only a comprehension one.

### Gaps
- **Graves & Frederiksen (literary expertise in describing texts):** my search did not find the paper, so I cannot report its findings.
- **Culler's "literary competence" (*Structuralist Poetics*, 1975):** not fetched. Its general claim is that literary reading requires internalised conventions, consistent with the above, but I have no URL here.
- **Fialho:** I found no source on Fialho's work on transformative or experiencing reading in this pass.
- **Burkett & Goldman on interpretive moves:** I did not locate the specific paper.
- **Adult learners:** I found no studies of how adult self-learners (not students) develop literary expertise over time.

## (a2) Expertise in reading philosophy and argument

### Takeaway
Direct evidence is thin. The best-known study (Geisler) has four participants. Novices read philosophy to absorb information. Experts read it as a move in an ongoing debate: they reconstruct the argument, place it among rival positions, and decide whether to assent or resist.

### Cited Findings
- **Geisler** compared two professional philosophers with two undergraduate novices. They read articles on paternalism and wrote essays. The experts used reading and writing to build mental models of the *socially configured* debate (who argues what against whom), while the novices did not. The work is exploratory. — [Geisler, *Academic Literacy and the Nature of Expertise*, ch. 7 (PDF)](https://innovationtest1.colostate.edu/books/textual_dynamics/chapter7.pdf)
- John Bean's practitioner summary describes novices as reading primarily to absorb information, and experts as reading to participate in a conversation and decide whether to assent or resist. This is not empirical. — [CSUB handout "Novice and Expert Readers"](https://www.csub.edu/eap-riap/day5/3%20Novice%20and%20Expert%20Readers%20.doc)
- Think-aloud work on expository prose finds that problem-solving moves increase significantly as texts get harder (ten undergraduates). — [Caron dissertation, UVic](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/2f5123ce-365d-4648-8b56-ef3637235175)
- A Spanish empirical study examined comprehension of philosophical texts among pre-university (COU) students. I saw the abstract only. — [UNIR record](https://investigacion.unir.net/documentos/619ca406a08dbd1b8f9fbfeb?lang=en)

### Inferences
- In philosophy the prerequisite is mostly **knowing the debate the text answers**: the position being attacked and the vocabulary inherited. A path into Kant should therefore build the empiricist-rationalist dispute first (Descartes, then Hume). Raw difficulty is the wrong ramp here too.

### Gaps
- I found no sizeable peer-reviewed expert-novice study of reading canonical philosophy (Plato, Kant).
- I found no studies of adults learning to read philosophy outside courses.

## (a3) Print exposure and fiction familiarity: does accumulated reading predict handling hard texts?

### Takeaway
Yes, correlationally. Print exposure measured by the Author Recognition Test (ART) predicts comprehension. Literary fiction exposure specifically predicts comprehension of both narrative and expository texts in young adults. Causation is likely reciprocal: reading builds skill, and skill makes reading rewarding.

### Cited Findings
- **The ART (Stanovich & West 1989).** Participants pick real authors from a list mixed with made-up names; the score estimates lifetime print exposure. Mar & Rain's version (ART-G) splits fiction from non-fiction exposure. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021 (PDF)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.662940/pdf); [Behavior Research Methods 2022 (ART norms)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-021-01784-2)
- **Fiction exposure and theory of mind.** Exposure to narrative fiction, but not non-fiction, correlates positively with theory of mind (Mar et al. 2006). — [PMC7101010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7101010)
- **Literary fiction and comprehension.** In 203 adults aged 18–30, only *literary* fiction exposure significantly explained comprehension of expository and narrative texts. Genre fiction exposure better predicted recent reading frequency. — [Search summary of the UCA (Argentina) study](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/handle/123456789/18358?locale=en)
- **Causation.** Reciprocal or bidirectional causation between print exposure and comprehension is noted as a likely explanation. — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.662940/pdf)

### Inferences
- Accumulated literary reading is a reasonable proxy for readiness. An app can estimate readiness from books completed on the platform, or from an ART-style onboarding item ("which of these authors have you read?").

### Gaps
- Stanovich & Cunningham's original papers (1991–1993) were not fetched directly.
- I found no longitudinal or causal study showing that reading easier classics first improves later comprehension of *specific* hard classics.

## (b) How established programs and thinkers sequence the classics, and why

### Takeaway
The major Great Books programs sequence **chronologically**, not by difficulty, and their stated rationale is intelligibility: later books answer, allude to and presuppose earlier ones. Chronology is therefore a dependency order, and it often *also* yields an easy-to-hard ramp within a given form; later modernist texts like Ulysses presuppose the tradition. Adler and Bauer, the two self-learner guides, combine chronology or influence with explicit reading *method* (inspectional to analytical, or grammar to logic to rhetoric). No program reports outcome data comparing orders.

### Cited Findings
- **St. John's College** (all-required "Program," adopted 1937).
  - Year 1 is the Greeks; Year 2 is Roman, medieval and Renaissance; Year 3 is the 17th and 18th centuries; Year 4 is the 19th and 20th centuries.
  - The catalogue states the order is "primarily a matter of convenience and intelligibility" and *not* a historical approach. The aim is "fundamental problems people face today and at all times." These quotations come from a forum reproduction of the catalogue text and should be verified on sjc.edu. — [Forum reproduction](https://www.askandyaboutclothes.com/threads/the-classics.48231); [SJC reading list](https://www.sjc.edu/reading-list)
  - All classes are seminars. — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/great-books/)
  - The core stayed largely stable over 70 years. — [GSU dissertation](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/eps_diss/37)
- **Columbia Literature Humanities** (since 1937).
  - A two-semester sequence "from Homer to Virginia Woolf," taught broadly chronologically. — [Columbia course listing](https://doc.sis.columbia.edu/subj/HUMA/C1001-20233-043/)
  - The Iliad, Oresteia, Oedipus the King and Inferno have never left the list. At least 152 works have appeared over its history. The syllabus is revised in light of the "insistent problems of the present." — [Columbia News: 75 years of Lit Hum](https://news.columbia.edu/node/564)
- **Adler & Van Doren, *How to Read a Book*.**
  - The book teaches graded levels of reading: elementary, inspectional, analytical and syntopical. — [Wikipedia: How to Read a Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Read_a_Book)
  - It advises reading the books that influenced an author before that author, which is an **influence and dependency principle**. — [Search summary of the Adler text](https://www.mathscinotes.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Adler-Mortimer-How-To-Read-A-Book.pdf)
  - The **Ten-Year Reading Plan** appears in the Great Books of the Western World guide (*The Great Conversation*) and differs between the 1952 and 1990 editions. Readers report that the books' difficulty, not the schedule, is what derails them. — [Great Conversation reading group: Ten Year Plan](https://links.billerickson.net/?p=280)
  - **Unverified:** whether the ten-year plan deliberately mixes shorter and more accessible selections early. I found no primary source on its ordering principle.
- **Susan Wise Bauer, *The Well-Educated Mind*** (written for adult self-learners). Bauer maps three stages of reading onto the trivium:
  - Grammar: understand what the text says.
  - Logic: critically evaluate it.
  - Rhetoric: articulate and discuss one's own view.
  - She presents the well-educated mind as the product of steady effort, not genius. Source: a [review at Exodus Books](https://www.exodusbooks.com/well-educated-mind/5931/?mediaid=4), which is secondary.
  - Her lists are organised by genre (novel, autobiography, history, drama, poetry, science) and chronological within each genre. This comes from general knowledge of the book and was not confirmed by a fetched source in this pass.
- **Plato reading orders.**
  - The Great Books editors propose a **thematic** order: dialogues on Socrates' life, character and death first, then method, then virtue, then love and friendship, with Cratylus last.
  - A pedagogical guide begins with Ion, Euthyphro, Apology, Crito and Phaedo, then the aporetic early dialogues, then Gorgias as the bridge.
  - Chronological lists of the "early" group vary, and one places Apology late within it.
  - Sources: [Logos community list of early dialogues](https://community.logos.com/discussion/comment/510505); [Audible: chronological order of Plato's works](https://www.audible.com/pd/B0GYRW3XDD) (secondary)
- **Kant.**
  - University courses pair Kant's *Critique* with Hume's *Enquiry*. — [MIT/OCW-mirrored syllabus](https://ocw.tau.edu.ng/courses/linguistics-and-philosophy/24-805-topics-in-theory-of-knowledge-a-priori-knowledge-spring-2005/readings)
  - On AskPhilosophers, a respondent suggests Descartes beforehand and notes Kant can feel like "just words on the page" to beginners. — [AskPhilosophers](https://askphilosophers.org/print/24118)
  - Kant's own *Prolegomena* says Hume interrupted his "dogmatic slumber." This is the standard reason for Hume-first, from general knowledge; the primary text was not fetched.
- **Ulysses** (informal advice only; I found no scholarly consensus).
  - Readers recommend reading the relevant episode guide before each episode, using an annotated edition (Gabler, keyed to Blamires' *New Bloomsday Book*), reading an episode in one sitting, and expecting to reread. — [UlyssesGuide: suggested reading habits](https://www.ulyssesguide.com/how-to-read); [The Drunken Odyssey](https://thedrunkenodyssey.com/2012/06/13/how-to-read-ulysses-for-the-first-time-3-2/)
  - One reader reports *Hamlet* and Goethe's *Faust* were more useful than the *Odyssey*, because Scylla and Charybdis is Hamlet-heavy and Circe parodies Faust. — [Goodreads thread](https://www.goodreads.com/topic/show/17529138-how-to-read-ulysses-advice-and-opinions?tab=book)
  - A Biblioklept essay argues against relying on how-to guides at all. — [Biblioklept](https://biblioklept.org/2010/06/16/how-to-read-james-joyces-ulysses-and-why-you-should-avoid-how-to-&)
  - Reading Joyce's earlier works first is advice from a forum poster. — [Online-Literature forum](https://www.online-literature.com/forums/archive/index.php/t-26658.html)
  - It is widely known that Stephen Dedalus continues from *A Portrait*, which makes Portrait (and Dubliners) natural prerequisites. This is general knowledge and was not sourced here.

### Inferences
- **Chronological and difficulty-based ordering are not really in conflict.** Programs use chronology because it follows **dependency**: texts presuppose and allude to their predecessors. For a reading app, the stronger principle is a *prerequisite graph*: Homer and Hamlet before Ulysses, Hume before Kant, Socratic dialogues before the Republic. Difficulty can be layered on top, through short and accessible works first and through supports.
- The user's intuition (Chopin → Ulysses is too big a jump) is consistent with the evidence. The gap is not mainly sentence difficulty. It is
  - (1) knowledge of the tradition Ulysses parodies (Homer, Shakespeare, Faust, Catholic liturgy, Irish history);
  - (2) expert conventions for handling deliberate difficulty, ambiguity and stylistic shifts (rules of notice).
- A plausible path is Chopin → Dubliners → Portrait → Odyssey and Hamlet → Ulysses. It is supported by dependency logic and informal reader advice, not by an outcome study.
- Programs pair the sequence with **social and guided reading** (seminars, method instruction). That pairing may matter as much as the order itself; Tinct's chat or companion could play the seminar role.

### Gaps
- **Yale Directed Studies, University of Chicago Core and Shimer College:** not covered in this pass; I have no fetched sources on their sequencing.
- **Outcomes:** I found no empirical study comparing chronological, thematic and difficulty-ordered Great Books sequences.
- **Primary sources not fetched:** Adler's own rationale for the ten-year plan's ordering, and Bauer's stated rationale for chronology within genre.

## (c) Graduated difficulty, deliberate practice and extensive reading

### Takeaway
I found no direct study applying Ericsson's deliberate-practice framework to adult reading of literary classics. The adjacent evidence points one way: expertise is domain-specific and knowledge-based (Zeitz); explicit instruction in conventions boosts interpretation (McCarthy & Goldman); exposure correlates with comprehension (ART studies); and difficulty prompts more active problem-solving (Caron). Together this supports a scaffolded, graduated approach, but it falls short of proving that graduated difficulty is necessary.

### Cited Findings
- Problem-solving strategies increase as expository text difficulty rises (think-aloud, n=10 undergraduates). This implies harder texts elicit active strategies when readers have them available. — [Caron, UVic](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/2f5123ce-365d-4648-8b56-ef3637235175)
- Explicit instruction in rules of notice and signification increases interpretive inferences (students). — [UIC thesis](https://indigo.uic.edu/articles/thesis/The_Role_of_Prior_Knowledge_in_Interpretive_Inferences_and_Reasoning/10854842)
- Rereading improves appreciation mainly through better understanding (Kuijpers & Hakemulder replication), which supports guided rereading over one-pass reading. — [Foregrounding (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foregrounding)

### Inferences
- For Tinct's design:
  - Sequence by prerequisite and allusion dependency.
  - Ramp the length and stylistic deviance of texts.
  - Explicitly teach rules of notice at the point where they are needed (for example, "Joyce changes style each episode; ask why").
  - Use the reader's own reading history as a readiness signal.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study of deliberate practice (Ericsson) applied to literary or philosophical reading.
- I found no study of extensive-reading programs for adult native-language readers of classics. The extensive-reading literature is mostly L2 (second-language) learning, which was not searched in this pass.
