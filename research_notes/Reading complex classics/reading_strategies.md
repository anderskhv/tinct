# Evidence base for reading and study strategies for understanding complex texts

Scope note: almost all of the evidence below comes from children, school pupils or undergraduates studying expository texts for a test. Almost none comes from adults reading literary or philosophical classics for pleasure or self-education. Each finding says which population and outcome it covers.

## 1. What do the major reviews say (Dunlosky 2013, NRP 2000, Okkinga 2018, Hattie, Rosenshine & Meister, Pressley)?

### Takeaway
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate two techniques high utility: practice testing and distributed practice.
- They rate self-explanation, elaborative interrogation and interleaving as moderate utility.
- They rate highlighting, rereading and summarization as low utility.
- Strategy-instruction meta-analyses find real but modest gains on standardized comprehension tests (d ≈ 0.19 in whole classrooms). Gains are larger on researcher-made tests (d ≈ 0.43) and on the strategies themselves (d ≈ 0.79).
- Headline figures such as Hattie's reciprocal teaching d = 0.74 come from small, researcher-run studies and overstate typical real-world effects.

### Cited Findings
**Dunlosky et al. 2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58 (ten techniques)**
- Ratings:
  - **High utility:** practice testing and distributed practice, "because they benefit learners of different ages and abilities and have been shown to boost students' performance across many criterion tasks and even in educational contexts."
  - **Moderate utility:** elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice, because "the evidence for their efficacy is limited."
  - **Low utility:** summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text learning, and rereading.
  - [Dunlosky et al. 2013 full text](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)
- **Learner caveat:** the authors say the techniques "will benefit only students who are motivated and capable of using them" and are "not a panacea." [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- **Domain knowledge** is one of the moderators the review codes for. It may be required for some techniques, such as elaborative interrogation, and "may enhance the benefits of summarization and highlighting as well." [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)

**National Reading Panel (NRP) 2000**
- The panel reviewed 203 studies covering 16 types of comprehension-strategy instruction.
- Seven types had solid scientific support: comprehension monitoring, cooperative learning, graphic/semantic organizers, question answering, question generation, summarization, and multiple-strategy instruction.
- Population: children in school (K–12), not adults. [Education Human and Development Journal review summarizing NRP](https://journal2.unusa.ac.id/index.php/EHDJ/article/download/2589/1680/14721); [NICHD NRP historical page](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/research/supported/nrp)

**Okkinga et al. 2018 (teacher-delivered, whole-class strategy interventions)**
- Scope: 52 studies, 125 effect sizes. [Rotterdam UAS record](https://www.rotterdamuas.com/research/projects-and-publications/pub/effectiveness-of-reading-strategy-interventio/4c38634a-4354-4885-9093-6405172b969d/)
- Effects by outcome:
  - Standardized comprehension tests: d = 0.186 ("very small").
  - Researcher-developed comprehension tests: d = 0.431.
  - Strategic ability: d = 0.786.
- Moderators: effects were larger when a researcher rather than the teacher delivered the training, larger when "setting reading goals" was included, and larger in grades 6–8.
- Population: schoolchildren.

**Hattie, *Visible Learning***
- Reciprocal teaching: d ≈ 0.74, highest when cognitive strategies (summarizing, questioning, clarifying, predicting) are taught explicitly before the reciprocal dialogue begins. [Wikipedia: Visible learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_learning); [Staffordshire EP leaflet](https://www.staffordshire.gov.uk/Education/Access-to-learning/Graduated-response-toolkit/School-toolkit/EPS-school-support-information-and-resources/EP-Recommended-Approach-to-Literacy/documents/Reciprocal-Teaching-leaflet.pdf)
- Metacognitive strategies: about 0.69. Self-verbalization/self-questioning: about 0.64. These two figures come from a mathematics-focused list. Hattie's "hinge point" is d = 0.40, which he treats as about one year's growth. [Origo effect-size sheet](https://www.origoeducation.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/effect-sizes.pdf)
- These are secondary summaries, not Hattie's own tables.

**Rosenshine & Meister 1994, *Review of Educational Research* 64, 479–530 (reciprocal teaching)**
- An earlier report version covered 19 experimental studies; secondary sources say the review covered 16. [Illinois IDEALS record](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/17829); [secondary summary](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10021679/1/FINAL%20LAST%20EVER%20Dec%202nd.pdf)
- A later dissertation says the 1994 review leaned on unpublished work. It also implies the review found larger effects on experimenter-made tests than on standardized tests. [UNL dissertation](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/dissertations/AAI3092542)

**Pressley & Afflerbach 1995, *Verbal Protocols of Reading: The Nature of Constructively Responsive Reading* (Erlbaum)**
- The book catalogues every conscious process that skilled readers reported in think-aloud studies. It concludes that skilled reading is "constructively responsive": readers actively construct meaning and adjust their strategies to the text and their goals. [Hiroshima Univ. analysis](https://ir.lib.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/00033991?locale=en); [book scan link](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Pressley%2C%20Afflerbach%20-%201995%20-%20Verbal%20Protocols%20of%20Reading.pdf)
- The authors call think-aloud protocol research "an immature methodology." [akademika listing](https://akademika.no/humaniora/sprak/verbal-protocols-reading/9780805815375)

### Inferences
- Standardized-test effects are much smaller than researcher-test effects (0.19 vs 0.43 in Okkinga). The strategies change how people read (d ≈ 0.79) more reliably than they raise general comprehension. An app should not promise large comprehension gains.
- The NRP list and reciprocal teaching share a core of predicting, questioning, clarifying, summarizing and monitoring. The skilled readers in Pressley's protocols do these things spontaneously. An AI companion can prompt or model these moves instead of teaching them as drills.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Rosenshine & Meister's exact effect sizes. They are widely reported elsewhere as roughly d ≈ 0.32 on standardized tests and d ≈ 0.88 on experimenter-made tests, but I could not verify these figures, so treat them as unconfirmed.
- I could not retrieve the NRP's per-strategy effect sizes or Hattie's original tables.
- I could not confirm Okkinga's journal. It is believed to be *Educational Psychology Review* 30(4), but the record I found does not name it.
- I could not retrieve Pressley & Afflerbach's specific list of expert-reader behaviours, such as overviewing before reading, reading selectively and evaluating. The PDF download failed.

## 2. Evidence for specific techniques

### Takeaway
- **Retrieval practice** has the strongest, most durable effect with prose. After one week it beat restudying 61% vs 40% of idea units, while learners wrongly felt more confident after restudying.
- **Self-explanation** has a robust meta-analytic effect (g = 0.55).
- **Elaborative interrogation** works mostly for factual material and for learners with domain knowledge.
- **Highlighting, rereading and summarizing** are weak on their own. Highlighting may hurt inference.
- **Readers misjudge their own comprehension badly.** Judgment–performance correlations average about 0.27.

### Cited Findings
**Retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, *Psychological Science*, undergraduates reading prose passages)**
- Experiment 1, one-week test: study-then-test recalled 56% vs 42% for study-study (d = 0.83). On the 5-minute test, restudying was better (81% vs 75%).
- Experiment 2, one-week test:
  - Recall: STTT (one study, three tests) 61%, SSST 56%, SSSS (four studies) 40%. The STTT group had read the passage only once.
  - Proportional forgetting over the week: SSSS 52%, SSST 28%, STTT 14%.
- Repeated studying raised students' confidence even though it produced worse retention.
- Sources: [Roediger & Karpicke 2006 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2006-roediger.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)

**Self-explanation (Bisra et al. 2018, *Educational Psychology Review*)**
- Scope: 69 effect sizes from 64 reports. Random-effects mean g = 0.55. Twenty moderators were coded, including task, subject, level of education and type of prompt. [SFU Summit](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/34322); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)
- Prompting learners to self-explain beat giving them written explanations. The authors recommend research on computer-generated self-explanation prompts. [Springer Nature round-up](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/may-2018-research-round-up); [Bisra 2018 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- Dunlosky's assessment: effects hold across content, age and outcome (memory, comprehension, transfer). Durability over long delays is unproven, and the technique has "nontrivial time demands." [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)

**Elaborative interrogation ("why is this true?")**
- Rated moderate utility. It is firmly established for associative memory of facts after short delays. Its benefit for comprehension and over long delays is unproven, and it may be limited for learners with low domain knowledge. [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)
- With longer texts, effects are "substantially diluted" (Callender & McDaniel 2007) or even reversed (Ramsay et al. 2010) when prompts appear only once every 1–2 pages. [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)

**Summarization**
- Rated low utility. It is effective for learners already skilled at summarizing, but children, high-schoolers and some undergraduates need extensive training.
- Summarization was as useful as note-taking but less powerful than generating explanations or self-questioning (King 1992).
- [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)

**Highlighting and underlining**
- Rated low utility: "In most situations… highlighting does little to boost performance."
- It may help when learners have the knowledge to highlight well, or when texts are difficult. It "may actually hurt performance on higher-level tasks that require inference making."
- [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)

**Rereading**
- Rated low utility. Most of the evidence is recall-based and comes from college students; the comprehension benefit is "less clear."
- Spaced rereading usually outperforms massed rereading.
- The largest strike against it is that it is much less effective than other techniques.
- [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)

**Comprehension monitoring and the illusion of knowing**
- Relative metacomprehension accuracy (how well a reader's judgments track test scores across texts) averages only about r = 0.27 in adults (Maki 1998). [Learning Scientists blog](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/3/30-1); [ERIC ED594995](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594995.pdf)
- Dunlosky & Lipko (2007, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*) describe two decades of research showing poor accuracy and propose ways to improve it. [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/j.1467-8721.2007.00509.x/)
- Poor monitoring leads readers to skip restudying material they understand poorly. [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-023-02349-4)

### Inferences
**Mapping to Tinct features**
- Highlighting and notes are weak on their own. Their value probably lies in feeding later retrieval and elaboration, for example "explain why you highlighted this," or an end-of-chapter recall before the summary is shown.
- Chapter summaries that the app gives readers are closer to rereading than to generation. Asking the reader to produce a recall or summary first, then showing the AI summary as feedback, turns the feature into retrieval practice.
- Self-explanation and why-questions suit an AI companion well, and Bisra explicitly recommends computer-generated prompts.

**Cautions**
- Prompts should be frequent and local. Sparse prompts in long texts lose their effect.
- Prompts should be scaffolded for readers with low domain knowledge, which is common with unfamiliar classics.
- Low metacomprehension accuracy means readers' feeling of "I got it" is unreliable. Light, low-stakes self-checks are justified.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Chi's original self-explanation studies (Chi et al. 1989, 1994) or effect sizes for SQ3R.
- I found no experimental evidence base for "close reading" as a technique. Its research is mostly classroom and practitioner literature.
- I did not retrieve the Ponce et al. (2022) highlighting meta-analysis.
- I did not retrieve Glenberg et al. (1982) on the illusion of knowing.
- Almost all of the evidence uses expository or factual texts and recall or short-answer tests, not interpretation of literary or philosophical argument.

## 3. Adler's *How to Read a Book* (inspectional → analytical → syntopical): empirical support?

### Takeaway
- No direct empirical test of Adler's method was found.
- The nearest component evidence comes from three sources: advance-organizer research (generally positive but inconsistent), expert-reader protocols (skilled readers do preview and read selectively), and question generation (supported by the NRP).

### Cited Findings
- **Advance organizers** (Luiten, Ames & Ackerson 1980, *American Educational Research Journal* 17, 211–218):
  - An updated review ("Thirty years after Ausubel") found effects were "generally positive." The first two decades of research were inconclusive. [Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)
  - The most consistently positive results came from learning-disabled students and preschool children. [Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)
- **Expert readers:** skilled readers' think-aloud protocols show they construct meaning actively and adjust to the text and their goals ("constructively responsive"). [Hiroshima Univ. analysis](https://ir.lib.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/00033991?locale=en)
- **Question generation and summarization:** both are among the NRP's seven supported strategies; these map to Adler's analytical reading. [EHDJ review](https://journal2.unusa.ac.id/index.php/EHDJ/article/download/2589/1680/14721)

### Inferences
- Adler's "preview, then read analytically" is consistent with the evidence on organizers and expert readers, but it is unvalidated as a package.
- A chapter preview or orientation before reading is a reasonable, low-risk feature. It should not be marketed as evidence-based in itself.

### Gaps
- I found no adult experiment on skimming or previewing a book before close reading.
- I found no empirical study of syntopical (multi-text) reading as Adler defines it. The related multiple-documents literature (for example Bråten and Strømsø) was not searched.

## 4. Does strategy instruction help skilled adult readers, or mainly struggling or younger readers? Diminishing returns?

### Takeaway
- The evidence comes overwhelmingly from children, struggling readers and adult basic-education learners.
- Effects are small on standardized tests and shrink in real classroom delivery.
- Willingham argues the benefit plateaus after about 5–10 hours of instruction. Critics say that argument is overstated.
- No meta-analysis focused on skilled adult readers was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Adult basic education:** a review of 17 experimental studies found an overall effect of Hedges g = 0.17. [KU CRL RER manuscript](https://kucrl.ku.edu/sites/kucrl/files/files/RER%20Manuscript%203-28-23.pdf)
- **Real classrooms:** effects shrink when teachers rather than researchers deliver the instruction (Okkinga 2018). [Rotterdam UAS](https://www.rotterdamuas.com/research/projects-and-publications/pub/effectiveness-of-reading-strategy-interventio/4c38634a-4354-4885-9093-6405172b969d/)
- **Willingham:** across about 12 meta-analyses, more instruction time adds nothing. 5–10 hours of instruction gives the same benefit as 20–30 hours. [Willingham 2024 reprint](https://info.multilit.com/hubfs/NOMANIS-17-JUN24/PDFS/WILLINGHAM-JUN-24.pdf); [ASCD](https://ascd.org/el/articles/beyond-comprehension); [NCTQ](https://nctq.org/blog/Dont-overdo-it-on-reading-comprehension-strategies)
- **Counterpoints:**
  - Shanahan notes that most studies ran six weeks or less, so the evidence on how much instruction is enough is weak and correlational. [Reading Rockets / Shanahan](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/how-much-comprehension-strategy-instruction)
  - Another critic argues Willingham's claim rests heavily on one 1996 question-generation study. [Authentic Education](https://authenticeducation.org/?p=2752)
- **Dunlosky's learner caveats:**
  - Summarization helps those already skilled at it.
  - Elaborative interrogation may need domain knowledge.
  - Whether self-explanation depends on knowledge or ability is unresolved.
  - [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)

### Inferences
- Skilled adult readers probably already use most comprehension strategies, as Pressley's expert-reader protocols suggest. They gain little from explicit strategy lessons.
- Skilled readers are more likely to benefit from techniques that build memory and depth: retrieval, spacing, self-explanation, and prompts that supply background knowledge.
- Background knowledge (context, vocabulary, allusions) is likely the main bottleneck for adults reading unfamiliar classics. An AI companion that supplies it on demand fits this.
- These points are inferences, not tested findings.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of strategy instruction for proficient adult or college readers was located.
- No studies of adults reading classics for pleasure or self-education were found.
- The Elleman meta-analysis on instructional duration was not retrieved.

## 5. Spacing, pacing and screen vs paper

### Takeaway
- Spaced rereading beats massed rereading on delayed tests, though massed rereading wins immediately.
- Paper beats screens modestly for informational texts (g = −0.21), especially under time pressure. There is no difference for narrative texts.
- Screen readers are more overconfident about how well they understood.

### Cited Findings
**Distributed practice**
- Rated high utility; "it works across students of different ages." [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)
- Rawson & Kintsch 2005, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 97(1), 70–80 (secondhand summary, 423 college students):
  - Immediate test: massed rereading beat a single reading.
  - Two-day delayed test: rereading spaced a week apart beat a single reading, and the massed advantage disappeared.
  - [Frontiers / PMC review citing it](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8554015/)
- Greving & Richter 2021 (seventh graders, non-repeated texts): massed reading was better immediately, the gap closed after one week, and there was no outright advantage for distributing the reading. [PMC8554015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8554015/)

**Screen vs paper: Delgado et al. 2018, *Educational Research Review* 25, 23–38**
- Scope: 54 studies, 171,055 participants, published 2000–2017. [Delgado et al. 2018 PDF](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)
- Overall effect: paper advantage of g = −0.21 (95% CI −0.28 to −0.14). The effect was robust to publication-bias checks and to removing the largest studies.
- Moderators:
  - Time-constrained reading: g = −0.26; self-paced reading: g = −0.09 (QB = 4.12, p = .04).
  - Informational texts: g = −0.27; mixed texts: g = −0.30; narrative-only texts: g = 0.01, no effect.
  - The paper advantage grew from 2000 to 2017.
  - Computers: g = −0.23; hand-held devices: g = −0.12 (non-significant). Scrolling required: g = −0.25; no scrolling: g = −0.13 (marginal).
  - No moderation by age group, text length or type of comprehension.
- The authors put the effect size in context: about 2/3 of a year's comprehension growth in elementary school, and about 1/2 of the effect of remedial interventions.

**Screen vs paper: Clinton 2019, *Journal of Research in Reading* 42(2), 288–325**
- Scope: 33 randomized studies, 2,799 participants.
- Screens had a small negative effect on performance, apparently limited to expository texts; there was no difference for narrative texts. The pattern was the same for literal and inferential questions.
- There was no reliable difference in reading time.
- Paper readers judged their own performance more accurately; screen readers were overconfident.
- [Reading Rockets abstract](https://www.readingrockets.org/research-by-topic/reading-paper-compared-screens-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis); [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/screen-reading-worse-for-comprehension-leads-to-overconfidence-new-meta-analysis-concludes/2019/04)

### Inferences
**Classics on screens**
- For fiction, which is narrative, the screen penalty is about zero.
- Philosophy and expository classics plausibly carry the informational-text penalty.
- Self-paced reading shrinks the gap to g ≈ −0.09. Tinct is self-paced and paginated rather than scrolling, which should minimize the penalty. That is consistent with Delgado's scrolling and time-frame moderators, but it is an inference.

**Features to counter overconfidence**
- Screen-reader overconfidence and poor metacomprehension (r ≈ 0.27) together argue for light retrieval checks.
- Reading in sessions with spaced return and a recall prompt on resuming ("what happened last time?") aligns with the spacing and testing evidence.

### Gaps
- I found no studies of spacing for long literary works read over weeks; the evidence uses short passages.
- I found no screen-vs-paper data specific to e-ink readers or to adult leisure reading of long books.
- I did not retrieve Clinton's exact effect sizes. The paper reports a small effect (often cited as g ≈ −0.25), but that figure is unverified.
