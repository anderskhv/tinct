# Motivation, Purpose-Setting and Background Knowledge as Preconditions for Comprehending Complex Texts

Scope note: almost all of the strong quantitative evidence below comes from **K-12 students**. Adult evidence comes mostly from short lab experiments with **college undergraduates** (schema and perspective studies, relevance instructions) and a small body of **adult/older-adult** structure-strategy work. **No study found tests these mechanisms on adults reading book-length literary or philosophical classics** (Ulysses, Plato, Kant). Every claim about classics below is an extrapolation.

Age flags used: **[K-12]** = children or adolescents; **[UG]** = undergraduates; **[Adult]** = general adults or older adults; **[Anecdotal]** = non-peer-reviewed data.

---

## (a) Reading motivation (intrinsic/extrinsic, interest, self-efficacy) and its effect on comprehension and persistence

### Takeaway
Motivation and comprehension are reliably but modestly linked (r ≈ .22 overall; r ≈ .27–.33 for interest and text learning). Interventions that raise motivation raise comprehension by about d = 0.27, and triggering **interest** is the mechanism with the clearest payoff. The causal arrow runs mostly from **reading skill to later motivation**, more than the reverse. For a classics app, this means early success on a manageable text is itself a motivator, so a "strong why" alone is not enough.

### Cited Findings
- **Motivation–achievement correlation [K-12]:** Toste et al. (2020), *Review of Educational Research* 90(3), 420–456, pooled 132 articles, 185 independent samples and 1,154 effect sizes and found **r = .22** between reading motivation and reading achievement. Which motivation construct was measured moderated the result. Reading domain, sample type and grade level did not. Longitudinal analyses showed a **bidirectional relationship, with earlier reading achievement a stronger predictor of later motivation than the reverse** — [UT Austin summary](https://education.utexas.edu/news/2020/04/08/review-of-education-research-features-article-by-toste-and-peng); [Iowa repository record](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-Meta-Analytic-Review-of-the-Relations/9984383293002771)
- **Interest and text learning [mostly UG/K-12 lab studies]:** Schiefele (1999), "Interest and Learning From Text," *Scientific Studies of Reading* 3(3), 257–279, reviewed 36 studies and reportedly found **r ≈ .27 for personal (topic) interest** and **r ≈ .33 for situational/text-based interest**. The relation is stronger for **deep-level** learning than for surface recall. These two figures come from a secondary citation; I did not see the original table — [Trames 2010 citing Schiefele](https://kirj.ee/public/trames_pdf/2010/issue_1/trames-2010-1-54-70.pdf); [Scinapse abstract](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2003388527)
- **Earlier interest–achievement meta-analysis:** Krapp, Schiefele & Winteler (1992), "Interest as a predictor of academic achievement: a meta-analysis of research." I did not retrieve its numbers — [Potsdam repository](https://publishup.uni-potsdam.de/opus4-ubp/frontdoor/index/index/docId/3169)
- **Motivation interventions, main effects [K-12, Grades 1–12 only]:** van der Sande, van Steensel, Fikrat-Wevers & Arends (2023), *Educational Psychology Review* 35:21, meta-analysed 39 experimental and quasi-experimental studies covering 40 interventions:
  - Affirming (intrinsic-type) motivation: **d = 0.38**
  - Extrinsic motivation: **d = 0.42**
  - Combined motivation: **d = 0.17**
  - Reading comprehension: **d = 0.27** (SE 0.05; 95% CI 0.17–0.37; little change after trim-and-fill)
  - Undermining motivation: **d = −0.01** (not significant)

  [van der Sande et al. 2023 (open access PDF)](https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/85465579/Effectiveness_of_Interventions_that_Foster_Reading_Motivation.pdf)
- **Moderators in the same meta-analysis:**
  - **Interventions that aimed to trigger interest** had the largest effects on both affirming motivation and comprehension.
  - The effect on affirming motivation faded from **d = 0.40 at immediate post-test to d = 0.19 (not significant) at delayed post-test**.
  - Comprehension effects were larger for **primary than secondary** students, larger for typical than struggling readers, and larger when researchers rather than teachers delivered the intervention.
  - Many studies did not report implementation fidelity.

  [van der Sande et al. 2023](https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/85465579/Effectiveness_of_Interventions_that_Foster_Reading_Motivation.pdf)
- **Guthrie & Wigfield's CORI (Concept-Oriented Reading Instruction) [K-12, elementary]:** CORI combines motivational support with strategy instruction inside a content domain such as science. Guthrie et al.'s (2007) meta-analysis found motivation effects of **d = 0.12 to 1.20 (median 0.30)** and comprehension effects of **d = 0.65 to 0.93**. These large comprehension effects come from a program that bundles **knowledge-rich content, strategies and motivation**, so they cannot be attributed to motivation alone — as summarized in [van der Sande et al. 2023](https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/85465579/Effectiveness_of_Interventions_that_Foster_Reading_Motivation.pdf)
- **Self-efficacy and broader interventions [K-12]:**
  - Unrau et al. (2018): reading self-efficacy interventions, **g = 0.33**.
  - McBreen & Savage (2020): motivation interventions, **g = 0.30 on motivation and g = 0.20 on reading achievement**.

  As reported in [van der Sande et al. 2023](https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/85465579/Effectiveness_of_Interventions_that_Foster_Reading_Motivation.pdf)
- **Interest strategy versus structure strategy [Adult, younger and older]:** Meyer & Poon (2001, *Journal of Educational Psychology*) gave younger and older adults 9 hours of structure-strategy training, interest-strategy training, or no training. **Structure-strategy training increased total recall and recall of the most important information.** The abstract does not report a comparable benefit for interest training — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/effects-of-structure-strategy-training-and-signaling-on-recall-of/)

### Inferences
- Motivation effects are real but small to moderate (d ≈ 0.2–0.4), and they **decay at delayed testing**. A one-time "strong why" at onboarding probably needs ongoing reinforcement through progress, social cues and interest triggers during reading.
- Because achievement predicts later motivation more strongly than the reverse (Toste et al.), **early competence experiences** probably matter as much as up-front purpose for persistence. Examples are an easier book on a reading path, or chapter 1 of a hard book with heavy support. This supports the "ramp" design.
- **Situational interest** (r ≈ .33) slightly outperforms personal topic interest (r ≈ .27). Pre-reading content that makes a specific text intriguing may therefore help more than asking about general interests. Such content could be a vivid hook, a "why it still matters" note, or a puzzle the book answers.
- For adults, structure training beat interest training in the one direct comparison found (Meyer & Poon 2001). Cognitive scaffolding may yield more than motivational scaffolding for complex texts.

### Gaps
- I found **no peer-reviewed study on adults' motivation and comprehension or completion for long literary or philosophical classics**. The evidence base is school children and short lab texts.
- I could not verify the original Schiefele (1999) figures or the Krapp et al. (1992) numbers first-hand. The .27/.33 values are secondary.
- I found no causal estimate linking motivation interventions to **book completion or persistence**. Outcomes are test comprehension and motivation questionnaires.

## (b1) Reading goals and purpose-setting: do they change what readers comprehend and remember?

### Takeaway
Yes, robustly, in adult and undergraduate lab studies. A reading perspective or goal changes **what** is encoded and recalled. Readers prioritise goal-relevant information, sometimes at the expense of other content. Purpose-setting is a **selective lens** more than a general boost, so the purpose given before reading should point at what matters in the book.

### Cited Findings
- **Perspective shapes encoding and retrieval [UG]:** Anderson & Pichert (1978), *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 17, 1–12. College undergraduates read a story about two boys skipping school from the perspective of either a **burglar or a home buyer**. When later told to shift perspective, they recalled **significantly more information important to the new perspective** that they had not reported the first time. Perspective therefore guides retrieval as well as encoding — [ERIC technical report ED142974](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED142974.pdf)
- **Earlier perspective study:** Pichert & Anderson (1977, *Journal of Educational Psychology*) showed that the assigned perspective changes the rated importance and recall of story elements. I could not retrieve the full text or effect sizes; the citation details come from my training knowledge.
- **Relevance instructions [mostly UG]:** McCrudden & Schraw (2007, *Educational Psychology Review*) reviewed the work. Instructions that tell readers what is relevant help them identify and learn goal-relevant segments. They give a taxonomy of four instruction types: **targeted segments, elaborative interrogation, perspective and purpose**. Their goal-focusing model explains effects through **attention allocation** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-006-9010-7)
- **General relevance instructions [UG]:** These improved fact learning, concept learning and essay quality **without increasing reading time**, which the authors called the "no-increased-effort" hypothesis — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-general-relevance-instructions-on-shallow-and-deepe/)
- **Relevance effects shrink as texts get harder [Adult EFL readers]:** One study found relevance-instruction effects were **larger for an easy text than for a difficult text** — [University of Hawaii, *Reading in a Foreign Language*](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/67412/1/34_1_10125-67412.pdf)
- **Knowing the topic beforehand [Adult, classic lab]:** Bransford & Johnson (1972) gave participants a deliberately vague passage (washing clothes) with the topic stated before reading, after reading, or not at all. **Only the title-before group** showed higher comprehension ratings and recall. Context given after reading did not help.
  - The commonly cited recall figures (about 5.8 versus 2.8 versus 2.6 idea units out of 18) come only from study-guide flashcards, so treat them as unverified — [Brainscape flashcard summary](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/cognitive-10303439/packs/18430409)
  - A 52-adult conference replication found the same direction of effect — [Atlantis Press 2021](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125955129.pdf)
  - A replication with 77 children aged 10–11 found that title-before helped "wholist" cognitive-style readers most — [Educational Psychology 1993](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0144341930130314)

### Inferences
- A pre-reading "reading angle" (Tinct's existing onboarding concept) is well-supported **as a lens**. It will raise recall of angle-relevant material and may lower attention to other material, which is a trade-off worth knowing.
- Bransford & Johnson implies the **framing must come before reading**. A summary after the chapter does less for initial comprehension than an orientation before it.
- The relevance-effect findings suggest goals help less on the hardest texts. On Kant or Ulysses, goal-setting likely needs to be paired with knowledge and structural support.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analytic effect size for purpose-setting or relevance instructions.
- I found no study of reading goals or perspectives across whole books, as opposed to short passages.

## (b2) Advance organizers, previewing, outlines and text-structure / signalling instruction

### Takeaway
Advance organizers have a **small positive** average effect (d ≈ 0.2), with large variation between studies. Explicit **text-structure instruction** shows moderate immediate effects, but they fade by delayed post-tests in Grades 4–6. Adult studies by Bonnie Meyer show that structure-strategy training improves recall of main ideas in younger and older adults. **Structural previews are worth doing but are not a silver bullet.**

### Cited Findings
- **Advance organizer meta-analyses:**
  - Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980, *American Educational Research Journal* 17, 211–218) meta-analysed advance-organizer effects on learning and retention. This was a response to Barnes & Clawson's earlier vote-count review of 32 studies, which had concluded organizers do not generally help. I could not retrieve Luiten et al.'s exact effect size — [Illinois IDEALS: Mahar 1992 updated meta-analysis](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)
  - Hattie's *Visible Learning* is quoted as summarising advance organizers as having "a small but facilitative effect on both learning and retention, with the effect increasing over time (**d = 0.21**)". This is a secondary quote; verify against Hattie's table — [Goodreads highlight of Visible Learning](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/6494760-visible-learning/78301694-brother-william/890a93b1-66fe-4bb1-82c3-68c1116510c5)
  - Stone (1983, *Journal of Experimental Education* 51, 194–199) also found organizers were associated with better learning and retention. Same source; no effect size retrieved.
  - Mahar's (1992) update covering studies from 1980–1990 found effects "generally positive despite wide variation" — [Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)
- **Text-structure instruction [K-12, Grades 4–6]:** Bogaerds-Hazenberg, Evers-Vermeul & van den Bergh (2021, *Reading Research Quarterly* 56(3), 435–462) meta-analysed 44 studies.
  - **Positive immediate effects** on comprehension, with large variation across outcome measures.
  - Moderate effects on summarisation, comprehension questions, structure knowledge and recall.
  - **No advantage over control groups on delayed post-tests.**

  I did not retrieve the exact g values — [Utrecht repository](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/408174); [ASHA evidence map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/61fb58ef-54ff-4a0f-a6f3-ae318f6ea0d5)
- **Other text-structure work [K-12]:** Shanahan summarises nearly 50 years of consistent findings, including the Hebert et al. (2016) and Pyle et al. (2017) meta-analyses, with effects "moderate to high". Benefits were greater when **writing** was included and when **graphic organizers and signal words** were used. Transfer was limited to texts with the same structure (Williams et al. 2016) — [Shanahan on Literacy](https://shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/does-text-structure-instruction-improve-reading-comprehension)
- **Structure strategy in adults [Adult]:**
  - Meyer & Poon (2001): about 9 hours of training raised total recall and recall of top-level ideas in both younger and older adults. Signalling affects **encoding** rather than retrieval — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/effects-of-structure-strategy-training-and-signaling-on-recall-of/)
  - Rice, Meyer & Miller (1989): 70 adults over 65 recalled more target ideas when medical texts were revised to place key ideas higher in the text structure — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/using-text-structure-to-improve-older-adults-recall-of-important-/)
  - Meyer & Rice (1981): among college-educated adults, only young adults showed the usual "levels effect" of better recall for main ideas — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/information-recalled-from-prose-by-young-middle-and-old-adult-rea/)

### Inferences
- A short **structural preview** before a chapter or book is a low-cost, evidence-consistent intervention. Examples: "this dialogue moves through three attempts to define justice", or "Ulysses chapter 3 is Stephen's interior monologue on the beach". The expected gain is small to moderate.
- Adult evidence (Meyer) supports teaching **how a text is organised**, especially for argumentative or expository texts such as Kant and Plato. Narrative-experimental texts such as Ulysses are untested.
- Effects fade without practice. Structural signalling probably needs repeating **throughout the book**, for example in chapter intros and in-reader structure markers, not only at onboarding.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve exact effect sizes for Luiten et al. (1980), Stone (1983) or Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2021).
- I found no studies of advance organizers for literary or philosophical classics read by adults.

## (b3) Background knowledge as a driver of comprehension

### Takeaway
This is the strongest and most consistent strand of evidence:
- Topic knowledge can **outweigh general reading skill**, as in the baseball study.
- Comprehension appears to have a **knowledge threshold** (about 59% on a topic test) below which reading is largely ineffective.
- A long-run knowledge-rich curriculum produced about **16 percentile points** of reading gains in a lottery-based RCT.

All of this evidence is **K-12**. For adults reading classics, the mechanism likely transfers: classics assume knowledge of myth, scripture, history and earlier philosophy. That transfer is untested.

### Cited Findings
- **Baseball study [K-12, Grades 7–8]:** Recht & Leslie (1988), *Journal of Educational Psychology*, "Effect of prior knowledge on good and poor readers' memory of text". Researchers crossed reading ability (high/low) with baseball knowledge (high/low) in **64 students** (16 per cell).
  - **Low-knowledge students did poorly regardless of reading ability.**
  - **Poor readers with high baseball knowledge outperformed good readers with low knowledge.**
  - It is a small behavioral study, and critics note it is often over-cited.

  [Wikipedia: Baseball Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_Study); [Core Knowledge Foundation summary](https://www.coreknowledge.org/baseball-experiment-two-wisconsin-researchers-discovered-comprehension-gap-knowledge-gap/); [Wexler on misconceptions](https://nataliewexler.substack.com/p/clearing-up-misconceptions-about-a44)
- **Knowledge threshold [K-12, high school]:** O'Reilly, Wang & Sabatini (2019), "How Much Knowledge Is Too Little? When a Lack of Knowledge Becomes a Barrier to Comprehension," *Psychological Science* 30(9), 1344–1351.
  - Sample: **3,534 high-school students in 37 U.S. schools**, who took an ecology background-knowledge test and then an ecology reading-comprehension test.
  - Broken-line regression found a threshold at about **33.5 points, or 59% correct**.
  - **Below the threshold, knowledge barely predicted comprehension (β = 0.18). Above it, the relationship was strong (β = 0.81).**
  - Certain key topic words (e.g., "ecosystem", "habitat") predicted being above the threshold.

  [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/knowledge-reading-comprehension.html); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797619862276); [Reading Rockets](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/how-much-knowledge-too-little-when-lack-knowledge-becomes-barrier)
- **Core Knowledge lottery RCT [K-12, K–6]:** Grissmer et al. (2023), EdWorkingPaper 23-755, also in *Education Next* (2024).
  - Design: 14 oversubscribed kindergarten lotteries for 9 Core Knowledge charter schools in Colorado, with **2,310 applicants and 688 selected**.
  - Grades 3–6 reading/ELA effects: **intent-to-treat 0.241 and treatment-on-the-treated 0.473** (both p < .001), equal to about **16 percentile points** cumulative by Grade 6.
  - Science ITT at Grade 5 was 0.15.
  - The effects were reportedly larger in schools with more disadvantaged students. That detail comes from my training knowledge, not from a page I checked.
  - The main criticism is generalisability, since all schools were charters.

  [Education Next PDF](https://www.educationnext.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/ednext_XIV_2_grissmer_et_al.pdf); [Fordham commentary](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/long-last-ed-hirsch-jr-gets-his-due-new-research-shows-big-benefits-core); [Arnold Ventures](https://www.arnoldventures.org/stories/long-term-follow-up-of-an-rct-of-core-knowledge-charter-schools); [Wexler on reception](https://nataliewexler.substack.com/p/is-the-buzz-about-knowledge-justified)
- **Willingham's argument (review/opinion, not new data):** Willingham argues that whether readers understand a text depends "far more on how much background knowledge and vocabulary they have relating to the topic than on how much they've practiced comprehension skills". His reasoning is that writers omit information they assume readers already have — "How Knowledge Helps," *American Educator* 30(1), Spring 2006 — [AFT, Spring 2006 issue](https://www.aft.org/ae/spring2006); [Keys to Literacy quoting Willingham](https://keystoliteracy.com/blog/background-knowledge-and-reading-comprehension/)
- **Willingham on strategy instruction:** His companion column says comprehension-strategy instruction is useful but brief — [Willingham, Comprehension Strategies](https://education.ufl.edu/patterson/files/2020/10/Willingham-ComprehensionStrategies.pdf)
- **Schema/context dependence in adults [Adult lab]:** Bransford & Johnson (1972), described in b1, shows that even skilled adult readers fail to comprehend when they cannot activate the relevant schema. This is the adult lab analogue of the knowledge effect.

### Inferences
- **The most defensible design implication for Tinct:** reading paths that build the specific knowledge a hard classic presupposes. Examples are the Odyssey and Hamlet before Ulysses, or Plato's Apology and Euthyphro before the Republic. Pre-reading primers on key concepts and vocabulary fit here too. This is closer to the knowledge-threshold logic than general "reading skill" practice.
- The threshold finding suggests a **short diagnostic quiz of key concepts** before a hard book could flag readers who need a ramp. The authors themselves propose pre-reading knowledge tests.
- A knowledge **glossary or cast list targeted at the "key words"** of a text may help readers cross the threshold. This parallels the O'Reilly et al. finding that particular topic words predicted performance.

### Gaps
- All rigorous knowledge evidence here is K-12. I found **no RCT of knowledge priming for adults reading classics**.
- I did not retrieve Smith et al. (2021) or other systematic reviews of background knowledge.
- The Grissmer effect is a multi-year curriculum effect. It says nothing about a brief pre-reading primer.

## (c) Book completion, abandonment and persistence (adults)

### Takeaway
There is essentially **no peer-reviewed research on adults abandoning hard books**. The available data are industry anecdotes and an explicitly "unscientific" Kindle-highlight index. Together they suggest that many readers quit challenging books early, often in the first 50–100 pages, but they cannot support quantitative claims.

### Cited Findings
- **Hawking Index [Anecdotal]:** Jordan Ellenberg (2014, *Wall Street Journal* blog) averaged the page positions of a book's top five Kindle highlights and divided by total length. Scores:
  - Hillary Clinton, *Hard Choices*: **1.9%**
  - Thomas Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*: **2.4%** (the last of its top highlights is on page 26 of about 700)
  - David Foster Wallace, *Infinite Jest*: **6.4%**
  - Stephen Hawking, *A Brief History of Time*: **6.6%**
  - Donna Tartt, *The Goldfinch*: **98.5%**

  Ellenberg called it "not remotely scientific", and it estimates average progress rather than completion rate — [Wikipedia: Hawking Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawking_Index); [CS Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/Books/chapter-and-verse/2014/0708/Which-are-the-most-unread-books-of-2014)
- **Goodreads abandonment survey [Anecdotal, 2013]:**
  - Goodreads analysed books its members marked as abandoned.
  - Among classics, *Catch-22* topped the abandoned list.
  - About **40% said they always finish a book**, about **15% quit before page 50** and **28% before page 100**.
  - Common reasons given were weak writing and slow pacing.

  [IOL](https://iol.co.za/entertainment/celebrity-news/2013-07-12-the-books-we-just-cant-finish/); [Globe and Mail](https://arc-dev.theglobeandmail.com/life/the-hot-button/the-top-summer-books-that-readers-couldnt-be-bothered-to-finish/article13107773)
- **Retailer observation [Anecdotal]:** Barnes & Noble reportedly found nonfiction more likely to be abandoned than novels — [Subscription Insider](https://www.subscriptioninsider.com/?p=3757)
- **Amazon page-read data:** Amazon holds per-page reading data that would show where readers quit, but it does not publish it — [Goodreads author blog (opinion)](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15535614-amazon-should-share-its-data)
- **Indirect evidence on persistence [K-12]:** Achievement predicts later motivation more strongly than the reverse (Toste et al. 2020 above), and motivation-intervention effects fade at delayed tests (van der Sande et al. 2023 above). Both suggest persistence depends on **ongoing experiences of comprehension success**, not on initial motivation alone.

### Inferences
- The highest risk of abandonment appears to be early in a hard book: the first 5–15%, or the first 50–100 pages. This argues for concentrating support (primers, structure previews, chat prompts) **at the start** and for treating chapter-1 completion as a key metric.
- Tinct's own reading-position data could become a better completion dataset than anything in the public literature. That is an opportunity, but it must be handled under the app's privacy commitments.

### Gaps
- I found **no peer-reviewed study of adult completion or abandonment rates for classics**, nor of the causes of abandonment for hard nonfiction or philosophy.
- I found no published Kindle or Amazon completion data, and no evidence that pre-reading onboarding raises adult book-completion rates.
