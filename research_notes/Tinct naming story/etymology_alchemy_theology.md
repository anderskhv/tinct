Research notes for the "Tinct" naming story. Quotes marked **[verified]** were checked word for word against a fetched primary text, either a digitized edition or a primary text quoted in a dictionary. Quotes marked **[unverified]** come only from secondary summaries or from memory, and the exact wording must be checked before use. Translations marked "my gloss" are my own literal renderings, not published translations.

## 1. Etymology: tingere, tinctus, tinctura, the PIE root, cognates, English entry dates and obsolete senses

### Takeaway
"Tinct" and "tincture" come from Latin *tingō / tinguō* ("to wet, moisten, dip; to dye"), past participle *tīnctus*, and *tīnctūra* ("act of dyeing"). These go back to a PIE root *\*teng-* meaning "to make wet / dip, soak". The same root probably gives Greek *τέγγω* and Germanic *tunken* / *dunk*. Through French *teindre / teint*, it also gives English **tint**, **taint** and **tinge**. The literal core meaning is *dipping into liquid until the colour takes*, which is exactly the transformation-by-immersion story. **Stain** is *not* from this root, though one sense of it was influenced by *distain*, which *is*.

### Cited Findings
- **Latin verb (Lewis & Short):** **tingo** (less correctly **tinguo**), with a root given as *tvak-* "to wet", Sanskrit *tuc-* and Greek **τέγγω**. Senses: **I.** "to wet, moisten, bathe with or in any liquid" (Cicero, Virgil, Ovid, Horace, Pliny). **I.B.1** "to soak in color, to dye, color, tinge". One sub-use is washing copper coins with gold or silver (*Digest* 48.10.8). **I.B.3** "to baptize (late Lat.)", illustrated with Lactantius 4.15.2, *"tinctus est ab Joanne propheta in Jordane flumine"* ("he was dipped by John the prophet in the river Jordan"). **II** (figurative): to be imbued with, or well furnished with, something (Cicero, *De Oratore* 2.20.85; *Brutus* 58.211). Derived forms: *tingens* "a dyer" (Pliny) and *tincta* "dyed stuffs" (Cicero, *Leg.* 2.18.45). — [Lewis & Short, *tingo*, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=tingo)
  - Note: Lewis & Short's own root (*tvak-*) is an older, 19th-century analysis. Modern references reconstruct *\*teng-* (next bullet).
- **PIE root:** Wiktionary reconstructs **\*teng-** "to make wet". Its references are LIV p. 628, Sihler 1995 p. 39 and Pokorny IEW p. 1067. Mallory & Adams (EIEC p. 639) note that the root has "limited attestation and may belong to Late Proto-Indo-European". Listed descendants include Greek **τέγγω** (aorist ἔτεγξα), Latin **tingō** (perfect **tīnxī**), Proto-Germanic **\*þinkaną / \*þunkōną** ("to make wet") and Old High German **\*dink** "moist". — [Wiktionary: Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/teng-](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/teng-)
  - Caution: the same page lists a **separate, homonymous root \*teng- "to think, perceive"**, the source of English *think* and *thank* and Latin *tongeō* "to know". These are two different roots. Do not merge them into "tinct = thinking" wordplay as if they were etymologically linked. As a pun it is fine, but it is not etymology. — [same page](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/teng-)
- **"Dunk" / German "tunken":** Wiktionary derives English *dunk* from Pennsylvania German *dunke*, from Middle High German *dunken*, from Old High German *dunkōn* "to dip, submerge, dunk", from Proto-West-Germanic *\*þunkōn* "to make wet". This is "possibly from" Proto-Germanic *\*þunkōną*, from PIE *\*teng-* "to moisten, wet". It is listed as "Cognate with German *tunken* 'to dunk', Latin *tingō* 'to wet, moisten', Ancient Greek τέγγω" and "related to *taint, tincture, tint*." — [Wiktionary: dunk](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/dunk)
  - Hedge: Wiktionary says "possibly". Use "dunk is probably a distant cousin", not "dunk comes from the same word".
- **Tincture (noun):** The noun comes from Late Middle English *tincture* (variants *tinctour, tinctur, tingture*). Its Middle English senses, per the Middle English Dictionary as cited by Wiktionary, include: "a dye, pigment; a colour, hue, tint; process of colouring or dyeing; medicinal ointment or salve…; (*alchemy*) transmutation of base metals into gold; ability to cause such transmutation; substance supposed to cause such transmutation". It is borrowed from Latin *tīnctūra* "act of dyeing", which is *tīnctus* "coloured, tinged; dipped in; impregnated with; treated" + *-tūra*. *Tīnctus* is the perfect passive participle of *tingō* ("to colour, dye, tinge; to dip (in), immerse; to impregnate (with); to moisten, wet; to smear"), ultimately from PIE *\*teng-* "to dip; to soak". The verb "to tincture" is derived from the noun. — [Wiktionary: tincture](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tincture), citing [MED *tinctūre*](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/m/middle-english-dictionary/dictionary/MED45897) and OED entries 202188 and 202189
  - So the **alchemical sense was already present in Middle English**, alongside dyeing and medicine. The three senses grew up together.
- **Tinct:** From Middle English *tynct* (noun) and *tincten / tyncten / tynkten* (verb), borrowed from Latin *tīnctus*. It is a doublet of *tint*. Senses: (archaic noun) "a tint or colour"; (verb) "to tint, tinge or colour"; (adjective) "tinged or lightly coloured"; also an abbreviation of *tincture*. — [Wiktionary: tinct](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tinct)
- **Tint:** "Alteration of earlier *tinct*, influenced by French *teinte*", from Latin *tīnctus*. Its cognates include French *teinte*, Italian/Spanish/Portuguese *tinta* and German *Tinte* ("ink"). — [Wiktionary: tint](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tint)
  - So German *Tinte* and Spanish *tinta* (ink) come from the same *tinctus*. That is a nice link for a reading app.
- **Taint:** From Middle French *teint*, from Old French *teint* (past participle of *teindre* "to dye, to tinge"), from Latin *tinctum*. Its obsolete senses include "Tincture; hue; colour". — [Wiktionary: taint](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/taint)
- **Tinge:** The verb comes directly from Latin *tingere* ("to dip; to moisten; to colour, dye, tinge"), with the OED as the cited source. — [Wiktionary: tinge](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tinge)
- **Stain is not a cognate:** *Stain* comes from Old Norse *steina* "to stain, colour, paint", from *steinn* "stone". However, some senses were "influenced by unrelated Middle English *disteynen* 'to discolor, remove the colour from'". That word comes from Anglo-Norman *desteindre* and Old French *des-* + *teindre* "to dye", from Latin *tingo*. — [Wiktionary: stain](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/stain)
- **Ingrain:** From Middle English *engreynen*, from the French phrase *en grain*, "reinforced by the phrase *(dyed) in grain*". See section 4. — [Wiktionary: ingrain](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ingrain)
- **Obsolete and figurative senses of "tincture"** (Wiktionary, with dated quotations):
  - Obsolete: a pigment or dye; the act of colouring or dyeing; "a slight addition of a thing to something else; a shade, a touch, a trace"; an imparted quality ("A goodness mixed with fear gives a tincture to all her behaviour", Steele, *Spectator* no. 144, 1711); **Christianity: synonym of baptism** (Drayton, *Poly-Olbion*, illustrations to Song 4: Rollo "honored in holy **tincture** of Christianity with the name of *Robert*"); and **alchemy**: "An immaterial substance or spiritual principle which was thought capable of being instilled into physical things; also, the essence or spirit of something". — [Wiktionary: tincture](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tincture)
- **Selected quotations for the story** (as quoted in Wiktionary, which cites the original printed editions):
  - Dryden, dedication to his *Juvenal* (1693), paraphrasing André Dacier: **"'Tis thus, says Dacier, that we lay a full Colour, when the Wool has taken the whole Tincture, and drunk in as much of the Dye as it can receive."** **[verified as quoted in Wiktionary; original spelling uses long-s]** — [Wiktionary: tincture](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tincture).   - *Spectator* no. 160 (3 Sept 1711): "The greatest genius which runs through the Arts and Sciences, takes a kind of tincture from them". — same
  - Thomson's *Works* (1762), "Life": "the life of a good writer is best read in his works; which can scarce fail to receive a peculiar tincture from his temper, manners, and habits". — same

  - Thomas Moffett, *The Silkewormes, and Their Flies* (1599), on silk: "A deathlesse tincture, sent us from the skies, / Whose colour stands, whose glosse is ne're appall'd". — same
- **Shakespeare uses of "tinct"** (checked in the Project Gutenberg Complete Works, eBook #100) **[verified]**:
  - *Hamlet* 3.4 (Gertrude): **"Thou turn'st mine eyes into my very soul, / And there I see such black and grained spots / As will not leave their tinct."** This line combines "grained" (dyed in grain, i.e. colour-fast) with "tinct".
  - *All's Well That Ends Well* 5.3 (King): **"Plutus himself, / That knows the tinct and multiplying medicine, / Hath not in nature's mystery more science / Than I have in this ring."** This is an explicitly alchemical use: the tincture that multiplies gold.
  - *Antony and Cleopatra* 1.5 (Cleopatra to Alexas): **"Yet, coming from him, that great medicine hath / With his tinct gilded thee."** Here the alchemical elixir gilds by contact.
  - *Cymbeline* 2.2 (Iachimo): "Under these windows white and azure, lac'd / With blue of heaven's own tinct."

  - — [Project Gutenberg, *The Complete Works of William Shakespeare*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt)
- **Spenser, *Shepheardes Calender*, "November" (1579):** "The blew in black, the greene in gray is tinct". This is the adjective sense. — [Wiktionary: tinct](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tinct)

### Inferences
- The literal Latin meaning runs from *wet → dip/immerse → dye → imbue*, and late Latin adds *baptize*. Every stage involves contact with a liquid that leaves the thing changed. That gives the brand a coherent arc: you dip into a book, it soaks in, and you come out coloured by it.
- *Tint*, *taint*, *tinge*, *tincture*, *tinct*, German *Tinte* and Spanish *tinta* (ink) form one word family. "Ink" being a cousin is useful for a reading product.
- English "tinct" is attested at least by 1579 (Spenser, adjective) and is common in Shakespeare. The Middle English forms *tynct / tincten* mean it existed before that, but see Gaps.

### Gaps
- **Exact OED and Etymonline first-attestation dates** for *tinct* and *tincture* could not be retrieved, because Etymonline returned a Cloudflare challenge and the OED is paywalled. From memory, Etymonline dates *tincture* to about 1400 and *tinct* (noun) to the late 1400s, but **this is unverified; do not print it without checking**. The MED headword confirms that *tincture* is Late Middle English (c. 1350–1500), with an alchemical sense.
- Merriam-Webster was not checked.

## 2. Alchemy: the tincture as transmuting agent, projection, multiplication, red vs white, and the main authors

### Takeaway
In alchemical usage the "tincture" is the colouring and transmuting virtue of the philosopher's stone. **Projection** means casting a small amount of that stone or tincture onto molten base metal or mercury. Alchemists claimed the medicine could be **multiplied** without limit, so that one part would turn 100, then 1,000 and up to "a thousand thousand" parts of base metal. Two texts state this outright in verse. George Ripley (1471) gives a doubling-table into "ten thousand myllyons", and Ben Jonson's *The Alchemist* (1610) satirises the same arithmetic. Red medicine makes gold and white makes silver. Ripley openly compares the alchemist's tincture to the **dyer's** colour that "will never forsake the cloth", which is the strongest primary-source bridge between the dyeing and alchemy meanings.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition (Jonson glossary, Gutenberg edition):** "TINCTURE, an essential or spiritual principle supposed by alchemists to be transfusible into material things; an imparted characteristic or tendency." "PROJECTION, the throwing of the 'powder of projection' into the crucible to turn the melted metal into gold or silver." These are editorial glossary entries, not Jonson's own words. — [Project Gutenberg #4081, *The Alchemist*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4081/pg4081.txt)
- **Projection overview:** "Projection was the ultimate goal of Western alchemy… Typically, the process is described as casting a small portion of the Stone into a molten base metal." — [Wikipedia: Projection (alchemy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_(alchemy))

**George Ripley, *The Compound of Alchymy* (dated 1471 in the text; dedicated to Edward IV; printed 1591)** **[verified against the Alchemy Web Site transcription]** — [Alchemy Web Site: The Compound of Alchymie](https://alchemywebsite.com/tcbcompound.html)
- **The dyer analogy** (from the prefatory material, before the First Gate):
  > "Att the Dyers craft ye may lerne this Science, / Beholding with Water how they decoctions make / Uppon theyr Wood and Maddre easyly and with patience, / Till the Tinctures appeare which the Cloath doth take / Therein so fixed that they wyll never forsake / The Cloth for washing after they joyned be; / Right so our Tinctures with Water of our Lake / We draw by boyling with Ashes of Hermes tree. / Which Tinctures when they be by craft are made parfite, / So dieth Mettalls with Colours evermore permanent, / After the qualitie if the Medycine Red or White; / That never away by eny Fire, will be brente"

  This passage is the anchor quote. In it, the alchemist's tincture works like a dyer's madder: it "dyes" metal with "Colours evermore permanent" that no washing or fire removes. It also names the **Red or White** medicine.
- Same section: "And yet the Whtye must come fyrst of the Red" and "**One ounce well worth a thousand pound.**"
- **The Twelfth Gate, "Of Projection":** "Therfore yf thy Tyncture be sure and not varyable, / By a lyytyll of thy Medcyn thus shall thou preve / Wyth Mettall or wyth Mercury as Pyche yt wyll cleve: / And Tynct in Projeccyon all Fyers to abyde, / **And sone yt wyll enter and spred hym full wyde.**" (stanza 1)
  - The last line, "soon it will enter and spread him full wide", describes a small amount that penetrates and spreads through the whole mass.
- Stanza 3: base metals "shalbe curyosely coleryd wyth Tyncture, / Whych at all assays for ever shall endure".
- Stanza 5: "thou must cast fyrst the lesse on the more, / Incresyng ever the Number".
- Stanzas 6–7 give the multiplication table: "Ten yf thou Multyply fyrst into ten, / One hundreth, that number wyll make sykerly; / Yf one hundreth into an hundreth be Multyplyed then, / Ten thousand ys that number… / That ys a thousand thousand… / Into as much more as a hundred myllyons ys. / That hundred myllyons beyng multyplyed lykewys, / Into ten thousand myllyons, that ys for to sey, / **Makyth so grete a number I wote not what yt ys**".
- The Eleventh Gate (Multiplication) compares the medicine to fire: one spark kindles more, so the Elixir can be augmented indefinitely. This is a summary, not a verified quote. — [search summary of the Art History Project transcription](https://arthistoryproject.com/artists/george-ripley/the-compound-of-alchemy/calcination-1/) **[paraphrase only, wording not verified]**

**Ben Jonson, *The Alchemist* (performed 1610, printed 1612)** **[verified against Gutenberg #4081]** — [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4081/pg4081.txt)
- Act 2 (Sir Epicure Mammon, in the opening scene of Act 2): **"But when you see th' effects of the Great Medicine, / Of which one part projected on a hundred / Of Mercury, or Venus, or the moon, / Shall turn it to as many of the sun; / Nay, to a thousand, so ad infinitum"**. He continues: "He that has once the flower of the sun, / The perfect ruby, which we call elixir, / Not only can do that, but, by its virtue, / Can confer honour, love, respect, long life… In eight and twenty days, / I'll make an old man of fourscore, a child."
  - In the alchemical code: Mercury = quicksilver, Venus = copper, moon = silver, sun = gold. The "perfect ruby" is the red stone.
- Act 2 (Subtle, answering Mammon's question "When do you make projection?"): **"Son, be not hasty, I exalt our med'cine… For look, how oft I iterate the work, / So many times I add unto his virtue. / As, if at first one ounce convert a hundred, / After his second loose, he'll turn a thousand; / His third solution, ten; his fourth, a hundred: / After his fifth, a thousand thousand ounces / Of any imperfect metal, into pure / Silver or gold"**. Wikipedia cites this passage as Act 2 Scene 1. Many modern editions place it in 2.3, so check the line numbering against the edition you cite.
- Act 1 (Subtle to Face): "Wrought thee to spirit, to quintessence… Giv'n thee thy oaths… Dice, or whatever gallant **tincture** else? … Do you fly out in the **projection**?" Here the alchemist frames remaking a man, Face, as an alchemical work.
- Other "tincture" lines: "Infuse vinegar, / To draw his volatile substance and his tincture"; "I mean to tinct C in sand-heat to-morrow"; "with a tincture make you as good Dutch dollars / As any are in Holland"; and Act 4 "Tincture of pearl, and coral, gold, and amber".
- Caveat: Jonson is satirising alchemists as con men. Quote these lines as comedy, not as earnest alchemical doctrine.

### Inferences
- "A tiny amount changes a great mass" is not a modern reading. Medieval and early-modern alchemists stated it explicitly as arithmetic: Ripley's "cast first the less on the more" and his doubling into "ten thousand myllyons", and Jonson's "one ounce… a thousand thousand ounces". Shakespeare calls it "the tinct and multiplying medicine".
- Ripley's dyer passage shows that in alchemists' own minds, the alchemical tincture was a deliberate extension of textile dyeing: a colour so fixed it survives washing and fire. Dyeing and alchemy are one metaphor, not two.
- For brand tone: alchemy also carries fraud connotations (Jonson's whole play is about con men; Maier and Khunrath catalogued tricks). The story should lean on the *dye* and *imbue* senses and use the alchemical "projection" image playfully. It should not promise "turn lead into gold".

### Gaps
- **No verified Newton quote containing "tincture"** was found. Newton certainly used the term, since it is standard in the texts he copied (Philalethes/Starkey and others), but the Chymistry of Isaac Newton search interface was not reachable. Do not attribute a specific tincture line to Newton without checking it at [chymistry.org / webapp1.dlib.indiana.edu/newton](https://webapp1.dlib.indiana.edu/newton/).
- **Sendivogius and Basil Valentine:** no primary-text tincture quotations were verified. (The "Twelve Keys" of Basil Valentine probably contain suitable lines, but they were not checked.)
- **Paracelsus:** no primary quotation was verified (site blocked).
- The ratios in van Helmont and Helvetius come from secondary English renderings.

## 3. Early Christian usage: Tertullian's *tinguere* = "to baptize"

### Takeaway
Tertullian (c. 200 AD) prefers the native Latin verb *tinguere / tingere* ("to dip, to dye") over the Greek loanword *baptizare*. In *De Baptismo* the Christian is literally "dyed" or "dipped": *tinctus*, *tinguimur* ("we are dipped"). He even renders Christ's Great Commission (Matt. 28:19) as *tinguentes eas* ("dipping them"). His most quotable line describes a man "let down into the water… dipped… rising again hardly any cleaner", and yet, Tertullian's point is, rising transformed. Later English kept a faint trace of this: Drayton could call baptism "holy tincture".

### Cited Findings
All Latin below is **[verified]** against the Latin Library text of *De Baptismo* (Reifferscheid–Wissowa / Evans text). Chapter numbers were derived from the CAP. markers in that text. English glosses are mine unless noted. — [The Latin Library: Tertullian, *De Baptismo*](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/tertullian/tertullian.baptismo.shtml)

- **De Bapt. 2.1** (the heart of the "dipped and changed" story): *"…homo in aqua demissus et inter pauca verba tinctus non multo vel nihilo mundior resurgit, eo incredibilis existimatur consecutio aeternitatis."* My gloss: "[because] a man is let down into the water and, amid a few words, dipped, and rises again not much — or not at all — cleaner, the attainment of eternity is thought incredible." Tertullian is reporting what sceptics say: so little outward change, so great a claimed inward change. That is the small-cause, great-effect theme again.
- **De Bapt. 1.3:** *"sed nos pisciculi secundum ἰχθύν nostrum Iesum Christum in aqua nascimur, nec aliter quam in aqua permanendo salvi sumus."* Gloss: "But we little fishes, after the example of our ΙΧΘΥΣ Jesus Christ, are born in water, and only by remaining in the water are we safe." (The online text renders the Greek as "i0xqu".)
- **De Bapt. 4.1:** the Spirit that moved over the waters at Creation is described as "*super aquas intinctorem moraturum*", the one who would linger over the waters as **the dipper** (*intinctor*, "the one who dips/dyes in").
- **De Bapt. 4.2:** *"numquid ipsis enim aquis tinguimur quae tunc in primordio fuerunt?"* Gloss: "Are we then dipped in the very waters that existed in the beginning?"
- **De Bapt. 4.3:** "…*quos Ioannes in Iordane et quos Petrus in Tiberi tinxit*" ("those whom John dipped in the Jordan and those whom Peter dipped in the Tiber").
- **De Bapt. 10:** *"…venturum mox qui tingueret in spiritu et igni: scilicet quia vera et stabilis fides spiritu tinguitur in salutem, simulata autem et infirma igni tinguitur in iudicium."* Gloss: "…[John said] that one was soon coming who would dip in spirit and fire: because true and steadfast faith is dipped in spirit unto salvation, but feigned and weak faith is dipped in fire unto judgement."
- **De Bapt. 12.6:** *"aliud est aspergi vel intercipi violentia maris, aliud tingui disciplina religionis."* Gloss: "It is one thing to be sprinkled or caught by the violence of the sea, another to be dipped by the discipline of religion." Getting splashed is not the same as being dyed, and that maps neatly onto surface colour versus colour that soaks through.
- **De Bapt. 13.3** (Matt. 28:19 in Tertullian's Latin): *"lex enim tinguendi imposita est, et forma praescripta: Ite, inquit, docete nationes tinguentes eas in nomine patris et filii et spiritus sancti."* Gloss: "For the law of dipping has been laid down and its form prescribed: Go, he says, teach the nations, dipping them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." Also *"itaque omnes exinde credentes tinguebantur"* ("and so from then on all believers were dipped").
- **De Bapt. 16.1:** *"Habeo, inquit, baptismo tingui, cum iam tinctus fuisset."* Gloss: "'I have', he says, 'a baptism to be dipped with', though he had already been dipped." This is the "second washing" of blood (martyrdom). Note that here Tertullian uses both *baptismus* and *tingui* in one sentence.
- **De Bapt. 19.1:** *"…cum et passio domini in qua tinguimur adimpleta est."* Gloss: "…when the Lord's passion, into which we are dipped, was fulfilled."
- Tertullian also applies *tinguuntur* to pagan rites: at the Apollinarian and Pelusian games people "are dipped" (*tinguuntur*) and presume this works "*in regenerationem*", for rebirth (De Bapt. 5.1).
- **Later Latin Fathers:** Lactantius (*Div. Inst.* 4.15.2) writes of Jesus: *"tinctus est ab Joanne propheta in Jordane flumine"*. This is the example Lewis & Short give for *tingo* = "to baptize (late Lat.)". — [Lewis & Short, *tingo*](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=tingo)
- **A published English translation** exists: E. Evans, *Tertullian's Homily on Baptism* (1964), online at the Tertullian Project. — [tertullian.org: Evans, De Baptismo](https://www.tertullian.org/articles/evans_bapt/evans_bapt_index.htm) (the index page link was confirmed, but the translation text was not fetched)

### Inferences
- For Tertullian's Latin readers, *tingere* was everyday dyers' vocabulary (*tingens* = dyer, *tincta* = dyed goods, per Lewis & Short). Calling baptism *tinguere* therefore carried the overtone of being dyed: plunged in, and coming out a different colour. This is an inference from the word's ordinary sense. I did not find a source where Tertullian explicitly draws the dyeing metaphor.
- The 2.1 passage, with little outward change and an eternal inward one, parallels the alchemical "small amount, great effect".

### Gaps
- Evans's (or any published) English wording was not verified, so the English above is my own gloss. If you want a published translation in the copy, fetch the Evans text and quote it exactly.
- The *Adversus Praxean* 26 passage on triple immersion ("*nec semel sed ter… tinguimur*") is recalled from memory. The Latin Library fetch returned no match, so **the wording is unverified**.
- Whether Tertullian or any Father explicitly plays on the dyeing sense was not established.
- Cyprian's usage was not checked.

## 4. Dyeing history: "dyed in grain", kermes, Tyrian purple, colour that soaks through

### Takeaway
"Grain" was the medieval name for **kermes**, the insect dye for the most prized scarlets. The insects' dried bodies looked like grains and were thought to be seeds. Cloth "dyed in grain" was therefore colour-fast, and the phrase drifted into "ingrained" (deep-set, permanent). Shakespeare uses it for colour that no water can remove. Tyrian purple was the ancient benchmark of a precious, colour-fast dye. Ripley (section 2) explicitly models the alchemical tincture on dyed cloth whose colour "will never forsake" it.

### Cited Findings
- Kermes "was called 'grain' (*grana*) in all Western European languages because the desiccated eggs resembled fine grains of wheat (or sand), and they were mistaken for plants; so textiles dyed with kermes were described as *dyed in the grain*" (citing Munro, "Medieval Woollens", in Jenkins 2003, pp. 214–215). — [Wikipedia: Kermes (dye)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermes_(dye))
- "By the 14th and early 15th century, brilliant *full grain* pure kermes scarlet was 'by far the most esteemed, most regal' colour for luxury woollen textiles" in the Low Countries, England, France, Spain and Italy (citing Munro 2007). Kermes-dyed silks in medieval Italy and Sicily "exceeded the legendary Tyrian purple 'in status and desirability'" (citing Schoeser 2007). — [Wikipedia: Kermes (dye)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermes_(dye))
- The Romans knew the insect as *grani coccum*, "the grain of scarlet". — [Wikipedia: Kermes (dye)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermes_(dye))
- *Ingrain* comes from Middle English *engreynen*, from French *en grain*, "reinforced by the phrase *(dyed) in grain*". — [Wiktionary: ingrain](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ingrain)
- **Shakespeare on "in grain"** **[verified, Gutenberg #100]**:
  - *Twelfth Night* 1.5 (Olivia, on her face): **"'Tis in grain, sir; 'twill endure wind and weather."**
  - *The Comedy of Errors* 3.2 (Dromio): **"No, sir, 'tis in grain; Noah's flood could not do it."**
  - *Hamlet* 3.4: "such black and grained spots / As will not leave their tinct." This is the negative mirror: a stain that soaks through can't be washed out either.
  - — [Project Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt)
- **Tyrian purple:** "The colour-fast (non-fading) dye was an item of luxury trade, prized by Romans, who used it to colour ceremonial robes." Pliny ranked Tyre's purple first and Meninx (Djerba) second in Africa. — [Wikipedia: Tyrian purple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrian_purple)
- **Wool that drinks in the whole dye:** in Dryden after Dacier (1693), "when the Wool has taken the whole Tincture, and drunk in as much of the Dye as it can receive". — [Wiktionary: tincture](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tincture)
- **Fixity as the alchemical ideal:** Ripley describes tinctures "so fixed that they wyll never forsake / The Cloth for washing", and says alchemical tinctures dye metals "with Colours evermore permanent… That never away by eny Fire, will be brente". — [Alchemy Web Site: Ripley](https://alchemywebsite.com/tcbcompound.html)

### Inferences
- The surface-versus-soaked-through contrast is built into the historical vocabulary. Cloth "dyed in grain" (and Tertullian's *tingui*, as opposed to being merely *aspergi*, sprinkled) is coloured through. A tint or wash sits on the surface.
- A tagline-level reading supported by the sources: a classic read deeply is "dyed in grain": it "will endure wind and weather".

### Gaps
- **Pliny's figures for murex** (for example the number of shells per pound of dye) and the Latin terms *dibapha* ("twice-dipped" purple, Pliny *NH* 9.137 / 9.63) were not verified in this pass. Check them in Pliny, *Natural History* book 9, before use.
- Primary medieval dye-guild sources were not consulted; the kermes material relies on Munro and Schoeser as cited by Wikipedia.
