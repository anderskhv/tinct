# Tinct: Name Associations, Naming-Story Precedents and the Quote That Goes With It

Researched 2026-10-09. Primary texts were downloaded from Project Gutenberg and Wikisource and searched directly. The quotes below are verbatim from those files.

## 1. Existing uses and associations of "tinct" / "tincture"; brands already using the name

### Takeaway
"Tinct" is an uncrowded name. The only live product using exactly "Tinct" that I found is a small paid colour-mixing puzzle game from Spain. The nearby "TINT" marketing company and the medical "tincture" sense are the main sources of confusion. The word's oldest meaning is about dye and colour, not medicine, and the alchemical sense ("tincture" = the agent that turns base metal into gold) is a strong, warm asset.

### Cited Findings
**Etymology and meanings**
- "Tincture" entered English from Latin *tinctura*, "dyeing". Dictionary.com dates it to 1350–1400, with the Middle English sense "dye". Etymonline traces it to *tinctus*, past participle of *tingere*, "to tinge, dye, soak in color", originally "moisten, wet, soak", from PIE *teng-* "to soak". — [Dictionary.com](https://dictionary.com/browse/tincture); [Etymonline: tincture](https://www.etymonline.com/word/tincture) (Etymonline content comes from a search summary; a direct fetch returned 403)
- Etymonline dates the noun "tinct" to c. 1600, from Latin *tinctus*, "a dyeing". — [Etymonline: tinct](https://www.etymonline.com/word/tinct) (search summary only)
- Sense history: the medical sense dates to c. 1400 (ointments called tinctures). The pharmacy sense, "solution of medicine in alcohol", is recorded in the 1640s. The figurative sense, "infused or derived quality or tone" (as in "a tincture of irony"), dates to the 1610s. The literal "dye/pigment" sense is now marked obsolete or dated (Collins). — [Etymonline: tincture](https://www.etymonline.com/word/tincture) (search summary)
- Heraldry: "tincture" is still the standard term for the colours, metals and furs of a coat of arms. — [Dictionary.com](https://dictionary.com/browse/tincture)
- Alchemy: in alchemical writing, "the tincture" is a name for the elixir or philosopher's stone, which turns inferior metals into gold when projected onto them. — [Source Library encyclopedia, "Physical Tincture"](https://sourcelibrary.org/encyclopedia/Physical%20Tincture); [Ficino Source Library, "Red Tincture"](https://ficino.sourcelibrary.org/encyclopedia/Red%20Tincture)

**Shakespeare uses "tinct" twice. Both are verified verbatim in Project Gutenberg texts.**
- *Hamlet* (3.4), Gertrude: "Thou turn'st mine eyes into my very soul, / And there I see such black and grained spots / As will not leave their tinct." This is a soul's colour that won't wash out. The context is dark (guilt). — [Project Gutenberg #1524, Hamlet](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1524/pg1524.txt)
- *Antony and Cleopatra* (1.5), Cleopatra to Alexas: "Yet, coming from him, that great medicine hath / With his tinct gilded thee." — [Project Gutenberg #1534](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1534/pg1534.txt)
- Cambridge's "Slow Shakespeare" commentary reads that line alchemically: Antony as the philosopher's stone, turning to gold whatever he touches, so Alexas is gilded by contact with him. This is the warm, transformative meaning of "tinct" ("medicine" is the alchemical elixir here, not pharmacy). — [Cambridge English Faculty, Slow Shakespeare 1.5.34-41](https://www.english.cam.ac.uk/research/slow-shakespeare/?p=129); see also [Sydney Studies in English article on alchemical allusions in the play](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/SSE/article/view/390/360)

**Marcus Aurelius already uses the word.** The 1634 Casaubon translation renders *Meditations* 5.16 with "tincture" (see section 4).

**Brands and products using the name**
- **"Tinct – Think in Color"**: a paid ($3.49 on Google Play) colour-mixing logic puzzle game by Piqture New Media, SL (Lleida, Spain), on iOS and Android. AppBrain's snapshot showed very few downloads (about 27) and no ratings. Its App Store version history showed 1.2.0 on 27 Aug. This is the only exact-name app found. It is a different category (games, not books/education), but it shares the app-store namespace. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ro/app/tinct-think-in-color/id6759222895); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.piqture.tinct&hl=en); [AppBrain](https://www.appbrain.com/app/tinct/com.piqture.tinct)
- **TINT**: a UGC and brand-community marketing platform (San Antonio, founded 2012), reportedly merged with Zinrelo into "TrueLoyal" in Aug 2025. It also has a Shopify app. The spelling differs, but search engines conflate "Tinct" with "Tint". — [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/compare/crowdstack-vs-hypemarks); [Shopify app](https://apps.shopify.com/tint)
- **Tincture**: a UK home-wellbeing and cleaning-products brand launched 2016 by Rain Communications. — [Gorkana](https://stage2.gorkana.com/2016/07/rain-communications-to-launch-new-home-wellbeing-brand-tincture/)
- A general "Tinct" search returns almost nothing under the exact name. The search engine itself fell back to "Tint" results, which is direct evidence of the spelling/pronunciation drift. — [search result set above; e.g., Tint (disambiguation), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tint_(disambiguation))

### Inferences
- The "cold/clinical" worry is about one sense, the pharmacy tincture (CBD and herbal tinctures dominate everyday usage, though I did not quantify this). The older and richer senses (dye, heraldic colour, alchemical gold-maker, Shakespeare, Marcus Aurelius) are all warm, literary and transformative. A naming story that leads with *dye* and *gold* will push the medical association aside.
- Pronunciation: people will likely say or hear "tint". That is not fatal, because tint is semantically adjacent (colour), but it hurts spoken word-of-mouth and search ("tint app"). Owning tinct.app helps. A spoken line like "Tinct, as in tincture" or a tagline that uses the word "tincture" would teach the spelling.
- The Shakespeare *Hamlet* line is memorable but guilt-coloured. The *Antony and Cleopatra* "gilded" line and Marcus Aurelius/Seneca are better fits for a brand story.

### Gaps
- No USPTO/EUIPO trademark search was performed. Those systems were not queried, so trademark conflicts in Classes 9/41/42 are unverified. Recommend a formal clearance search.
- No Crunchbase check was done. Whether there are design studios, bitters or spirits brands, or Shopify stores named "Tinct" outside app stores is not established; the general web search surfaced none.
- I did not quantify how dominant the CBD/herbal "tincture" sense is today (e.g., via Google Trends).

## 2. How Kindle and other reading/heritage brands got their names, and what made the stories work

### Takeaway
The best-documented precedent is Kindle. Branding consultants Michael Cronan and Karin Hibma chose an old, plain word meaning "to set alight" because it was a concrete image of what reading does to a person. They checked it had literary roots (a Voltaire line about kindling), and it worked in ordinary speech ("curling up with my Kindle"). Patagonia and Oura show two other mechanics: an evocative far-off image, and a hidden second meaning tied to the customer's choice.

### Cited Findings
**Kindle**
- Amazon's Lab126 hired Michael Cronan and Karin Hibma, a husband-and-wife brand consultancy in the Bay Area, to name the e-reader. The device was codenamed "Fiona" before that. — [Print magazine, Steven Heller, "Who Named the Kindle (and Why)?"](https://www.printmag.com/daily-heller/who-named-the-kindle-and-why/) (direct fetch returned 403; details from search summary and secondary reports); [How-To Geek, Joe Fedewa, 16 Feb 2023](https://www.howtogeek.com/870829/why-is-it-called-an-amazon-kindle-ereader/); [ifanr (Chinese, citing Heller), 2011](https://www.ifanr.com/54315)
- According to Hibma, Bezos wanted a name that conveyed the future of reading without sounding "techie". The name had to work in phrases like "curling up with my Kindle", and the team liked the metaphor of "lighting a fire" of excitement about reading. — [How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/870829/why-is-it-called-an-amazon-kindle-ereader/) (its cited source is Nancy Friedman's 2008 "Away With Words" blog post, which is now dead/redirecting)
- Hibma said she "verified that it had deep roots in literature" and pointed to a Voltaire quotation about books kindling fire in readers. — [Print magazine via search summary](https://www.printmag.com/daily-heller/who-named-the-kindle-and-why/). Note: Print spells her name "Karen"; most sources use "Karin". How-To Geek misspells it "Himba".
- Amazon later used the Voltaire line in the voiceover of its Kindle Fire launch ad (2011). Adweek reported that Amazon derived the Kindle names from the Voltaire quote. — [Adweek, "Voltaire Stumps for Amazon in First Ad for Kindle Fire"](https://www.adweek.com/?p=135322)
- "Kindle" comes from Old Norse *kyndill*, "candle". — [ifanr](https://www.ifanr.com/54315); [Wikipedia: Amazon Kindle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Kindle)

**Patagonia** (the company's own account)
- The name was chosen in 1973 for the clothing line. "Chouinard" was rejected so as not to dilute the climbing-tool brand or tie the clothes only to climbing. The company wrote that, to most people then, Patagonia was a name like "Timbuktu or Shangri-La, far-off, interesting, not quite on the map", conjuring "romantic visions of glaciers tumbling into fjords, jagged windswept peaks, gauchos and condors". — [Patagonia, "What Can a Brand Name Mean? (And How Did Ours Come to Be?)"](https://www.patagonia.ca/story-20353.html) (wording from search summary of the company page)

**Oura**
- Oura's original name was "Jouzen", from Finnish *joutsen* (swan, the national bird) plus "zen" for calm. It also deliberately echoed English "chosen", since members have "chosen" to prioritise their health. It was renamed "Oura" before launch. — [Oura blog, "History of Oura"](https://ouraring.com/blog/history-of-oura/) (fetched directly)

**Libby (OverDrive)**
- I found no official OverDrive explanation. A library FAQ says the new name and brand were meant to attract a new audience and give lapsed users a fresh reason to return. Libby launched in beta in Dec 2016 and generally in June 2017. — [Lennox & Addington County Library](https://lennox-addington.on.ca/news/introducing-libby-overdrive); [Wikipedia: Libby (service)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libby_(service))

**Scribd**
- The founding story is documented: Trip Adler's father waited 18 months for a medical paper to be published; the company was founded by Adler, Jared Friedman and Tikhon Bernstam; it was in Y Combinator's first batch in 2006 and launched in March 2007. No source explains the name itself. — [Wikipedia: Scribd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribd)

### Inferences
Patterns in the stories that work (Kindle, Patagonia, Oura):
1. **One concrete physical image** (a fire being lit; glaciers and condors; a swan) rather than an abstract benefit.
2. **The image is the product promise.** Kindle = reading lights you up. For Tinct, the parallel is "reading dyes you": you come out a different colour.
3. **A literary or heritage pedigree that can be checked.** Hibma explicitly "verified" roots in literature, and Amazon later put Voltaire in an ad. Tinct has an unusually strong pedigree here: Marcus Aurelius (in the 1634 English, using the literal word "tincture"), Seneca and Shakespeare.
4. **Works in everyday speech** ("curling up with my Kindle"). Test phrases for Tinct: "I read it on Tinct"; "a tinct of Homer every night". The verb or noun use is less natural than Kindle's, so this is the weakest link.
5. **A hidden second layer is a bonus, not the main point** (Oura's "chosen"). For Tinct, the second layers are alchemy (tincture = what turns lead to gold) and heraldry (tinctures = a house's colours). The latter fits Tinct's "Houses → Shelves" library taxonomy.
6. Stories are told briefly by the brand itself (Patagonia's own blog post) and retold by press. A one-paragraph "Why Tinct?" page plus a one-line tagline is the right format.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the full Print magazine (Heller) article, so the exact Hibma quotes and the exact Voltaire wording she cited are unverified at source. The Voltaire Kindle Fire ad line is usually given as "Let us read, and let us dance; these two amusements will never do any harm to the world", plus a fire-kindling line, but I did not verify the exact ad text. Do not quote it without checking.
- Audible, Standard Ebooks and Hermès naming stories were not researched (tool budget). No official Libby or Scribd name origin was found.

## 3. Quotes about reading or thought "dyeing" or "colouring" the soul, to pair with the name

### Takeaway
There are two excellent, verified, public-domain classical sources. Both are from authors whose books are natural Tinct titles. Marcus Aurelius 5.16 ("the soul is dyed by the thoughts"; Casaubon 1634: "the soul doth as it were receive its tincture") and Seneca Letter 71.31 (wool that takes some dyes at once but must be "soaked and steeped" in others; philosophy must not "merely" colour the soul but "thoroughly permeate" it). The Seneca passage maps neatly onto slow, deep reading of the classics.

### Cited Findings
- **Seneca, *Moral Letters to Lucilius* 71.31** (Gummere's translation, 1917–25, on Wikisource; fetched and verified verbatim): "Just as wool takes up certain colours at once, while there are others which it will not absorb unless it is soaked and steeped in them many times; so other systems of doctrine can be immediately applied by men's minds after once being accepted, but this system of which I speak, unless it has gone deep and has sunk in for a long time, and has not merely coloured but thoroughly permeated the soul, does not fulfil any of its promises." — [Wikisource, Letter 71](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_71)
- **Marcus Aurelius 6.30** (George Long): "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye". Casaubon (his VI.XXVII): "lest of a philosopher thou become a mere Cæsar in time, and receive a new tincture from the court." This is a warning about bad dye; it is useful as a contrast but not as a tagline. — [Gutenberg #15877 (Long)](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt); [Gutenberg #2680 (Casaubon)](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt)
- **Shakespeare**, *Hamlet* 3.4 ("such black and grained spots / As will not leave their tinct") and *Antony and Cleopatra* 1.5 ("that great medicine hath / With his tinct gilded thee"). — see section 1 sources.

### Inferences
- Candidate brand lines built on verified sources (suggestions, not quotations):
  - "The soul is dyed by the thoughts." — Marcus Aurelius (Long)
  - "Not merely coloured, but thoroughly permeated." — Seneca, after Gummere. This is a good description of what slow reading with original + modern text and a companion aims for.
  - Casaubon's line, "the soul doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies", is the only one containing the brand word itself, and it is ideal for an "About the name" page.
- Story arc that uses these: *tinct* (Latin *tinctus*, "dyed") → Marcus Aurelius: "the soul is dyed by the thoughts" → you become the colour of what you read → Seneca: the great books take many soakings → Tinct is the place to steep in them.

### Gaps
- Montaigne and Emerson were not searched (tool budget). I have no verified "dye"/"colour" quotes from them. Do not use any without verification.
- Seneca's Latin (Ep. 71.31, wool and *colores*) was not verified at source; only the Gummere English was checked.

## 4. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.16: verified wording and context

### Takeaway
Verified verbatim in two public-domain translations. **George Long (1862): "Such as are thy habitual thoughts, such also will be the character of thy mind; for the soul is dyed by the thoughts."** **Meric Casaubon (1634, numbered V.XV in that edition): "...For the soul doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies, and imaginations."** The widely quoted modern Gregory Hays wording ("The things you think about determine the quality of your mind. Your soul takes on the color of your thoughts.") is copyrighted (Modern Library, 2002). It is attested only via reader-quote sites, not a primary source.

### Cited Findings
- **George Long translation** (Project Gutenberg #15877, "Thoughts of Marcus Aurelius Antoninus", Translator: George Long), Book V, §16, verbatim:
  > "16. Such as are thy habitual thoughts, such also will be the character of thy mind; for the soul is dyed by the thoughts. Dye it then with a continuous series of such thoughts as these: for instance, that where a man can live, there he can also live well. But he must live in a palace; well then, he can also live well in a palace. And again, consider that for whatever purpose each thing has been constituted, for this it has been constituted, and towards this it is carried; and its end is in that towards which it is carried; and where the end is, there also is the advantage and the good of each thing. Now the good for the reasonable animal is society; for that we are made for society has been shown above. Is it not plain that the inferior exists for the sake of the superior? But the things which have life are superior to those which have not life, and of those which have life the superior are those which have reason."
  — [Project Gutenberg #15877](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)
- **Meric Casaubon translation** (Project Gutenberg #2680, "Meditations"; the edition's notes say it is Casaubon's text, prepared from the 1634 and 1635 editions). Numbered "XV" in the Fifth Book, verbatim:
  > "XV. Such as thy thoughts and ordinary cogitations are, such will thy mind be in time. For the soul doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies, and imaginations. Dye it therefore and thoroughly soak it with the assiduity of these cogitations. As for example. Wheresoever thou mayest live, there it is in thy power to live well and happy. But thou mayest live at the Court, there then also mayest thou live well and happy..."
  — [Project Gutenberg #2680](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt). The same file's introduction lists the English translations: Casaubon 1634, Collier 1701, Thomson 1747, Graves 1792, McCormac 1844, Long 1862, Rendall 1898, Jackson 1906.
- **Context:** the passage is a practical instruction to deliberately "dye" one's mind with chosen thoughts, through repetition. Its example is that one can live well anywhere, even in a palace. It then turns to the claim that rational beings are made for society. The dye metaphor is the opening thesis; the rest is an example of a thought to dye with.
- **Gregory Hays (2002, copyrighted)**: "The things you think about determine the quality of your mind. Your soul takes on the color of your thoughts." — attested by [Goodreads quote page](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/7209150-the-things-you-think-about-determine-the-quality-of-your) and a [Goodreads reader highlight](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/36680837-meditations/27205530-jeff/e4e9b341-1bc5-47f1-9e38-309919b028ad). Neither gives the section number, though it corresponds to 5.16 by content.
- **Collier (1701)**: not checked. Gutenberg #2680's introduction calls it "a most coarse and vulgar copy of the original".

### Inferences
- For public-facing use, Long's "the soul is dyed by the thoughts" is safe (public domain) and short. Casaubon's "the soul doth as it were receive its tincture" is the uniquely on-brand version, since it contains the word "tincture" and comes from the first English translation (1634). Hays's line is better known in modern culture, but quoting it in marketing would draw on a copyrighted translation. A short attributed quote is likely fair use, but verify against the book before quoting, and prefer Long or Casaubon.
- Note "thoughts" vs "reading": Marcus is about habitual thoughts, not books. The bridge ("what you read becomes what you think") is Tinct's own claim, not Marcus's, and should be phrased that way.

### Gaps
- The Greek original (commonly given as βάπτεται γὰρ ὑπὸ τῶν φαντασιῶν ἡ ψυχή, "the soul is dyed by the impressions/imaginings") was not verified against a source text in this session.
- The Hays wording and page or section number were not checked against the printed book.
