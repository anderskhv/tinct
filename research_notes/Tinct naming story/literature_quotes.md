# "Tinct" and "tincture" in English literature: candidate naming stories for Tinct

Method: each quotation below comes from a full primary text. I downloaded the texts from Project Gutenberg (complete Shakespeare #100, Paradise Lost #26, Paradise Regained #58, Montaigne/Cotton #3600, Marcus Aurelius/Casaubon #2680, Marcus Aurelius/Long #15877, Donne/Grierson vols. 1–2 #48688/#48772, Chaucer #22120, Browne #586, Bacon Essays #575, Emerson Essays I #2944, Augustine/Pusey #3296) and searched them for `tinct`, `tincture`, `dye` and `dyer`. I took Shakespeare act/scene/line numbers from the Folger Shakespeare online text, which I fetched page by page. Background on word senses: "tinct" means a colour or dye, from Latin *tingere*, "to dye". In alchemy it also meant the transmuting essence or elixir that "colours" base metal into gold. Most of the strongest passages play on both senses.

## Shakespeare: every use of tinct and tincture, with context and tone

### Takeaway
Shakespeare uses "tinct" four times and "tincture" four times. The warmest uses are Antony and Cleopatra 1.5 (love as an alchemical medicine that gilds the messenger), All's Well 5.3 (Plutus, the alchemist's "tinct and multiplying medicine") and Sonnet 54 (the rose's "perfumèd tincture", whose truth verse distils). The most famous use, Hamlet 3.4, is dark: guilt as a dye that will not wash out. Sonnet 111's "dyer's hand" is the best dye image about being shaped by what you work in.

### Cited Findings
- **Antony and Cleopatra 1.5.41–43 (Folger)**. CLEOPATRA: "How much unlike art thou Mark Antony! / Yet, coming from him, that great medicine hath / With his tinct gilded thee." Context: Alexas has just come from Antony carrying a pearl. Cleopatra says that Antony, "that great medicine" (the alchemical elixir), has gilded even this messenger with his tinct. Tone: warm, loving and playful. It is the clearest "contact with greatness transforms you" image. Already in the Tinct library. — [Folger, A&C 1.5](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/antony-and-cleopatra/read/1/5/); [Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt)
- **All's Well That Ends Well 5.3.118–120 (Folger)**. KING: "Plutus himself, / That knows the tinct and multiplying med'cine, / Hath not in nature's mystery more science / Than I have in this ring." (Gutenberg spells it "medicine".) Context: the King recognises the ring he gave Helen. Plutus, god of wealth, is pictured as a master alchemist who knows the "tinct", the elixir that turns metal into gold and multiplies it. Tone: neutral to tense. It is a moment of recognition inside an accusation scene. — [Folger, AWW 5.3](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/alls-well-that-ends-well/read/5/3/); [Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt)
- **Hamlet 3.4.100–102 (Folger)**. QUEEN: "O Hamlet, speak no more. / Thou turn'st mine eyes into my very soul, / And there I see such black and grainèd spots / As will ⟨not⟩ leave their tinct." Context: the closet scene. Hamlet's words make Gertrude see her own guilt as an ingrained ("grained") dye. Folger prints "not" in pointed brackets, which marks a reading taken from the Folio into its Q2 base text. Tone: dark (guilt and stain). It does fit a "words reach the soul" story, but the colour here is sin. Already in the library. — [Folger, Hamlet 3.4](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/hamlet/read/3/4/); [Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt) ("As will not leave their tinct.")
- **Cymbeline 2.2.24–26 (Folger)**. IACHIMO: "...now canopied / Under these windows white and azure, laced / With blue of heaven's own tinct. But my design: / To note the chamber." Context: Iachimo has crept out of a trunk into the sleeping Imogen's bedchamber. He describes her closed eyelids as white with veins "of heaven's own tinct". Tone: the phrase itself is very beautiful, but the scene is voyeuristic and sinister. Use the phrase with care. Already in the library. — [Folger, Cymbeline 2.2](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/cymbeline/read/2/2/)
- **Julius Caesar 2.2.94 (Folger)**. DECIUS: "...and that great men shall press / For tinctures, stains, relics, and cognizance." Context: Decius flatters Caesar by reinterpreting Calphurnia's dream of a bleeding statue. In his version, Romans will dip cloths in Caesar's blood as treasured relics. Tone: dark and ironic, a manipulation on the morning of the murder. Already in the library. — [Folger, JC 2.2](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/julius-caesar/read/2/2/)
- **The Winter's Tale 3.2.226 (Folger), not 5.1 as the brief suggested**. PAULINA: "...if you can bring / Tincture or luster in her lip, her eye, / Heat outwardly or breath within, I'll serve you / As I would do the gods." Context: Paulina announces that Hermione is dead. "Tincture" here means the colour of life. Tone: dark at this point, though it is redeemed when the "statue" comes to life in 5.3. Already in the library. I found no "tincture" in Winter's Tale 5.1. — [Folger, WT 3.2](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/the-winters-tale/read/3/2/); [Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt) (spelled "lustre")
- **Sonnet 54, lines 5–6 and 13–14**. "The canker blooms have full as deep a dye / As the perfumèd tincture of the roses... And so of you, beauteous and lovely youth, / When that shall vade, by verse distils your truth." Context: true beauty, like a real rose, has an inner essence ("truth"/"odour") that survives and can be distilled. The poet's verse distils it. Tone: warm, and it links tincture, distillation and words that preserve truth. The final line differs by text: Folger has "When that shall vade, by verse distils your truth", while Gutenberg #100 has "When that shall fade, my verse distills your truth". — [Folger, Sonnet 54](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/shakespeares-sonnets/read/54/); [Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt)
- **The Two Gentlemen of Verona 4.4.164 (Folger)**. JULIA (disguised as Sebastian): "The air hath starved the roses in her cheeks / And pinched the lily tincture of her face." Context: Julia describes "herself" with sad irony. Tone: melancholy. — [Folger, TGV 4.4](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/the-two-gentlemen-of-verona/read/4/4/)
- **Sonnet 111, lines 5–7 (dye imagery, not the word "tinct")**. "Thence comes it that my name receives a brand, / And almost thence my nature is subdued / To what it works in, like the dyer's hand." Context: the poet's public trade has stained his nature the way dye stains a dyer's hand. Tone: rueful and self-reproaching, but this is the canonical English image of being coloured by what you work in. — [Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt)

### Inferences
- The best brand fits are Antony and Cleopatra 1.5 ("that great medicine hath / With his tinct gilded thee"), which says contact with something great gilds you, and Sonnet 54 (tincture, distillation, verse preserving truth). Both plays or poems are in or close to the library, so the app can link to the exact passage.
- Hamlet 3.4 is the best-known use but expresses guilt. Use it as a "did you know" fact, not as a slogan.
- The Cymbeline phrase "heaven's own tinct" is gorgeous, but its scene makes it risky to pull out of context.

### Gaps
- I did not cross-check the Folger line numbers against Arden or Riverside numbering. Lineation differs slightly between editions.
- I did not independently check the exact Q2 reading of Hamlet 3.4.102 (said to be "As will leave there their tinct"). I could only confirm Folger's bracket notation and the Folio-based reading "As will not leave their tinct".

## George Herbert, "The Elixir" (The Temple, 1633)

### Takeaway
"The Elixir" is the most beautiful and positive literary use of "tincture". God's "tincture" makes even the meanest act "grow bright and clean", and the poem ends "This is the famous stone / That turneth all to gold". The earlier Williams manuscript version was titled "Perfection".

### Cited Findings
- Full 1633 text (CCEL transcription of the 1633 edition):
  > Teach me, my God and King, / In all things thee to see, / And what I do in any thing, / To do it as for thee: // Not rudely, as a beast, / To runne into an action; / But still to make thee prepossest, / And give it his perfection. // A man that looks on glasse, / On it may stay his eye; / Or if he pleaseth, through it passe, / And then the heav'n espie. // All may of thee partake: / Nothing can be so mean, / Which with his tincture (for thy sake) / Will not grow bright and clean. // A servant with this clause / Makes drudgerie divine: / Who sweeps a room, as for thy laws, / Makes that and th' action fine. // This is the famous stone / That turneth all to gold: / For that which God doth touch and own / Cannot for lesse be told.
  — [CCEL, The Elixir (1633)](https://www.ccel.org/h/herbert/temple/Elixir.html)
- The poem survives in the Williams manuscript (Dr. Williams's Library; shelfmark usually cited as MS Jones B 62, which is my recollection and unverified here), whose text is earlier. There it was originally titled "Perfection" and later retitled "The Elixer". Its first stanza began "Lord, teach me to refer / All things I do to thee", and two further stanzas were crossed out and never printed. Source cited for this: John Drury, *Music at Midnight* (2013), pp. 144–149, which includes a facsimile. — [Hymnology Archive, "Teach Me, My God and King"](https://www.hymnologyarchive.com/teach-me-my-god-and-king) (note: this page gives the MS reference as "MS 28.169")
- The Williams manuscript is the earlier of the two main witnesses. It was copied under Herbert's supervision and corrected in his hand, and it holds early versions of 78 English poems. — [Folger CELM, George Herbert](https://celm.folger.edu/introductions/HerbertGeorge.html)
- The 1656 seventh edition altered line 3 of the tincture stanza. "Tincture" and "elixir" are near-synonyms, and the "elixir stone" is the philosopher's stone. — [Hymnology Archive](https://www.hymnologyarchive.com/teach-me-my-god-and-king)
- John Wesley recast the poem as the hymn "Teach me, my God and King" in 1738. — [CCEL](https://www.ccel.org/h/herbert/temple/Elixir.html)
- Criticism: Clarence H. Miller, "Christ as the philosopher's stone in George Herbert's 'The Elixir'", is linked from the CCEL page. — [CCEL](https://www.ccel.org/h/herbert/temple/Elixir.html)

### Inferences
- Herbert gives Tinct its warmest origin story: a tincture is the thing that turns ordinary into gold, and the stanza "Nothing can be so mean, / Which with his tincture... / Will not grow bright and clean" is entirely positive. The poem is religious, which may matter for a secular brand. The secular reading still works: attention and meaning turn the ordinary into gold.
- The "glasse" stanza, about looking through the glass rather than at it, is also a nice match for an app that is a lens onto the original text.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the full Williams manuscript text. The Hymnology Archive declined to reproduce the stanzas, and the 1977 facsimile is not online. For exact wording, use Hutchinson's edition (1941) or Helen Wilcox's Cambridge *English Poems of George Herbert* (2007).
- Poetry Foundation returned 403, so I verified the text only against the CCEL transcription of the 1633 edition.

## Donne, Milton, Chaucer, Browne, Montaigne, Bacon and others

### Takeaway
Montaigne's "Of Pedantry" (Cotton translation) is the single best "reading/learning as dye" passage. Learning should not merely be tinctured onto the soul but give it "a thorough and perfect dye". Donne's "Resurrection, imperfect" ("Hee was all gold when he lay downe, but rose / All tincture") and Browne's "let not the ocean wash away thy tincture" are strong secondary candidates. Milton has two lovely celestial uses. I found no tincture or tinct in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales text, Bacon's Essays or Emerson's Essays (First Series).

### Cited Findings
- **Montaigne, Essays I.24 "Of Pedantry" (Cotton translation, ed. Hazlitt; Villey numbering I.25)**: "We are not to tie learning to the soul, but to work and incorporate them together: not to tincture it only, but to give it a thorough and perfect dye; which, if it will not take colour, and meliorate its imperfect state, it were without question better to let it alone." It directly follows the Seneca tag "Non vita, sed scolae discimus" ("We do not study for life, but only for the school"). Tone: positive and demanding. True learning changes you all the way through; surface learning does not. Already in the library. — [Gutenberg #3600](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3600/pg3600.txt)
- Other Montaigne (Cotton) uses:
  - II.1 "Of the Inconstancy of our Actions", on virtue: "'Tis a vivid and strong tincture which, when the soul has once thoroughly imbibed it, will not out but with the piece."
  - III.2 "Of Repentance": "It is not a patch, but rather an universal tincture, with which I am stained."
  - I.22 "Of Custom": "Human reason is a tincture almost equally infused into all our opinions and manners."
  - "who, that has his brain never so little tinctured with the true philosophy".
  - "the best virtue I have has in it some tincture of vice".
  
  — [Gutenberg #3600](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3600/pg3600.txt)
- **Donne, "Resurrection, imperfect" (lines 13–16, Grierson ed.)**: "Hee was all gold when he lay downe, but rose / All tincture, and doth not alone dispose / Leaden and iron wills to good, but is / Of power to make even sinfull flesh like his." Context: Christ in the tomb is pictured as a mineral that becomes the alchemical tincture, which transforms base metal and base people. Tone: exalted and positive. — [Gutenberg #48688](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48688/pg48688.txt)
- **Donne, "To the Countesse of Bedford. On New-yeares day" (l. 16)**: "Verse embalmes vertue;'and Tombs, or Thrones of rimes, / Preserve fraile transitory fame... / Mine are short-liv'd; the tincture of your name / Creates in them, but dissipates as fast, / New spirits". Context: the poet says his verse is quickened by the "tincture" of the patron's name. Tone: courtly and positive, and it links verse with tincture. — [Gutenberg #48688](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48688/pg48688.txt)
- **Donne, other uses**:
  - "To Sir Edward Herbert, at Juliers": "As Soules (they say) by our first touch, take in / The poysonous tincture of Originall sinne" (dark).
  - *The First Anniversary* (ll. 179–180): "Shee that could drive / The poysonous tincture, and the staine of Eve, / Out of her thoughts... / ...by a true religious Alchymie" (mixed tone).
  - "The Litanie", stanza 1: "purge away / All vicious tinctures, that new fashioned / I may rise up from death" (penitential).
  - "Ecclogue 1613" (l. 66): "all tinctures move / From higher powers".
  
  — [Gutenberg #48688](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48688/pg48688.txt). "The Ecstasy" does not contain "tincture" in this text.
- **Milton, Paradise Lost Book V (the seraph Raphael descending; standard line ≈5.285)**: "the third his feet / Shadowed from either heel with feathered mail, / Sky-tinctured grain." Tone: radiant. — [Gutenberg #26](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26/pg26.txt)
- **Milton, Paradise Lost Book VII (Creation of the stars; standard line ≈7.367)**: "Hither, as to their fountain, other stars / Repairing, in their golden urns draw light, / And hence the morning-planet gilds her horns; / By tincture or reflection they augment / Their small peculiar". Context: lesser stars draw light from the sun and grow brighter "by tincture or reflection". Tone: luminous and positive. As a metaphor it is very apt: lesser lights brightened by drawing from a great source. — [Gutenberg #26](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26/pg26.txt)
- Milton, *Paradise Regained*: no "tinct" or "tincture" found. — [Gutenberg #58](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/58/pg58.txt)
- **Sir Thomas Browne, *A Letter to a Friend***: "Persons lightly dipt, not grained, in generous honesty are but pale in goodness and faint-hued in sincerity. But be thou what thou virtuously art, and let not the ocean wash away thy tincture." Tone: warm exhortation, and the "dipt vs. grained" dye contrast echoes Montaigne's. — [Gutenberg #586](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/586/pg586.txt)
- Browne, *Religio Medici* Part I: "I have no taint or tincture" of heresy. Also: "have also an impression and tincture of reason". — [Gutenberg #586](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/586/pg586.txt)
- Chaucer, Canon's Yeoman's Tale: the Gutenberg Canterbury Tales text contains no "tincture" or "tinct"; its alchemy vocabulary is different. — [Gutenberg #22120](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/22120/pg22120.txt)
- Bacon, *Essays*, and Emerson, *Essays: First Series*: no "tincture" or "tinct". — [Gutenberg #575](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/575/pg575.txt); [Gutenberg #2944](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2944/pg2944.txt)
- Augustine, *Confessions* (Pusey): no tincture or dye passage found. — [Gutenberg #3296](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3296/pg3296.txt)
- Dictionaries: Webster 1828 marks "tinct" (noun) as obsolete, meaning stain or colour. Webster 1913 calls it archaic, with Shakespeare and Tennyson citations. — [1828 dictionary](https://1828dictionary.com/word/tinct/); [Webster 1913](https://websters1913.timcieplowski.com/word/tinct/)

### Inferences
- Montaigne's sentence is the near-perfect brand thesis: reading should not just tincture the mind but dye it through, otherwise "better to let it alone". It comes from a library book, so the app can deep-link to it. A tagline such as "Not to tincture it only, but to give it a thorough and perfect dye" is accurate to Cotton.
- Milton's "By tincture or reflection they augment / Their small peculiar" works as a metaphor for a companion edition. The modern version and the AI draw light from the original and help the reader brighten.
- Donne's "All tincture" and Herbert's "famous stone" both use the alchemical sense: tincture as the agent of transformation into gold.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Milton line numbers (5.285, 7.367) against a numbered edition such as the Dartmouth Milton Reading Room, because the Gutenberg text is unnumbered. The book placement is verified.
- I did not search Donne's sermons, Ben Jonson (*The Alchemist* is full of "tincture" vocabulary but unsearched), Samuel Johnson, Coleridge or Woolf because of time limits. I could not verify Johnson's 1755 *Dictionary* entry for "tinct".
- I believe the Browne "dipt, not grained" passage also appears in *Christian Morals*, but I did not check this. It is verified only in *A Letter to a Friend*.

## Dye and tinge imagery about the mind: Marcus Aurelius and similar

### Takeaway
Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.16 is verified in two public-domain translations. George Long has "the soul is dyed by the thoughts". Meric Casaubon (1634) uses the actual word "tincture": "the soul doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies". *Meditations* is already in the library, which makes this a strong naming story.

### Cited Findings
- **George Long translation, 5.16**: "Such as are thy habitual thoughts, such also will be the character of thy mind; for the soul is dyed by the thoughts. Dye it then with a continuous series of such thoughts as these: for instance, that where a man can live, there he can also live well." — [Gutenberg #15877](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt). The wording often quoted, "the soul becomes dyed with the colour of its thoughts", is not Long's. I did not find it in either Gutenberg translation. It may come from a later translation (Hays's wording unverified).
- **Meric Casaubon translation (1634), Fifth Book, XV (his numbering)**: "Such as thy thoughts and ordinary cogitations are, such will thy mind be in time. For the soul doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies, and imaginations. Dye it therefore and thoroughly soak it with the assiduity of these cogitations." Tone: positive and practical; you become what you dwell on. — [Gutenberg #2680](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt) (its introduction lists "Meric Casaubon, 1634" as the first English translation)
- **Casaubon, Sixth Book (= Long 6.30)**: "take heed, lest of a philosopher thou become a mere Cæsar in time, and receive a new tincture from the court." Long's version: "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye." — [Gutenberg #2680](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt); [Gutenberg #15877](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)
- **Long 3.4**: the good man as "a fighter in the noblest fight... dyed deep with justice". — [Gutenberg #15877](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)

### Inferences
- Casaubon's "receive its tincture... Dye it therefore and thoroughly soak it" may be the strongest single naming story overall. It uses the word itself, it is about the mind being shaped by what it takes in, it is positive, and it comes from a book already in Tinct.
- Ranked shortlist for warm, quotable naming stories:
  1. Marcus Aurelius 5.16 (Casaubon/Long).
  2. Montaigne, "Of Pedantry".
  3. Herbert, "The Elixir".
  4. Antony and Cleopatra 1.5.
  5. Milton PL 7 "By tincture or reflection".
  6. Sonnet 54.
  
  Hamlet 3.4 works as a famous-trivia footnote, not as a tagline.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Gregory Hays (2002) wording of 5.16. It is copyrighted and was not checked. Do not attribute "dyed with the colour of its thoughts" to any named translator without checking.
- I did not do a broad search for other famous book/reading quotes using dye imagery (Woolf, Coleridge and so on). None are verified here.
