A moral judgment can feel immediate: this is noble, that is shameful, this deserves punishment. Nietzsche asks how those feelings came to acquire their authority.

His three essays investigate the history and psychology of valuation. Resentment, debt, cruelty and the wish to give suffering meaning enter explanations of practices usually presented as obviously good or necessary. The effect is to make conscience seem less like a voice outside history and more like something formed within it.

These are forceful, contestable interpretations, not a settled history of every moral tradition. Their value for a reader includes the demand to notice what a principle does for the people who uphold it.

Read with particular attention when indignation feels satisfying. Does discovering a belief's origin discredit it, or create a new responsibility to decide whether it is worth keeping?
