A ruler's power requires an explanation. Locke refuses to begin with the assumption that some people are naturally entitled to govern everyone else.

He asks what free and equal people could authorise a government to do for them. Protection of life, liberty and property becomes central, but so does the possibility that an authority entrusted with those purposes could betray them. Political obligation therefore includes a question about its limits.

The argument opens difficult problems of its own. What counts as consent? How does labour justify ownership, and what happens when property becomes unequal? Locke's language of freedom must also be read alongside the colonial world and exclusions of his period.

Read for the difference between power a person possesses and authority they can justify. If government is a trust, who should be able to decide that the trustees have broken its terms?
