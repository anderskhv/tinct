Sara Crewe arrives at boarding school with beautiful clothes, money and the assurance that she is loved. She likes imagining herself a princess. The interesting question is what that idea means when the people around her stop treating her as someone important.

Burnett gives imagination practical work to do. A story can help a child bear discomfort, preserve dignity or offer kindness when there is little else to give. It can also reveal how much of adult respectability depends on whom a person believes it is safe to mistreat.

Sara's generosity is tested by hunger, humiliation and loneliness. The novel invites us to care about a child's inner life when the household has decided that her needs no longer matter. Its comforts are part of a demanding wish: that someone should notice, and that the person suffering should still be able to recognise herself.

Read for the difference between being called a princess and choosing how to behave. Which qualities would remain yours if nobody rewarded you for having them?
