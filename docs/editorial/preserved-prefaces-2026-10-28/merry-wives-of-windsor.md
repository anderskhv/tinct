Falstaff decides that two married women might solve his financial difficulties. He writes to both, assuming each will welcome his attention and remain unaware of the other. The women compare notes.

The pleasure of the play lies in watching confidence meet cooperation. Falstaff imagines himself directing a private intrigue; the people he underestimates begin arranging the scene around him. Meanwhile, jealousy makes one husband unusually willing to be fooled in a different way.

Windsor is full of practical interests: marriage prospects, money, hospitality and the wish to appear clever. Shakespeare gives domestic judgment room to answer the self-importance of a celebrated character. Knowing a neighbourhood can be more useful than having an impressive story about oneself.

Read for the small exchanges through which people test what is really being asked of them. How much trouble could be prevented by talking to the other person someone hoped you would never compare notes with?
