An ordinary meal depends on work you did not see and people you will never meet. Smith asks how such extensive cooperation becomes possible, and why some societies can produce so much more than others.

The division of labour, exchange and the accumulation of capital help explain the abundance. They also create dependencies and vulnerabilities. Specialisation can increase production while narrowing a worker's daily life. Merchants who compete for customers may share an interest in securing advantages at the public's expense.

The book is more observant and less reducible to a slogan than its political reputation suggests. Smith examines taxes, institutions, trade restrictions and public responsibilities alongside market exchange. The reader should keep asking whose interest a particular arrangement serves.

Read for the hidden organisation inside familiar things. When a policy is defended as good for commerce, how would you tell whether the benefit reaches the people whose lives the economy is supposed to sustain?
