Douglass begins with information many people take for granted: knowledge of their age, family and origins. Slavery makes even these ordinary facts matters someone else can withhold.

His narrative shows domination through daily arrangements as well as spectacular violence. Work, hunger, literacy, religion and separation become means by which one person tries to control another's sense of what is possible. Learning to read opens a new world and makes the conditions of the present harder to bear.

The authority of the book lies in its precision. Douglass names practices, exposes contradictions and makes a case through experiences he refuses to leave to somebody else's account. His criticism of slaveholding Christianity distinguishes religious profession from the conduct it is used to excuse.

Read with attention to how freedom begins to become imaginable before it can become secure. What capacities do people discover when the world insisting on their inferiority loses the right to define them?
