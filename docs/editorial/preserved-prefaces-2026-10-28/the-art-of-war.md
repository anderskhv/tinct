A commander can win a battle and still leave a country weaker. The Art of War begins from the seriousness of that possibility. Force is expensive; confidence can be dangerous; information matters.

Its advice asks the reader to understand circumstances before imposing a plan. Know the terrain, the opponent and the limits of your own resources. Avoid making pride the measure of success. A conflict prevented, divided or resolved before direct confrontation may achieve more than a spectacular victory.

The book is often carried into business and private life. That can illuminate a problem, but the analogy needs care: a colleague or partner is not automatically an enemy to defeat. Its most useful challenge may be to our appetite for contest itself.

What would change if you measured a strategy by the future it preserved rather than the opponent it humiliated?
