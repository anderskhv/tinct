Richard tells the audience what he intends to do, and then goes to work. He can be witty, intimate and startlingly effective. People onstage struggle to understand him while we enjoy being admitted to his confidence.

That privileged position is uncomfortable. Knowing a manipulation is happening does not prevent us from admiring its execution. Shakespeare makes spectators feel the seduction of competence: the person pursuing a terrible aim may also be the liveliest intelligence in the room.

The play surrounds that energy with the people whose losses it creates. Memory and grief resist the speed with which an ambitious ruler wants to move on. The theatrical Richard is a dramatic construction, not an impartial biography of the historical king.

Read for when complicity stops feeling like cleverness. What does the pleasure of understanding a ruthless person make us overlook about everyone else?
