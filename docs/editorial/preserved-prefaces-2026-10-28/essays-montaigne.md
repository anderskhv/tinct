Montaigne begins with a subject always available and never entirely understood: himself. His habits, illnesses, friendships, fears and changes of mind become material for thought.

The essays wander because their author is testing what he thinks rather than always presenting a conclusion already secured. An anecdote from antiquity can meet a bodily discomfort or an admission of ignorance. The movement gives philosophical inquiry the texture of an actual person discovering the limits of his own account.

That intimacy does not make the book merely private. Montaigne keeps returning to education, cruelty, custom and the ease with which people call unfamiliar practices barbarous. Knowing oneself can become a way of losing confidence in one's right to judge everybody else.

Read a single essay without insisting that every turn serve a straight line. What could you learn if changing your mind ceased to feel like evidence that you had failed?
