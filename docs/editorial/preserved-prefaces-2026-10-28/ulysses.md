Leopold Bloom has errands to do. He will eat, walk through Dublin, attend a funeral and look for somewhere to spend the hours. Stephen Dedalus is moving through the same city with a different burden of memories. It is 16 June 1904. A very ordinary day is about to become an enormous book.

Joyce gives passing thoughts the attention usually reserved for heroic deeds. Hunger, embarrassment, desire, grief and stray advertising slogans share a person's mind. A city lives through the voices people overhear as much as through the events anyone thinks worth recording.

The writing keeps changing its rules. A chapter may imitate a newspaper, a catechism or a theatrical spectacle. Some of this will be puzzling. Much of it is funny, bodily and unexpectedly tender. You do not have to catch every reference to recognise the wish to be welcomed somewhere.

Homer supplies a distant pattern, but Bloom earns our attention through the ordinary work of getting through a day among other people. How much of a life becomes visible when we stop deciding in advance what deserves to count?
