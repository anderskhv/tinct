A respectable doctor has made a disturbing arrangement. If he dies or disappears, a man named Edward Hyde is to receive his property. His lawyer, Utterson, cannot understand why his old friend would give such power to someone who seems to inspire immediate disgust.

Utterson suspects that Jekyll is being threatened. He sets out to protect him, with the caution of a man who values loyalty and knows how easily a reputation can be ruined. It is an excellent way into a story about the things respectable people help one another conceal.

Even if the names in the title seem to tell you everything, pay attention to the way Stevenson withholds knowledge. We follow witnesses, documents and half-understood encounters. The city offers doorways into lives that are almost impossible to see whole.

Underneath that mystery lies an attractive possibility: doing what you want without letting it touch the person everyone thinks you are. Many people live with smaller versions of that division. A private indulgence can be explained away as an exception. Then the exception begins to require arrangements of its own.

The horror gathers in those arrangements, in the effort to protect one account of a life while another grows beyond it. Friendship and discretion can preserve a person, but they can also preserve a dangerous secret.

How much of being good depends on what we choose, and how much on the fear that someone might find out?
