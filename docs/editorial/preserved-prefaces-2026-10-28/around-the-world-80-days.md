Phileas Fogg believes a journey around the world can be completed in eighty days. He wagers a fortune on the proposition and departs with a servant who had hoped for a quiet life.

The pleasure begins with the collision between the timetable and the world. Ships sail, connections fail, misunderstandings multiply, and a man who seems to value punctuality above everything must keep deciding what deserves a delay. His companion Passepartout experiences the journey with considerably less composure.

Verne makes modern speed feel both miraculous and precarious. A network can promise mastery of distance while leaving travellers dependent on strangers, weather and the next available departure. The map grows smaller; the number of things that can go wrong does not.

Follow the race, but notice what reveals Fogg's character when a calculation would suggest one course and a human claim suggests another. Reaching a destination on time is a clear measure of success. What does that measure leave out?
