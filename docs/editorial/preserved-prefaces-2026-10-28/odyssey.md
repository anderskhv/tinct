Would you refuse to become a god?

Calypso, a beautiful goddess, offers Odysseus an immortal life with her. On her island, he could remain her lover, untouched by age, for ever. He wants to leave.

He wants his wife, his son and his home on Ithaca. All three belong to a world in which people grow old, disappoint one another and die. He knows that Penelope cannot rival a goddess in beauty. Still he longs for the life he has been kept from living.

This is one of the Odyssey's most arresting choices. What could make a limited, difficult life worth choosing over an endless one? And how much of what we want depends on being free to choose it? Calypso's island is beautiful, but Odysseus is her captive.

The first word of the poem in Greek means “man”. This particular man is clever, brave, proud and capable of making a terrible situation worse. Gods interfere in his life; storms can destroy what he has worked for. Yet his choices matter. Early in the poem, Zeus complains that mortals blame the gods for suffering they bring upon themselves. How much of our trouble is fate, and how much is our own doing?

The poem gives these questions the movement of an adventure. There are monsters, storms, disguises and escapes that depend on a well-timed lie. Odysseus can think his way out of dangers that strength alone could never overcome. His cleverness is thrilling. His pride makes it dangerous.

Meanwhile, home has its own struggle. Penelope must hold off men who are consuming her household's wealth and pressing her to marry. Telemachus must grow into adulthood in the absence of the father whose name defines him. Both need resourcefulness of their own.

Much of what we hear about the wandering comes from Odysseus himself. Notice what he chooses to tell, what he admires in his own conduct, and what it costs other people. A man can be worth following through a story without being easy to admire.

Reading Homer brings us close to one of the beginnings of Western literature. Later writers keep returning to this traveller and his questions: what we owe a stranger, what makes a good person, and where we belong. We still recognise the man who has survived a war and cannot get home, the child growing up in his absence, and the woman keeping a household together while everyone else presses her to move on.

His desire to return makes the poem unexpectedly intimate. After all the places we might go and all the people we might become, what would make us choose the life that is ours?
