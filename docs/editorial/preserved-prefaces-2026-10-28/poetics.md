A tragedy can make us feel fear and pity for events we would never want to witness in life. Aristotle asks what kind of arrangement allows that experience to become something we seek out.

His attention falls on action: how one event follows another, what a character's choice reveals, and why a reversal or recognition can change the meaning of what has already happened. A collection of impressive moments is not yet a compelling whole.

The surviving text is compressed and sometimes difficult. Read it as a set of investigations into effects you already know from theatre, novels or films. Then test its distinctions against actual stories, including ones that do not fit comfortably.

What makes an ending feel earned? Why can a simple change in what someone knows transform an entire plot? The questions can sharpen the pleasure of reading and the judgment with which you meet a story's attempt to move you.
