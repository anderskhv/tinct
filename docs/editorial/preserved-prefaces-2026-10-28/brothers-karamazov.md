The Karamazovs are a family with unfinished business. Their father is selfish and provocative; his sons meet him with different mixtures of need, contempt, rivalry and longing. Arguments about money and love quickly become arguments about what people owe one another.

Dostoevsky gives those disputes an enormous reach. One brother presses on the suffering of the innocent. Another tries to live through faith and active care. Desire and resentment keep upsetting everyone's account of what they believe. A position that sounds convincing in conversation becomes harder to maintain when somebody is crying in the next room.

The novel's power lies in allowing its strongest objections to remain strong. Faith must face cruelty; freedom must face responsibility; love must do more than admire humanity from a distance. Nobody gets to settle the problem merely by sounding intelligent.

Read the family drama with the same attention as the famous speeches. When we say that the world ought to be different, what follows for the person who is standing immediately in front of us?
