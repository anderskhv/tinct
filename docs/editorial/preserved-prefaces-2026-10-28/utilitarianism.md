An action can be praised for its intention, its obedience to a rule or its consequences. Mill asks us to take the happiness and suffering it produces as central to moral judgment.

The proposal raises immediate objections. Whose happiness counts? Are all pleasures alike? Could a calculation excuse treating one person unjustly for the benefit of many? Mill takes up these pressures while trying to show that concern for general happiness can support a richer life than its critics assume.

The book is short enough to keep the argument visible, but none of its main terms is as simple as it first appears. Happiness includes questions of quality; justice has a force that a moral theory needs to explain.

Read by testing the principle against a case in which you feel pulled in different directions. What exactly would you be refusing to count if consequences did not matter?
