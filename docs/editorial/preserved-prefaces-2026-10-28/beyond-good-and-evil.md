A philosopher presents an argument as though it were a view from nowhere. Nietzsche asks who needed that argument to be true.

Beyond Good and Evil brings motives, temperament and valuation into places where readers may expect pure reasoning. The challenge is exhilarating because familiar certainties become available for inspection. It is also uncomfortable: suspicion of everyone else's motives can become another form of unexamined superiority.

The book moves through short sections that provoke, distinguish, exaggerate and return from new angles. Its attacks on inherited morality do not amount to a simple invitation to do whatever feels good. Nor should its aristocratic and exclusionary claims be softened into agreeable advice about individuality.

Read as someone prepared to be challenged and to answer back. Which of your convictions would survive discovering the comfort, pride or fear that helped you acquire it?
