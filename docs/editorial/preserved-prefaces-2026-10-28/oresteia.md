A king is coming home from war. His return should restore a household. The household has been waiting with injuries of its own.

Across three plays, Aeschylus lets one demand for justice become the cause of the next. Each act can be explained by a previous wrong. The explanation does not erase the new suffering it creates. A child may inherit a duty to avenge a parent without having chosen the world that makes the duty seem necessary.

The trilogy asks how people could ever leave such a pattern. Who can judge when every participant has a claim? What must a community build if punishment is to become something other than the next family's grievance?

Listen to the voices that insist their pain cannot simply be set aside. A stable settlement may need authority, persuasion and concessions that nobody finds entirely satisfying. When does accepting an imperfect judgment become more courageous than pursuing the justice you know you deserve?
