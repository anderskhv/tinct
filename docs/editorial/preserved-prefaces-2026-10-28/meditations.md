Marcus Aurelius has an argument with himself about getting out of bed.

It is a small, recognisable moment in the private writing of a Roman emperor. He can direct armies and make decisions that alter thousands of lives. Yet on this particular morning the bed is warm, the day will make demands, and he would rather stay where he is.

This is one reason to read Meditations. The distance between knowing how we ought to live and actually doing it does not disappear when we gain power, education or experience. Marcus keeps returning to patience, vanity, resentment and the brevity of life because the same problems keep returning to him.

He practises putting a pause between what happens and the judgment he makes about it. Someone is rude. Must that decide the rest of his day? Someone praises him. Must he start organising his life around hearing it again? His body will age, people will misunderstand him, and his name will eventually mean nothing to those who follow. What remains worth doing?

His answer repeatedly brings him back to other people. They can be difficult, selfish or mistaken; he still has work to do with them and obligations towards them. There is a demanding tenderness in the effort to remember that, especially when he is least inclined to feel it.

You need not agree with every argument, or imagine that writing these things made Marcus a perfect ruler. The interest lies partly in hearing someone rehearse a standard he knows he can fail to meet.

Read a little, then leave room for it to meet an actual day. Which of your convictions still holds when you are tired, offended, frightened, or simply reluctant to get out of bed?
