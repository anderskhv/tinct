A new tenant calls on his landlord and congratulates himself on finding a place far from society. Within a few visits, he has mistaken almost every relationship in the house. Lockwood’s confidence gives us a useful way into Wuthering Heights: we begin among strangers, with names to learn and judgments to reconsider.

The housekeeper Nelly Dean offers to explain. Her memories take us back to the childhood of Catherine Earnshaw and Heathcliff, the unknown boy brought into Catherine’s home. Affection grows beside resentment; lessons, clothes and the right to sit at a table become ways of deciding who belongs.

The two houses, Wuthering Heights and Thrushcross Grange, stand within reach of each other across the moors. Their inhabitants cannot keep their histories separate. Marriage and inheritance carry private quarrels into the lives of children, while repeated names make family likeness both intimate and confusing.

Nelly has acted in the history she tells, and Lockwood writes down a story he is still learning to understand. Notice who speaks, who carries a message, and who decides to keep something back. The force of a feeling does not settle whether its account is fair.
