Creon has forbidden the burial of a man he considers an enemy of the city. Antigone regards the dead man as her brother, and burial as a duty no ruler can cancel.

Both understand their position as an obligation. Creon wants public authority to survive civil conflict. Antigone refuses to let political classification decide what she owes the dead. Their confrontation becomes dangerous partly because each believes that yielding would betray something larger than personal preference.

Sophocles gives courage an uncomfortable companion: the inability to hear a warning. Who gets to interpret divine duty? When does defending order become defending one's own authority? What happens to everyone nearby when two people make compromise a form of disgrace?

Read for the quieter voices trying to open a space between surrender and collision. We admire people who stand by their principles. How do we recognise when that admiration has stopped us asking what those principles require us to see?
