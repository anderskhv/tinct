The man speaking wants you to know that he is unpleasant. He will confess, contradict himself, attack an imagined objection and then accuse you of misunderstanding him. He is very intelligent. That does not seem to be helping.

His quarrel is with the idea that human beings could be made happy if someone worked out their interests correctly. What if a person deliberately chooses something harmful, simply to avoid becoming predictable? The narrator turns that possibility into a defence of freedom, then gives us reasons to wonder what his freedom has become.

The argument soon acquires a personal history. Humiliation, vanity and the longing to be recognised make his reasoning painfully concrete. He can see through his own motives and still repeat them. Self-awareness offers no automatic escape from cruelty.

The voice can be funny, abrasive and embarrassingly familiar. Read for the difference between understanding a wound and making it the organising principle of a life. When has proving a point cost you something you actually wanted?
