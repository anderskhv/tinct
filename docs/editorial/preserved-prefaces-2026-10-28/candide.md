---

Candide has been given a reassuring education. His tutor can explain why everything is arranged for the best. It is a useful belief to carry out into the world, provided you do not look too closely at what happens there.

Voltaire looks closely, then tells the story at an outrageous speed. Armies march, disasters arrive, fortunes vanish. A character scarcely has time to recover from one calamity before the next begins. The explanations keep coming too. Almost anything can be made to fit a theory if somebody else is paying the price.

The surprise is how funny this can be. The calm sentences and confident reasoning become absurd beside the things they describe. Laughter gives us a moment's distance, then the human cost catches up.

Candide's trust makes him easy to fool, but it also gives him reasons to keep going. He wants people to be good; he wants love and a place in the world. Losing every hope would be a kind of defeat too. His companions offer other ways of meeting misfortune, from sweeping pessimism to the practical business of finding food and getting someone out of trouble.

Voltaire's targets are particular as well as philosophical: religious persecution, the glamour of war, the wealth made from other people's suffering. There are things here that should resist our appetite for a comforting explanation.

The book asks what becomes possible when we stop needing the world to prove our theory right. If an explanation for suffering leaves the sufferer exactly where they were, what has it helped us do?
