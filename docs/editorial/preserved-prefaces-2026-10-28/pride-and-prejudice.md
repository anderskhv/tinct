Elizabeth Bennet knows exactly what she thinks of Mr Darcy. He is proud, disagreeable and far too impressed with his own importance. He has also insulted her within earshot. Disliking him is going to be a pleasure.

Elizabeth is good company. She notices absurdity, enjoys a sharp reply and refuses to be dazzled by money. Austen makes it easy to laugh with her. The difficulty is that enjoying someone's judgment can make us forget to question it. Once we like a person, how much evidence do we need to believe them? Once we dislike someone, how much would it take to change our minds?

Behind the jokes, the Bennet family has a real problem. There are five daughters, and the family estate will pass to a male cousin. Their mother's determination to find them husbands can be mortifying, but the insecurity behind it is real. A marriage might offer affection, money, a home, social advancement, or some uneasy combination. Choosing badly could shape the rest of a woman's life.

The brilliance of the novel is how much of this can happen over dinner, at a dance, or in a conversation that everyone remembers differently. An interruption reveals a family. A compliment betrays a calculation. Someone with impeccable manners can be intolerable; someone who makes us uncomfortable may deserve a second look.

Read for the comedy, and notice whose version of events you are enjoying. Elizabeth's intelligence is part of her appeal. It also makes her very good at finding reasons to believe what she already wants to believe. How sure are you that you don't do the same?
