Abraham is asked to sacrifice his son. The story can become so familiar in religious life that its terror disappears. Fear and Trembling insists that we feel the difficulty again.

Its speaker circles the difference between an action others can recognise as an ethical sacrifice and a faith that cannot make itself publicly intelligible. From outside, the act may look appalling. From within the biblical story, Abraham is praised. What could account for that distance?

Kierkegaard does not offer a comfortable formula for licensing private certainty. The pseudonymous narrator repeatedly confronts what he cannot understand. The reader is asked to resist both easy admiration and the impulse to make faith an ordinary achievement of moral reasoning.

Read slowly around the repetitions. What would distinguish trust in God from confidence in a demand one has invented? The possibility that the difference cannot be made easy is part of the book's pressure.
