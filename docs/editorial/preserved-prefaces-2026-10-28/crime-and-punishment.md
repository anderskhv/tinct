---

Before Raskolnikov raises an axe, he has to make murder sound reasonable.

He is a former student living in a cramped room in St Petersburg, hungry, isolated and furious at the humiliations of poverty. An old pawnbroker lends money at other people's expense. Why should her life stand between someone like him and everything he might accomplish? History, after all, is full of celebrated men who stepped over bodies.

Dostoevsky lets us feel the pull of that argument from inside the mind making it. A generous impulse can sit beside contempt; a wish to help can become a claim to superiority. Raskolnikov can be painfully alert to another person's suffering and strangely unable to see the person in front of him.

The suspense comes partly from how close we are made to stand. Every encounter can feel like exposure. An ordinary question becomes a threat. We want to know what he will do, but we may also begin recognising smaller versions of his evasions: the explanations that make our own conduct seem exceptional, the injuries that seem to entitle us to something.

His idea of freedom is to cross a boundary other people cannot cross. Dostoevsky presses on the cost of that freedom. Can a person place himself above everyone else without cutting himself off from them? What happens when someone offers care that he has done nothing to deserve?

The novel's Christian imagination makes the question more demanding. A person can be fallen and still bear the image of God. If that dignity survives even terrible wrongdoing, neither contempt nor an easy declaration of innocence will do. Responsibility remains, alongside the possibility of mercy.

Read for the arguments, but also for the bodies in the room: the fever, hunger, embarrassment, kindness and sudden laughter. Ideas become much harder to keep tidy when somebody has to live them.

What would freedom mean for a man who could justify a murder, yet could not bear to be known?
