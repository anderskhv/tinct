Second paper: Generative Adversarial Nets

Same deep test, a new paper: Goodfellow et al., 2014, nine pages, with theorems, proofs and a results table. It ran 41 actions in each product, scored on the same rubric. Tinct comes out at 3.8 out of 5 (3.4 on the first paper) and Gloss at 4.6 (4.7 before). Tinct did better here because this paper has no outline, which sidesteps the bugs that broke whole-paper questions and the primer last time. Gloss made real mistakes this time.

Tested 15 October 2026 on the same live version as the first deep test; none of the fix PRs had shipped. Same rubric, scorer and scripts. Gloss again ran with 87–99% of the paper cached.

What the second paper tells us

  • The worst failures come from papers with an outline. This paper has no outline, and both whole-paper questions and the primer worked. On “Attention”, which has an outline, they answered from one page and one section. So the fix is in how outline units choose their context: send the whole file whenever it fits, which is item A1 of the fix plan.
  • Define’s AI fallback works only when the dictionary lacks the word. When the dictionary has the word with the wrong sense, as with “saturates” here or “perplexity” and “constituency” last time, the wrong sense wins. Hyphenated words were cut in two on both papers.
  • The prompt problems hold on every paper. Explain runs too long. Simplify opens with a description. Answers contain Markdown and em dashes. Quiz question 1 drifts off the selection. Fact-check cites the document itself. All are already in the fix plan.
  • Speed is the steadiest gap. Tinct is 3 to 6 times slower on every action except Define.

New flaws in Tinct

  • MajorTwo-page citations show as raw text. “{p. 4, p. 5}” appeared literally in an answer. Either the parser accepts a comma list, or the prompt allows only one page per brace.
  • MajorCorrections show mid-answer. “…beating DBN and Stacked CAE (2110 ± 50)... actually Stacked CAE is slightly higher there.” Add to the shared rules: check figures before writing them, and never leave a correction in the answer.
  • MinorA paper is called a “chapter”. The whole-paper answer said “Across the chapter” about a paper. For PDFs, use “the document”, or its section names.
  • MinorExplain asserts small things the text doesn’t say. In one answer it attributed a Nash equilibrium to the abstract. The Explain prompt should say “do not attribute to the text what it does not say”.

Gloss is not flawless

These are worth knowing for positioning, and they show what we must not copy.

  • Its re-flow changes facts. The abstract reads “D equal to 1 everywhere”; the paper says ½. The theorem section shows broken maths such as “fracp_data (x)p_data (x)+p_g(x)”. Tinct shows the real PDF.
  • One factual error on the results table. It said GANs lose to Deep GSN on TFD; they beat it.
  • Irrelevant or weak sources. These appeared in two of five fact-checks, for example medical “best evidence” guides cited for a claim about likelihood estimation.
  • Leaks and unsupported claims. Highlight markup leaked (“==…==”), and once it made a claim about the authors that it could not support.
  • Reliability. The page once failed to load (“upstream request failed”), as its Thoreau sample did earlier.

Additions to the fix plan

  1. Citations. Parse comma lists inside braces, or restrict the prompt to one citation per brace.
  2. Shared rules. Add: “Check numbers before you state them; never correct yourself mid-answer.” And: “Do not attribute to the document anything it does not say.”
  3. Define. Prefer the AI meaning in context whenever the passage is technical and define_sense returns 0. That is the existing PR B item; this test confirms it on a second paper.
  4. Wording. Call PDFs “the document”, not “the chapter”, in the whole-paper and primer prompts.
