The Lighthouse Keepers’ Library

On a rock in the North Atlantic, three men kept a lending library alive for forty years. What did they read, and why?
By Ruth Adeyemi
October 20, 2026

TThe first book on the shelf at Skarvholm Light was a ship’s copy of Robinson Crusoe, stamped by a Norwegian freighter that ran aground nearby in 1951. The keepers took it in the way they took in everything the sea gave them: they dried it on the stove, pressed it flat under a toolbox, and wrote the date inside the cover.

By 1990, when the light was automated and the last keeper rowed ashore, the collection had grown to eleven hundred volumes. A ledger recorded every loan. Most of the borrowers were the keepers themselves; a few were fishermen who sheltered on the rock in bad weather and were not allowed to leave without a book.

“A man alone with the sea needs a story that is longer than the storm.”

I found the ledger in a municipal archive in Ålesund, in a box labelled only fyr, lighthouse. Its entries are terse. A title, a name, two dates. But read in sequence they become a kind of diary. One keeper, a man named Arvid Sætre, wrote beside a return of Moby-Dick in March 1963: “A man alone with the sea needs a story that is longer than the storm.”

The ledger shows that winter reading was long and summer reading short. In January the keepers borrowed Tolstoy, Undset, Dickens. In July they borrowed detective stories, often the same one three times.

WWhen I visited Skarvholm last spring, the shelves were empty. The books had been given to a school on the mainland in 1991, and the school had since closed. I went looking for them anyway.

Some had been sold at a church fair. Some had been pulped. Forty-two were in the attic of a retired teacher, who handed them to me in two apple crates and asked only that I bring them back.

Inside the cover of the Crusoe there were nine dates, the last of them in 1989. Under it, in pencil, someone had written a single word: Igjen. Again.
