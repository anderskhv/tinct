## Abstract

We examined whether the kind of annotation adult readers make while reading a literary text predicts what they recall a week later. Ninety-six participants read a short story under one of three conditions: highlighting, writing marginal questions, or no annotation. Participants who wrote questions recalled significantly more of the story’s causal structure (d = 0.61), while highlighting offered no benefit over reading alone. We discuss the implications for digital reading tools.

Keywords: annotation, recall, literary reading, marginalia

## 1 Introduction

Readers have written in the margins of books for as long as there have been margins. Medieval glosses, Renaissance commonplace books and the pencilled “!” in a library copy are all versions of the same act: a reader talking back to a text. Yet most digital reading tools reduce this act to a single gesture, the highlight, which marks a passage without asking the reader to say anything about it.

Earlier work found that highlighting is at best weakly related to comprehension (Fowler & Barker, 1974; Dunlosky et al., 2013). Less is known about generative annotation, in which the reader writes something of their own. If the benefit of annotation comes from elaboration rather than selection, then questions written in the margin should help more than highlights, and the effect should be largest for the causal links that hold a narrative together.

### Notes

[1] We use “comprehension” for immediate tests and “recall” for tests after a delay of at least 24 hours.

## 2 Method

### 2.1 Participants

Ninety-six adults (64 women, 31 men, 1 non-binary; age 19–71, M = 38.2) took part online. All were native or fluent readers of English. None had read the story before.

### 2.2 Materials and procedure

The text was a 4,200-word short story in the public domain, presented in a reading app that allowed the assigned form of annotation only. After reading, participants answered five distractor questions. Seven days later they wrote everything they could remember about the story and answered twelve cued-recall items (Table 1).

## 3 Results

Free recall was scored for 40 idea units, 14 of which were causal. Participants in the question condition recalled more causal units (M = 7.9, SD = 2.4) than those who highlighted (M = 6.3, SD = 2.6) or did not annotate (M = 6.1, SD = 2.7; see Figure 1). The difference between highlighting and no annotation was not reliable.

Figure 1. Mean recall by condition (error bars: 95% CI).

Detail items showed no effect of condition. Readers who highlighted were, if anything, slightly worse at recalling the boat’s colour, a detail that a quarter of them had highlighted.
