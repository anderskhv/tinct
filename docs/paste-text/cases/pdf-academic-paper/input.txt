Margins of Attention: Annotation Habits and
Delayed Recall in Adult Readers
Maya Okafor1 and Lukas Brandt2
1 Department of Psychology, University of Northﬁeld
2 Institute for Reading Research, Hamburg
Abstract
We examined whether the kind of annotation adult readers make while reading
a literary text predicts what they recall a week later. Ninety-
six participants read a short story under one of three conditions: high-
lighting, writing marginal questions, or no annotation. Participants who
wrote questions recalled signiﬁcantly more of the story’s causal structure
(d = 0.61), while highlighting oﬀered no beneﬁt over reading alone. We
discuss the implications for digital reading tools.
Keywords: annotation, recall, literary reading, marginalia
1 Introduction
Readers have written in the margins of books for as long as there have been
margins. Medieval glosses, Renaissance commonplace books and the pencilled
“!” in a library copy are all versions of the same act: a reader talking
back to a text. Yet most digital reading tools reduce this act to a single
gesture, the highlight, which marks a passage without asking the reader to
say anything about it.
Earlier work found that highlighting is at best weakly related to compre-
hension (Fowler & Barker, 1974; Dunlosky et al., 2013).1 Less is known about

2 Method
2.1 Participants
Ninety-six adults (64 women, 31 men, 1 non-binary; age 19–71, M = 38.2) took
part online. All were native or ﬂuent readers of English. None had read the
story before.
2.2 Materials and procedure
The text was a 4,200-word short story in the public domain, presented in a
reading app that allowed the assigned form of annotation only. After
reading, participants answered ﬁve distractor questions. Seven days later
they wrote everything they could remember about the story and answered
twelve cued-recall items (Table 1).
