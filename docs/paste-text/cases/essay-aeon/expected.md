## The ladder of knowledge

On the evening of 8 September 1930, the German mathematician David Hilbert stepped up to a microphone in Königsberg and addressed his countrymen by radio. He was 68, recently retired, and at the height of his fame. The talk was a defence of pure reason, delivered in the city of Immanuel Kant, and it ended with two phrases that would later be carved on his tombstone: _Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen_. We must know. We shall know.

![David Hilbert. Courtesy the University of Göttingen](https://images.aeonmedia.co/user_image_upload/e46e5597-25cb-4eb5-a7b9-b9ecc49e9c94/hilbert-cr_university-of-gottingen.webp?width=3840&quality=75&format=auto)

Are certain questions closed to the human mind? Are there truths about the world that we can never know? Hilbert thought that, at least within mathematics, this could not be the case. For him, mathematics was a game played with symbols: one could fix the right axioms as rules of the game and every mathematical problem could be solved. He was wrong.

The day before his Königsberg address, at a small academic meeting in the same city, a 24-year-old Austrian named [Kurt Gödel](https://aeon.co/essays/kurt-godel-his-mother-and-the-argument-for-life-after-death) had quietly announced that no such system for arithmetic could exist. Whatever reasonable set of rules you fix, something true must lie beyond them and thus beyond our knowledge. We can’t know. We won’t know. Gödel’s results, known as the Incompleteness Theorems, are [landmark achievements](https://aeon.co/essays/what-godels-incompleteness-theorems-say-about-ai-morality) of 20th-century logic. Recent work in philosophy and mathematics has begun to illuminate the gap they opened, and with it something deeper about the foundations of knowledge itself. The story begins in the same city, but two centuries earlier.

When Kant remembered a book by [David Hume](https://aeon.co/essays/hume-is-the-amiable-modest-generous-philosopher-we-need-today) he’d read years earlier, he was woken from what he called his ‘dogmatic slumber’. Kant had believed that pure reasoning could teach us every absolute truth about the world, but Hume had asked a question that that stumped him: what reason do we actually have to believe that the future will resemble the past? We may have seen the Sun rise a thousand times, but how do we know it will rise tomorrow? The belief that the future will resemble the past is, Hume argued, merely a kind of habit. Kant couldn’t answer Hume’s challenge and was silenced for a decade.

When Kant finally broke his silence with the _Critique of Pure Reason_ (1781), he offered a radical answer. We can know the world only as it appears to our minds, and that is where our science sits; the noumenal world – the _Ding an sich_ (the ‘thing-in-itself’) – simply lies beyond our reach. Causation is not something we discover in the world, but something the mind supplies for experience to be coherent at all. When we watch a cat push a glass off a table, we don’t see a bare sequence of motions and then infer, by some metaphysical principle, that the push caused the fall. No: we see the push as causing the fall. The causal structure is not a hypothesis we add to the experience; it is part of how the experience comes to us in the first place. It is in our minds already.

So far, so good: Kant has met Hume’s challenge, and the workings of science are intelligible: we are structuring our experiences. But the work is not done, for there remains mathematics, the very language in which the sciences are framed. Hume was content that arithmetic and algebra are merely the unpacking of definitions; that proving a theorem is like unpacking a suitcase to find an outfit. Kant could not leave it there. If mathematics were only such unpacking, how could it underwrite the sciences? Either the sciences tell us nothing new about the world, or something has gone missing from the account.

Does one unpack ‘add 7 and 5’ to find ‘12’? Kant thought not. Rather, one _constructs_ 12 from 7 and 5 in the mind. More than a century later, a young Dutch mathematician called L E J Brouwer, following Kant’s thought, argued that, if the mathematician’s work consists in such mental constructions, then one cannot assume any statement is either true or false. That was controversial: to strip the mathematician of that assumption, Hilbert retorted, would be like ‘proscribing … to the boxer the use of his fists’.

[Gottlob Frege](https://aeon.co/essays/paraconsistent-logics-find-structure-in-our-inconsistent-world), working in the late 19th century, found Kant’s account of mathematics intolerable for different reasons: arithmetic, he claimed, is _not_ psychological. The numbers exist whether anyone counts them or not. He set about reducing arithmetic to pure logic, grounded in the mind-independent categories of True and False. The project consumed two decades of his life and produced some of the most consequential advances in the philosophy of language and mathematics in history. It is widely regarded as having sparked the modern discipline of logic. And yet, as the second volume of his masterwork, the _Grundgesetze der Arithmetik_, sat at the printer in 1902, the foundations cracked. The British philosopher [Bertrand Russell](https://aeon.co/essays/philosophy-at-war-nationalism-and-logical-analysis) sent Frege a letter pointing out an inconsistency at the heart of the system. A melancholy note in his diary later [summed](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00201749608602425) up his defeat: ‘My efforts to become clear about what is meant by number have resulted in failure.’

Russell had been wrestling with the foundations of mathematics for some years before his letter. It was only after meeting the Italian mathematician Giuseppe Peano at the International Congress of Philosophy in 1900 that he acquired the tools to attempt the problem. In place of Frege’s edifice, Russell set out to [build his own](https://aeon.co/videos/a-fanatic-against-fanaticism-and-other-pleasures-of-bertrand-russell-in-his-own-words), together with his colleague Alfred North Whitehead. _Principia Mathematica_ appeared in three massive volumes between 1910 and 1913: all of mathematics, grounded in pure logic.

Russell’s student Ludwig Wittgenstein spent the war in the Austro-Hungarian artillery, volunteering again and again for the most exposed posts he could find – an observation position under fire on the Eastern Front, from which he was decorated for bravery at least three times. He carried notebooks. In them, between bombardments, he worked the Frege-Russell philosophy into a complete account of how language touches the world: the world is made of facts, a sentence is a [picture of a fact](https://aeon.co/ideas/do-you-see-a-duck-or-a-rabbit-just-what-is-aspect-perception), and whatever can be said at all can be said clearly. He finished the manuscript in an Italian prisoner-of-war camp in 1918 and sent it to Russell from the camp. Wittgenstein took himself to have solved the problems of philosophy, gave away his inherited fortune, and [left to teach](https://aeon.co/essays/learning-for-wittgenstein-is-a-whole-life-undertaking) primary school in the Austrian mountains.

If meaning comes from practices of assertion, then our concepts are settled only as far as our practices have settled them. A system of definitions is a kind of constitution that determines something. The US Constitution, for example, is meant to enshrine a form of democracy. But what a constitution defines answers to how it is amended.

_Principia Mathematica_ is a constitution for arithmetic. To read it as a constitution is to assume it is consistent; otherwise, it would be nonsense. So, when we amend it by asserting that ‘_Principia Mathematica_ is consistent,’ we are not changing the subject: the new axiom says aloud only what the practice already did. Gödel’s theorems apply afresh to the enlarged constitution: the amended system has its own unprovable truth, its own consistency it cannot certify. No amendment is the last; each faithful extension opens room for the next. To grasp a concept like arithmetic is not to survey a finished structure; it is to hold a principle for going on: a rule for laying the next stretch of line, not a map of where the line ends. Dummett [called](https://philpapers.org/rec/DUMWIM) this openness _indefinite extensibility_.

_Faithful_ is the word doing the work, because not every amendment is. Gödel claimed the US Constitution could deliver a dictatorship. That does not make dictatorship a form of democracy. Whatever loophole he had in mind, it would have contradicted the practices the Constitution already enshrined. An amendment can betray what a constitution defines. But it can also unfold it, drawing out what the founding commitments demanded all along. Only the second kind is what Dummett has in mind.

The constitution of our language is our current practice for public discourse. As it is extensible in this way, what we claim as ‘true’ is not a fixed structure with a ceiling we are forever failing to reach, but something that can change. Gödel’s verdict never applied to mathematics, but only to some edition of the constitution. Within the Sandqvist system, we can attend to the extension systematically. Hilbert’s words outlive his programme: _Wir werden wissen._ We shall know. Not because the answers are waiting at the end of a ladder, but because truth goes exactly as far as we have built the rungs.
