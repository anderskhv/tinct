## On reading with a pencil

Marginalia have a bad reputation in libraries and a good one in archives. The same note that a librarian calls damage, a historian calls evidence.

Coleridge was the great annotator of the Romantic period; his friends lent him books in the hope that he would write in them. Charles Lamb complained that Coleridge returned books “with usury”, enriched with notes, which is the opposite of how most lenders feel.

Not everyone agrees. There is a whole etiquette of the pencil: soft lead only, never ink, never in a borrowed book.

The best argument for writing in books is that it slows you down at exactly the moments that matter.

### Notes

[1] See H. J. Jackson, _Marginalia: Readers Writing in Books_ (Yale, 2001), for the history.

[2] Many of his annotated copies survive; the [British Library](https://www.bl.uk/) holds several.

Lamb’s phrase comes from his essay “The Two Races of Men”, about borrowers and lenders.

[3] My own mother would rather I dog-eared a page than wrote on it, and she was a teacher.

[4] Try it with a short book first.
