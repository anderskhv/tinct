The Odyssey is about a man trying to get home after a war, and a family who no longer know whether to expect him. Odysseus has been gone for twenty years. His son was a baby when he left; his wife, Penelope, has spent much of her life married to someone who may be dead. Everyone knows stories about him, but nobody can tell them whether he is coming back.

The Cyclops, the Sirens, the voyages from one dangerous island to another have passed through so many retellings that they have acquired lives of their own, far beyond Homer’s poem. Alongside the Bible, Homer’s poems belong among the works that have most deeply shaped Western literature. Generations of writers have borrowed from the Odyssey, argued with it, and tried to write it again.

One of its pleasures is discovering how much the familiar outline leaves out. Homer begins with the son. Telemachus has grown up hearing about his remarkable father, while watching men eat through the family’s wealth and press his mother to marry again. He would like someone to come and put things right. He is also reaching the age when people expect him to do something himself. The father’s absence has become the condition of the son’s whole life, and waiting for him is beginning to look dangerously like a way of never growing up.

Odysseus, meanwhile, has been offered something that ought to make the journey unnecessary. The goddess Calypso wants to keep him with her and make him immortal. He could escape old age and death, and he spends his days on the shore grieving for home. When Calypso compares herself to his wife, he concedes that Penelope is less beautiful. Penelope will age, too. He wants her anyway. The goddess can offer him an endless future, but she cannot give him the years he has already lived with someone else.

There is room in this poem for that kind of attachment alongside a considerable appetite for adventure. Odysseus is curious, resourceful, vain, persuasive, and sometimes cruel. Watching him talk his way through danger is part of the fun, especially as you begin to understand how much he enjoys being the cleverest person present. His gifts and his failings are closely entangled; you may admire him enormously in one passage and lose patience with him in the next.

The distance between his world and ours makes these encounters more interesting. These people take omens seriously, depend on strangers for shelter, and care fiercely about names and ancestry. Their great fighting men also cry without much embarrassment. You begin to notice which things you recognise immediately and which things you had assumed were simply human but turn out to belong to your own time.

For all its monsters and divine interventions, the Odyssey gives extraordinary attention to the ordinary life its hero is trying to recover. A meal, a bed, news of someone loved: Homer understands how much these can matter to a person who has gone without them. He gives them the full weight of things someone might cross the world to have again.
