Introduction and plan of the work

Each nation's annual labour is the original source of all the necessities and comforts it consumes in the course of a year. These consist either of the direct produce of that labour or of what it buys from other nations with that produce.

How well the nation is supplied therefore depends on how much of that produce, or what it buys, there is for the number of people who have to consume it.

In every nation this proportion is determined by two things. The first is the skill, dexterity and judgement with which people generally apply their labour. The second is the proportion between the number engaged in useful work and the number who are not. Whatever the soil, climate or extent of a particular country, the abundance or scarcity of its annual supplies must, in its particular circumstances, depend on these two things.

The first seems to matter more than the second. Among the hunting and fishing peoples described as savage, everyone able to work does some useful labour, endeavouring to provide as well as possible for himself and those of his family or tribe who are too old, young or infirm to hunt or fish. Yet such peoples are so miserably poor that mere want often drives them, or at least leaves them believing that they are driven, either to kill their infants, their old people and their chronically sick outright, or to leave them to starve or be devoured by wild animals. In civilised and flourishing nations, in contrast, many people do no work at all. Many of them consume the produce of ten, often a hundred, times as much labour as most of the people who work. Yet the whole society produces so much that there is often plenty for everyone. A worker of the poorest and lowest rank, if thrifty and industrious, may enjoy a greater share of the necessities and comforts of life than anyone among those other peoples can obtain.

The first book of this inquiry considers what has made labour so much more productive and how its produce is naturally distributed among people of different ranks and circumstances in society.

So long as the skill, dexterity and judgement with which a nation applies its labour remain the same, the abundance or scarcity of its annual supplies must depend on the proportion between those employed in useful labour each year and those who are not. As will appear later, the number of useful and productive workers is everywhere proportionate to the amount of capital used to put them to work and to the way that capital is employed. The second book therefore examines what capital is, how it is gradually accumulated and how much labour it sets in motion when used in different ways.

Nations fairly well advanced in the skill, dexterity and judgement with which they work have followed very different policies in directing labour as a whole. Those policies have not all been equally favourable to the size of its produce. Some nations have particularly encouraged rural industry, others the industry of towns. Scarcely any nation has dealt impartially with every kind. Since the fall of the Roman Empire, European policy has been more favourable to the crafts, manufactures and commerce of towns than to agriculture, the industry of the countryside. The third book explains how this policy seems to have begun and taken hold.

These different policies may originally have sprung from the private interests and prejudices of particular groups, with no thought or foresight of their consequences for the general welfare. Yet they have given rise to very different theories of political economy. Some exalt the importance of industry in the towns, others that of industry in the countryside. These theories have considerably influenced both learned opinion and the public conduct of princes and sovereign states. In the fourth book I have tried to explain, as fully and distinctly as I can, these various theories and their principal effects in different times and nations.

The purpose of the first four books is to explain what the income of the people as a whole consists of, or what funds have supplied their annual consumption in different times and nations. The fifth and last book examines the revenue of the sovereign or commonwealth. First I ask what expenses a sovereign or commonwealth must incur, which should be paid for by contributions from the whole society, and which by particular parts or members of it. Second, I examine the different ways the whole society can be made to contribute to the expenses that fall on it as a whole, and the main advantages and disadvantages of each. Third and last, I ask what reasons have led nearly all modern governments to mortgage part of this revenue or incur debts, and what effect those debts have had on the real wealth of society, its annual produce of land and labour.
