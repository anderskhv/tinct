Opening commendatory poems

Urganda the Unknown to the book of Don Quixote of La Mancha
If you approach the good,
my book, with care,
no loudmouthed chatterer will say
you have stepped wrong.
But if you grow impatient
to reach the hands of idiots,
you will see in no time
that they never hit a nail,
though they bite their fingers
to show their critical discernment.
And since experience teaches
that whoever leans against a good tree
finds shelter in a good shade,
your lucky star in Béjar
offers you a royal tree
that bears princes as its fruit,
and on which a duke has flowered,
a new Alexander the Great.
Go into his shade, for fortune
favours those who dare.
You will tell the adventures
of a nobleman from La Mancha
whose idle reading
turned his head:
ladies, arms and knights
excited him so much
that, like Orlando the Furious
blended with Orlando in Love,
he won by strength of arm
Dulcinea del Toboso.
Put no obscure hieroglyphs
upon your shield,
for when your hand is all picture cards
you bid with a poor score.
If your dedication is modest,
no one will say mockingly:
“What Álvaro de Luna,
what Hannibal of Carthage,
what King Francis in Spain
is complaining of Fortune?”
Since heaven did not wish you
to be as learned
as the black Juan Latino,
avoid speaking Latin.
Do not show off your sharp wit
or appeal to philosophers,
for with a twisted mouth
someone who sees through the tricks
will say close beside your ear:
“Why try your tricks on me?”
Do not get into tangled affairs
or pry into other people’s lives;
where a matter does not concern you,
it is wise to pass by.
Those who crack jokes
often get a blow on the cap;
burn your eyebrows instead
in earning a good name,
for whoever prints foolishness
puts it out at perpetual interest.
Remember how foolish it is,
when your roof is made of glass,
to take stones in your hands
and throw them at your neighbour.
Let the man of judgment,
in the works he writes,
proceed with feet of lead;
whoever publishes pages
to amuse young ladies
writes for fools and at random.

Amadis of Gaul to Don Quixote of La Mancha — Sonnet
You who imitated the tearful life
I lived, absent and rejected,
on the great height of Peña Pobre,
turned from happiness to penance;
you whose eyes supplied you
with abundant drink, though salty,
and who, deprived of silver, tin and copper,
ate earth’s food upon the earth:
live certain that forever,
at least while bright Apollo
drives his horses through the fourth sphere,
you will have a shining name for courage;
your homeland will be first among them all,
and your wise author unique in the world.

Don Belianis of Greece to Don Quixote of La Mancha — Sonnet
I broke, cut, dented, spoke and acted
more than any wandering knight on earth;
I was skilful, brave and proud;
a thousand wrongs I avenged, a hundred thousand I repaired.
I gave Fame deeds to make immortal;
I was a courteous and tender lover;
every giant was a dwarf to me,
and I satisfied every point of honour.
I had Fortune prostrate at my feet,
and my good sense dragged bald Opportunity
about by her forelock.
Yet though my fortune always
stood raised above the crescent moon,
I envy your achievements, great Quixote!

Lady Oriana to Dulcinea del Toboso — Sonnet
Beautiful Dulcinea, if only I could,
for greater comfort and repose,
set Miraflores down in El Toboso
and exchange its London for your village!
If only I could adorn soul and body
with your desires and livery
and watch some unequal combat
of the famous knight you made fortunate!
If only I had escaped as chastely
from Señor Amadis as you did
from the courteous gentleman Don Quixote!
Then I would be envied, not envious;
the time that was sad would be happy,
and I would enjoy the pleasures without paying my share.

Gandalin, squire to Amadis of Gaul, to Sancho Panza — Sonnet
Hail, famous man, for whom Fortune,
when she put you into the squire’s trade,
arranged things so gently and wisely
that you passed through without a mishap.
Now the hoe and sickle scarcely conflict
with the wandering profession; now
plain squirely manners are in fashion, and with them I rebuke
the proud man who tries to trample on the moon.
I envy your donkey and your name,
and equally envy your saddlebags,
which showed your sensible foresight.
Hail once more, Sancho, such a good man,
to whom alone our Spanish Ovid
offers his hand to kiss, then deals a mocking blow.

The Witty One, a poet of mixed sorts, to Sancho Panza
I am Sancho Panza, squire
to Don Quixote of La Mancha.
I took to my heels
to live like a sensible man;
for silent Villadiego
summed up all his reason of state
in a retreat,
as Celestina tells us,
a divine book, in my opinion,
if only it concealed the human more.

The Witty One to Rocinante
I am the famous Rocinante,
great-grandson of the great Babieca.
For my sins of weakness
I fell into the hands of a Don Quixote.
I ran in a pair for feebleness;
but with a horse’s thieving fingers
I let no barley escape me.
That I learned from Lazarillo
when I gave him the straw
to steal the blind man’s wine.

Orlando the Furious to Don Quixote of La Mancha — Sonnet
Though you are not a peer, you have no peer;
among a thousand peers you could be one,
and none can equal you where you are,
unconquered conqueror, never overcome.
I am Orlando, Quixote, who, lost
for Angelica, saw distant seas,
offering on Fame’s altars
the courage that Oblivion spared.
I cannot be your equal, for that honour
is due to your achievements and your fame,
although you lost your mind as I did.
But you will be mine if you subdue the proud Moor
and fierce Scythian, who today calls us
equals in unsuccessful love.

The Knight of Phoebus to Don Quixote of La Mancha — Sonnet
My sword did not equal yours,
Spanish Phoebus, accomplished courtier,
nor my hand your lofty glory of courage,
though it struck like lightning where day is born and dies.
I scorned empires; the monarchy
the red East offered me in vain
I abandoned to see the sovereign face
of Claridiana, my beautiful dawn.
I loved her as a unique and wondrous miracle;
and, absent and out of favour with her,
I made hell itself fear the arm that tamed its fury.
But you, Gothic Quixote, illustrious and bright,
are everlasting in the world through Dulcinea,
and she through you is famous, chaste and wise.

Solisdan to Don Quixote of La Mancha — Sonnet
Though foolishness, Sir Quixote,
has knocked your brain about,
no one will ever reproach you
as a man of vile and sordid deeds.
Your achievements will be your judges,
for you have travelled righting wrongs,
though beaten a thousand times
by cowardly, wretched, wicked ruffians.
And if your lovely Dulcinea
commits an injustice against you
and shows no kindness toward your troubles,
let this be your comfort in that misfortune:
Sancho Panza was a poor go-between;
he was foolish, she was hard, and you no lover.

Dialogue between Babieca and Rocinante — Sonnet
Babieca: Why are you so thin, Rocinante?
Rocinante: Because there is never food, and always work.
Babieca: Then what becomes of the barley and the straw?
Rocinante: My master leaves me not a mouthful.
Babieca: Come, sir, you are very badly bred,
when your donkey’s tongue insults your master.
Rocinante: An ass is an ass from cradle to shroud.
Want proof? Look at him in love.
Babieca: Is loving foolish? Rocinante: It is not very prudent.
Babieca: You are metaphysical. Rocinante: It is because I do not eat.
Babieca: Complain to the squire. Rocinante: That will not do.
How can I complain of my troubles
when master and squire, or steward,
are just as much hacks as Rocinante?
