The Author’s Preface to Part One

Reader with time to spare: you can believe me without an oath when I say I wish this book, the child of my understanding, were the most beautiful, spirited and intelligent book imaginable. But I have not been able to overturn nature’s rule that everything produces something like itself. What could my barren, poorly cultivated intelligence produce but the story of a dry, shrivelled, whimsical child, full of assorted thoughts that nobody else ever imagined? Just the sort of child conceived in a prison, where every discomfort has its seat and every sad noise its home. Peace, a pleasant retreat, lovely fields, clear skies, murmuring springs and a quiet mind do much to make even the most barren muses fruitful and bring children into the world who fill it with wonder and delight. A father sometimes has an ugly, graceless son, but his love blindfolds him: he cannot see the faults, or rather takes them for intelligence and charm and describes them to his friends as wit and grace. But I, who seem to be Don Quixote’s father although I am his stepfather, will not follow that custom. Nor will I beg you, dearest reader, almost in tears as others do, to forgive or overlook the faults you find in this child of mine. You are neither his relation nor his friend. You have your own soul and as much free will as anyone. You are in your own house, as much its master as the king is master of his taxes, and you know the saying: Under my cloak I kill the king. All this releases you from every consideration and obligation. You can say whatever you think of the story without fearing abuse for your criticism or expecting a reward for your praise.

I would like to give it to you plain and bare, without the ornament of a preface or the endless catalogue of customary sonnets, epigrams and praises put at the beginning of books. For although writing the story cost me some labour, nothing seemed harder than making this preface you are reading. Many times I picked up my pen to write it and many times put it down, not knowing what to say. On one such occasion I sat hesitating, with the paper before me, the pen behind my ear, my elbow on the desk and my cheek in my hand. While I was thinking what to say, a witty, intelligent friend unexpectedly came in. Seeing me so deep in thought, he asked why. I hid nothing: I was considering the preface I had to write for Don Quixote’s history, and it troubled me so much that I wanted neither to write it nor to publish so noble a knight’s achievements.

“How could I help worrying about what that ancient lawgiver called the public will say? After all these years asleep in the silence of oblivion, here I come with my years upon my back and a story as dry as esparto grass, without invention, scant in style, poor in ideas and lacking all scholarship and learning. There are no references in the margins or notes at the end, unlike other books I see. Though entirely fictional and worldly, those books are so full of sayings from Aristotle, Plato and the whole crowd of philosophers that they astonish their readers, who take the authors for well-read, learned and eloquent men. And when they quote Holy Scripture! You would think they were so many Saint Thomases and other doctors of the Church. Their sense of propriety is so ingenious that in one line they portray a distracted lover and in the next deliver a little Christian sermon delightful to hear or read. My book must go without all that. I have nothing to cite in the margin or annotate at the end. Still less do I know which authors I follow, so as to list them at the beginning in alphabetical order as everyone does, starting with Aristotle and ending with Xenophon, Zoilus or Zeuxis, though one was a slanderer and the other a painter. My book must also lack opening sonnets, at least ones by dukes, marquises, counts, bishops, ladies or celebrated poets. Yet if I asked two or three friends who know the trade, I am sure they would provide some that the most famous names in Spain could not equal.

“In short, my friend,” I went on, “I have decided that Señor Don Quixote must remain buried in his archives in La Mancha until heaven provides someone to furnish everything he lacks. My inadequacy and scant learning make me incapable of supplying it, and I am naturally too idle and lazy to hunt for authors to say what I can say without them. That explains the hesitation and abstraction in which you found me. What you have heard is reason enough.”

My friend slapped his forehead and burst out laughing. “By God, brother, now I am finally freed of an error I have lived in all the time I have known you!” he said. “I always thought you sensible and prudent in everything you did. Now I see you are as far from that as heaven is from earth. How can such trifling, easily remedied matters hold up and bewilder a mature intelligence like yours, accustomed to breaking through much greater difficulties? This comes not from a lack of ability but from an excess of laziness and a shortage of thought. Want to see whether I am right? Listen, and in the blink of an eye I will sweep away all the difficulties and supply everything you say is missing, everything that stops and discourages you from publishing the history of your famous Don Quixote, the light and mirror of all knight-errantry.”

“Tell me, then,” I replied, “how do you propose to fill the gap left by my fear and bring clarity to the chaos of my confusion?”

He answered: “Your first concern is the sonnets, epigrams and praises you lack at the beginning, which ought to be written by important people with titles. Solve that by taking a little trouble to write them yourself. Afterwards you can christen them with whatever names you like, assigning their parentage to Prester John of the Indies or the Emperor of Trebizond, who I understand were famous poets. Even if they were not, and some pedants or bachelors snap at you behind your back and question the truth, do not care two maravedis. Even if they prove you a liar, they will not cut off the hand you wrote with.

“As for references in the margins to the books and authors supplying your sayings and maxims, simply fit in some Latin phrases you know by heart, or can find without much trouble. When discussing freedom and captivity, for example, put:

Non bene pro toto libertas venditur auro.
Freedom is not profitably sold for all the gold in the world.

“Then cite Horace in the margin, or whoever said it. If you discuss death’s power, bring in:

Pallida mors aequo pulsat pede pauperum tabernas,
Regumque turres.
Pale Death knocks with an equal foot at the cottages of the poor
and the towers of kings.

“For friendship and the love God commands us to bear our enemy, go straight to Holy Scripture. A little searching will do. Use no less than God’s own words: Ego autem dico vobis: diligite inimicos vestros — But I say to you: Love your enemies. For evil thoughts, use the Gospel: De corde exeunt cogitationes malae — From the heart come evil thoughts. For the instability of friends, there is Cato with his couplet:

Donec eris felix, multos numerabis amicos,
Tempora si fuerint nubila, solus eris.
While fortune favours you, you will count many friends;
when dark days come, you will be alone.

“With these scraps of Latin and others like them, people will at least take you for a grammarian, which brings no small honour and profit these days.

“You can safely add notes at the end in this way. If you mention a giant, make him Goliath. At almost no expense, that gives you a grand note: The giant Golias, or Goliath, was a Philistine whom the shepherd David killed with a mighty stone-throw in the Valley of Terebinth, as related in the Book of Kings — in whatever chapter you find it written.

“Next, to show your learning in literature and cosmography, arrange to mention the River Tagus. There is another splendid note ready-made: The River Tagus was named after a king of Spain; it rises in such-and-such a place and ends in the ocean, kissing the walls of the famous city of Lisbon; its sands are believed to be gold, and so on. If you discuss robbers, I will give you the story of Cacus, which I know by heart. For prostitutes, the Bishop of Mondoñedo will lend you Lamia, Laida and Flora; notes on them will bring you great credit. For cruel women, Ovid will supply Medea. For enchanters and witches, Homer has Calypso and Virgil Circe. For brave commanders, Julius Caesar will lend you himself in his Commentaries, and Plutarch will give you a thousand Alexanders. If you discuss love, a scrap of Tuscan will lead you to Leon the Hebrew, who will fill your measure. If you do not want to travel abroad, you have Fonseca’s On the Love of God at home, containing everything you or the most inventive mind could desire on the subject. In short, just mention these names or touch on these stories and leave the notes and references to me. I swear I will fill your margins and use four printed sheets at the end.

“Now for the citations of authors other books have and yours lacks. The remedy is easy. Find a book that cites them all from A to Z, as you say, and put that same alphabet into yours. Even if the lie is obvious because you had so little need of them, it does not matter. Someone may be simple enough to believe you used them all in your plain, straightforward story. If it does nothing else, the long list will instantly give your book an air of authority. Besides, nobody will bother to check whether you followed them: it is no concern of theirs. And if I understand correctly, your book needs none of the things you say it lacks. It is an attack on books of chivalry from beginning to end, books Aristotle never thought of, Saint Basil never discussed and Cicero never knew. Their fantastic nonsense has nothing to do with the exactness of truth or the observations of astrology. Geometrical measurements and refutations of rhetorical arguments are of no importance to it. Nor must your book preach at anybody, mixing human and divine things into a patchwork no Christian understanding should wear. You need only make use of imitation in your writing: the more perfect the imitation, the better the result. Since your work aims only to destroy the authority and acceptance books of chivalry enjoy in the world and among the public, there is no need to beg maxims from philosophers, advice from Holy Scripture, fables from poets, speeches from rhetoricians or miracles from saints. Simply make your sentences sound clear and lively, using meaningful, appropriate, well-placed words. Represent your purpose as well as you can and make your ideas understood without tangling or darkening them. Aim also to make the melancholy reader laugh, and the cheerful reader laugh still more; to keep the simple reader from boredom, make the discerning admire your invention, prevent the serious from despising it and win praise from the wise. Keep your sights on bringing down the badly founded structure of these chivalric books, hated by many and praised by many more. If you achieve that, you will have achieved no small thing.”

I listened to my friend in profound silence. His words made such an impression on me that I accepted them without argument and decided to make this very preface from them. Here, gentle reader, you will see my friend’s good sense, my good fortune in finding such a counsellor when I most needed one, and your own relief at receiving the story of the famous Don Quixote of La Mancha so plainly and without complications. All the inhabitants of Campo de Montiel consider him the chastest lover and the bravest knight seen in that district for many years. I will not magnify the service I do you by introducing so noble and honourable a knight. But I do want your thanks for introducing his famous squire, Sancho Panza, in whom I believe I have gathered all the squirely jokes scattered throughout the crowd of empty chivalric books. And with that, may God give you health and not forget me. Farewell.
