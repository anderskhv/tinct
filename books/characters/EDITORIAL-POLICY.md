# Character cards: orientation and spoilers

Adopted 2026-09-09 after Anders tested The Awakening’s first-encounter cards.

A card answers **“Who is this?”** first. Give the reader a useful identity, not a paraphrase of the sentence they just selected. A description that could identify several people (“the woman at the house”) fails this test.

Ordinary, non-concealed identity can be available from the first mention: family relationships, occupation, the role someone has in the opening setting, and the protagonist’s identity. The text may explain these a few paragraphs later. This is an explicitly reviewed editorial allowance, not a rule permitting all later facts. A deliberately concealed identity, disputed relationship, or relationship revealed as a surprise must stay gated.

For example, Léonce Pontellier is Edna’s husband and a New Orleans broker; Edna is the novel’s central character. Madame Lebrun owns and runs the Grand Isle summer boarding house and cottages. Birds and a newspaper are incidental scene details and should not replace these identities.

Later actions, changing relationships, private motives, revelations, and outcomes remain tied to the point where the reader encounters them. Robert can be identified as Madame Lebrun’s son immediately; his feelings for Edna, departure, return, and farewell remain separate gated developments. Arobin’s first incidental mention must not announce his later relationship with Edna or his whole-book importance.

Use the shortest useful reminder once a person is familiar. Identity alone can remain sufficient for many chapters; do not create running plot summaries or add a snapshot just because something happened. Change a card only when it improves recognition or prevents confusion. Avoid repeating the same identity in both a subtitle and body. When a subtitle is used, keep it accurate as the story progresses. Once the reader knows the protagonist, omit repeated framing such as “the young mother whose summer we follow”; begin with the relevant development. If identity itself changes or is revealed, author a gated subtitle change rather than applying this pilot’s stable-subtitle approach mechanically to another book.

For cultural references, explain the reference plainly. Do not merely say “the figure mentioned here.” Add only useful, checked context; avoid irrelevant biography or the ending of an embedded story. Keep uncertainty explicit: the text does not establish who or what Gluglu is, or name the Goncourt book. Attribute gossip and interpretation rather than turning them into narrator-certified facts.

## Review before compiling

- Read the first card at the selected name, before its paragraph-end reminder. Does it identify the person usefully?
- Review the next reminder too: it must not lose the identity supplied at first encounter.
- Check ordinary identity facts against the book, including family relationships and the direction of actions such as lending a book.
- Inspect early, middle, and late cards. Preserve meaningful developments and their release boundaries.
- Check that returning to an earlier passage restores the appropriate earlier card and hides later people and role labels.
- Regenerate the editorial reading copy, source worksheet, compiled bindings, and runtime asset. Verify both editions without changing their text.

## Approved categories

- **Central:** a principal figure whose experience or perspective carries the work.
- **Major:** substantial involvement across the work without being one of its central figures.
- **Supporting:** a recurring or locally important participant.
- **Reference:** a person or family chiefly mentioned, cited, or discussed rather than participating substantially.

Classification is editorial and work-specific. A historical figure may be central or major, not automatically Reference. Whole-book importance differs from prominence in the opening scene. Gate a category when showing it would advertise future importance. Categories do not justify a longer card.

For philosophical dialogues, classify participants by their part in the dialogue; cited thinkers normally remain references. For treatises, documents, and essays, provide useful people/reference identification without inventing a fictional cast or calling the author a protagonist. For scripture and collections, record the relevant section as well as whole-work context; no chapter should display a gallery of everyone appearing anywhere in the collection.
