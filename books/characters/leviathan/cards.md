# Leviathan: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Marcus Brutus · reference

The assassin of Julius Caesar, cited (under the printed spelling "Marcus Brutes") as an example of a vivid fearful dream at Philippi the night before his battle with Augustus Caesar -- which Hobbes argues was likelier a dream than a true vision.

## Julius Caesar · reference

Named directly and by full name at several points: his assassination by senators, his deification by the Senate after death (the origin of Roman "canonizing"), and his triumphal honors. The bare title "Caesar," used generically or ambiguously some twenty times elsewhere in the book, is left unbound; only the full name "Julius Caesar" binds here.

## Augustus Caesar · reference

Named by full name as the general Brutus feared meeting in battle at Philippi, and later as the emperor who took the offices of Pontifex Maximus and Tribune of the People when he converted the Republic into a Monarchy.

## Cadmus · reference

Legendary son of Agenor, King of Phoenicia, credited with bringing the alphabet into Greece -- cited as the reputed, if unverifiable, first source of Greek letters.

## Agenor · reference

King of Phoenicia and father of Cadmus in the legend Hobbes cites for the origin of the Greek alphabet.

## Cicero · reference

Cited repeatedly as an authority-name people trust without understanding, and directly quoted for his remark that there is no absurdity philosophers have not maintained, and for the anecdote of Cassius's "cui bono" method of judging accusations.

## Livy · reference

The Roman historian, cited as an example of an author whose incredible claims (a cow made to speak by the gods) reflect on his own credibility as a witness, not on God's.

## Alexander the Great · reference

Cited as an example of a man whose glorious deeds people believe on trust from historians, and separately for cutting the Gordian knot with his sword rather than untying it.

## Francisco Suarez · reference

The Jesuit scholastic philosopher, quoted (via a mocking, untranslatable chapter title from his first book) as an example of School-Divinity's insignificant speech.

## Numa Pompilius · reference

The legendary second King of Rome, cited as an example of a lawgiver who claimed private revelation -- from the nymph Egeria -- to make his laws more easily received.

## Egeria · reference

The nymph from whom Numa Pompilius claimed to receive the ceremonies he instituted at Rome.

## Mahomet · reference

Cited as an example of a religious founder who pretended conferences with the Holy Ghost in the form of a dove, to make his new religion more easily received.

## Homer · reference

The poet whose hymn is cited for the story of Mercury inventing music and stealing Apollo's cattle in a single day; his verses are also cited as material used for a form of lottery-divination (dipping into Homer for an omen).

## Perseus · reference

Half of the mythological pair (with Andromeda) whose tragedy, performed on a very hot day at Abdera, was blamed for a mass outbreak of fever-induced delirium among the audience.

## Andromeda · reference

The mythological princess of the same Abdera tragedy-outbreak anecdote as Perseus.

## Catiline · reference

The failed Roman conspirator, cited as an example of the difference between one action of a whole body (the Senate condemning Catiline) and a multitude of separate actions by many individual senators (killing Caesar).

## Aristotle · reference

Cited constantly and at length -- as an authority-name trusted without understanding, as the source of the Schools' "vain philosophy" of Separated Essences and Metaphysiques, and by name for specific doctrines in his Politiques and Ethiques that Hobbes disputes at length in chapters 46-47.

## Plato · reference

The Athenian philosopher who taught in the public walks called Academia (giving his followers the name Academiques), and who required geometry of all who entered his school.

## Academus · reference

The figure from whom the Academia -- the public walk where Plato taught -- took its name.

## Carneades · reference

The Greek philosopher whose public lectures at Rome, as ambassador, alarmed Cato into urging the Senate to send him home quickly for fear of corrupting Roman youth.

## Cato · reference

The Roman senator who, alarmed at Carneades's popularity with Roman youth, urged the Senate to dispatch him quickly.

## Zeno · reference

Founder of the Stoic school, which took its name (Stoiques) from the Stoa, the covered walk where he taught.

## Socrates · reference

Cited by name once: Hobbes suggests Aristotle may have known his own philosophy was false but wrote it anyway, "fearing the fate of Socrates" -- his execution for impiety.

## Varro · reference

The Roman grammarian, named alongside Cicero as an author whose Latin the barbarous jargon of Schoole-Divinity would baffle.

## John Selden · reference

Cited by name ("Mr. Seldens most excellent Treatise") as the authority to consult for the detailed history of titles of honour such as Duke, Count, Marquis, and Baron.

## Sir Edward Coke · reference

Cited (as "Cokes Commentaries on Litleton") for a point of English law on attainder of treason and descent of the Crown, and later quoted directly on the presumption of guilt from flight in felony cases.

## Sir Thomas Littleton · reference

The medieval jurist whose "Tenures" is the subject of Coke's Commentaries, cited by Hobbes for a point on the descent of the Crown after attainder.

## Aristides · reference

Cited as an example ("an Aristides") of a man ostracized by the Athenians for his reputation for justice.

## Hyperbolus · reference

The Athenian demagogue cited as an example of a man ostracized as a mere "scurrilous Jester," to make sport of the practice of ostracism itself.

## Marius · reference

Roman general, named (with Sylla, Pompey, and Caesar) as one of the popular leaders whose successive civil wars extinguished the Roman democracy; also cited for the observation that what Marius made a crime, Sylla later made meritorious, and Caesar a crime again.

## Sylla · reference

Roman dictator (Sulla), named alongside Marius, Pompey, and Caesar in the same account of Rome's civil wars and reversing legal judgments.

## Pompey · reference

Named alongside Julius Caesar as one of the popular leaders whose rivalry ended the Roman democracy.

## Tiberius Gracchus · reference

Named with his brother Caius as one of the popular tribunes whose seditions, arising from an unbalanced distribution of power between Senate and People, began Rome's slide toward civil war.

## Caius Gracchus · reference

Named with his brother Tiberius in the same account of the seditions that began Rome's civil wars.

## Lucius Saturninus · reference

Named (as printed, "Lucius Saturnius") alongside the Gracchi as one of the tribunes whose seditions weakened the Roman Senate's power relative to the People.

## Solon · reference

Named as the Athenian lawgiver from whose laws ("Let no man, for private debt, seize or make servant of his debtor") Hobbes draws an example on debt-bondage.

## Tarquin · reference

The Roman king cited for the story of his rebellious son's insolent act, which he could not punish without protecting his son from ruinous consequences to himself.

## Ursinicus · reference

The antipope (Ursinus) whose disputed election against Damasus in 366 AD, as related by Ammianus Marcellinus, caused a violent sedition at Rome that Hobbes cites as evidence of what the papacy's temporal stakes could provoke.

## Pope Damasus I · reference

The Pope whose contested 366 AD election against the antipope Ursinicus, as told by Ammianus Marcellinus, is cited for the violence it caused. One further occurrence of this schism prints "Damascus" instead of "Damasus" -- a compositor error kept as printed and left unbound rather than conflated with the many genuine references to the city of Damascus elsewhere in the book.

## Ammianus Marcellinus · reference

The Roman historian cited as the source for the Damasus/Ursinicus schism and Juventius's role in it.

## Juventius · reference

The Roman prefect who, per Ammianus Marcellinus, was unable to keep the peace during the Damasus/Ursinicus schism.

## Constantine the Great · reference

The first Christian Roman Emperor, credited with causing Christian religion to be regulated under his own authority by the Bishop of Rome, and cited for the origin of the titles Duke, Count, and Marquis in his reign.

## Pope Sylvester I · reference

Named to make the point that the Church's head at Constantine's conversion was Constantine himself, not Sylvester.

## Julian the Apostate · reference

Named among the Roman Emperors -- Nero, Diocletian, Julian, Valens -- whom the early Christians never attempted to depose by force despite persecution.

## Diocletian · reference

Named in the same list of persecuting Emperors the early Christians did not attempt to depose.

## Nero · reference

Named repeatedly in the same list, and specifically as the Emperor "constituted over" the early Christians in Paul's own time, whom the Apostles never tried to depose despite lacking the temporal force to do so.

## Valens · reference

The Arian Christian Emperor named in the same list of persecuting rulers the early Church did not try to depose.

## Theodosius · reference

The Emperor whom St. Ambrose excommunicated -- an episode Hobbes cites (with reservation, "if it were true hee did so") among examples of clerical power over sovereigns.

## Justinian · reference

Cited for the seven sorts of Civil Laws found in his Institutions.

## Domitian · reference

The Emperor in whose reign Josephus is said to have written his reckoning of the Hebrew scriptural canon.

## Charlemagne · reference

Named for the translation of the Roman Empire to him, and separately as the point after which Church Canons (previously voluntary) began to be treated as binding law by Christian princes.

## William the Conqueror · reference

The Norman king whose conquest is cited three times: as the origin of English land tenures, for his oath to the clergy at his coronation, and to make the point that a king's authority cannot depend on the justice of the conquest that first won his line the crown.

## William Rufus · reference

William the Conqueror's son and successor, cited for buying the Barons' support, against the Church's liberties, in transferring the succession from his elder brother.

## Henry II of England · reference

The king against whom Thomas Becket, as Archbishop of Canterbury, was supported by the Pope -- cited as an example of subjects using clerical power to weaken a neighbouring sovereign.

## Thomas Becket · reference

Archbishop of Canterbury, cited by full name (printed "Thomas Beckett") as supported by the Pope against Henry II -- an example distinct from the book's other, unbound and merely rhetorical "a Thomas" and from the separately bound Apostle "S. Thomas."

## St. Thomas · reference

The Apostle, said -- doubtfully, per Hobbes ("though the Church have not received" it) -- to have written of the Trinity alongside Barnabas; distinct from the book's other, unbound and merely rhetorical "a Thomas" and from Thomas Becket, bound separately above.

## Emperor Frederick · reference

Frederick I Barbarossa, whom Hobbes declines to blame for holding the stirrup for Pope Adrian at his reception, given the political necessity of the moment.

## Pope Adrian IV · reference

"Our countryman" -- the only English Pope -- for whom Frederick Barbarossa held the stirrup at his imperial reception.

## Henry VIII · reference

Named, with Elizabeth, for casting out the Pope's spiritual power in England "by his Exorcisme" -- Hobbes's own ironic figure for the Reformation.

## Queen Elizabeth I · reference

Named for totally dissolving the Pope's power in England, after which the Bishops exercised their functions in the Queen's right rather than the Pope's.

## Chilperic · reference

The King of France cited (via the printed "Chilperique") as deposed by Pope Zachary for no cause but the Pope's own authority -- an example of Papal usurpation over civil sovereigns.

## Cardinal Bellarmine · reference

The Catholic controversialist whose arguments for the Pope's temporal power and for Purgatory Hobbes answers at great length across chapters 42-45 and 48, engaging his specific citations point by point.

## Theodore Beza · reference

The Reformed theologian who, alone among the authorities Hobbes canvasses, argues Christ's Kingdom began at the Resurrection -- a claim Hobbes spends a full chapter refuting.

## St. Ambrose · reference

Cited for excommunicating the Emperor Theodosius, an episode Hobbes treats with some scepticism as to whether it truly happened.

## St. Jerome · reference

Cited repeatedly in the survey of the Old Testament canon (ch. 34) for his reckoning of the number of scriptural books, and for testifying that Job's original Hebrew was written in prose rather than verse.

## St. Cyprian · reference

Cited for calling the See of St. Peter "the Head, the Source, the Roote, the Sun" of the Church -- part of Hobbes's survey of the textual basis claimed for Papal supremacy.

## St. Augustine · reference

Cited as the source of the rhetorical application of Psalm 37 ("rebuke me not in thy wrath") to the doctrine of Purgatory -- an example, per Hobbes, of a text stretched to a use its own words do not support.

## St. Bernard · reference

Named alongside Pope Gregory as having "somewhat of Apparitions of Ghosts" reportedly seen in Purgatory -- cited, with reservation, among the sincere but overly credulous Fathers whose reported testimony Hobbes treats as report-of-report rather than eyewitness fact.

## Pope Clement I · reference

Credited with having collected the Apostolical Canons, in Hobbes's survey of who assembled the New Testament canon.

## Pope Innocent III · reference

Named by full title, "Innocent the third," for the Fourth Lateran Council's decree that subjects are absolved of obedience to a king who fails to purge heresy, and for establishing the doctrine of Transubstantiation "not above 500. years agoe." The book's many other, unrelated uses of the common adjective "innocent" are left unbound -- see the namesake note above.

## Pope Leo III · reference

Named by explicit numeral, "Leo 3," in a list of Popes -- Gregory 1, Greg. 2, Zachary, and Leo 3 -- whose judgments in their own cause Hobbes holds void.

## Pope Zachary · reference

The eighth-century Pope who deposed Chilperic, King of France, for no cause but his own authority; also appears bare in the same numbered list of Popes as Leo III.

## Pope Gregory I · reference

Named by explicit numeral, "Gregory 1," in the same list of Popes whose self-interested judgments Hobbes holds void. A separate, unnumbered "Gregory the Pope" (ch. 47, credited with ghost-story apparitions of souls in Purgatory) is left unbound rather than assumed to be this same Pope -- see the namesake note above.

## Pope Gregory II · reference

Named by explicit numeral, "Greg. 2," in the same list of self-judging Popes.

## Jupiter · reference

Cited repeatedly as chief of the Heathen gods -- for his adulteries celebrated rather than dishonoured in the Poets, for deposing his father Saturn, and as the god invoked in the Heathen oath-form Hobbes contrasts with the Christian one.

## Mercury · reference

Cited for his frauds and thefts celebrated by the Poets (per Homer's hymn), and as the god to whom Craft and Subtilty were ascribed.

## Venus · reference

Cited as the deity to whom the Gentiles ascribed the cause of Foecundity.

## Apollo · reference

Cited (also as Phoebus and Appollo) as the god of Arts, whose cattle Mercury stole per Homer's hymn, and whose oracle at Delphi is named among the famous ambiguous Oracles.

## Aeolus · reference

Cited as the deity to whom the Gentiles ascribed Tempests and storms.

## Bacchus · reference

Named among the "mongrill Gods" the Gentiles believed born of a god and a mortal.

## Hercules · reference

Named among the deified mortals of Gentile religion, and separately cited for his fight with the many-headed Hydra as an image of a contentious multitude that only grows for every grievance answered.

## Saturn · reference

Cited for the Heathen belief that he was deposed by his own son Jupiter -- an example, per Hobbes, of successful wickedness gaining the name of virtue.

## Pan · reference

The god from whose fables "Panique Terrour" -- fear without any apprehended cause -- takes its name; also named among the spirits believed to haunt the plains.

## Neptune · reference

Cited as an example of a crude, unhewn stone set up to represent a god the worshippers' own fancy pictured quite differently.

## Ceres · reference

Named among the gods to whose operation the Greeks sometimes attributed madness.

## Chaos · reference

The unformed matter of the world, worshipped by the Gentiles under this name as their oldest god.

## Charon · reference

Named among Hell's "spirituall Officers" of Gentile religion -- ferryman of the dead.

## Cerberus · reference

The three-headed hound of Gentile Hell, named alongside Charon and the Furies as one of Hell's spiritual officers.

## The Hydra · reference

The many-headed monster whose fight with Hercules Hobbes uses as an image of a grievance-driven multitude that grows a new head for every one struck off.

## Moloch · reference

Named among the deities to whom the Gentiles gave human and animal shapes and passions alike.

## Beelzebub · reference

"The Prince of Daemons" per Scripture -- Hobbes's own name for the single universal king he compares to the Fairies' legendary King Oberon, and cited earlier as "the Prince of the Power of the Air" ruling the Kingdome of Darknesse.

## King Oberon · reference

The Fairies' legendary universal king in English folklore, to whom Hobbes compares Beelzebub as Prince of Daemons in his extended satire equating the Papacy's hierarchy to the Kingdome of Fairies.

## The Sadducees · reference

The Jewish sect Hobbes twice singles out as the one group who, denying the existence of spirits altogether, came nearest to direct atheism among the ancient Jews.

## The Thirty Tyrants · reference

The Spartan-installed oligarchy at Athens, cited as an example of how the name "Tyranny" came to mean not just monarchy but monarchy hated by the people who lived under it.

## The Septuagint · reference

The seventy Jewish scholars Hobbes credits with translating the Hebrew scriptures into Greek at the order of Ptolemy Philadelphus.

## Ptolemy Philadelphus · reference

The Egyptian king who, per Hobbes, caused the Hebrew scriptures to be translated into Greek by the Septuagint's seventy scholars.

## Josephus · reference

The Jewish historian cited for his own reckoning of the number of canonical scriptural books, and (with Philo) as an example of a learned Jew who wrote eloquently in Greek.

## Philo · reference

The Jewish philosopher named alongside Josephus as an example of a Hebrew who wrote eloquently in Greek.

## Adam · reference

The first man, taught directly by God to name the creatures; cited at length on the conditional immortality he forfeited by sin, and as the ancestor whose fallen estate the Reprobate are argued to remain in after the Resurrection.

## Eve · reference

Named alongside Adam as the first humans, and cited for the temptation and fall recounted in Genesis.

## Cain · reference

Named as Adam's son in the genealogical material Hobbes surveys when discussing the age and authorship of Genesis.

## Enoch · reference

Cited directly, quoting Genesis 5:24, "Enoch walked with God, and he was not; for God took him" -- the clearest scriptural case Hobbes allows for a bodily translation without death, restricted (he argues) to those who please God rather than a universal natural immortality.

## Enos · reference

Named (ch. 39) as one of two scriptural exceptions to universal death, "except Enos and Elias" -- almost certainly Hobbes's own slip for Enoch, the figure ch. 45 elsewhere quotes correctly by name for exactly this claim (Gen. 5:24). Bound under the printed name per the book's own compositor-error convention rather than silently merged with Enoch.

## Noah · reference

Cited for God's post-Flood promise never again to "smite every Living Creature," and for the length of his household's stay in the Ark, in the survey of Genesis's chronology.

## Abraham · reference

Named repeatedly as a founder of Divine Politiques and receiver of God's covenant, and cited for God speaking to him "by a Voyce, that is, by Vision" the same way he spoke to Samuel.

## Isaac · reference

Named with Abraham and Jacob as living "by promise" of the Resurrection in Christ's own argument against the Sadducees, per Hobbes's reading.

## Jacob · reference

Named with Abraham and Isaac in the same argument on the Resurrection, and cited for blessing Ephraim and Manasseh, the sons of Joseph, by the laying on of hands.

## Esau · reference

Named as Isaac's son in the genealogical survey of Genesis's authorship and chronology.

## Joseph · reference

The Genesis patriarch sold into Egypt, who interpreted Pharaoh's dreams and was blessed, through his sons Ephraim and Manasseh, by his father Jacob. Bare "Joseph" is bound here as the dominant sense; see the namesake note above for two minority occurrences (Joseph, husband of Mary) that the same bare string cannot be distinguished from.

## Joseph the Just · reference

One of the two disciples put forward, with Matthias, to fill Judas's vacated place among the Twelve by lot (Acts 1:23) -- not chosen, and named by his own distinguishing epithet, distinct from the patriarch Joseph and from Joseph the husband of Mary.

## Hagar · reference

Named for receiving an angelic apparition, in Hobbes's survey of how God's messages came to different figures in Genesis.

## Lot · reference

Named for receiving a dream apparition from angels, in the same survey of how divine messages came to Genesis's figures.

## Abimelech · reference

The king to whom God spoke in a dream (Genesis 20) warning him against Sarah, cited in the same survey of scriptural dream-revelations.

## Moses · reference

The founder of Divine Politiques and lieutenant of God over Israel, cited constantly -- for personating God without Scripture ever specifying how God spoke to him supernaturally, for consecrating the Tabernacle and Aaron without any Exorcism, and as the source of the Kingdom of God that "ended in the election of Saul."

## Aaron · reference

Moses's brother and the first High Priest, consecrated by washing and anointing rather than any charm; also named for the making of the Golden Calf, done, per Hobbes, without divine authority and therefore Idolatry.

## Miriam · reference

Named in the genealogical survey of Exodus's early chapters.

## Jethro · reference

Moses's father-in-law, named in the survey of the events and authorship of Exodus.

## Joshua · reference

Moses's successor, quoted directly (Deut. 34:9) as "full of the Spirit of Wisdome" from Moses's laying on of hands, and named as the presumed author of the Book of Joshua in Hobbes's survey of scriptural authorship.

## Achan · reference

Named in the survey of Joshua's narrative for his transgression and its consequences.

## Othniel · reference

Named (as printed, "Othoniel") as the first Judge in the survey of the Book of Judges's chronology.

## Gideon · reference

Named among the Judges to whom God spoke "alwaies by a Vision, or by a Dream," per Hobbes's catalogue of Old Testament revelation.

## Jephthah · reference

Named (as printed, "Jeptha") among the Judges, in the survey of the Book of Judges.

## Samson · reference

Named among the Judges in the same scriptural survey.

## Korah · reference

Named (as printed, "Corah") with Dathan and Abiram as leaders of the rebellion against Moses's authority in the wilderness, swallowed alive by the earth.

## Dathan · reference

Named with Corah and Abiram as a leader of the same rebellion against Moses.

## Abiram · reference

Named with Corah and Dathan as a leader of the same rebellion; one occurrence is printed "Abirom."

## Samuel · reference

The last Judge and first of the great prophets after Moses, cited for anointing Saul, for his sons' corrupt judging that led Israel to demand a king, and for the disputed apparition raised at Endor.

## King Saul · reference

The first King of Israel, whose election Hobbes repeatedly cites as the moment the direct Kingdom of God "ended" -- discussed at length for his pursuit of David, his prophesying under the Spirit of God, and his death at the Witch of Endor's séance. Bare "Saul" binds here as the dominant sense; see the namesake note above for the minority "Saul of Tarsus" occurrences the same bare string does not distinguish.

## Jonathan · reference

Saul's son, named for the fault revealed against him by lot, and for David's fasting at news of his and Saul's deaths.

## King David · reference

Named throughout for his conduct toward Saul, his sin against Uriah, his fasting for Saul and Jonathan and for Abner, and his appointment of Temple officers -- cited as the model of a sovereign whose personal grief at his own child's death did not extend to those whose deaths cost him nothing.

## Abner · reference

Saul's general, named for David's mourning at his death (2 Sam. 3), cited alongside Saul as an example of honorific rather than self-interested fasting.

## Nathan · reference

The prophet under David, named among the recognized authors of prophetic writings referenced but not preserved as separate scriptural books.

## Uriah · reference

David's officer, killed by David's own contrivance -- cited as an act unjust to God, per Hobbes, though not, strictly, an injury to Uriah, who as a soldier owed his sovereign unlimited obedience.

## Solomon · reference

Named for his wisdom, his dedication of the Temple without any exorcism or charm, his deposing of the High Priest Abiathar in his own right, and as the presumed author of Ecclesiastes, Proverbs, and the Song of Songs.

## Abiathar · reference

The High Priest whom Solomon deposed and replaced by his own civil right, cited as proof the Priesthood was subordinate to the Crown even before Saul's reign fully ended sacerdotal rule.

## Zadok · reference

Named among David's officers in the survey of 1 Chronicles.

## Rehoboam · reference

Solomon's son, named for the loss of ten tribes to Jeroboam, and for the plundering of the Temple by Shishak, King of Egypt.

## Jeroboam · reference

The idolatrous king to whom ten of Israel's tribes seceded under Rehoboam; named as the object of an unfulfilled prophecy against his altar.

## Ahab · reference

The king whose war for Ramoth Gilead is the setting for the story of the true prophet among four hundred false ones.

## Micaiah · reference

The lone true prophet among four hundred false ones consulted by Ahab before the war for Ramoth Gilead, struck by the false prophet Zedekiah for his prophecy. Both Hobbes spellings, "Micaiah" and "Michaiah," name this same court prophet in three of the book's four occurrences; the fourth, a different person entirely, is left unbound -- see the namesake note above.

## Zedekiah (false prophet) · reference

The false prophet who struck Micaiah and claimed the Spirit of the Lord had left Micaiah for himself, in the Ramoth Gilead narrative.

## Athaliah · reference

The queen deposed by the High Priest acting in the right of the young king Joash, not in his own right -- an example Hobbes uses against claims of independent priestly power to depose monarchs.

## Joash · reference

The young king in whose right, not the High Priest's own, Athaliah was deposed.

## King Jehu · reference

Anointed by a young prophet whom Jehu's own soldiers dismissed as "that mad-man" -- cited as evidence the Jews of the time used "mad-man" and "Prophet" almost interchangeably for unusual, spirit-driven behaviour.

## The Prophet Jehu · reference

A different, later prophet of the same name, who reproved King Jehosaphat for aiding the King of Israel against the Syrians (2 Chron. 19:2) -- bound separately from King Jehu via his own "the Prophet Jehu" epithet.

## Jehoshaphat · reference

The king reproved by the Prophet Jehu for aiding the King of Israel against the Syrians.

## Uzzah · reference

Struck dead for touching the Ark to steady it, per 2 Sam. 6 -- cited in the survey of 2 Samuel's authorship and events.

## Naaman · reference

The Syrian officer healed of leprosy and converted "in his heart" by Elisha, cited as scriptural precedent for the liberty of outward conformity to an idolatrous sovereign's ceremony while inwardly rejecting it.

## Elisha · reference

The prophet who permitted Naaman's outward conformity in the house of Rimmon; also named among the prophets to whom God spoke "by a Vision, or by a Dream."

## Elijah · reference

Named (also as Elias and Eliah) among the two scriptural exceptions to universal death, and among the prophets who received visions; appears with Moses at the Transfiguration, which Hobbes reads as itself a Vision.

## Hezekiah · reference

The king who broke the Brazen Serpent Moses had set up, because the people had begun to burn incense to it -- cited as the scriptural precedent for a Christian sovereign's duty to break down images his subjects have taken to worshipping.

## Manasseh · reference

Named in the survey of the Books of Kings' chronology and authorship.

## Josiah · reference

The good king to whom Pharaoh Necho's warning words are cited as an example of true prophecy from an idolater's mouth; named (also as Josias) in the survey of 2 Kings.

## Hilkiah · reference

The High Priest who found the Book of the Law in Josiah's reign, named in the survey of 2 Kings's events.

## Huldah · reference

The prophetess (as printed, "Hulda") consulted after Hilkiah's discovery of the Book of the Law.

## Pharaoh Necho · reference

The Egyptian Pharaoh whose warning to King Josiah, though he was "an Idolator," Hobbes cites as scriptural evidence that true prophecy is not restricted to a holy man's own mouth.

## Isaiah · reference

Named (also as Esay) among the prophets to whom God spoke by vision, and as author of the Book of Isaiah in Hobbes's survey of the prophetic canon.

## Jeremiah · reference

Named as a major prophet of the Captivity era in the survey of the prophetic books' authorship and chronology.

## Ezekiel · reference

Named (also as printed "Exekiel") as a prophet of the Captivity, alongside Daniel, Haggai, and Zechariah.

## Daniel · reference

Named as a prophet of the Captivity era, in the same survey as Ezekiel, Haggai, and Zechariah.

## Hosea · reference

Named among the most ancient prophets, alongside Zephaniah, Jonah, Amos, Isaiah, and Micah.

## Amos · reference

Named among the most ancient prophets in the same survey as Hosea and Isaiah.

## Joel · reference

Named among the minor prophets in Hobbes's survey of the prophetic canon.

## Obadiah · reference

Named (as printed, "Abdias") among the minor prophets.

## Jonah · reference

Named (as printed, "Jonas") among the most ancient prophets alongside Zephaniah, Amos, and Hosea.

## Micah · reference

The writing prophet, cited directly (Micah 4:7, "the Lord shall reign over them in Mount Zion") and, only in the modern edition, named among the most ancient prophets in the same survey as Zephaniah, Jonah, Amos, and Hosea -- the original edition spells that survey mention identically to the unrelated court prophet Micaiah ("Michaiah"), so only the unambiguous scripture citation binds there; see the namesake note above.

## Nahum · reference

Named among the minor prophets in the survey of the prophetic canon.

## Habakkuk · reference

Named among the minor prophets in the same survey.

## Zephaniah · reference

Named (as printed, "Sophoniah") as the most ancient of the prophets surveyed in chapter 34.

## Haggai · reference

Named (as printed, "Aggeus") as a prophet of the Captivity alongside Ezekiel, Daniel, and Zechariah.

## Zechariah · reference

The Old Testament prophet, quoted twice as "the Prophet Zachary" (Zech. 13:8-9, on the coming Messiah's refining fire) and named as "Zacharias" among the prophets of the Captivity alongside Ezekiel, Daniel, and Haggai -- distinct from the eighth-century Pope Zachary named elsewhere in the book; see the namesake note above.

## Malachi · reference

Named among the prophets in Hobbes's survey of the Old Testament's prophetic books.

## Ezra · reference

Credited by Hobbes as the probable compiler of the Pentateuch and much of the historical Old Testament after the Captivity, under the name Esdras.

## Nehemiah · reference

Named in the survey of the post-Captivity historical books' authorship.

## Esther · reference

Named as the subject of her own book in the survey of Old Testament authorship.

## Mordecai · reference

Honoured by the King of Persia -- paraded through the streets in royal robes on a royal horse -- cited as Hobbes's example of Civil Honour conferred entirely at a sovereign's discretion.

## Job · reference

Named throughout as the speaker of the Book of Job, cited at length (ch. 32, 38, 45) for his complaints against God's prosperity of the wicked and for the doctrine of the soul Hobbes draws from Job 14.

## Agur · reference

Named ("Agur the son of Jakeh") as the reputed author of part of Proverbs, in Hobbes's survey of that book's composite authorship.

## King Lemuel · reference

Named for the sayings collected "of the Mother of King Lemuel" in the same survey of Proverbs's authorship.

## The Prophet Ahijah · reference

Named among the otherwise-unpreserved prophetic writings cited in the Books of Chronicles as sources for lost histories of the Kings.

## Jehdo · reference

Named for "the Vision of Jehdo," one of the lost prophetic source-texts cited by the Chronicler.

## The Prophet Serveiah · reference

Named for the lost "Books of the Prophet Serveiah," cited alongside Jehdo and Addo as sources the Chronicler names but does not preserve.

## The Prophet Addo · reference

Named for the lost "Books of the Prophet Addo," the last of the three otherwise-unknown source-prophets cited by the Chronicler.

## Hashabiah · reference

Named (1 Chron. 26:30) as one of the Hebronite officers David appointed over Israel, in Hobbes's survey of the priesthood's civil subordination.

## Balaam · reference

Named (as printed, "Baalam") as an example of a false prophet whose divination God nonetheless is said to have foretold through, per Numbers.

## Jesus Christ · reference

The book's central religious figure throughout Parts III-IV: the sole article of faith necessary for salvation ("Jesus Is The Christ"), whose Kingdom Hobbes argues has not yet come, and whose Transfiguration, temptation, and miracles are read closely for whether Scripture requires literal possession by spirits.

## The Virgin Mary · reference

Named for receiving the angelic annunciation, and later for the images of her venerated (per Hobbes, mistakenly) as Divine Worship rather than a civil honouring of the person she was.

## John the Baptist · reference

Named for his singular fasting, which the Jews of his time took as a sign he "had a Devill" -- an example, per Hobbes, of how loosely that accusation was actually meant.

## St. Peter · reference

The Apostle Simon, "surnamed Stone" -- Cephas in Syriac, Petrus in Greek -- whose confession that Jesus is the Christ founds Hobbes's central doctrine; also cited for the parable of his overfull net of fish as an image of Church-power outgrowing its restraint.

## St. Paul · reference

Named constantly across the doctrinal chapters -- for his epistles' teaching on the Resurrection, on marriage and the clergy, on spiritual bodies, and (as Saul of Tarsus before his renaming) for persecuting the Church and later being commissioned at Antioch with Barnabas.

## St. John (the Evangelist) · reference

Named as a Gospel author and cited for the report, based on a misunderstood saying of Christ's, that he would not die before the Second Coming.

## St. Matthew · reference

Named as a Gospel author, cited directly for his account of Christ's temptation in the wilderness and the Transfiguration.

## St. Mark · reference

Named as a Gospel author, cited for his account of the Transfiguration and of the signs promised to true believers.

## St. Luke · reference

Named as a Gospel and Acts author, cited for his account of the Transfiguration, of Christ's temptation, and of events in Acts including Paul's missionary journeys.

## St. James · reference

Named among the Apostles present at the Transfiguration.

## Judas Iscariot · reference

Named for Satan's "entring into him" before he betrayed Christ -- read by Hobbes as a figure of speech for his own treacherous intention rather than literal possession.

## Matthias · reference

Chosen by lot over Joseph the Just to replace Judas among the Twelve (Acts 1:23-26).

## Barnabas · reference

Named among the Antioch prophets and teachers set apart with Saul for missionary work, and (rhetorically, in the satire on repurposed pagan images) as an example of a Jupiter-image renamed for a saint.

## Simeon Niger · reference

One of the prophets and teachers at Antioch alongside Barnabas, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen, and Saul (Acts 13:1).

## Lucius of Cyrene · reference

Named among the same Antioch prophets and teachers.

## Manaen · reference

Named among the same Antioch prophets and teachers, identified as having been brought up with Herod the Tetrarch.

## Philip the Deacon · reference

One of the seven appointed to serve tables in Acts 6, who preached and baptized in Samaria and baptized the Ethiopian eunuch -- explicitly distinguished by the text itself from Philip the Apostle, who remained at Jerusalem throughout.

## Philip the Apostle · reference

One of the Twelve, explicitly distinguished in the text from Philip the Deacon, who alone (not this Philip) preached in Samaria and baptized the eunuch.

## St. Stephen · reference

The first Christian martyr (printed "St. Steven"), stoned after pleading his cause before the High Priest -- cited by Hobbes as proof his death followed formal, publicly authorized condemnation rather than private zealotry.

## Ananias · reference

The disciple struck down for lying about the price of his land (Acts 5) -- Peter's rebuke, "whilest the Land remained, was it not thine?", is cited by Hobbes on the limits of communal property obligations.

## Ananias of Damascus · reference

The disciple who restored Paul's sight and laid hands on him at his baptism (Acts 9) -- a different Ananias from the one struck down in Acts 5, distinguished in the text by his own epithet, "Ananias at Damascus."

## Timothy · reference

Paul's disciple and correspondent, named as an Elder (also called a Bishop in Hobbes's reading), cited for the instructions Paul gives him on laying on of hands and other pastoral advice.

## Titus · reference

Paul's disciple, named alongside Timothy as a Pastor to whom apostolic letters of advice, not new articles of faith, were addressed.

## Apollos · reference

Named with Paul and Cephas as one of the factional leaders the Corinthian church divided behind (1 Cor. 1:12), cited again as one of the names the post-Episcopal Independency of England could follow "every man as he liketh best."

## Crispus · reference

One of the only three people Paul personally baptized at Corinth (1 Cor. 1:14,16), cited as evidence baptizing was properly a Deacon's office, not the Apostle's own.

## Gaius · reference

Named (as printed, "Cajus") alongside Crispus and Stephanus as one of the only three people Paul personally baptized at Corinth.

## Stephanus · reference

Named alongside Crispus and Gaius as one of the only three people Paul personally baptized at Corinth.

## Jason · reference

The disciple at Thessalonica dragged before the city's rulers for harbouring Paul and Silas (Acts 17:6), cited for the charge brought against them, "that there is another King, one Jesus."

## Martha · reference

Named for her confession at Lazarus's tomb, "I beleeve that thou art the Christ" (John 11:26-27), cited as an example of the single article of faith Hobbes argues is sufficient for salvation.

## Mary Magdalene · reference

Named among the women who ministered to Christ, "out of whom went seven Devills" (Luke 8:2-3).

## Joanna · reference

Named among the same ministering women, "the wife of Chuza," Herod's steward.

## Chuza · reference

Herod's steward, named as Joanna's husband in the same list of women who ministered to Christ.

## Susanna · reference

Named among the same women who ministered to Christ (Luke 8:3), alongside Mary Magdalene and Joanna.

## Zacchaeus · reference

Named in Hobbes's survey of Luke's Gospel narrative.

## Jairus · reference

Named as the ruler whose daughter's raising is recounted in the Gospels, in Hobbes's survey of Christ's miracles.

## Pontius Pilate · reference

Named for delivering Christ to be crucified "to gratifie the Jews" despite finding no fault in him -- cited by Hobbes as not contrary to the laws of Caesar, since Pilate acted from Roman authority, not against it.

## Herod the Great · reference

The king who, warned by the Magi's visit, sought to kill the infant Christ -- distinct from Herod the Tetrarch, whom the text names separately with his own epithet; see the namesake note above.

## Herod Antipas (the Tetrarch) · reference

Named by his own epithet, "Herod the Tetrarch," as the ruler with whom the Antioch teacher Manaen had been "brought up" -- distinct from Herod the Great, named separately above.
