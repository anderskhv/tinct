# Poetics: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Homer · reference

The ancient Greek poet traditionally credited with the Iliad and Odyssey.

## Empedocles · reference

The Greek philosopher who wrote about nature in verse.

## Sophron · reference

The writer of mimes whom Aristotle pairs with Xenarchus.

## Xenarchus · reference

The writer of mimes whom Aristotle pairs with Sophron.

## Chaeremon · reference

The poet whose Centaur combines different metres.

## Polygnotus · reference

The Greek painter Aristotle cites for noble portrayals of people.

## Pauson · reference

The painter Aristotle contrasts with Polygnotus.

## Dionysius · reference

The painter cited for portraying people as they are.

## Cleophon · reference

The poet cited for ordinary subjects and plain diction.

## Hegemon · reference

The Thasian poet whom Aristotle credits with inventing parodies.

The poet named as the author of the Deiliad.

## Philoxenus · reference

The poet and musician whose treatment of the Cyclops is compared with Timotheus’s.

## Magnes · reference

An early comic poet mentioned alongside Chionides.

The Athenian tragic playwright, credited here with increasing the number of actors.

The Athenian comic playwright credited with moving beyond personal lampoon.

## Euripides · reference

The Athenian tragic playwright, author of Medea and other plays discussed here.

The playwright named as the author of the Cyprians.

The Greek sophist whose criticism of Homer’s opening invocation is discussed.

The poet whose diction is cited alongside Cleophon’s.

The critic who ridiculed the unusual phrasing of tragic poets.

The Greek poet and thinker associated with criticism of stories about the gods.

The interpreter cited for resolving passages through accent or breathing.

The critic cited on readers who blame poets on the basis of their own assumptions.

The actor criticized for exaggerated movement.

The performer from Opus cited for excessive gesture in lyrical competition.

The foolish hero whose name titles the comic poem attributed here to Homer.

The Greek hero whose many exploits illustrate Aristotle’s point about unity of action.

The Greek hero of the Odyssey, husband of Penelope and father of Telemachus.

The legendary king of Thebes, central to the tragedy Aristotle repeatedly discusses.

Agamemnon’s daughter and Orestes’s sister, the subject of several plays discussed here.

The wife of Agamemnon and mother of Orestes.

The mother of Alcmaeon in the tragic family story.
