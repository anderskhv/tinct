# The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Sherlock Holmes · central

A private consulting detective with rooms in Baker Street, London. Clients bring him problems the police cannot solve or will not touch; his friend Dr. Watson narrates the cases.

## Dr. Watson · major

A doctor and Holmes’s friend, who narrates these cases. He shared rooms with Holmes at Baker Street before his marriage; some cases date from those bachelor days, others from after he returned to medical practice.

## Mrs. Hudson · supporting

The woman who keeps house at Holmes’s Baker Street rooms and sees to his meals. Holmes jokes that he should have her check his dinner’s woodcock for jewels.

After source chapter 8, paragraph 8: The woman who keeps house at Holmes’s Baker Street rooms. She answers the door to early callers and has lit the fire for Holmes’s new client.

## Irene Adler · major

A retired American-born opera singer living in London. Watson says she is the one woman Holmes always calls ‘the woman’, though not out of love.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 62: An American-born contralto, born in New Jersey in 1858, once prima donna of the Imperial Opera of Warsaw and now retired and living in London. The King of Bohemia, who calls her a well-known adventuress, met her in Warsaw some five years ago, became entangled with her, and wants back the compromising letters he wrote her.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 142: An American-born contralto, formerly prima donna of the Imperial Opera of Warsaw, now living at Briony Lodge, St. John’s Wood. She holds a compromising photograph of herself with the King of Bohemia. She has just married the lawyer Godfrey Norton in a hurried ceremony at the Church of St. Monica, with Holmes, in his groom’s disguise, pulled in as witness.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 247: An American-born opera singer who outwits Holmes. She saw through his clergyman act after the fire alarm, followed him home in men’s clothes to wish him good night, and left England with her new husband, Godfrey Norton. In her letter, signed ‘Irene Norton, née Adler’, she promises not to trouble the King, keeping the photograph only for her own protection.

## King of Bohemia · major

The hereditary King of Bohemia, of the House of Ormstein. He is the masked visitor who called himself Count Von Kramm, and he has just torn off his mask and admitted who he is.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 103: Wilhelm Gottsreich Sigismond von Ormstein, the thirty-year-old hereditary King of Bohemia, in London incognito. He is about to announce his betrothal to Clotilde Lothman von Saxe-Meningen and wants Holmes to recover a compromising photograph of himself with Irene Adler, which she has threatened to send to his bride’s family.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 258: Wilhelm Gottsreich Sigismond von Ormstein, hereditary King of Bohemia. Irene Adler’s letter convinces him she will never use the photograph, and he declares himself satisfied. He gives Holmes her portrait as a reward; Holmes takes it, bows, and turns away without taking the King’s outstretched hand.

## Count Von Kramm · supporting

The name given to Holmes by a masked, very tall visitor with a strong German accent, who says he is a Bohemian nobleman.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 56: A false name. The masked visitor who called himself Count Von Kramm, a Bohemian nobleman, is the King of Bohemia himself. He tears off his mask after Holmes addresses him as ‘your Majesty’.

## Slim youth in an ulster · supporting

A slim young man in an ulster who hurries past Holmes’s door in Baker Street at night and wishes him good night by name. Holmes is sure he has heard the voice before.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 244: Irene Adler in men’s clothes. After the fire alarm she suspected the clergyman, put on what she calls her ‘walking clothes’, followed Holmes home to make sure who he was, and then wished him good night.

## Godfrey Norton · supporting

A dark, handsome lawyer of the Inner Temple and Irene Adler’s only regular male visitor, who calls at Briony Lodge once or twice a day.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 142: A dark, handsome lawyer of the Inner Temple who has just married Irene Adler in a hurried ceremony at the Church of St. Monica, with the disguised Holmes dragged in as their witness.

After source chapter 1, paragraph 238: A lawyer of the Inner Temple and Irene Adler’s new husband. The morning after the wedding the couple leave England for the Continent on the 5:15 train from Charing Cross.

## Clotilde Lothman von Saxe-Meningen · supporting

The second daughter of the King of Scandinavia, from a family of strict principles, whom the King of Bohemia is about to marry.

## John · supporting

The coachman who drives Irene Adler’s landau.

## Mrs. Turner · supporting

The landlady at the Baker Street lodgings, who brings Holmes a tray of supper.

## Elderly servant at Briony Lodge · supporting

An elderly woman on the steps of Briony Lodge who watches Holmes’s party with a sardonic eye and greets him by name.

## Clergyman at St. Monica’s · supporting

The clergyman in a surplice at the Church of St. Monica in the Edgeware Road, seen arguing with a couple at the altar.

## Mary Jane · reference

The clumsy, careless servant girl in Watson’s household, whom his wife has given notice.

## John Hare · reference

An English actor of Watson’s day, named as the only performer who could have equalled Holmes in a part.

## Jabez Wilson · major

A stout, elderly pawnbroker with blazing red hair who runs a small business in Saxe-Coburg Square, near the City. He has come to Holmes with a strange story about a newspaper advertisement.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 93: A stout, elderly red-headed pawnbroker from Saxe-Coburg Square. Hired by the Red-Headed League to copy the Encyclopædia Britannica for £4 a week, he worked eight weeks until he found the office locked and a card announcing that the League was dissolved.

## Vincent Spaulding · major

Jabez Wilson’s assistant at the pawnshop. Wilson says he is very capable and could earn twice as much elsewhere, but works for half wages to learn the business.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 57: Jabez Wilson’s assistant, who works for half wages to learn the business. He showed Wilson the Red-Headed League’s advertisement, urged him to apply, and pushed him through the crowd of applicants. Wilson’s only complaint is his passion for photography, which keeps him in the cellar developing pictures.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 141: Jabez Wilson’s assistant: a small, quick, beardless man of about thirty with an acid stain on his forehead and pierced ears. He showed Wilson the League’s advertisement and pushed him to apply. After a brief look at him, Holmes calls him the fourth smartest man in London and says he has known something of him before.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 209: The name John Clay used while working as Jabez Wilson’s assistant. Holmes explains that he took the job at half wages, urged Wilson to apply to the sham League, and used the hours Wilson was away to dig a tunnel from the cellar to the bank behind the shop.

## John Clay · major

A young master criminal—murderer, thief, passer of counterfeit coin and forger—whom Peter Jones of Scotland Yard names as the man he most wants to arrest. Jones says Clay has royal ancestry, went to Eton and Oxford, and has never been seen by him despite years of pursuit.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 195: A notorious young criminal whom Peter Jones of Scotland Yard has hunted for years. Holmes names him as the clean-cut, boyish young man who climbs up through the floor of the bank cellar.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 203: A notorious young criminal whom Peter Jones of Scotland Yard has hunted for years. Caught climbing up through the floor of the bank cellar, he is handcuffed by Jones, insists on his royal blood, and is led off coolly to the police station.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 209: A notorious young criminal caught tunnelling into the bank cellar and arrested by Peter Jones. Holmes explains that Clay, under the name Vincent Spaulding, had worked as Jabez Wilson’s assistant and devised the Red-Headed League with a red-haired accomplice to get Wilson out of the shop each day.

## Duncan Ross · major

The man named in the Red-Headed League’s newspaper advertisement as the person to apply to at the League’s offices, 7 Pope’s Court, Fleet Street.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 63: A small man with hair even redder than Jabez Wilson’s, who interviews the applicants at 7 Pope’s Court, chooses Wilson, and says he is himself a pensioner of the League’s fund.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 104: The red-headed man who hired Jabez Wilson for the Red-Headed League. After the League closed, the landlord said he had never heard the name: he knew the tenant as William Morris, a solicitor, and the forwarding address turned out to be a factory where nobody knew either name.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 206: The man who ran the Red-Headed League’s office and hired Jabez Wilson. Holmes explains that the League was a scheme by John Clay and his red-haired accomplice to keep Wilson away from his shop: one rogue kept the temporary office while the other urged Wilson to apply.

## William Morris · supporting

The name the landlord at 7 Pope’s Court gives for the red-headed man who rented the room used as the League’s office—the man Jabez Wilson knew as Duncan Ross. The landlord says he was a solicitor who moved out the day before.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 104: The name the landlord at 7 Pope’s Court gives for the red-headed tenant Jabez Wilson knew as Duncan Ross. The forwarding address, 17 King Edward Street, proved to be an artificial-kneecap factory where no one had heard of either name.

## Archie · supporting

A small, pale young man with a shock of very red hair who is pulled up through the cellar floor by the first intruder. His companion calls him Archie.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 206: The small, pale, red-haired man who came up through the cellar floor with John Clay and dived back down the hole when Holmes sprang. Holmes says three men were waiting at the house’s door, and that Clay took the idea for the Red-Headed League from the colour of his accomplice’s hair.

## Peter Jones · supporting

Peter Jones, an official police agent from Scotland Yard whom Watson already knows. He is waiting with Holmes at Baker Street for the night’s expedition.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 203: Peter Jones of Scotland Yard, self-important but, Holmes says, as brave as a bulldog and as tenacious as a lobster. In the bank cellar he handcuffs John Clay and takes him to the police station.

## Mr. Merryweather · supporting

A long, thin, sad-faced man with a shiny hat and an oppressively respectable frock-coat, introduced by Holmes as a companion for the night’s adventure.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 168: A long, thin, sad-faced man with a shiny hat and an oppressively respectable frock-coat, introduced by Holmes as a companion for the night’s adventure. He laments missing his Saturday-night game of cards for the first time in twenty-seven years.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 173: A long, thin, sad-faced man in an oppressively respectable frock-coat. Holmes explains that he is a bank director with a personal interest in the night’s business.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 182: Chairman of the directors of one of the principal London banks. He leads the party into the cellar of its City branch, where the bank keeps 30,000 French napoleons borrowed from the Bank of France and has had warnings of an attempt on them.

## Ezekiah Hopkins · reference

The late benefactor of Lebanon, Pennsylvania, named in the Red-Headed League’s advertisement; the notice says his bequest funds a vacancy paying £4 a week for nominal work.

After source chapter 2, paragraph 53: The late benefactor named in the Red-Headed League’s advertisement. According to Vincent Spaulding, he was an eccentric American millionaire, red-headed himself, who left his fortune in trust to provide easy, well-paid posts for red-headed men.

## Sarasate · reference

Pablo de Sarasate, the celebrated Spanish violin virtuoso and composer. Holmes invites Watson to hear him play at St. James’s Hall.

## Gustave Flaubert · reference

French novelist, author of Madame Bovary. Holmes quotes a line he attributes to Flaubert’s letters to George Sand: ‘The man is nothing; the work is everything.’

## George Sand · reference

Pen name of the French novelist Aurore Dupin, a friend and long-time correspondent of Gustave Flaubert.

## Mary Sutherland · major

A client of Holmes’s whose “very simple problem” he mentions in passing. Her own case is told in A Case of Identity.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 18: A short-sighted young typist with a small private income of her own. She has come to Holmes in distress to find out what has become of her fiancé, Mr. Hosmer Angel.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 137: A short-sighted young typist with a small private income. Her vanished fiancé ‘Hosmer Angel’ was her stepfather in disguise, but Holmes decides not to tell her, believing she would not accept it. She has vowed to stay true to Hosmer.

## Hosmer Angel · major

The man Mary Sutherland was engaged to. He has disappeared, and she has come to Holmes to find out what became of him.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 57: A shy, softly spoken gentleman, as Mary describes him, whom she met at the gasfitters’ ball and became engaged to. On their wedding morning he got into a separate cab to the church and was not in it when it arrived.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 125: Not a real man. Holmes shows that ‘Hosmer Angel’ was Mary’s stepfather, James Windibank, in disguise, with tinted glasses, false whiskers and a whispering voice. He used it, with his wife’s help, to court Mary himself and so keep her and her income at home.

## Mr. Windibank · major

Mary Sutherland’s stepfather, only about five years older than she is. He married her widowed mother and works as a travelling salesman for a wine importer. He refused to go to the police or to Holmes about her missing fiancé.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 125: Mary Sutherland’s stepfather. Holmes shows that, with his wife’s help, he disguised himself as ‘Hosmer Angel’ and courted Mary so that she, and the hundred a year he had the use of, would stay at home.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 130: Mary Sutherland’s stepfather, who disguised himself as ‘Hosmer Angel’ to keep her and her income at home. Faced with the evidence, he claimed it began as a joke and insisted that nothing he did was legally actionable. He fled when Holmes reached for a hunting crop.

## Mary Sutherland’s mother · supporting

Mary’s mother, a plumber’s widow who has remarried. Her new husband, Mr. Windibank, is nearly fifteen years younger than she is. She lives with him and Mary in Camberwell.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 125: Mary’s mother, who remarried the much younger James Windibank. Holmes concludes that she knew of and helped with her husband’s disguise as ‘Hosmer Angel’, which explains her enthusiasm for the suitor.

## Mary Sutherland’s late father · reference

Mary’s father, now dead. He ran a plumbing business in the Tottenham Court Road, which her mother carried on for a time before selling it.

## Mr. Hardy · supporting

The foreman of the plumbing business left by Mary’s late father. He escorted Mary and her mother to the gasfitters’ ball.

## Uncle Ned · reference

Mary’s uncle in Auckland, New Zealand. He left her £2,500 in New Zealand stock, and she receives only the interest from it.

## Mrs. Etherege · reference

An acquaintance of Mary’s whose husband Holmes found after the police had given him up for dead. She told Mary about Holmes.

## Westhouse & Marbank · reference

A large firm of claret importers in Fenchurch Street. Mr. Windibank travels for them.

After source chapter 3, paragraph 135: The claret importers of Fenchurch Street who employ James Windibank as a traveller. When Holmes sent them the Hosmer Angel description with the disguise removed, they replied that it matched Windibank in every respect.

## Balzac · reference

Honoré de Balzac, the French novelist. Holmes notes that Hosmer Angel’s letters quote him once.

## Hafiz · reference

The Persian poet Hafiz. Holmes cites ‘the old Persian saying’ and puts his wisdom on a level with Horace’s.

## Horace · reference

The Latin poet Horace, whom Holmes pairs with Hafiz as a source of worldly wisdom.

## John Turner · major

The largest landowner in Boscombe Valley, who made his fortune in Australia and came home to England some years ago. A widower with one daughter, he knew Charles McCarthy in the colonies and lets him Hatherley Farm.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 120: The largest landowner in Boscombe Valley, about sixty, who made his fortune in the Victorian goldfields. Long in poor health, he has taken to his bed since McCarthy’s death. Lestrade says he was McCarthy’s great benefactor and let him have Hatherley Farm rent free.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 193: The largest landowner in Boscombe Valley, an old acquaintance of Charles McCarthy from the Australian colonies and his landlord. Gravely ill, he comes to Holmes at the hotel; when Holmes says he knows all about McCarthy, Turner breaks down and says he would have spoken out rather than let young McCarthy be condemned.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 209: The largest landowner in Boscombe Valley, dying of diabetes. In a signed confession he tells Holmes that he was once the goldfields bushranger Black Jack of Ballarat; that Charles McCarthy, the convoy driver whose life he spared, later found him in England and blackmailed him for twenty years; and that he struck McCarthy down at Boscombe Pool when McCarthy pressed for James to marry his daughter Alice.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 215: The largest landowner in Boscombe Valley. He confessed to Holmes that he, once the bushranger Black Jack of Ballarat, killed his blackmailer Charles McCarthy at Boscombe Pool. Holmes kept the confession unused, and Turner lived seven months more before he died. Watson writes that his daughter and young McCarthy may yet live happily together, unaware of the past.

## Charles McCarthy · major

A former Australian colonist who rents Hatherley Farm from his old acquaintance John Turner. A widower with one son, James.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 18: A former Australian colonist who rented Hatherley Farm from his old acquaintance John Turner, a widower with one son, James. On Monday 3 June he walked to Boscombe Pool for an important three o’clock appointment and never came back alive.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 38: A former Australian colonist who rented Hatherley Farm from his old acquaintance John Turner, a widower with one son, James. On Monday 3 June he walked to Boscombe Pool for an important three o’clock appointment and never came back alive. His son describes him as cold and forbidding in manner and not a popular man.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 209: A former Australian colonist who lived rent-free at Hatherley Farm. John Turner’s confession reveals that McCarthy was the wagon-driver of a gold convoy that Turner’s gang robbed in Victoria; years later he recognised Turner in London and blackmailed him for twenty years. When he pressed for his son James to marry Turner’s daughter Alice, Turner killed him at Boscombe Pool.

## James McCarthy · major

The eighteen-year-old only son of Charles McCarthy of Hatherley Farm. Like his father, he is fond of sport and often seen at local race meetings.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 20: The eighteen-year-old only son of Charles McCarthy. He reported finding his father dying beside Boscombe Pool; a witness had seen the two quarrelling, his gun lay near the body and there was blood on his hand. The police arrested him, the inquest returned a verdict of wilful murder, and he awaits trial at the Assizes.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 114: The eighteen-year-old son of Charles McCarthy, held for trial for his father’s murder; Miss Turner and Holmes believe him innocent. Holmes learns that he loves Miss Turner but, about two years ago, secretly married a Bristol barmaid. That is why he resisted his father’s pressure to propose, and why he spent the days before the killing in Bristol. The barmaid has now broken with him, revealing she already had a husband.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 215: The son of Charles McCarthy, wrongly held for his father’s murder. He had secretly married a Bristol barmaid who turned out to be married already. He was acquitted at the Assizes on objections Holmes prepared for the defence, and may yet marry Alice Turner without ever learning the truth about the past.

## Miss Turner · major

John Turner’s only daughter, about eighteen. She believes James McCarthy is innocent and has engaged Lestrade, and through him Holmes, on his behalf.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 87: John Turner’s only daughter, who has known James McCarthy since childhood and is sure he is innocent. She brought in Lestrade and Holmes to clear him. She tells Holmes that Charles McCarthy wanted her to marry James, a match both James and her father opposed.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 201: Alice Turner, John Turner’s only daughter, who has known James McCarthy since childhood and is sure he is innocent. She brought in Lestrade and Holmes to clear him. She told Holmes that Charles McCarthy wanted her to marry James, a match her father opposed.

## Lestrade · supporting

A Scotland Yard detective whom Holmes and Watson know from an earlier case. A practical, sceptical man, he brings Holmes in when a case puzzles him.

## William Crowder · supporting

A gamekeeper employed by John Turner. He testified that he saw Charles McCarthy walking towards Boscombe Pool, followed a few minutes later by his son carrying a gun.

## Patience Moran · supporting

The fourteen-year-old daughter of the lodge-keeper on the Boscombe Valley estate. While picking flowers in the woods she saw Charles McCarthy and his son quarrelling violently by the pool.

## The lodge-keeper · supporting

The keeper of the lodge on John Turner’s Boscombe Valley estate and father of Patience Moran. His lodge was the nearest house to the pool.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 136: Moran, keeper of the lodge on John Turner’s Boscombe Valley estate and father of Patience Moran. His lodge was the nearest house to the pool.

## The coroner · supporting

The official who conducted the inquest into Charles McCarthy’s death and questioned James McCarthy closely about his story.

## John Cobb · reference

The McCarthys’ groom, who drove Charles McCarthy to Ross on the morning he died.

## Dr. Willows · reference

The doctor attending John Turner, who says Turner’s nerves are shattered and will not allow him visitors.

## The Bristol barmaid · supporting

A barmaid in Bristol, mentioned by Holmes after his visit to James McCarthy in prison.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 114: A Bristol barmaid whom James McCarthy secretly married at a registry office about two years ago; he spent the three days before his father’s death with her. On reading that he might hang, she broke with him and wrote that she already had a husband in the Bermuda Dockyard.

## Black Jack of Ballarat · supporting

The name a highway robber went by on the Victorian goldfields in the early 1860s.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 203: The name John Turner went under as a young highway robber on the Victorian goldfields in the early 1860s, one of the six men of the Ballarat Gang.

## The Ballarat Gang · reference

A band of six highway robbers on the Victorian goldfields in the early 1860s, still remembered in the colony.

After source chapter 4, paragraph 203: A band of six highway robbers on the Victorian goldfields in the early 1860s, still remembered in the colony. John Turner rode with them under the name Black Jack of Ballarat.

## Petrarch · reference

The fourteenth-century Italian poet. Holmes carries a pocket edition of his work to read on the train.

## George Meredith · reference

A Victorian English novelist and poet, contemporary with the story. Holmes proposes discussing him to put the case aside for the evening.

## John Openshaw · major

A young man of about twenty-two who comes through a storm from Horsham, in Sussex, to ask Holmes for advice and help about a strange chain of events in his family.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 147: The young man from Horsham who brought Holmes the story of the orange-pip warnings. A newspaper reports that his body was recovered from the Thames near Waterloo Bridge the same night he left Baker Street, and calls his death an accident.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 178: The young man from Horsham who brought Holmes the story of the orange-pip warnings, and who drowned near Waterloo Bridge that same night. Holmes traced the men he holds responsible to the barque Lone Star, and Watson’s narration calls them John Openshaw’s murderers; they were never brought to justice.

## Elias Openshaw · major

John Openshaw’s uncle. He emigrated to America as a young man, became a planter in Florida and a colonel in the Confederate army, then returned around 1869–70 to a small estate near Horsham, where he lived as a hard-drinking recluse.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 45: John Openshaw’s uncle, a former Confederate colonel who lived as a recluse near Horsham. Some weeks after a letter of five orange pips marked ‘K. K. K.’ arrived, he was found dead face-down in a shallow garden pool; the jury called it suicide, though John doubts it.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 134: John Openshaw’s uncle, found dead in a shallow garden pool after receiving the orange-pip warning. Holmes believes he left America in 1869 carrying the papers of the Ku Klux Klan, and that several determined men, not chance, explain his death.

## Joseph Openshaw · supporting

John Openshaw’s father and Elias Openshaw’s brother. He had a small factory at Coventry, patented the Openshaw unbreakable tyre for bicycles, and sold the business to retire comfortably.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 65: John Openshaw’s father, who inherited the Horsham estate from his brother Elias. Days after receiving five orange pips and the order ‘Put the papers on the sundial’, he was found with a shattered skull in a chalk-pit near Portsdown Hill and died; the jury called it an accident, but John suspects a plot.

## K. K. K. · supporting

Three letters, K. K. K., that Elias Openshaw sees on an envelope from Pondicherry holding five dried orange pips. He recognises them with terror, but what they stand for is not explained.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 128: Initials on the orange-pip warnings sent to the Openshaws. Holmes concludes they are not one person’s initials but the badge of a society: several resourceful men determined to recover papers Elias Openshaw took from America.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 134: The Ku Klux Klan: a terrorist secret society formed by ex-Confederate soldiers after the American Civil War, which sent warnings such as orange pips before its murders. Holmes links its sudden collapse in 1869 to Elias Openshaw’s flight with its papers, and believes its most implacable members have pursued the family since.

## Captain James Calhoun · major

Captain of the American barque Lone Star, of Savannah, Georgia. Holmes addresses to him an envelope of five orange pips, marked ‘S. H. for J. O.’

After source chapter 5, paragraph 166: Captain of the barque Lone Star, of Savannah. Holmes calls him the leader of the gang behind the orange-pip warnings and sends him five pips of his own, signed ‘S. H. for J. O.’

After source chapter 5, paragraph 178: Captain of the barque Lone Star, of Savannah, whom Holmes named as the gang’s leader. Holmes learned that he and his two mates were away from the ship on the night John Openshaw died and arranged for a murder charge to meet them in Savannah, but the Lone Star was apparently lost in the Atlantic gales and the pips never reached him.

## Lone Star · reference

A barque (a sailing ship) whose home port is Savannah, Georgia.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 173: An American barque out of Savannah. Holmes found that it called at Pondicherry early in 1883 and was at Dundee in January 1885, matching the postmarks of the first two orange-pip warnings.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 178: An American barque out of Savannah, which Holmes traced to Pondicherry, Dundee and London when the orange-pip warnings were posted. It sailed for Savannah the morning after John Openshaw’s death and was apparently lost in that year’s gales; only a stern-post carved ‘L. S.’ was ever seen.

## Major Prendergast · reference

An acquaintance of John Openshaw who told him about Holmes. Holmes had cleared him in the Tankerville Club scandal, when he was wrongly accused of cheating at cards.

## Major Freebody · reference

An old friend of Joseph Openshaw who commands one of the forts on Portsdown Hill, near Portsmouth. Joseph goes to stay with him.

## Mr. Fordham · reference

A lawyer (solicitor) in Horsham whom Elias Openshaw sends for to witness his will.

## Mary · reference

Someone in Elias Openshaw’s household at Horsham, apparently a servant, whom he wants told to light a fire in his room.

## Police-Constable Cook · reference

A London police constable of the H Division, on duty near Waterloo Bridge, named in a newspaper report.

## Hudson · reference

A name in an entry dated 4 March 1869 on a torn page of Elias Openshaw’s diary: ‘Hudson came. Same old platform.’ The page does not say who he was.

## McCauley · reference

A name on a torn page of Elias Openshaw’s diary from March 1869: ‘Set the pips on McCauley, Paramore, and John Swain’, and two days later, ‘McCauley cleared.’

After source chapter 5, paragraph 136: A man named in Elias Openshaw’s 1869 diary. Holmes reads ‘set the pips on’ as sending him the society’s warning, and ‘McCauley cleared’ as meaning that he left the country.

## Paramore · reference

A name on a torn page of Elias Openshaw’s diary from March 1869: ‘Set the pips on McCauley, Paramore, and John Swain’, and later, ‘Visited Paramore. All well.’

After source chapter 5, paragraph 136: A man named in Elias Openshaw’s 1869 diary. Holmes reads the entries as meaning he received the society’s warning, did not leave, and was then ‘visited’, Holmes fears with a sinister result.

## John Swain · reference

A man ‘of St. Augustine’ named on a torn page of Elias Openshaw’s diary from March 1869: ‘Set the pips on … John Swain’, then ‘John Swain cleared.’

After source chapter 5, paragraph 136: A man of St. Augustine (Florida) named in Elias Openshaw’s 1869 diary. Holmes reads the entries as meaning he received the society’s warning and then left the country.

## The Sholtos · reference

A family from an earlier case of Holmes and Watson, the one Watson calls the Sign of Four. Holmes says John Openshaw faces even greater dangers than they did.

## Clark Russell · reference

W. Clark Russell, a popular Victorian writer of sea stories; Watson is reading one of his books during the storm.

## Cuvier · reference

Georges Cuvier, the French naturalist, famous for reconstructing whole animals from single fossil bones; Holmes cites him as a model for reasoning.

## Lee · reference

Robert E. Lee, commander of the Confederate army, whose surrender in 1865 ended the American Civil War; John dates his uncle’s return to his plantation from it.

## Hood · reference

A Confederate general, generally taken to be John Bell Hood, under whom Elias Openshaw served and rose to colonel.

## Jackson · reference

A Confederate general in whose army Elias Openshaw first fought; usually taken to be Thomas ‘Stonewall’ Jackson.

## Isa Whitney · supporting

Kate Whitney’s husband and one of Watson’s patients. He fell into opium addiction after trying to copy De Quincey’s accounts of opium dreams, and has been a slave to the drug for many years.

## Kate Whitney · supporting

Isa Whitney’s wife and an old school friend of Watson’s wife. She arrives late at night, frightened, to ask the Watsons to bring her husband home from an opium den.

## Elias Whitney · reference

Isa Whitney’s late brother, a Doctor of Divinity who was Principal of the Theological College of St. George’s.

## Thomas De Quincey · reference

English writer best known for Confessions of an English Opium-Eater (1821). Isa Whitney read his description of his opium dreams and tried to reproduce them.

## Watson’s wife · supporting

Watson’s wife, an old school friend of Kate Whitney. Friends in trouble tend to come to her for comfort.

## The Lascar · supporting

The Lascar (an Indian sailor) who runs the Bar of Gold opium den in Upper Swandam Lane. Holmes calls him a rascal who has sworn revenge on him.
