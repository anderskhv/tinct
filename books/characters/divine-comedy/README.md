# The Divine Comedy character package

All three canticles are authored: **Inferno (1–34), Purgatorio (35–67) and
Paradiso (68–100)** — 100 cantos, 4,812 paragraphs per edition, the largest
source in the library.

Current state: 589 entities authored, 574 bound in the original and
587 in the modern, 1,144 and 1,211 exact mentions. **No entity is
missing from both editions**; every remaining gap is one edition paraphrasing a
name the other prints. Content revision 2026-09-11.2.

## Why this book matters most for cards

Almost everyone in the Comedy is a real person — a Florentine neighbour, a pope,
an emperor, a classical poet, a saint — and a reader without notes simply cannot
place them. Farinata, Cacciaguida, Piccarda, Pier della Vigna: the poem assumes
you know who they are. The Paradiso is worse: Thomas Aquinas names twenty-three
people in two cantos and explains none of them.

## Verse against prose

The original is Longfellow's blank verse; the modern edition is prose. Both run
to 100 cantos and 4,812 paragraphs and align paragraph for paragraph. As with the
Aeneid, Longfellow capitalises every verse line-opening, so the entity inventory
was built from the prose edition and checked back against the verse.

**Longfellow spells the guide Virgilius where the prose says Virgil**, and both
are aliases of one entity. He also writes **Guidoguerra** as one word,
**Francis of Accorso** for Francesco d'Accorso, **Ganellon**, **Soldanier**,
**Launcelot** and **Diomed**. The Paradiso adds a further run of them —
**Mutius**, **Arabians**, **Beda**, **Sigier**, **Brissus**, **Ubald**,
**Bonaventura**, **Ubertin**, **Guenever**, **Ethiop**, **Calixtus**,
**Caorsines**, **Agostino**, **Sichaeus**, **Rudolph**, **Orlando**,
**Phaeton**, **Anselmus**, **Albertus**, **Mangiador** — all carried as aliases.

**Dante names himself exactly once in the whole poem**, when Beatrice calls him
by name in the earthly paradise (64:18). A test pins that to one location in
both editions; it is the kind of fact a reader should be able to check.

## Editorial checks

**1. Namesakes.** The hardest problem in this poem, and the reason the builder
carries a position table rather than a name list.

*Inferno.* **Five different men are called Guido**: Cavalcanti's son (10:20),
Guido Guerra (16:12), Guido Bonatti (20:39), Guido del Cassero of Fano (28:25)
and Guido di Romena (30:25). Nothing but the canto separates them. Also **two
named Brutus** — the republican who drove out Tarquin, seen in Limbo, and
Caesar's assassin in Lucifer's mouth; **two named Alexander**; **two named
Buoso**; **Michael** the archangel against **Michael Scot** the astrologer;
**Jacopo** Rusticucci against Jacopo da Sant'Andrea; **Adam** the first father
against **Master Adam** the counterfeiter.

*Purgatorio.* **Pope Boniface the Eighth against Boniface of Ravenna**; Saint
Nicholas against Pope Nicholas; Clement the Fourth against Clement the Fifth;
**three men called Ugolin** — the count in the tower, Ugolin d'Azzo and Ugolin
de' Fantoli; Pallas the goddess in the carved rout of the giants; and Peter of
Aragon, whom Longfellow calls **Pier**. Manfred speaks of **two Constances in
one canto** — his grandmother the Empress and his own daughter — and the
Paradiso means the Empress again; each is now bound to the right paragraph.

*Paradiso.* **Four men called Peter inside two cantos**: Peter Lombard (77:35),
Peter Mangiatore and Peter of Spain in a single line (79:44), Peter Damian
(88:40) — with Saint Peter everywhere else, and Pietro Bernardone, Francis's
father, at 78:29. **Two men called Bernard**: Bernard of Quintavalle, the first
man to follow Francis (78:26), and Bernard of Clairvaux, who guides the last
three cantos. **Two called Dionysius**: the tyrant of Syracuse boiling in the
blood, and the Areopagite who got the angelic orders right. **Two called
Thomas**: Aquinas, who speaks, and the apostle, who only lends his feast day to
Hugh the Great (83:42). **Three called William**: the Marquis of Monferrato in
Purgatory, William of Orange in the cross of Mars, and William the Good of
Sicily in the Eagle's eye — the last of whom Longfellow calls *Guglielmo*, the
same word he uses for Guglielmo Borsiere in Hell and Guglielmo Aldobrandeschi in
Purgatory. **Two called Anselm**: Ugolino's small grandson, and the archbishop
of Canterbury. **Two called Hugh**: Capet and Saint Victor. **Four called
Charles**: of Anjou, of Valois, Charles II of Naples and Charles Martel, who
speaks. **Albert** is Alberto of Siena, Albert degli Alberti, Albert of Germany
and Albertus Magnus, depending on the canto.

**2. Person or not.** Places, rivers, mountains and book titles are excluded.
The rivers of Hell are cast only as the powers they are where the poem treats
them so. Three cases in the Paradiso needed deciding:

- **Lombard** is a language at 27:6, an adjective for Sordello at 40:20 and for
  Marco and Guido da Castel in Purgatory, the Lombard *people* at 73:31, the
  surname of **Peter Lombard** at 77:35, and **Bartolommeo della Scala** — "the
  great Lombard" — at 84:23. Only the last three are cast.
- **Virtues** is the three theological virtues at Purgatorio 41:11 and an
  ordinary plural noun at 69:23 before it becomes the fifth angelic order at
  95:40. Only the order is cast.
- **Frederick Novello** and **Frederick Tignoso** carry their own surnames; the
  emperor Frederick the Second must not be bound over the top of them. A test
  pins that.

**3. Scriptural and mythological figures.** The Paradiso is denser in these than
either canticle before it: Jephthah and Iphigenia paired as the same crime made
twice; Melchisedec, Esau and Jacob as proof that nature does not follow family;
Rahab of Jericho at the highest rank of Venus; Nathan, Sarah, Rebecca, Judith
and Anna in the rose. Each is cast for what the poem uses them for, not for the
whole of their story.

**4. Ambiguous references.** Left unbound where the text does not settle them:

- **"whichever John you choose"** (71:9) — Beatrice means the Baptist or the
  Evangelist and declines to say which. Not bound.
- **"Count Guido"** (83:32) — a Guido descended from the Ravignani, and the poem
  gives no more. Not bound to any of the five Guidos.
- **Can Grande della Scala** (84:25) — "one who at his birth was so marked by
  this star". Unnamed, and the two editions word the circumstance differently
  enough that there is no shared span. Not bound.
- **Agamemnon** (72:23), the **Sultan** Francis preached to (78:33), **Boethius**
  at Cieldauro (77:42) and **Hezekiah** in the Eagle’s eye (87:16) are all
  identified by circumstance the two editions phrase differently. Not bound.
- **Ariadne** is named in the prose at 80:4 and called "Minos' daughter" by
  Longfellow. Recorded as an original-edition omission rather than bound over a
  span that already carries Minos.

Five figures the poem does not name **are** bound, because the circumstance
survives in both editions: **Solomon** ("the fifth light", "regal prudence" /
"kingly prudence"), **Hugh the Great** ("the great baron"), **Orosius** ("the
advocate of the Christian ages"), **Charles II** ("the Cripple of Jerusalem"),
and **Clement the Fifth** ("the Gascon"). Solomon is the notable one: his name
appears nowhere in the poem, and a test asserts that no mention of him quotes it.

**5. Spot-read.** Twenty mentions drawn at random, ten per edition, read back
against the paragraph they sit in. Nineteen were right. The twentieth was
**Currado da Palazzo at 50:41 bound to Currado Malaspina** — the alias `Currado`
sat on the Malaspina card and swallowed the other man's first name. That is
exactly the class of error no coverage test catches, and it is why the spot-read
is a required step rather than a nicety. Fixed with a position table, and a
second sweep over every mention whose matched text abuts a capitalised word
turned up two more of the same kind: Longfellow's **"Francis of Accorso"**
binding to Francis of Assisi, and **Franco Bolognese's** surname binding to the
Bolognese people. Both fixed the same way.

**6. Both editions independently.** Fifteen entities are missing from the
original and two from the modern, and **none is missing from both** — the
check that matters, since a name absent from one edition is paraphrase and a name
absent from both is a binding that was never made. Recorded as `omittedEntities`
and asserted by a test.

## A decision worth flagging

Cacciaguida's roll-call of the old Florentine houses (Paradiso XV–XVI) names
about thirty families in the space of forty lines — Ughi, Catellini, Filippi,
Greci, Ormanni, Alberichi, Ravignani, Sacchetti, Giuochi, Fifanti, Barucci,
Galli and the rest. Thirty cards each reading "an ancient Florentine house"
would be noise. They are bound instead to **one card**, *The old houses of
Florence*, which tells the reader what the roll-call is and why Dante recites it.
Gianni del Soldanieri, who is a person and a traitor in the ice, keeps his own
card; a test pins him to Inferno 32:40 and the roll to cantos 82–83.

## Deliberately not invented

Eight entities in the Inferno–Purgatorio draft came from knowledge of Dante
rather than from these two texts — Cavalcante, Griffolino, Napoleone, Ceres,
Annas and others — and the compiler reported every one as having no mentions.
They were removed rather than bound to something approximate. This is the failure
mode the authoring rules exist to prevent, and it is why the both-editions
omission check is the last thing run before a package is marked validated.

## Source defects

None found. Both editions parse cleanly, align paragraph for paragraph across all
100 chapters, and carry no printed line numbers or compositor debris. The
divergences recorded in `omittedEntities` are translation choices, not defects.

## Validation

`python3 books/characters/build_divine_comedy.py --check`, then
`python3 -m unittest discover -s books/characters -p 'test_*.py'`. Eighteen
focused tests. No edition changes, no network generation, no production
verification claimed here.
