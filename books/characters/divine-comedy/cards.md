# The Divine Comedy: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Dante · central

The poet who is also the pilgrim of his own poem. He names himself exactly once in the whole Comedy, when Beatrice calls him by name in the earthly paradise.

## Virgil · central

The Roman poet of the Aeneid, sent to guide Dante through Hell and Purgatory; a soul of Limbo, saved from damnation but not from exclusion. Longfellow spells him Virgilius.

## Beatrice · central

The Florentine woman Dante loved, now among the blessed; she sends Virgil to his aid and takes over as his guide in the earthly paradise and through Heaven.

## Statius · major

The Roman poet of the Thebaid, who joins the travellers in Purgatory and reveals that Virgil’s own verses converted him.

## Lucifer · major

The emperor of the kingdom of grief, fixed in ice at the centre of the earth; also called Dis, Satan and Beelzebub.

## Charon · supporting

The ancient ferryman with wheels of flame about his eyes, who carries the damned across Acheron.

## Minos · supporting

The judge of the damned, who winds his tail about himself to sentence each soul to its circle.

## Cerberus · supporting

The three-throated dog who howls over the gluttons in the rain.

## Plutus · supporting

The hoarse-voiced guardian of the fourth circle, where the misers and spendthrifts roll their weights.

## Phlegyas · supporting

The boatman who ferries Dante and Virgil across the marsh of Styx.

## Megaera · supporting

One of the three Furies on the tower of Dis.

## Alecto · supporting

One of the three Furies on the tower of Dis.

## Tisiphone · supporting

One of the three Furies on the tower of Dis.

## Medusa · supporting

The Gorgon the Furies call for, whose face would turn Dante to stone.

## The Minotaur · supporting

A Florentine glutton who prophesies the city’s coming strife.

Farinata degli Uberti, the Ghibelline leader who rises out of his burning tomb and speaks of Florence.

Guido Cavalcanti, the poet and Dante’s friend, asked after by his father in the tombs of the heretics.

The emperor Frederick the Second, named as lying among the Epicureans, and whose leaden capes are a byword.

Ottaviano degli Ubaldini, named only by his office among the heretics.

## Anastasius · reference

The pope whose tomb Dante passes, said to have been led from the true path.

The deacon said to have led Pope Anastasius astray.

## Alexander · 

A tyrant boiling in the river of blood, named beside Dionysius.

## Alexander · 

Alexander the Great, whose army in India saw flames fall unbroken from the sky.

The tyrant of Syracuse, boiling in the river of blood.

## Azzolino · reference

Ezzelino da Romano, the black-haired tyrant in the river of blood.

Obizzo d’Este, the fair-haired tyrant in the river of blood, killed by his own stepson.

The scourge of the earth, deep in the boiling blood.

A tyrant in the boiling blood.

A tyrant in the boiling blood.

A highway robber, boiling in the river of blood.

## Rinier Pazzo · 

A highway robber, boiling in the river of blood.

## The one who held both keys · supporting

Frederick the Second’s chancellor, who turned his master’s heart softly in locking and unlocking, and killed himself when envy turned the court against him. He is the speaking tree of the wood of the suicides, and the canto never gives his name.

## Lano · reference

A Sienese squanderer, hunted through the wood by black hounds.

## Jacopo da Sant’Andrea · 

A Paduan squanderer, torn apart by the hounds in the wood of the suicides.

## The Florentine who hanged himself · supporting

The bush who speaks last in the wood, from the city that changed its patron saint to the Baptist.

## Capaneus · supporting

One of the seven kings who besieged Thebes, who held God in contempt and holds him in contempt still on the burning sand.

## Brunetto · major

Ser Brunetto Latini, the Florentine scholar who taught Dante how man makes himself eternal, and whom Dante finds among the sodomites.

## Priscian · reference

A grammarian named among the clerks on the burning sand.

## Francesco d’Accorso · reference

A Bolognese jurist named among the clerks on the burning sand. Longfellow calls him Francis of Accorso.

## Guido Guerra · supporting

A Florentine of great worth, grandson of the good Gualdrada, one of the three who circle on the burning sand. Longfellow writes the name as one word, Guidoguerra.

The Florentine woman named as Guido Guerra’s grandmother.

## Tegghiaio · reference

Tegghiaio Aldobrandi, whose counsel Florence should have heeded.

## Jacopo Rusticucci · 

A Florentine who blames his savage wife for his ruin, one of the three on the burning sand.

A Florentine newly arrived in Hell, who has been telling the others how the city has changed.

An Etruscan diviner of the hills of Luni.

Tiresias’s daughter, the virgin seer who settled where Mantua now stands; Virgil tells her story to correct the legend of his own city.

## Puccio · reference

A Florentine thief, the only one of the five who was not transformed.

The Greek whose cunning took Troy, and who tells of his last voyage beyond the pillars of Hercules; he burns in a double flame with Diomedes.

Ulysses’s companion, burning in the same flame. Longfellow spells him Diomed.

Achilles’s bride, who still mourns him; the cunning that took him from her is part of what Ulysses burns for.

The Titan father of the sun in the old mythology; Dante looks back down through the spheres and names the planets by their gods.

## Maia · reference

The mother of Mercury, and so Dante’s name for the planet.

## James · supporting

The apostle, who examines Dante on hope; the baron of Compostela, for whose sake Galicia is a place of pilgrimage.

## John · supporting

The evangelist who lay on Christ’s breast and was taken from the cross to care for his mother; he examines Dante on love, and puts down the legend that he went to Heaven in the body.

## Ananias · reference

The disciple whose hands restored Saul’s sight; Beatrice’s look does as much for Dante’s.

## Linus · reference

An early pope and martyr, named by Saint Peter with Cletus among those whose blood was not shed to buy gold.

## Cletus · reference

An early pope and martyr, named with Linus in Peter’s indictment of his own successors.

## Sixtus · reference

A martyred pope, named in Peter’s roll of those who died for the life of Heaven and not for a bank.

## Pius · reference

A martyred pope, named with Sixtus, Callixtus and Urban.

## Callixtus · reference

A martyred pope in Peter’s roll.

## Urban · reference

A martyred pope, the last named in Peter’s roll before he turns on the living ones.

## The Gascons · reference

With the Cahorsines, the nations Peter says are preparing to drink the blood of the Church — Clement the Fifth’s countrymen and John the Twenty-Second’s.

## The Cahorsines · reference

Named with the Gascons in Peter’s prophecy; Cahors was a byword for usury and the birthplace of Pope John XXII.

## Europa · reference

The girl Jove carried off in the shape of a bull; Dante marks how far he has travelled by the shore where she was taken.

## Leda · reference

The mother of the Twins; Dante calls Gemini her fair nest, and Beatrice’s look lifts him out of it.

## The Thrones · supporting

The third angelic order, mirrors in which God’s judgement is reflected down to the spheres below.

## The Dominions · reference

The fourth angelic order, first of the second hierarchy.

## The Virtues · reference

The fifth angelic order, named between the Dominions and the Powers.

## The Powers · reference

The sixth angelic order, closing the second hierarchy.

## The Principalities · reference

The seventh angelic order, wheeling in the penultimate dances.

## The Archangels · reference

The eighth angelic order, named with the Principalities.

## Boreas · reference

The north wind, whose gentler cheek leaves the sky clear; Dante uses it for the air after Beatrice has swept an error away.

## Jerome · reference

The church father who wrote that the angels were made long ages before the rest of creation — which Beatrice contradicts, gently, on the authority of scripture.

## Anthony · reference

Saint Anthony the Abbot, whose pig is fattened on the offerings of the credulous, along with others the poem says are more piggish still.

## The Spaniards · reference

Named with the Indians and the Jews as witnesses that the darkness at the Crucifixion was seen everywhere, not only at Jerusalem.

## The Indians · reference

Named for their forests and their thirst, and with the Spaniards as witnesses to the eclipse at the Crucifixion.

## Bernard · central

Bernard of Clairvaux, who takes over from Beatrice for the last three cantos, shows Dante the order of the rose, and makes the prayer to the Virgin that wins him the final sight.

## The Veronica · reference

The cloth at Saint Peter’s said to carry the face of Christ; Dante uses the pilgrim who comes from Croatia to stare at it for his own staring at Bernard.

## Sarah · reference

Abraham’s wife, seated in the rose in the line of Hebrew women that divides the flower.

## Rebecca · reference

Isaac’s wife, named in the line of Hebrew women below Eve, Mary, Rachel and Beatrice.

## Judith · reference

The woman who killed Holofernes, seated in the rose with Sarah and Rebecca.

## Anna · reference

The mother of Mary, seated opposite Saint Peter and so content with looking at her daughter that she will not turn her eyes even to sing.

## Augustine · supporting

Augustine of Hippo, seated with Francis and Benedict beneath John the Baptist; the advocate of the Christian ages supplied him his rhetoric.

## The advocate of the Christian ages · reference

Paulus Orosius, whose history against the pagans armed Augustine with his arguments; the poem names his work and not his name.

## The Sibyl · reference

The prophetess whose answers were written on light leaves and scattered by the wind — Dante’s figure for how much of the vision he has already lost.
