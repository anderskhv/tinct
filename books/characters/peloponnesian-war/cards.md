# History of the Peloponnesian War: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Thucydides · central

The Athenian who wrote this. He began at the moment the war broke out, judging it would be greater than any before, and he says plainly what he did about the speeches: he made the speakers say what the occasion called for, keeping as close as he could to the general sense of what was really said. The narrative he tested by the most severe scrutiny he could manage, since eye-witnesses of the same event do not agree. He wrote it, he says, not as an essay to win the applause of the moment but as a possession for all time.

## Thucydides · reference

One of the three commanders of the forty ships that reinforced the siege of Samos. He is named without a patronymic, and the history never says whether the author means himself.

## Hellen · reference

Deucalion’s son, whose strength in Phthiotis gave the country its name — though a long time passed, Thucydides says, before Hellenes meant all of it.

## Deucalion · reference

Hellen’s father.

## Homer · major

Thucydides’s chief witness for the age before the war, and his chief problem with it. Homer never calls the Greeks Hellenes except for Achilles’s men, never uses the word barbarian, and puts the fleet at twelve hundred ships — evidence to be used, with the exaggeration a poet is licensed to use discounted out of it.

## Achilles · reference

Whose followers out of Phthiotis are the only men Homer calls Hellenes.

## Minos · supporting

The first man tradition names as having built a navy. He took what is now the Hellenic sea, ruled the Cyclades, expelled the Carians and installed his own sons, and put down piracy — a necessary step, Thucydides notes, to secure the revenues for himself.

## Agamemnon · supporting

Who raised the force against Troy — by superiority of strength rather than by Tyndareus’s oaths, and by fear as much as goodwill, in Thucydides’s reading. His own contingent was the largest and he supplied the Arcadians’ ships; Homer calls him king of many an isle and of all Argos, which he could not have been without a fleet.

## Tyndareus · reference

Whose oaths bound Helen’s suitors to follow Agamemnon — not, Thucydides thinks, the real reason they went.

## Pelops · supporting

Who arrived from Asia among a needy population with vast wealth and acquired such power that, foreigner though he was, the country was named after him.

## Eurystheus · supporting

Killed in Attica by the Heraclids. He had entrusted Mycenae and the government to his mother’s brother Atreus when he set out, and did not return.

## Atreus · supporting

Eurystheus’s mother’s brother, who had left his father after the death of Chrysippus, and who took up the sceptre of Mycenae at the wish of the Mycenaeans when Eurystheus did not come back — so putting the line of Pelops above the line of Perseus.

## Chrysippus · reference

Whose death had driven Atreus from his father.

## Perseus · reference

Whose descendants the descendants of Pelops came to surpass.

## Philoctetes · reference

Whose ships in Homer’s catalogue carry fifty men each, all of them rowers and bowmen — the minimum against the Boeotian hundred and twenty.

## Ameinocles · reference

The Corinthian shipwright who built four ships for the Samians, nearly three hundred years before the end of this war — Thucydides’s earliest date.

## Cyrus · supporting

First king of the Persians, under whom the Ionians built up their naval strength; he dethroned Croesus, overran everything between the Halys and the sea, and did not stop until he had taken the cities of the coast.

## Cambyses · reference

Cyrus’s son, in whose reign Polycrates held Samos.

## Polycrates · supporting

Tyrant of Samos, whose navy reduced many of the islands; he took Rhenea and consecrated it to the Delian Apollo.

## Darius · supporting

Cambyses’s successor, who reduced the islands with the Phoenician navy; the Sicilian tyrants and the Corcyraeans got their galleys only shortly before his death.

## Xerxes · major

Whose expedition closes Thucydides’s naval history: after it there were no fleets of consequence in Hellas until the war with Aegina and the prospect of invasion let Themistocles build the one that fought at Salamis.

## Croesus · reference

Dethroned by Cyrus.

## Hipparchus · major

Hippias’s brother, killed by Harmodius and Aristogiton near the temple of the daughters of Leos while he was arranging the Panathenaic procession. The Athenian public imagines he was tyrant when he died; Thucydides puts the correction in his chapter on how carelessly men take the traditions even of their own country.

## Harmodius · major

Who with Aristogiton suspected on the day itself that they had been betrayed, concluded that Hippias had been warned, and — unwilling to be arrested and lose their lives for nothing — fell on the brother instead of the tyrant.

## Aristogiton · major

Harmodius’s fellow conspirator.

## Hippias · major

The eldest of Pisistratus’s sons and the man who actually held supreme power — the one the conspirators meant to kill, and the one their accomplices had warned.

## Pisistratus · supporting

Whose sons were Hippias, Hipparchus and Thessalus.

## Thessalus · reference

The third of Pisistratus’s sons.

## Leos · reference

Whose daughters’ temple stood where Hipparchus was killed.

## Phalius · reference

Eratocleides’s son, of the Heraclid family, summoned from Corinth as ancient custom required to found Epidamnus for Corcyra.

## Eratocleides · reference

Phalius’s father.

## Aristeus · supporting

Pellichas’s son, first-named of the three admirals of the seventy-five ships Corinth sent to Epidamnus — and beaten off Leukimme with fifteen ships lost. Not the Aristeus who commanded at Potidaea.

## Pellichas · reference

Aristeus the admiral’s father.

## Callicrates · reference

Callias’s son, the second of the three Corinthian admirals.

## Callias · reference

Callicrates the Corinthian admiral’s father. Not the Athenian general killed at Potidaea.

## Timanor · reference

Timanthes’s son, the third Corinthian admiral.

## Timanthes · reference

Timanor’s father.

## Archetimus · reference

Eurytimus’s son, who commanded the Corinthian troops with Isarchidas.

## Eurytimus · reference

Archetimus’s father.

## Isarchidas · reference

Isarchus’s son, the other commander of the Corinthian troops.

## Isarchus · reference

Isarchidas’s father.

## Meikiades · reference

First-named of the three admirals of the hundred and ten Corcyraean ships at Sybota, each commanding one squadron of the line.

## Aisimides · reference

The second Corcyraean admiral.

## Eurybatus · reference

The third Corcyraean admiral.

## Xenoclides · reference

Euthycles’s son, the Corinthian commander of the hundred and fifty ships, with four colleagues.

## Euthycles · reference

Xenoclides’s father.

## Lacedaemonius · reference

Cimon’s son, first-named of the three commanders of the ten Athenian ships sent to Corcyra under orders not to collide with the Corinthian fleet — orders they kept until the rout made them impossible.

## Cimon · major

Miltiades’s son. He took Eion on the Strymon from the Medes and enslaved it, enslaved Scyros, won both battles at the Eurymedon on the same day and destroyed two hundred Phoenician ships, brought the Athenians to Ithome when Sparta asked for their siege-craft, and died at the siege of Kitium in Cyprus. Lacedaemonius was his son.

## Miltiades · reference

Cimon’s father.

## Diotimus · reference

Strombichus’s son, the second of the three commanders of the ten ships.

## Strombichus · reference

Diotimus’s father.

## Proteas · reference

Epicles’s son, the third commander of the ten ships.

## Epicles · reference

Proteas’s father.

## Glaucon · reference

Leagrus’s son, who brought the twenty Athenian reinforcing ships up through the corpses and the wrecks to the Corcyraean camp at Leukimme after dark — and was very nearly taken for the enemy.

## Leagrus · reference

Glaucon’s father.

## Andocides · reference

Leogoras’s son, the other commander of the twenty ships.

## Leogoras · reference

Andocides’s father.

## Perdiccas · major

Alexander’s son and king of the Macedonians, turned from an old Athenian friend and ally into an enemy because Athens allied with his brother Philip and with Derdas. He worked on Lacedaemon and on Corinth to get Potidaea into revolt, persuaded the Chalcidians to demolish their seaboard towns and settle inland at Olynthus on land he gave them round Lake Bolbe, and changed sides twice in one campaign — deputing Iolaus to command the cavalry he had just brought back to the Potidaean side.

After source chapter 8, paragraph 32: King of the Macedonians and nobody’s reliable ally. Reconciled to Athens and given Therme back, he at once joined Phormio against the Chalcidians; he had promised Sitalces something for the reconciliation and never paid it, and Sitalces came down with a hundred and fifty thousand men and the pretender Amyntas in his baggage. Perdiccas bought the retreat by promising his sister to Sitalces’s nephew — and gave Stratonice to Seuthes as promised. He also sent a thousand Macedonians to Cnemus in Acarnania without the Athenians knowing, and they arrived too late.

## Alexander · supporting

Perdiccas’s father, king of the Macedonians. Pydna was in his dominions.

## Philip · supporting

Perdiccas’s brother, leagued with Derdas against him and allied with Athens; the six hundred Macedonian horse that rode with the Athenians to Potidaea were his followers and Pausanias’s.

## Derdas · supporting

Leagued with Philip against Perdiccas; his brothers invaded Macedonia from the interior alongside the Athenians.

## Pausanias · reference

The Macedonian whose horsemen rode with Philip’s on the Athenian side to Potidaea. Thucydides says nothing else about him, and he is not the Spartan regent.

## Archestratus · supporting

Lycomedes’s son, in command of the thirty ships and thousand infantry first sent to Macedonia, with orders to take hostages from the Potidaeans, raze the wall, and watch the neighbouring cities.

## Lycomedes · reference

Archestratus’s father.

## Aristeus · major

Adimantus’s son, always a steady friend of the Potidaeans — most of the Corinthian volunteers went out of love for him. Made general of all the infantry, he won with his own wing and lost with the rest, and got most of his men into Potidaea at a run along the breakwater through the sea. When the investment closed he advised all but five hundred to sail out so the provisions would last, offered to stay himself, could not persuade them, and slipped past the guardships alone to carry on the war from Chalcidice. Not the admiral beaten at Epidamnus.

After source chapter 7, paragraph 23: Adimantus’s son, the prime mover at Potidaea and in the Thracian towns, who went with the Lacedaemonian envoys to the King to ask for money. Sadocus seized them in Thrace and handed them over, and the Athenians killed them all the same day without a trial and threw the bodies into a pit — afraid that if Aristeus got away he would do them still more harm.

## Adimantus · reference

Aristeus of Potidaea’s father.

## Callias · supporting

Calliades’s son, the Athenian general of the two thousand infantry and forty ships sent against the revolted towns, and one of the hundred and fifty Athenian dead in the victory at Potidaea. Not the Corinthian admiral’s father.

## Calliades · reference

Callias the Athenian general’s father.

## Iolaus · reference

Perdiccas’s deputy in command of the cavalry at Potidaea.

## Phormio · major

Asopius’s son, who brought sixteen hundred Athenian infantry round to the Pallene side of Potidaea, fixed his headquarters at Aphytis, and raised the works nobody had dared to raise — closing the investment on both sides. He then used the same force to ravage Chalcidice and Bottica and take some of the towns.

After source chapter 8, paragraph 22: Asopius’s son, with twenty ships at Naupactus against forty-seven. He sailed round the Peloponnesian circle until the morning wind came down the gulf and their oars fouled, then gave the signal and took twelve ships. Six days later, with seventy-seven against his twenty, he was forced along the shore and lost nine of them — until one Athenian ship rounded a moored merchantman, rammed the Leucadian chasing her, and turned the day. He told his men beforehand that the Peloponnesians feared their irrational audacity more than they would have feared a proportionate force.

## Asopius · reference

Phormio’s father.

## Archidamus · major

The Lacedaemonian king with the reputation of being wise and moderate, who told the assembly what a war against a distant people with money, ships, horses and tributary allies would cost: they were inferior at sea, far worse off for money, and could not end it by burning fields. He asked for embassies and two or three years of preparation, said he feared they would leave the war as a legacy to their children, and was voted down.

After source chapter 8, paragraph 4: Zeuxidamus’s son, king of Lacedaemon and leader of the invasions. He told the assembled generals that confidence in an army of invasion must be matched by caution, wasted the first campaign in front of Oenoe hoping Athens would submit while her land was untouched — for which the army blamed him — and then sat at Acharnae to see whether three thousand Acharnian hoplites would force a battle on the rest of the city. At Plataea he heard the envoys out, offered them neutrality or a receipt for their land, and when they refused called the gods and heroes of their own country to witness that he had made many fair proposals and had not succeeded.

## Sthenelaidas · supporting

The ephor who answered Archidamus — the Athenians had said a great deal in praise of themselves and nowhere denied the wrong, so they deserved double punishment, for having ceased to be good and for having become bad. He then refused to judge the shouting and made the assembly divide by walking to two sides of the room, so that they would have to declare themselves and their ardour for war would rise.

## Leotychides · supporting

King of the Lacedaemonians and commander of the Hellenes at Mycale, who went home with the Peloponnesian allies while the Athenians and the Ionians stayed to take Sestos.

## Themistocles · central

The commander at Salamis, whom Thucydides calls the ablest judge of a sudden crisis and the best prophet of a distant future that ever was — genius by native ability, neither shaped nor supplemented by study. He got Athens walled by going to Sparta himself and stalling there while the whole city, women and children included, tore down buildings for materials; he finished Piraeus, told Athens to stick to the sea, and laid the foundations of the empire.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 22: The commander at Salamis and the ablest man of his time, in Thucydides’s judgement, at meeting an emergency. Implicated in the inquiry into Pausanias’s Medism, already ostracised and living at Argos, he was hunted through the Peloponnese, refused by Corcyra, and took sanctuary at the hearth of Admetus the Molossian with the king’s child in his arms. He reached Artaxerxes by letter, asked a year’s grace to learn Persian, and ended governor of Magnesia — fifty talents a year for bread, Lampsacus for wine, Myos for the rest. Disease killed him, though there is a story of poison; his bones were brought home secretly, since it is against the law to bury a traitor in Attica.

## Abronichus · reference

Lysicles’s son, one of the two colleagues Themistocles had kept back in Athens until the wall was high enough to defend.

## Lysicles · reference

Abronichus’s father.

## Aristides · supporting

Lysimachus’s son, Themistocles’s other colleague on the embassy that told Sparta the wall was up.

## Lysimachus · reference

Aristides’s father.

## Pausanias · major

Cleombrotus’s son, sent out as commander-in-chief of the Hellenes, who took Cyprus and Byzantium and then lost the command by playing the autocrat: the Ionians and the newly liberated went over to the Athenians to be rid of him, and Sparta recalled him on charges of which Medism was the best founded and acquitted him of it.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 18: The hero of Plataea, and the reason the confederacy changed hands. Sent the King his captured kinsmen with a cover story and a letter offering to marry his daughter and make Sparta and Hellas subject to him; came back from Byzantium in Median dress with a Persian table and a bodyguard of Medes and Egyptians. He had put his own couplet on the tripod at Delphi and had it erased, was intriguing with the Helots with promises of freedom and citizenship, and was undone by his own courier, who counterfeited the seal and found the order for his death. He ran for the temple of the goddess of the Brazen House, was walled in, and was carried out to die on the threshold; Delphi made Sparta move the tomb and give the goddess two bronze statues for the one body.

## Cleombrotus · reference

Pausanias the regent’s father, and Nicomedes’s.

## Dorkis · reference

Sent out with a few others and a small force to replace Pausanias, and found the allies no longer willing to grant Lacedaemon the supremacy. He left, and Sparta sent no one after him.

## Hellanicus · reference

Who touched on the fifty years between the wars in his Athenian history — briefly, and not accurately in his dates. The only predecessor Thucydides names.

## Inaros · supporting

Psammetichus’s son, a Libyan king of the Libyans on the Egyptian border with his headquarters at Marea, who raised almost all Egypt against Artaxerxes and called in the Athenians — and who was betrayed, captured and crucified.

## Psammetichus · reference

Inaros’s father.

## Artaxerxes · supporting

Xerxes’s son, newly come to the throne when Themistocles wrote to him; the king Egypt revolted from.

## Amyrtaeus · reference

The king in the marshes, whom the Persians could not take because of the size of the marsh and because the marshmen are the most warlike of the Egyptians — and who afterwards got sixty of Cimon’s ships.

## Leocrates · reference

Stroebus’s son, who commanded the landing and the siege after the great sea-battle off Aegina.

## Stroebus · reference

Leocrates’s father.

## Myronides · supporting

Who marched into the Megarid with the old men and the boys left in the city, fought the Corinthians to a draw, and when they came back twelve days later to set up their own trophy cut off the party setting it up and stoned a whole detachment to death in a walled field. Sixty-two days after Tanagra he beat the Boeotians at Oenophyta.

## Nicomedes · supporting

Cleombrotus’s son, acting for the boy king Pleistoanax, who brought fifteen hundred Lacedaemonians and ten thousand allies to the help of Doris and then had to sit in Boeotia deciding which way home — encouraged by a party in Athens that wanted the democracy and the Long Walls stopped.

## Pleistoanax · supporting

Pausanias’s son, king of the Lacedaemonians: a minor when Nicomedes acted for him, and in command himself of the invasion that reached Eleusis and Thrius and went no further.

After source chapter 6, paragraph 24: Pausanias’s son and king of Lacedaemon, whose invasion fourteen years earlier had turned back at Eleusis and Thria — which cost him his exile, since it was thought he had been bribed to retreat. While Archidamus’s army was on the same ground, the Athenians remembered it and still hoped he would go no further.

## Tolmides · supporting

Tolmaeus’s son, who sailed round the Peloponnese, burnt the Lacedaemonian dockyard, took Chalcis and beat the Sicyonians — and who was caught at Coronea on the way home from Chaeronea by the Boeotian exiles, and lost Boeotia with the battle.

## Tolmaeus · reference

Tolmides’s father.

## Megabazus · reference

The Persian sent to Lacedaemon with money to buy an invasion of Attica and draw the Athenians out of Egypt, and recalled with what was left of it when the money was simply being wasted. Not Megabuzus.

## Megabuzus · supporting

Zopyrus’s son, sent into Egypt with a large army instead: he beat the Egyptians in the field, drove the Hellenes out of Memphis, penned them on Prosopitis for a year and a half, and then drained the canal, left their ships dry, and marched across on foot. Not Megabazus.

## Zopyrus · reference

Megabuzus’s father.

## Orestes · reference

Echecratidas’s son, the Thessalian king in exile who persuaded Athens to restore him — and went home with them again, the expedition having accomplished nothing because of the Thessalian cavalry.

## Echecratidas · reference

Orestes’s father.

## Pericles · central

Xanthippus’s son, who took a thousand men from Pegae to Sicyon and Acarnania, brought the army back from Euboea when Megara revolted and then subdued Euboea outright, and commanded the forty-four ships that beat the Samians off Tragia.

After source chapter 5, paragraph 30: Xanthippus’s son, the most powerful man of his time and the foremost Athenian statesman, ablest alike in counsel and in action. He was connected with the Cylonian curse on his mother’s side, which is why Sparta’s first embassy demanded the accursed be driven out — not expecting to succeed, but to make his countrymen think the war his bad luck. He answered the ultimatum with no concession: the trifle of the Megara decree, he said, contains the whole trial of their resolve; Athens should think of herself as an island, guard the sea and the city, and mourn the loss of men rather than of houses and land, because men gain houses and land and not the reverse. What he feared, he said, was Athenian blunders rather than the enemy’s designs.

After source chapter 7, paragraph 21: The first citizen of Athens, whose funeral speech for the first of the war’s dead calls the city the school of Hellas and says that heroes have the whole earth for their tomb. He would not let the army out while Archidamus burned Acharnae, would not call an assembly while anger was in the ascendant, and handed over his own estate to the state in case Archidamus spared it out of friendship. They fined him, and then elected him general again. He outlived the outbreak by two years and six months, and told them to wait quietly, attend to the navy, attempt no new conquests and risk nothing — advice they did the opposite of. What was nominally a democracy became in his hands government by the first citizen.

## Xanthippus · reference

Pericles’s father.

## Pissuthnes · supporting

Hystaspes’s son, satrap of Sardis, who took the Athenian garrison and its commanders off the Samian rebels.

## Hystaspes · reference

Pissuthnes’s father.

## Stesagoras · reference

Who left Samos with five ships to bring up the Phoenician fleet — which is why Pericles was off Caria when the Samians broke out and held their own waters for fourteen days.

## Hagnon · supporting

One of the three commanders of the forty ships that reinforced the siege of Samos.

After source chapter 7, paragraph 14: Nicias’s son, who took Pericles’s own force to the Chalcidians and Potidaea and brought the plague with him: he lost one thousand and fifty out of four thousand heavy infantry in about forty days, achieved nothing worthy of his preparations, and took his ships home. He went out again with Sitalces’s expedition as the Athenian general — for a fleet that never came.

## Tlepolemus · reference

Who brought twenty more ships to Samos with Anticles.

## Anticles · reference

Tlepolemus’s colleague.

## Cylon · major

An Olympic victor of good birth and powerful position, married to a daughter of Theagenes of Megara, who asked at Delphi and was told to seize the Acropolis at the grand festival of Zeus. He took it during the Olympic festival in the Peloponnese, never asking which festival the god meant and never being told — the Athenians have a grand festival of Zeus Meilichios of their own, the Diasia. He and his brother got away; the rest starved at the altar, were raised on a promise of no harm, and were killed. The men who killed them were the accursed, they and their descendants, and driving them out was the first thing Sparta demanded of Athens before the war.

## Theagenes · supporting

Tyrant of Megara, whose daughter Cylon married and whose force he used.

## Cleomenes · supporting

Of Lacedaemon, who with an Athenian faction drove the accursed out a second time: the living expelled, the bones of the dead taken up.

## Gongylus · supporting

The Eretrian whom Pausanias put in charge of Byzantium and the captured kinsmen of the King, and who carried the letter offering to make Sparta and Hellas subject to Persia.

## Artabazus · supporting

Pharnaces’s son, sent down to the sea to replace Megabates in the satrapy of Daskylion, to show Pausanias the royal seal, and to carry the King’s business with him with all care and fidelity.

## Pharnaces · reference

Artabazus’s father.

## Megabates · reference

The governor of the satrapy of Daskylion whom Artabazus was sent to replace.

## Pleistarchus · reference

Leonidas’s son, the boy king Pausanias was regent for, and his first cousin.

## Leonidas · reference

Pleistarchus’s father.

## Admetus · supporting

The Molossian king, not on friendly terms with Themistocles, who was out when Themistocles arrived; his wife told the suppliant to take their child in his arms and sit by the hearth. He raised him up with the boy still in his arms, refused the Lacedaemonians whatever they said, and sent him overland to the other sea.

## Ramphias · supporting

First-named of the three ambassadors who brought the Lacedaemonian ultimatum: not a word on any of the old subjects, only that Lacedaemon wished the peace to continue and there was no reason it should not, if Athens would leave the Hellenes independent.

## Melesippus · supporting

The second of the three ambassadors.

## Agesander · reference

The third of the three ambassadors.

## Zeus · supporting

Whose grand festival Cylon was told to wait for and did not identify — the Athenians have one of their own, to Zeus Meilichios the Gracious, kept outside the city at the Diasia with bloodless offerings. It is his suppliant at Ithome that an old oracle told Lacedaemon to release.

## Hera · supporting

In whose temple at Corcyra the Epidamnian ambassadors sat as suppliants and were dismissed empty-handed.

## Apollo · major

The god at Delphi, who told Epidamnus to hand the city to Corinth, told Cylon to take the Acropolis at the grand festival of Zeus without saying which, told Lacedaemon that if they put their whole strength into the war victory would be theirs and that he himself would be with them invoked or not, and ordered Pausanias’s tomb moved to the place of his death. His temple stands at Actium, and Polycrates gave him Rhenea.

## Poseidon · supporting

From whose temple at Taenarus the Lacedaemonians raised the Helot suppliants and led them away and killed them — for which they believe the great earthquake at Sparta was retribution, and which the Athenians told them to drive out as a curse of their own.

## The Athenians · central

Whose growth in power, and the alarm it inspired in Lacedaemon, Thucydides gives as the real cause of the war — the cause most kept out of sight in formal speech. Never displaced from Attica because the soil was poor, they laid aside their arms first, took to the sea after the Mede, and turned the confederacy into an empire.

After source chapter 7, paragraph 10: Whose country population came into the city and camped in the temples and the towers, and among whom the plague then broke out — fever, thirst, and a despair that took away the power of resistance the moment a man felt himself sickening. The doctors died thickest, because they visited the sick most. Men who had nothing succeeded to the property of men who had everything, and spent it quickly; burial rites collapsed; and nobody expected to live to be tried for anything. An old verse said a Dorian war would come and a plague with it, and they argued about whether the word had been plague or dearth.

## The Lacedaemonians · central

Slow to go to war except under necessity, with the same form of government for more than four hundred years and no tyrants; they put down the tyrannies of the rest of Hellas, led the alliance against the Mede, and exacted no tribute from their allies, only oligarchies. The Corinthians tell them to their faces that they are the only Hellenes who are inactive, and who defend themselves not by doing anything but by looking as though they might.

## The Peloponnesians · major

Farmers without funds private or public, without experience of long wars overseas, and without the single council-chamber that prompt action needs — a diet of many peoples in which each state has an equal vote and pushes its own ends, so that nothing is done. That is Pericles’s account of them; their own is that they have numbers, experience, obedience, and the temple funds at Olympia and Delphi to hire away Athenian sailors with.

## The Corinthians · major

Set on an isthmus and a commercial hub from time immemorial, the first to build galleys in Hellas and the first Hellenes to fight a sea-battle. They took over Epidamnus from a colony that despised them, lost at Leukimme, claimed the victory at Sybota, and canvassed the Peloponnese city by city for the war — speaking last at both congresses, after letting the other speakers inflame the room.

## The Corcyraeans · major

Wealthy enough to stand comparison with the richest in Hellas, proud of a naval reputation they trace to the Phaeacians, and so placed that foreign ships must put in at them while they need not sail to anyone. They refused their own colonists, refused their mother city arbitration only after the siege was laid, and came to Athens with the offer of a hundred and twenty galleys and an argument about where the coming war would be fought.

## The Hellenes · major

Thucydides’s word for the Greeks as one people: the name did not exist before Hellen, Homer never uses it of all of them, and it was long in attaching itself to the whole. All of them were drawn into this war sooner or later, though some at first stayed neutral.

## The Potidaeans · major

A Corinthian colony on the isthmus of Pallene and tributary allies of Athens, ordered to raze the wall facing Pallene, give hostages and dismiss the Corinthian magistrates — and who revolted instead, with the Chalcidians and Bottiaeans, on a Lacedaemonian promise to invade Attica.

## The Epidamnians · supporting

Whose city on the Ionic gulf grew large and then tore itself apart: the commons expelled the nobles, the nobles joined the barbarians and plundered them by sea and land, and their embassy to Corcyra was dismissed empty-handed from the temple of Hera. Delphi told them to give the city to Corinth.

## The Taulantians · reference

An Illyrian people, the barbarian neighbours of Epidamnus.

## The Illyrians · reference

Whose help the Corcyraeans enlisted for the siege of Epidamnus.

## The Ambraciots · supporting

Who sent volunteer settlers and troops to Epidamnus, eight ships for the convoy and twenty-seven for the fleet, and held the right wing with the Megarians at Sybota.

## The Leucadians · supporting

Whose city was a Corinthian colony: they sent settlers to Epidamnus and ten ships twice, and had their territory ravaged by the Corcyraeans for it.

## The Megarians · major

Who left the Lacedaemonian alliance over a boundary war Corinth forced on them, were given long walls and an Athenian garrison, revolted and cut the garrison off — and who came to the congress with a long list of grievances, chief of them exclusion from the ports of the empire and the market of Athens. The decree excluding them was the one thing that could still have averted the war.

## The Aeginetans · supporting

Who sent no formal representatives to the congress for fear of Athens and were in private among the most insistent advocates for war, claiming Athens had denied them the independence the treaty guaranteed.

After source chapter 6, paragraph 30: Expelled from Aegina with their wives and children in the first summer of the war, as the chief agents in bringing it on — and because the island lay too close to the Peloponnese to be left in their hands. Lacedaemon gave them Thyrea, partly out of the obligation they had put her under at the time of the earthquake and the Helot revolt; the rest scattered over Hellas.

## The Samians · major

For whom Ameinocles built the first four ships, and who went to war with Miletus over Priene, were given a democracy, stole back their hostages from Lemnos, revolted, and held their own waters for fourteen days before a nine-month siege ended it.

## The Milesians · supporting

Beaten by Samos over Priene, and the complainants who brought Athens into the Samian war.

## The Thebans · major

Asked for money toward the Epidamnus convoy; and the people whose city had given the Mede his base of operations, which is the argument Sparta used against walls.

After source chapter 6, paragraph 5: Whose Boeotarchs took three hundred men into Plataea in peacetime, because they foresaw the war and wanted to surprise an old enemy before it began. They would not kill the opposite faction as the traitors wanted, made a conciliatory proclamation instead, and lost the whole party. The relieving force was held up by a river swollen with the night’s rain and arrived to find them dead.

## The Phliasians · reference

Asked for money toward the convoy.

## The Eleans · supporting

Asked for hulls as well as money; the Corcyraeans burnt Cyllene, their harbour, for it.

## The Sicyonians · supporting

Whose envoys went with the Corcyraeans to Corinth, who were beaten by Pericles at Sicyon and by Tolmides before him, and who were let into Megara before the revolt.

## The Chalcidians · supporting

Of the Thracian seaboard, who joined the Potidaean revolt and, at Perdiccas’s urging, demolished their own towns and moved inland to Olynthus.

## The Bottiaeans · reference

Who joined the revolt with the Chalcidians and the Potidaeans.

## The Macedonians · supporting

Perdiccas’s people, whose six hundred horse followed Philip and Pausanias on the Athenian side at Potidaea and then rode back to the Athenians when the battle was decided.

## The Thracians · supporting

Who cut the ten thousand Athenian settlers off at Drabescus, taking the settlement at Nine Ways for an act of war.

## The Carians · supporting

Great pirates who colonized most of the islands, expelled from the Cyclades by Minos; more than half the graves opened when Athens purified Delos were theirs, known by the weapons and the manner of burial they still use.

## The Phoenicians · supporting

Islanders and pirates with the Carians; afterwards the King’s navy, which reduced the islands for Darius, lost two hundred ships at the Eurymedon, and was always expected and sometimes never came.

## The Pelasgians · reference

The name the country chiefly went by before Hellen, and the stock the Greek language branched off from.

## The Boeotians · major

Driven out of Arne by the Thessalians sixty years after Ilium and settled in the Cadmeis; beaten at Oenophyta and masters again after Coronea. Homer gives their ships a hundred and twenty men each, the maximum in the catalogue.

## The Thessalians · supporting

Who drove the Boeotians out of Arne; whose cavalry went over to the Lacedaemonians during the battle at Tanagra; and whose horse kept the Athenians inside their camp at Pharsalus.

## The Dorians · supporting

Who with the Heraclids became masters of the Peloponnese twenty years after the Boeotian resettlement, and whose settlement in Lacedaemon was followed by faction for an unparalleled length of time.

## The Ionians · major

The colonies Athens sent out when Attica grew too small, whose golden grasshoppers and linen came from Athens; they built naval strength under Cyrus, were the first to ask Athens to take the lead against Pausanias, and are the people a Dorian city is being besieged by, which the Corinthians call a complete reversal of the natural order.

## The Heraclids · supporting

Who killed Eurystheus in Attica and, with the Dorians, became masters of the Peloponnese; Phalius the founder of Epidamnus was of the family.

## The Mycenaeans · reference

Who feared the Heraclids and wanted Atreus to take the sceptre.

## The Trojans · supporting

Who held the field for ten years because the invaders were always dispersed for piracy and farming, and were always a match for the detachment left on duty.

## The Medes · major

The invader of the two invasions, and the measure Thucydides sets this war against: the Median War was the greatest event of past times and was settled in two sea-battles and two land-battles. The rock the barbarian was wrecked on, the Corinthians tell Sparta, was himself.

## The Persians · supporting

Whose grandees’ quarters were the few houses left standing in Athens, and who held the White Castle at Memphis and Sestos and Byzantium until they were taken.

## The Phocians · supporting

Who made an expedition against Doris, the original homeland of the Lacedaemonians, and were made to give the town back; Athens put the temple at Delphi into their hands.

## The Locrians · supporting

The Ozolian Locrians, who still carry weapons in the old piratical way and from whom Athens took Naupactus; and the Opuntian Locrians, a hundred of whose richest men were taken as hostages after Oenophyta.

## The Aetolians · reference

Who still live in the old way, with the custom of carrying arms kept up.

## The Acarnanians · supporting

Who still live in the old way, like the Aetolians and the Ozolian Locrians.

After source chapter 8, paragraph 12: Invaded by land and threatened by sea, they made no combined resistance but stayed to defend their own homes — and the Stratians alone broke the Chaonians with ambushes and then slung at the Hellenic divisions from a distance, which is the kind of warfare Thucydides says they excel at.

## The Carthaginians · reference

Beaten in a sea-fight by the Phocaeans while they were founding Marseilles.

## The Phocaeans · reference

Who beat the Carthaginians at sea while founding Marseilles.

## The Egyptians · supporting

Most of whom revolted from Artaxerxes under Inaros and were beaten back into subjection — except the marshmen, who are the most warlike of them and whose king in the marshes could not be taken.

## The Libyans · reference

Inaros’s people, on the Egyptian border.

## The Helots · major

Most of them descendants of the old Messenians enslaved in the famous war, which is why they all came to be called Messenians. They seceded to Ithome after the earthquake; Pausanias promised them freedom and citizenship to join him; and the suppliants raised from the temple of Poseidon at Taenarus and killed were Helots.

## The Perioeci · reference

The Thuriats and Aethaeans among them seceded to Ithome with the Helots.

## The Messenians · supporting

The name all the Helots came to carry, because most of them descended from the Messenians enslaved in the famous war; the rebels at Ithome held out ten years and were let out of the Peloponnese on condition of never setting foot in it again.

After source chapter 8, paragraph 20: Settled by Athens at Naupactus after Ithome, and now the garrison that marched along the shore beside Phormio’s ships — and that waded into the sea in armour to board the disabled Athenian hulls the Peloponnesians were towing away, and took them back.

## The Thuriats · reference

Of the Perioeci, who seceded to Ithome.

## The Aethaeans · reference

Of the Perioeci, who seceded to Ithome with the Thuriats.

## The Edonians · reference

From whom Athens took Nine Ways, and at whose town of Drabescus the settlers were cut off.

## The Thasians · supporting

Who revolted over the trading posts on the Thracian coast and the mine, were beaten at sea and besieged, appealed to Lacedaemon, and gave up walls, ships, mainland possessions and the mine in the third year.

## The Dolopians · reference

The population of Scyros, enslaved by Cimon.

## The Cyprians · reference

Beaten with the Phoenicians and Cilicians off Salamis in Cyprus.

## The Cilicians · reference

Beaten with the Phoenicians and Cyprians off Salamis in Cyprus.

## The Argives · supporting

Sparta’s enemy, whom the Athenians allied with the moment they were sent home from Ithome, and a thousand of whom marched with them to Tanagra. Themistocles in exile lived among them and kept secret hoards there.

## The Achaeans · supporting

One of Homer’s names for the Greeks; and the people Pericles picked up on the way from Sicyon to Acarnania.

## The Danaans · reference

One of Homer’s three names for the Greeks, with the Argives and the Achaeans.

## The Delphians · reference

Into whose hands Lacedaemon put the temple in the sacred war, and out of whose hands Athens took it again.

## The Histiaeans · reference

Alone of Euboea not settled by agreement: Athens expelled them from their homes and took the territory.

## The Byzantines · reference

Who revolted with the Samians and agreed to be subjects again, as before.

## The Sermylians · reference

Many of whom Aristeus cut off in an ambush near their city.

## The Zacynthians · reference

A thousand of whose heavy infantry came to help the Corcyraeans and were posted on Point Leukimme.

## The Molossians · reference

Admetus’s people, with whom Themistocles took sanctuary.

## The Eretrians · reference

Whose old war with Chalcis was the nearest thing to a coalition in Hellas before the Median War; and Gongylus was one of them.

## The Arcadians · reference

Whose part of the Peloponnese was poor enough to be left alone by the changes of masters, and whose ships at Troy were supplied by Agamemnon.

## The Epidaurians · supporting

Who sent five ships for the Epidamnus convoy, beat the Athenians with the Corinthians at Haliae, and were let into Megara before the revolt.

## The Hermionians · reference

Who sent one ship for the convoy; Pausanias took a galley of theirs on his own responsibility for his second voyage out.

## The Troezenians · reference

Who sent two ships for the convoy.

## The Chians · supporting

Who with the Lesbians alone were never deprived of their ships and made to pay money instead, and who sent twenty-five ships to the siege of Samos.

## The Lesbians · supporting

Who with the Chians alone kept their ships.

## The Phaeacians · reference

Corcyra’s old inhabitants, from whom the Corcyraeans date the naval reputation they were at times openly proud of.

## The Tanagraeans · reference

Whose walls the Athenians dismantled after Oenophyta.

## The Argilians · reference

One of them was Pausanias’s favourite and most trusted servant, and the courier who counterfeited the seal, found the postscript ordering his own death, and turned informer.

## Chrysis · supporting

The priestess of Hera at Argos, in the forty-eighth year of whose office the war began. Thucydides dates the outbreak by her, by the ephor at Sparta and by the archon at Athens, because there was no common calendar. Not Eumachus’s father.

## Aenesias · reference

The ephor at Sparta in whose year the war began.

## Pythodorus · reference

The archon at Athens in the last month but two of whose year the war began.

## Pythangelus · supporting

Phyleides’s son, one of the two Boeotarchs who led the three hundred Thebans into Plataea in the first watch of the night.

## Phyleides · reference

Pythangelus’s father.

## Diemporus · supporting

Onetorides’s son, the other Boeotarch of the night attack.

## Onetorides · reference

Diemporus’s father.

## Naucleides · supporting

The Plataean who opened the gates to the Thebans, meaning to kill the opposite faction, hand the city to Thebes and hold power in it.

## Eurymachus · supporting

Leontiades’s son, a man of great influence at Thebes, through whom the traitors in Plataea had made their arrangements — and one of the hundred and eighty prisoners the Plataeans put to death.

## Leontiades · reference

Eurymachus’s father.

## Diacritus · reference

Melesippus’s father.

## Zeuxidamus · reference

Archidamus’s father. The text calls him Zeuxis once and Zeuxidamus twice.

## Cecrops · reference

Under whom, and the first kings after him, Attica was a country of independent townships.

## Theseus · major

The king of equal intelligence and power who abolished the council-chambers and magistrates of the smaller Attic cities and merged them into one council-chamber and town hall at Athens, leaving a great state behind him. The Synoecia, the Feast of Union, is still kept at public expense for it.

## Eumolpus · reference

Who led the Eleusinians against Erechtheus — one of the wars the Attic townships fought against their own king.

## Erechtheus · reference

The king of Athens the Eleusinians went to war with under Eumolpus.

## Polymedes · reference

One of the two party leaders in Larisa who commanded the Larisaean horse that came to Athens under the old alliance.

## Aristonus · reference

The other Larisaean party leader and commander.

## Menon · reference

The Pharsalian general of the Thessalian contingent.

## Carcinus · supporting

Xenotimus’s son, one of the three commanders of the hundred ships sent round the Peloponnese in the first summer of the war.

## Xenotimus · reference

Carcinus’s father.

## Socrates · reference

Antigenes’s son, the third commander of the hundred ships.

## Antigenes · reference

Socrates’s father.

## Brasidas · major

Tellis’s son. In command of a guard in Laconia when the Athenians assaulted Methone, he ran a hundred heavy infantry straight through an army scattered over the country, lost a few men getting in, and saved the place — the first officer in the war to be publicly commended at Sparta. He was sent out afterwards as one of the three commissioners to stiffen Cnemus’s fleet, and was in the party that talked itself out of the raid on Piraeus. The older translation misprints him Bradidas once.

## Tellis · reference

Brasidas’s father.

## Cleopompus · supporting

Clinias’s son, who took thirty ships round Locris and Euboea, sacked places on the coast, took hostages from Thronium and beat the Locrians at Alope — and afterwards went with Hagnon to Potidaea and the Chalcidians.

## Clinias · reference

Cleopompus’s father.

## Nymphodorus · supporting

Pythes’s son, an Abderite whose sister was married to Sitalces. Athens had counted him an enemy and now made him her proxenus and sent for him: he brought in the Thracian alliance, got Sitalces’s son Sadocus made an Athenian citizen, and reconciled Athens with Perdiccas.

## Pythes · reference

Nymphodorus’s father.

## Sitalces · major

Teres’s son and king of the Thracians, whose alliance Athens bought with a citizenship for his son and the return of Therme to Perdiccas. He came down into Macedonia with a hundred and fifty thousand men, a third of them horse, and frightened every people between Thermopylae and the Danube — and then, with no provisions and bad weather, took his nephew’s advice and went home after thirty days, having achieved none of it.

## Teres · supporting

Sitalces’s father, the first to build the great Odrysian kingdom out of a Thrace that was mostly independent. Thucydides is at pains to say he is in no way related to the Tereus of the nightingale story: different part of Thrace, different name.

## Tereus · supporting

Who married Pandion’s daughter Procne and lived at Daulis in what is now Phocis. It was there that the women did the deed on Itys, which is why the poets call the nightingale the Daulian bird. Not Teres.

## Pandion · reference

Procne’s father, who would have weighed the advantages of mutual help in marrying her — an argument Thucydides uses to keep Tereus out of Odrysian Thrace.

## Procne · reference

Pandion’s daughter, whom Tereus married out of Athens.

## Itys · reference

On whom the women of Daulis perpetrated the outrage.

## Sadocus · supporting

Sitalces’s son, made an Athenian citizen — and who repaid it by seizing the Lacedaemonian envoys on their way to the King and handing them to the Athenians, who killed them the same day.

## Evarchus · supporting

Tyrant of Astacus, expelled by the hundred ships and put back by forty Corinthian ones the following winter, partly with mercenaries he hired himself.

## Euphamidas · supporting

Aristonymus’s son, first-named of the three commanders of the forty Corinthian ships that restored Evarchus.

## Aristonymus · reference

Euphamidas’s father.

## Timoxenus · reference

Timocrates’s son, the second commander of the forty ships.

## Timocrates · reference

Timoxenus’s father. Not the Lacedaemonian commissioner who killed himself off Naupactus.

## Eumachus · reference

Chrysis’s son, the third commander of the forty ships.

## Chrysis · reference

Eumachus the Corinthian’s father. Not the priestess of Hera at Argos.

## The Acharnians · supporting

Of the largest of the Athenian demes, three thousand heavy infantry of them — which is why Archidamus camped on their land and waited, reckoning they would force a battle on the rest of the city; and they were the loudest in pressing for the sortie.

## The Oropians · reference

Who hold Graea from Athens, and whose territory the Peloponnesians ravaged on the way home.

## The Cranians · reference

Of Crane in Cephallenia, who agreed to terms with the Corinthians and then fell on them suddenly.

## The Pellenians · reference

Of the one Achaean city that joined the war from the start, the rest following her lead later.

## Nicias · reference

Hagnon’s father. Neither the Cretan of Gortys nor the Athenian general of the later books.

## Aneristus · reference

One of the envoys Lacedaemon sent to the King for money, seized in Thrace by Sadocus and killed at Athens the same day without a trial.

## Nicolaus · reference

A second of the Lacedaemonian envoys to the King, killed with the rest.

## Stratodemus · reference

A third of the Lacedaemonian envoys, killed with the rest.

## Timagoras · reference

A Tegean travelling with the Lacedaemonian envoys to the King, and killed with them.

## Pollis · reference

A private individual from Argos travelling with the envoys, and killed with them.

## Pharnabazus · reference

The Persian to whom the envoys were to be conveyed across the Hellespont, and who would have sent them up country to the King.

## Learchus · supporting

Callimachus’s son, one of the two Athenian ambassadors at Sitalces’s court who talked his son into handing over the Lacedaemonian envoys, and who escorted them to Athens.

## Callimachus · reference

Learchus’s father. Not Phanomachus’s father.

## Ameiniades · supporting

Philemon’s son, the other Athenian ambassador at Sitalces’s court.

## Philemon · reference

Ameiniades’s father.

## Amphilochus · supporting

Amphiaraus’s son, who came home from Troy dissatisfied with the state of affairs, built a city in the Ambracian gulf and named it Argos after his own country.

## Amphiaraus · reference

Amphilochus’s father, and Alcmaeon’s. The older translation misprints him Amphiraus once.

## Melesander · supporting

Who took six ships to Caria and Lycia to collect tribute and keep the Peloponnesian privateers from setting up there — and was defeated and killed going up country into Lycia.

## Xenophon · supporting

Euripides’s son, first-named of the three Athenian generals who took Potidaea’s capitulation, and afterwards the commander beaten and killed at Spartolus.

## Euripides · reference

Xenophon the general’s father.

## Hestiodorus · reference

Aristocleides’s son, the second of the three generals at Potidaea.

## Aristocleides · reference

Hestiodorus’s father.

## Phanomachus · reference

Callimachus’s son, the third of the three generals at Potidaea — blamed at home, with his colleagues, for granting terms without instructions.

## Callimachus · reference

Phanomachus’s father. Not Learchus’s father.

## Cnemus · major

The Spartan high admiral: he ravaged Zacynthus and got nowhere, slipped past Phormio into the gulf with a thousand heavy infantry and a barbarian army, lost the Chaonians to an ambush at Stratus, and had three commissioners sent out in anger to stiffen him after his fleet was beaten in the Crissaean gulf.

## Photys · reference

One of the two members of the Chaonian royal family entrusted with the chieftainship for the year; the Chaonians have no king.

## Nicanor · reference

The other Chaonian chieftain of the year.

## Sabylinthus · reference

Guardian of the boy king Tharyps, who led the Molossians and Atintanians into Acarnania.

## Tharyps · reference

The Molossian king, still a minor, for whom Sabylinthus acted.

## Oroedus · reference

King of the Paravaei, who brought them into Acarnania and was given the thousand Orestians to command as well.

## Antiochus · reference

King of the Orestians, who sent a thousand of them under Oroedus rather than lead them himself. The older translation misprints him Antichus.

## Machaon · reference

One of the three Corinthian commanders of the forty-seven ships Phormio broke in the mouth of the Crissaean gulf.

## Isocrates · reference

The second Corinthian commander.

## Agatharchidas · reference

The third Corinthian commander.

## Timocrates · supporting

One of the three Lacedaemonian commissioners sent out in anger after the first defeat. He was aboard the Leucadian that an Athenian ship sank off a merchantman at Naupactus, killed himself when she went down, and was washed up in the harbour. Not Timoxenus’s father.

## Lycophron · reference

The third of the three Lacedaemonian commissioners, with Timocrates and Brasidas.

## Nicias · supporting

A Cretan of Gortys and the Athenians’ proxenus, who talked twenty ships out of joining Phormio and into attacking Cydonia — really to oblige the Polichnitans next door. Bad weather kept them in Crete through the battle.

## Amyntas · supporting

Philip’s son, whom Sitalces brought with him and intended for the throne of Macedonia; Idomene, Gortynia and Atalanta came over out of attachment to him.

## Seuthes · supporting

Spardacus’s son, Sitalces’s nephew and highest officer, and afterwards his successor, who raised the Odrysian tribute to four hundred talents. Perdiccas secretly bought him with the promise of his sister, and he advised the retreat; Perdiccas gave him Stratonice as promised.

## Spardacus · reference

Seuthes’s father.

## Stratonice · supporting

Perdiccas’s sister, promised to Seuthes with a rich dowry for the advice that ended Sitalces’s invasion, and given to him afterwards.

## Archelaus · supporting

Perdiccas’s son, who on his accession built most of the strongholds the country has, cut straight roads, and did more for the kingdom’s horses and heavy infantry than the eight kings before him together.

## Cynes · reference

Theolytus’s son, whom Phormio restored to Coronta.

## Theolytus · reference

Cynes’s father.

## Alcmaeon · supporting

Amphiaraus’s son, told by Apollo in his wanderings after killing his mother that he would have no release until he found land that had not been seen by the sun at the time of the murder. He settled on the Achelous’s new deposits by Oeniadae and named the country after his son Acarnan.

## Acarnan · reference

Alcmaeon’s son, from whom Acarnania is named.

## The Odrysians · major

Teres’s kingdom and Sitalces’s, whose empire ran from Abdera to the Danube and inland thirteen days’ journey, and whose custom was the opposite of the Persian one: more disgrace in refusing a present than in asking for one and being refused. Nothing could be done among them without a present.

## The Chaonians · major

A nation with no king, led by two of the royal family for a year at a time, and with the highest reputation for courage on that part of the mainland. They would not wait to make camp at Stratus, rushed the town for the sole glory of it, and were cut to pieces from the city and the ambushes.

## The Thesprotians · reference

Who came with the Chaonians into Acarnania and, like them, have no king.

## The Atintanians · reference

Whom Sabylinthus led into Acarnania with the Molossians.

## The Paravaeans · reference

Who came under their own king Oroedus.

## The Orestians · reference

A thousand of them, subjects of King Antiochus, who put them under Oroedus’s command.

## The Amphilochians · supporting

Whose Argos was founded by Amphilochus and who learned their Hellenic speech from the Ambraciots they invited in — and were then expelled by them, upon which they joined the Acarnanians, called in Athens, and got the town back.

## The Agraeans · reference

Through whose country the Achelous runs on its way to the Acarnanian plain.

## The Stratians · supporting

Of the Acarnanian capital, who saw how the invading divisions had strung out, set ambushes round the town, broke the Chaonians between the city and the ambushes, and then slung at the Hellenes from a distance — which, Thucydides says, is the kind of warfare the Acarnanians excel at.

## The Getae · supporting

Beyond Haemus, who border on the Scythians and are armed like them, all mounted archers; they supplied more of Sitalces’s cavalry than anyone but the Odrysians.

## The Dii · supporting

The independent hill Thracian swordsmen of Mount Rhodope, the most warlike of Sitalces’s infantry, some of whom came as mercenaries and some as volunteers.

## The Agrianes · reference

One of the Paeonian tribes in Sitalces’s empire, on its edge, with the Strymon running through their country.

## The Laeaeans · reference

The Paeonian tribe at the inland limit of the Odrysian empire, where it ends and the independent Paeonians begin.

## The Paeonians · supporting

Some of them in the Odrysian empire and some independent; Sitalces cut his road to Macedonia through their timber on an earlier campaign against them.

## The Triballi · reference

Independent, and the neighbours of the Treres and Tilataeans.

## The Treres · reference

Who live north of Mount Scombrus and extend west as far as the Oskius.

## The Tilataeans · reference

Who live with the Treres north of Mount Scombrus.

## The Sintians · reference

Divided from the Paeonians by the desolate range of Cercine, which Sitalces crossed by a road he had cut himself.

## The Maedians · reference

On Sitalces’s left as he crossed the mountains into Paeonia.

## The Lyncestae · reference

Macedonians by blood and allies and dependants of their kindred, but with their own separate government.

## The Elimiots · reference

Like the Lyncestae, Macedonians by blood with a government of their own.

Expelled from Pieria by Alexander’s ancestors and settled at Phagres beyond the Strymon; the coast they left is still called the Pierian gulf.

## The Eordians · reference

Driven out of Eordia, most of them killed, a few still living around Physca.

## The Almopians · reference

Driven out of Almopia by the same kings.

## The Temenids · reference

From Argos, the stock the kings of Macedonia came from.

## The Magnetes · reference

Subject to the Thessalians, and among the peoples who made their preparations when they heard how large Sitalces’s army was.

## The Panaeans · reference

One of the independent Thracian peoples of the plains beyond the Strymon who feared Sitalces might be invited against them.

## The Odomanti · reference

Independent Thracians of the plains beyond the Strymon.

## The Droi · reference

Independent Thracians of the plains beyond the Strymon.

## The Dersaeans · reference

Independent Thracians of the plains beyond the Strymon.

## The Scythians · supporting

With whom, Thucydides says, no people in Europe can be compared, and no single nation in Asia is a match for them united — though they are not on a level with other races in general intelligence and the arts of civilized life.

## The Polichnitans · reference

Neighbours of the Cydonians, to oblige whom Nicias of Gortys got twenty Athenian ships diverted to Crete.

## The Cydonians · reference

Whose land the twenty ships and the Polichnitans laid waste instead of joining Phormio.

## The Anactorians · reference

Whose one ship joined the hundred and fifty at Sybota, and who marched into Acarnania with Cnemus and held the right with the Leucadians.

## The Tegeans · supporting

Of Tegea in Arcadia. Timagoras, who travelled with the Lacedaemonian envoys to the King and was killed with them at Athens, was one of them.

## The Sicilians · supporting

Whose tyrants had galleys before the Persian war; whose cities Lacedaemon ordered to build five hundred ships between them and to stay neutral meanwhile; and who in the end joined the enemies of Athens against her.

## The Abderites · reference

Nymphodorus, whose sister married Sitalces and who brought Athens the Thracian alliance, was one of them.

## The Plataeans · major

Thebes’s old enemy and Athens’s oldest ally. Three hundred Thebans came into their town in the first watch of a rainy night; they dug through their own party walls to join up unseen, barricaded the streets with wagons, drove the Thebans into the mud and the dark, and killed a hundred and eighty of them. Besieged two years later, they answered the mound with a wooden wall, tunnelled out its footings, built a crescent behind it, snapped the battering rams with beams hung on chains, and came within a wind of being burnt alive. The siege closed on four hundred of their own citizens, eighty Athenians, and a hundred and ten women to bake the bread.

## Athene · supporting

On whose statue there were forty talents of pure gold, all of it removable — which Pericles counted among the city’s resources for a last extremity, to be used and paid back. The other deities’ temples are in the citadel with hers.

## Demeter · supporting

Whose Eleusinian temple was one of the few places kept closed when the country population camped in the city.

## Dionysus · supporting

Of the Marshes, in whose honour the older Dionysia are still held in Anthesterion — by the Athenians and by their Ionian descendants.
