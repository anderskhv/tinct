# Paradise Lost: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Satan · central

The rebel archangel, once Lucifer, who leads a third of Heaven into war, is thrown into Hell, and crosses Chaos alone to ruin the new world. Milton gives him the poem’s best speeches and then shows what they cost.

## God · central

The Father, who foresees the fall, declares that Man fell by his own free will, and accepts the Son’s offer to die for him.

## The Son · central

The Son of God, who volunteers to become Man and die for him, drives the rebel angels out of Heaven in his chariot, makes the world in six days, and comes down to judge Adam and Eve and clothe them.

## Adam · central

The first man, made in God’s image and given everything except one tree. He eats knowing exactly what he is doing, because he will not be left without Eve.

## Eve · central

The first woman, made from Adam’s rib, who wants to work apart from him and meets the serpent alone. She eats first, and she is also the first of the two to ask for forgiveness.

## Beelzebub · major

Satan’s second, the first to answer him on the burning lake; he puts Satan’s own plan to the council as if it were his own.

## Moloch · major

The fiercest of the devils, who argues for open war — and, on earth, the horrid king smeared with the blood of children passed through fire in the valley of Hinnom.

## Belial · major

The devil who counsels ignoble ease in words that sound like reason; on earth, the spirit of the sons of Belial who fill the streets with riot.

## Mammon · major

The devil who proposes that Hell be mined and made comfortable; the least erected spirit that fell, who kept his eyes on the golden pavement even in Heaven.

## Mulciber · supporting

The architect of Pandemonium, thrown out of Heaven and remembered by the Greeks as the smith-god who fell all day and landed on Lemnos.

## Azazel · supporting

The tall cherub who unfurls Satan’s standard over the fallen host.

## Nisroch · supporting

Chief of the Principalities, who stands up in the rebel assembly to complain that pain has unmanned them.

## Adramelech · reference

A rebel angel beaten down in the war in Heaven.

## Asmadai · reference

A rebel angel overthrown beside Adramelech.

## Ariel · reference

A rebel angel scorched and overthrown by Abdiel on the second day of battle.

## Arioch · reference

A rebel angel overthrown beside Ariel.

## Ramiel · reference

A rebel angel whose violence Abdiel blasted.

## Asmodeus · reference

The demon of the Book of Tobit, driven off by a fish’s smoke; Milton uses him for a spirit less pleased with Eden’s scents than Satan was.

## Michael · major

The archangel who leads Heaven’s armies on the first day of the war and wounds Satan; afterwards he drives Adam and Eve out of Paradise and shows Adam the future first.

## Gabriel · major

Chief of the angelic guard at the gate of Paradise, who catches Satan squatting at Eve’s ear and faces him down.

## Raphael · major

The affable archangel sent to warn Adam; he tells the whole story of the war in Heaven and the Creation over an afternoon meal, and answers Adam’s questions about astronomy and about love.

## Uriel · major

Regent of the sun and the sharpest-sighted spirit in Heaven, who is nevertheless deceived by Satan disguised as a young cherub — hypocrisy, Milton says, is the one evil that walks invisible.

## Uzziel · reference

Gabriel’s next in power, sent to sweep the south of the garden.

## Ithuriel · supporting

One of the two cherubs who find Satan at Eve’s ear and make him resume his own shape with a touch of the spear.

## Zephon · supporting

Ithuriel’s companion, who tells Satan to his face that he is no longer recognisable as an angel.

## Zophiel · reference

The swiftest-winged of the cherubim, who brings warning that the rebel host is coming on again.

## Abdiel · major

The one seraph in Satan’s whole province who refuses him and walks out alone — faithful only he among the faithless — and who strikes the first blow of the war.

## Sin · major

Satan’s daughter, who sprang fully formed from his head in Heaven, bore him Death, and keeps the key of Hell-gate. Woman to the waist and serpent below, she is both the personage and the thing itself.

## Death · major

Satan’s son by his own daughter, and her rapist; a shape that is not a shape, black as night and crowned. Milton never lets the reader separate the figure at the gate from the sentence passed on Adam.

## Chaos · major

The Anarch old, who rules the abyss between Hell and Heaven and cheers Satan on because every new world costs him territory. Milton uses the one word for the ruler and for the region he rules.

## Night · supporting

Chaos’s sable-vested consort, eldest of things, whose standard Satan promises to raise again over the new world.

## Demogorgon · reference

A dreaded name among the powers of the abyss, spoken and not explained.

## Orcus · reference

One of the powers standing by the throne of Chaos.

## Ades · reference

One of the powers standing by the throne of Chaos; Hades under an older spelling.

## Discord · supporting

Daughter of Sin, who brings death among the animals by setting them against each other.

## Rumour · reference

One of the courtiers of Chaos, named with Chance, Tumult and Confusion.

## Tumult · reference

One of the courtiers of Chaos.

## Confusion · reference

One of the courtiers of Chaos, all of them embroiled together.

## Chemos · supporting

The obscene dread of Moab’s sons, worshipped from Aroar to Nebo; Peor was his other name, when he enticed Israel at Sittim.

## Baalim · reference

The general name of the male gods worshipped between the Euphrates and the brook of Egypt — spirits who can take either sex, or both.

## Ashtaroth · reference

The general name of the female gods of the same coast, for whose sake Israel often forsook its living strength.

## Astoreth · supporting

Queen of heaven with the crescent horns, called Astarte by the Phoenicians, to whom Sidonian virgins paid their vows by moonlight — and for whom Solomon built a temple.

## Thammuz · supporting

The god whose annual wound ran the river of Lebanon purple, and whose Syrian women wailed for him — the idolatry Ezekiel saw in the porch of the Temple.

## Dagon · supporting

The sea-monster god of the Philistines, man above and fish below, whose own image fell headlong before the Ark in his own temple.

## Rimmon · reference

The god of Damascus, who lost a leper and won a king in the same day.

## Osiris · reference

The chief of the Egyptian gods, worshipped in brutish forms — and remembered by Milton as the calf the Israelites made in Oreb.

## Isis · reference

One of the Egyptian gods named with Osiris and Orus.

## Orus · reference

One of the Egyptian gods named with Osiris and Isis; Horus under an older spelling.

## Belus · reference

A Babylonian god, named with Serapis for the magnificence Egypt and Assyria built for their idols.

## Serapis · reference

An Egyptian god, named with Belus in the same comparison.

## Titan · reference

Heaven’s first-born, whose birthright his younger brother Saturn seized — the first of the three usurpations Milton uses to make the Greek gods a family of rebels.

## Saturn · reference

The god who took the birthright from Titan and lost it in turn to his own son by Rhea; he fled over the Adriatic to Italy and the Celtic west.

## Jove · supporting

The youngest usurper, who drove out his father Saturn and reigned — a god by conquest, which is exactly Milton’s point. Called Jupiter and, in Libya, Ammon.

## Rhea · reference

Saturn’s consort and Jove’s mother.

## Ops · reference

Saturn’s consort under her Latin name, named in Eve’s catalogue of the gods who ruled Olympus before Jove.

## Javan · reference

The son of Japheth, from whose offspring the Ionian gods were held to descend.

## Bacchus · reference

The florid son whom Ammon hid with Amalthea from his stepdame’s eye; his revellers tore Orpheus apart, which is why Milton fears for his own voice.

## Pan · reference

The god of field and grove, whose dance with the Graces and the Hours led on the eternal Spring — a Paradise the real one still beats.

## Sylvanus · reference

A woodland god named with Pan for the bowers that never matched Eve’s.

## Faunus · reference

A woodland spirit named with the nymphs who never haunted Eden’s inner bower.

## Proserpine · supporting

The girl gathering flowers in the field of Enna when gloomy Dis gathered her — herself a fairer flower, and the cost of her was all her mother’s pain.

## Ceres · reference

Proserpine’s mother, who sought her through the whole world; Milton uses that search for the price of Eden.

## Dis · reference

The lord of the underworld who carried Proserpine off.

## Juno · reference

The wife on whom Jupiter smiles when he impregnates the clouds that shed May flowers.

## Neptune · reference

The sea god whose grudge, with Juno’s, troubled the Greeks and Aeneas in the older epics Milton means to outdo.

## Aphrodite · reference

Aeneas’s mother, named once when Milton lists the quarrels of the classical epics.

## Hermes · reference

The messenger god, whose charmed pipe put the hundred eyes of Argus to sleep and who brought Pandora to Japhet’s unwiser son; Milton also uses his name for quicksilver.

## Maia · reference

Hermes’s mother; Raphael descends in the likeness of her son.

## Flora · reference

The goddess of flowers, on whom Zephyrus breathes; Adam wakes Eve as gently.

## Zephyrus · reference

The west wind, whose breath on Flora is Milton’s measure of a gentle voice.

## Pomona · reference

The goddess of orchards, whose arbour Eve’s bower resembles, and who fled from Vertumnus.

## Vertumnus · reference

The god who pursued Pomona through every shape he could take.

## Pales · reference

The goddess of pastures, named with Pomona for what Eve most resembled.

## Diana · reference

The huntress goddess, whose train of nymphs Eve resembles at her gardening.

## Bellona · reference

The goddess of war, whose engines storm cities — a small thing, Milton says, beside the artillery of the angels.

## Urania · supporting

The Heavenly Muse Milton calls on from the first line, and names only in Book 7 — insisting even then that he means the meaning and not the name. She conversed with eternal Wisdom before the hills were made, which is why she and not one of the nine can tell him what happened in Heaven.

## The Muses · supporting

The nine of Olympus, whose haunts Milton still wanders even though his own Muse is not one of them; the one who was Orpheus’s mother could not save him.

## The Graces · reference

Attendants of beauty, knit with the Hours in Pan’s dance and waiting on Eve as on a queen.

## The Hours · reference

The circling Hours who wake the morning and unbar the gates of light.

## Eurynome · reference

With Ophion, the first ruler of Olympus in the older story, driven out by Saturn and Ops; Milton offers her as a rumour of Eve.

## Ophion · reference

The serpent the Greeks say ruled Olympus first — Milton’s hint that they were remembering Satan.

## Hymen · reference

The god of marriage, whose torches were lit for the first weddings Adam sees in his vision of the future.

## Leucothea · reference

The sea goddess of the dawn, who wakes and sheds her dew on the earth.

## Themis · reference

The goddess at whose shrine Deucalion and Pyrrha prayed to restore the human race after the flood.

## Argus · reference

The herdsman covered with eyes, all of which Hermes charmed asleep; the cherubim of the gate have more, and stay awake.

## Medusa · supporting

The Gorgon set to guard the ford of Lethe, so that no damned soul can reach the water that would let it forget.

## Gorgons · reference

Named with Hydras and Chimeras among the shapes that haunt the frozen continent of Hell — worse, Milton says, than fables ever invented.

## Hydras · reference

Named with Gorgons and Chimeras in the same catalogue.

## Chimeras · reference

Named last in the same catalogue: Gorgons, and Hydras, and Chimeras dire.

## The Furies · reference

The harpy-footed powers who haul the damned from fire to ice and back; Death stands as fierce as ten of them.

## Scylla · reference

The monster of the strait between Calabria and Sicily, whose hell-hounds Milton says were less abhorred than Sin’s.

## Charybdis · reference

The whirlpool Ulysses steered away from, at the cost of the other side.

## Typhon · reference

One of the giants who warred on Jove, kept in a den near Tarsus; Milton uses him to size Satan.

## Briareos · reference

The hundred-handed giant, named with Typhon in the same measurement.

## Leviathan · supporting

The hugest of living creatures, which a night-bound pilot mistakes for an island and moors to — Milton’s last and best image for the size of Satan.

## Behemoth · reference

The biggest of earth-born creatures, heaving himself out of the ground on the sixth day.

## Python · reference

The huge serpent bred out of the mud after the flood, which Satan outgrows in the hall of Pandemonium.

## Pegasus · reference

The winged horse whose flight Milton claims to have passed, and off whose rider he begs not to fall.

## Bellerophon · reference

The rider thrown from the winged horse for aiming too high, who wandered the Aleian field alone — Milton’s warning to himself.

## Hercules · reference

Called Alcides; crowned with conquest, he felt the poisoned robe and threw the man who brought it into the sea.

## Lichas · reference

The messenger Hercules hurled from the top of Oeta into the sea for bringing him the robe.

## Orpheus · supporting

The Thracian poet torn apart by Bacchus’s revellers while his Muse-mother could not defend him; Milton, blind and out of favour, asks not to end the same way.

## Pandora · reference

The woman the gods adorned and sent to Japhet’s unwiser son, to the ruin of mankind — a story Milton lays beside Eve’s and does not soften.

## Japhet · reference

The father of the unwiser son who accepted Pandora.

## Deucalion · reference

With Pyrrha, the survivor of the flood who prayed at the shrine of Themis to restore mankind; Adam and Eve praying together are the older pair.

## Pyrrha · reference

Deucalion’s wife, named with him for the same prayer.

## Circe · reference

The enchantress whose beasts came at her call; the animals of Eden came at Eve’s more readily.

## Cadmus · reference

Turned into a serpent in Illyria with Hermione — one of the shapes Milton says was less lovely than the one Satan chose.

## Hermione · reference

Cadmus’s wife, transformed with him.

## Cham · reference

Ham under his old name, whom the Gentiles called Ammon and the Libyans Jove.

## Amalthea · reference

Hidden by Ammon from his wife’s eye, with her son Bacchus, on the Nyseian isle.

## Achilles · reference

The hero whose wrath Milton names as the old epic subject his own exceeds.

## Turnus · reference

The rival whose fury over Lavinia Milton names in the same breath as Achilles’s wrath.

## Lavinia · reference

The bride Turnus lost.

## Aeneas · reference

Aphrodite’s son, whom Neptune and Juno hounded through the older epic.

## Alcinous · reference

The king whose gardens hosted Odysseus, and which Milton says were fabled where Eden was real.

## Odysseus · reference

The guest of Alcinous, and the sailor who shunned Charybdis; Milton names him under both his Greek and Latin names.

## Tantalus · reference

The man from whose lips the water always fled — as the water of Lethe flees the damned.

## Arthur · reference

Uther’s son, girt with British and Armoric knights; Milton names the romances he had once meant to write about and did not.

## Uther · reference

Arthur’s father, named for him.

## Charlemagne · reference

The emperor who fell with all his peerage at Fontarabbia — the last of the romance names Milton lays aside.

## Thamyris · reference

A blind poet of legend, named among the company Milton hopes to join.

## Maeonides · reference

Homer under the name of his supposed birthplace, named among the blind.

## Tiresias · reference

The blind prophet of Thebes, named with Phineus among the blind who saw.

## Phineus · reference

A blind prophet of the Argonaut story, named last in Milton’s list of the blind.

## Amram · reference

Moses’s father; the potent rod of Amram’s son is Milton’s figure for Satan’s spear calling up the fallen host like locusts.

## Moses · supporting

The shepherd on Oreb who first taught the chosen how the heavens and earth rose out of Chaos, and one of the two brothers sent to bring Israel out of Egypt.

## Aaron · reference

Moses’s brother, sent with him to Pharaoh; the twelve stones of his breastplate are Milton’s measure for the light of Heaven.

## Pharaoh · reference

The impious king over whose realm the locusts hung like night, and whose army the sea swallowed.

## Busiris · reference

The Egyptian king whom the Red Sea overthrew with his chivalry while he pursued the sojourners of Goshen.

## Solomon · supporting

The wisest heart in Israel, led by fraud to build a temple to Astoreth on the hill of scandal; later, the king who enshrined the Ark.

## Josiah · reference

The good king who drove the idols from their shrines and threw them into hell.

## Eli · reference

The priest whose sons turned atheist and filled the house of God with violence.

## Ezekiel · reference

The prophet who was led by vision to see the dark idolatries of alienated Judah in the sacred porch.

## Samson · supporting

The champion who rose from the treacherous lap of Delilah shorn of his strength — Milton’s image for Adam and Eve waking after the fall.

## Delilah · reference

The Philistine woman in whose lap Samson lost his hair and his strength.

## Tobit · reference

The blind man of the apocryphal book whose son Raphael travelled with.

## Tobias · reference

Tobit’s son, whose marriage Raphael secured against the demon Asmodeus.

## Abraham · supporting

The patriarch who leaves Ur and his gods on nothing but a call, and in whose seed all nations are to be blessed — the first of the figures Michael shows Adam.

## Isaac · reference

Abraham’s son, named in the line that runs down to Joshua.

## Jacob · supporting

The grandson who saw the ladder of angels at Luz while fleeing from Esau, and whom the angels met again at Mahanaim.

## Esau · reference

The brother Jacob was fleeing when he dreamed of the ladder.

## Joshua · supporting

The commander who leads Israel into Canaan and at whose word the sun stands still over Gibeon; Michael points out that his name is the name of Jesus.

## David · supporting

The shepherd king of the royal house from which the Messiah is promised to come.

## Noah · supporting

The one just man, who builds the ark and rides out the flood; Michael shows Adam the whole of it.

## Mary · supporting

The second Eve, hailed with the same word the angel used to the first; the Virgin Mother of the promised seed.

## Peter · reference

The keeper of the keys at Heaven’s wicket, seen in vision above the Paradise of Fools.

## John · reference

The evangelist who saw the angel standing in the sun.

## Dominic · reference

The founder whose habit the dying put on to be sure of Paradise — a fraud, Milton says, that blows into the Limbo of Vanity with the Franciscan robes.

## Israel · supporting

The chosen race, who forsook their living strength for Baalim and Ashtaroth and fell before despicable enemies.

## The Ammonites · reference

Moloch’s worshippers in Rabba and the watery plain.

## The Phoenicians · reference

Who called Astoreth by the name Astarte.

## The Canaanites · reference

The nation Israel skirts on the march out of Egypt, to avoid a war that might frighten them back into slavery.

## The Chaldeans · reference

The people of Ur, whose gods Abraham left behind.

## The Philistines · reference

Dagon’s people, in Gath and Ascalon and Accaron and Gaza.

## The Gentiles · reference

Milton’s word for the nations who kept the fallen angels’ names alive as gods.

## The Greeks · reference

Who honoured Mulciber as their smith-god, and whom Neptune and Juno hounded in the older epic.

## The Apostles · reference

On whom the Spirit was first poured, and who confounded their proudest persecutors.

## The Americans · reference

Whom Columbus found girt with feathers — Milton’s comparison for Adam and Eve in their first clothing.

## The named winds · supporting

Milton gives the winds their old names and lets them loose on the world after the fall: Boreas, Caecias, Argestes and Thrascias out of the north, Notus and Afer from the south, Eurus and Zephyr from east and west, with Sirocco and Libecchio. Before the fall, no wind blew hard.

## Galileo · supporting

The Tuscan artist with the optic glass, viewing the moon from Fesole — the only living person Milton names in the whole poem, and he names him twice.

## Empedocles · reference

Who leapt into Etna’s flames to be thought a god, and blows about the Limbo of Vanity for it.

## Cleombrotus · reference

Who leapt into the sea to reach the afterlife Plato had described, and ended in the same Limbo.

## Plato · reference

Whose Elysium Cleombrotus drowned himself to reach.

## Xerxes · reference

Who bridged the Hellespont to yoke the freedom of Greece and whipped the sea for breaking it — Milton’s comparison for Sin and Death bridging Chaos.

## Scipio · reference

Rome at its height, born of a woman a god was said to have visited.

## Olympias · reference

Alexander’s mother, visited in the same way; both stories are serpents, which is the point.

## Columbus · reference

Who found the Americans naked and feathered.

## Montezuma · reference

Whose seat at Mexico is one of the kingdoms Michael shows Adam from the hill.

## Atahualpa · reference

Whose richer seat at Cusco is named beside Montezuma’s.

## Tamerlane · reference

Whose throne at Samarkand is another of the empires in Adam’s view.
