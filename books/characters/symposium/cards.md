# Symposium: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Socrates · central

The Athenian philosopher attending Agathon’s banquet.

## Apollodorus · supporting

The narrator retelling the banquet conversation from Aristodemus’s account.

## Aristodemus · major

Socrates’ companion at the banquet, whose eyewitness account Apollodorus retells.

## Agathon · major

The tragic playwright hosting the banquet to celebrate his victory.

## Phaedrus · major

The guest whose wish to hear Love praised inspires the evening’s speeches.

## Pausanias · major

Agathon’s lover and one of the guests giving a speech about love.

## Eryximachus · major

The physician at the banquet, son of Acumenus, who proposes the order of speeches.

## Aristophanes · major

The comic playwright attending Agathon’s banquet.

## Diotima · major

The wise woman from Mantineia whose teaching about love Socrates recounts.

## Alcibiades · major

The Athenian statesman and commander, an admirer of Socrates, who joins the banquet.

## Apollodorus’s listener · supporting

The unnamed companion asking Apollodorus to retell the banquet conversation.

## Acumenus · reference

Eryximachus’s father, also a physician.

## Homer · reference

The ancient Greek epic poet repeatedly cited by the speakers.

## Euripides · reference

The Athenian tragic playwright quoted by the guests.

## Prodicus · reference

The sophist recalled for his prose treatment of Heracles.

## Hesiod · reference

The Greek poet whose account of the gods’ beginnings is cited.

## Parmenides · reference

The Greek philosopher and poet cited on the origins of Love.

## Acusilaus · reference

The early Greek prose writer who recorded myths and genealogies.

## Aeschylus · reference

The Athenian tragic playwright whose depiction of Achilles and Patroclus is disputed by Phaedrus.

## Aristogeiton · reference

The Athenian associated with Harmodius in the opposition to tyranny.

## Harmodius · reference

The Athenian associated with Aristogeiton in the opposition to tyranny.

## Heraclitus · reference

The Greek philosopher cited for harmony arising from opposing forces.

## Gorgias · reference

The celebrated teacher of rhetoric invoked in Socrates’ wordplay about Agathon’s speech.

## Lycurgus · reference

The traditional Spartan lawgiver, cited for the lasting value of his laws.

## Solon · reference

The Athenian lawgiver cited among creators of lasting civic achievements.

## Olympus · reference

The legendary flute musician associated with Marsyas, whose melodies Alcibiades discusses.

## Pericles · reference

The Athenian statesman and orator whom Alcibiades compares with Socrates.

## Laches · reference

The Athenian soldier and commander who retreats beside Socrates at Delium in Alcibiades’ account.

## Brasidas · reference

The Spartan commander used as an example of heroic courage.

## Charmides · reference

The son of Glaucon whom Alcibiades names among those drawn to Socrates.

## Glaucon · reference

Charmides’ father, named in Alcibiades’ account.

## Euthydemus · reference

The son of Diocles whom Alcibiades names among Socrates’ admirers.

## Diocles · reference

The father of Euthydemus.

## Aristotle · reference

The Greek philosopher cited in the translator’s references to the Politics.

## Alexander Pope · reference

The English poet whose translation of Homer supplies the quoted line.

## Agamemnon · reference

The commander of the Greek army at Troy, Menelaus’s brother.

## Menelaus · reference

The Greek king of Sparta and brother of Agamemnon in the Homeric comparison.

## Dionysus · reference

The Greek god of wine and theater, invoked as judge of the poets’ and speakers’ contest.

## Melanippe · reference

The mythological heroine whose words in a play by Euripides Eryximachus borrows.

## Eros (Love) · reference

The figure of Love whose nature and power the guests debate.

## Heracles · reference

The Greek hero recalled in Prodicus’s moral tale and in Alcibiades’ exclamation.

## Aphrodite · reference

The Greek goddess of love and beauty.

## Chaos · reference

The primordial void in Hesiod’s account of the beginnings of the gods.

## Earth · reference

The primordial Earth, or Gaia, in the quoted account of the gods’ beginnings.

## Alcestis · reference

The wife of Admetus and daughter of Pelias, offered as an example of devoted love.

## Pelias · reference

Alcestis’s father, a king in Greek myth.

## Admetus · reference

Alcestis’s husband in the myth recalled by the speakers.

## Orpheus · reference

The mythical singer who seeks his wife in the underworld.

## Oeagrus · reference

Orpheus’s father.

## Eurydice · reference

Orpheus’s wife, whom he seeks in the underworld.

## Achilles · reference

The Greek hero of the Iliad, companion of Patroclus.

## Patroclus · reference

Achilles’ companion in the Iliad, whom Phaedrus describes as his lover.

## Thetis · reference

Achilles’ mother, the sea goddess who tells him of his possible fates.

## Hector · reference

The Trojan hero opposed to Achilles in the Iliad.

## Uranus · reference

The primordial sky god, father of the heavenly Aphrodite in Pausanias’s account.

## Zeus · reference

The chief Olympian god in the stories and comparisons told by the guests.

## Dione · reference

The goddess named with Zeus as the parent of the common Aphrodite.

## Asclepius · reference

The Greek god of healing, whom Eryximachus credits with founding medicine.

## Urania · reference

The heavenly Muse in Eryximachus’s comparison of kinds of musical love.

## Polyhymnia · reference

The Muse contrasted with Urania in Eryximachus’s account of music and love.

## Otus · reference

The giant called Otys, who with Ephialtes attempts to reach the gods.

## Ephialtes · reference

The giant paired with Otus in the assault on heaven.

## Apollo · reference

The Greek god who helps reshape humans in Aristophanes’ myth, also associated with healing and prophecy.

## Hephaestus · reference

The divine smith whom Aristophanes imagines offering to join lovers permanently.

## Iapetus · reference

The Titan invoked as an example of a very ancient god.

## Kronos · reference

The Titan who is Zeus’s father, invoked in the account of the older gods.

## Necessity · reference

The personified force to which Agathon attributes the old conflicts among the gods.

## Ate · reference

The goddess of delusion and ruin, quoted as an example of delicacy in Homer.

## Ares · reference

The Greek god of war, described as mastered by love for Aphrodite.

## The Muses · reference

The goddesses of music and poetry.

## Athena · reference

The Greek goddess associated with weaving, called Athene here.

## The Gorgon · reference

The petrifying monster evoked by Socrates’ joke about Gorgias’s rhetoric.

## Poros (Plenty) · reference

The personification of resourcefulness, son of Metis, in Diotima’s story of Love’s birth.

## Metis (Discretion) · reference

The personification of wisdom and resourceful thought, Poros’s mother in Diotima’s story.

## Penia (Poverty) · reference

The personification of poverty in Diotima’s story of Love’s birth.

## Codrus · reference

The legendary Athenian king cited as an example of self-sacrifice for lasting fame.

## Poseidon · reference

The Greek god of the sea, invoked by Alcibiades.

## Silenus · reference

The satyr-like figure whose hollow statuettes Alcibiades compares with Socrates.

## Marsyas · reference

The mythical satyr and flute-player whose musical power is compared with Socrates’ speech.

## Diomedes · reference

The Greek hero called Diomede, recalled for exchanging bronze armor for gold in the Iliad.

## Ajax · reference

The mighty Greek hero of the Trojan War, used as a comparison for resistance to injury.

## Nestor · reference

The elderly Greek king renowned for wise speech in Homer.

## Antenor · reference

The Trojan elder and counselor compared with Pericles for his eloquence.

## The divine · reference

A general reference to gods and divine power in the speakers’ discussion.

## Agathon’s welcoming servant · supporting

The household servant who brings Aristodemus into the banquet.

## The servant sent for Socrates · supporting

The attendant who reports Socrates standing absorbed in the neighboring portico.

## Agathon’s servants · supporting

The household attendants serving the banquet.

## The banquet flute-player · supporting

The female musician present before the guests begin their speeches.

## Alcibiades’ flute-player · supporting

The female musician accompanying Alcibiades on his arrival.

## Alcibiades’ attendants · supporting

The companions helping Alcibiades into the banquet.

## The late revelers · supporting

The group that enters Agathon’s house near the end of the night.

## Admetus’s parents · reference

The father and mother contrasted with Alcestis in Phaedrus’s example of devotion.

## The original humans · reference

The double-bodied ancestors in Aristophanes’ invented account of human love.
