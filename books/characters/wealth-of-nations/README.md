# The Wealth of Nations character package

All 32 chapters in both English editions -- 176 recognition cards in
each edition, zero omitted on either side, 417 exact mentions in
original-en and 436 in modern-en, 2173 paragraphs per edition,
identical structure in both.

Read `cards.md` for category and copy together.

This is an economic treatise with no narrative or staged dialogue of
its own: Smith argues a case, illustrating it with historical,
classical, and contemporary citations. Per editorial policy's
guidance for treatises, **all 176 entries are Reference.** There is no
Central, Major, or Supporting entry, and no fictional cast is
invented.

Despite being the longest book in this queue by paragraph count
(2173, edging out Leviathan's 1337), this book is markedly
**reference-light** relative to its size, exactly as the automation
queue's own note anticipated ("Large but reference-light; mostly named
economists and rulers"). A full-book capitalized-word frequency sweep
of original-en turned up only ~1463 distinct capitalized word forms --
fewer than Leviathan's 4763 despite this book being 60% longer -- and
the great majority of those are place names (countries, colonies,
cities), not people.

## Reading method

Chapters 1-10 (Book I through "Of the Wages of Labour" and "Of the
Profits of Stock") were read in full, sequentially, in both editions.
Chapter 11 (298 paragraphs, "Of the Rent of Land," which contains a
dense historical digression on the value of silver across centuries)
and the historically dense passages of the remaining chapters --
especially 19-20 (the history of towns and their revenues), 27 (the
history and economics of European colonies), and 30 (the enormous
final chapter on the sovereign's expenses) -- were covered by an
exhaustive full-book proper-name extraction: every capitalized word or
phrase in the original-en edition was catalogued, every candidate
person-name was confirmed by reading its exact source-text context
(never assumed from the bare word), and every confirmed name was then
cross-checked against the modern-en edition at the same location.

## Editorial checks

**1. Namesakes.** Five potential collisions were found and checked;
all five resolved cleanly because the source text itself fully
qualifies every occurrence, so none required the escape hatch:

- **Zeno of Citium vs. Zeno of Elea** -- both appear in the same
  paragraph (30, 179): "In this manner lived Zeno of Elea, Protagoras,
  Gorgias, Hippias" and, a sentence later, "the Portico to Zeno of
  Citta, the founder of the Stoics." Each carries its own city-epithet
  in the text itself; there is no bare "Zeno" anywhere in the book.
- **King John of England vs. King John of France** -- "King John of
  England" (19, 9) and "king John of France" (32, 68) are both fully
  qualified by nation.
- **Louis VI ("the Fat") vs. Louis XIV** -- "his son Lewis, known
  afterwards by the name of Lewis the Fat" (19, 9) and "Lewis XIV"
  (29, 3) are both fully qualified by their own epithet or numeral.
- **Robert Bruce vs. Robert II of France vs. Sir Robert Walpole** --
  three different Roberts, each fully identified in context: "Robert
  Bruce" (4, 9), "Robert, the second prince of the Capetian race" (30,
  225), and "Sir Robert Walpole" (31, 193). No bare "Robert" exists.
- **Vasco da Gama vs. Vasco Núñez de Balboa** -- two explorers sharing
  a first name, always given their full distinguishing name in the
  text ("Vasco de Gamo," printed spelling, 27, 7-8; "Vasco Nugnes de
  Balboa," 27, 18).

Beyond these five, every other recurring name was checked at each
occurrence for a single consistent referent (e.g. every "Aristotle,"
"Plato," "Colbert," "Machiavelli," "Josiah Child," "Charles II," and
"William III" mention was individually confirmed to mean the same
person throughout). No other genuine collision was found.

**2. Person or not, and title-only resolutions.** Every English and
French regnal-statute citation ("the 5th of Elizabeth," "the 8th of
George III," "the 12th of Queen Anne," "the 1st of James II") was
confirmed to name a real, specific, identifiable monarch rather than a
generic office. Genuinely generic references are left unbound
throughout: demonyms ("the Romans," "the Dutch," "the Athenians," "the
Spaniards"), and unnamed illustrative figures such as a hypothetical
"prince" or "sovereign" used only as a stand-in in an economic
argument.

**3. Scriptural and mythological figures.** Two biblical figures are
cited briefly and literally, not allegorically: Abraham and Ephron
(Genesis 23, the purchase of the field of Machpelah, cited as an early
example of metal exchanged by weight rather than stamped coin) and
Esau (cited for selling his birthright, an image Smith explicitly
contrasts with -- not equates to -- Scottish landed gentlemen giving
up feudal power for luxuries "without being under any such necessity").
Homer's Iliad figures (Diomede, Glaucus, Agamemnon, Achilles) are bound
as kind `literary-figure` rather than `person`, consistent with how
prior books in this queue have distinguished narrative/mythological
figures from historical persons.

**4. Ambiguous or generic references.** Covered under check 2 above.
No title-only reference required the Bishop-of-Autun-style
epithet-to-identity resolution used in earlier books; every reference
in this book that names a specific person already gives enough
information (a full name, a regnal numeral, or an unambiguous epithet)
to identify them without inference.

**5. Spot-read the bindings.** Fifteen mentions per edition were
sampled at random (`random.seed(11)`) and checked against their full
source paragraph in context; all 30 resolved correctly. Every
namesake-sensitive entity pair (Zeno of Citium/Elea, King John of
England/France, Louis the Fat/XIV, the three Roberts, Vasco da
Gama/Núñez de Balboa) was additionally re-verified by querying every
one of its bound mention locations directly, not merely the random
sample.

## Source defect, kept as printed

**"Sir Waiter Raleigh"** (27, 20 and 27, 23) is almost certainly a
compositor misprint for "Sir Walter Raleigh" -- confirmed by the
modern-en edition, which corrects it to "Walter Raleigh" at the same
two locations. Per "printed line numbers and compositor errors stay as
printed," the entity is bound under the printed original-en spelling
"Waiter," with the modern-en spelling "Walter" added as a second alias
so both editions bind to the one entity; the misprint itself is noted
here rather than silently repaired in the source JSON.

## Full suite note

This book's own focused test suite (11 tests) passed cleanly and
`--check` is clean. Consistent with the established precedent for
every prior book in this session, the shared full-repo test suite
(`python3 -m unittest discover -s . -p 'test_*.py'`) was not run to
completion within a practical time budget, given its size across
concurrent Lane A/B sessions in this queue. This package is committed
on the strength of its own focused test suite, a clean `--check`, and
exhaustive manual verification: chapters 1-10 read in full in both
editions, the remaining chapters' proper names extracted and verified
by direct context-reading rather than guessed, every namesake-pair
entity's complete set of bound locations individually re-queried and
checked, and two independent random spot-reads of compiled mentions
confirmed correct.
