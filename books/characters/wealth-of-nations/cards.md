# The Wealth of Nations: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Draco · reference

The Athenian lawgiver, cited for the severity of his laws: "like the laws of Draco, these laws may be said to be all written in blood."

## Solon · reference

The Athenian lawgiver, cited twice: once for the general judgment that his laws, "though not the best in itself, is the best which the interest, prejudices, and temper of the times would admit of," and once for the specific law excusing children from maintaining parents who had not taught them a trade.

## Lycurgus · reference

The Spartan lawgiver, cited for the laws restricting entrance into Lacedaemon which the wealth of conquest eventually broke through.

## Plato · reference

Cited for the ideal republic's proposal to maintain 5,000 idle warriors, for the splendid style of living of Hippias and Protagoras which he describes, for his own reportedly magnificent way of life, and as the philosopher to whom the Academy is said to have been assigned after his death.

## Aristotle · reference

Cited for the state of ancient Greek land cultivation in his time, as tutor to Alexander (munificently rewarded by both Alexander and Philip), and as the philosopher assigned the Lyceum after his death.

## Zeno of Citium · reference

"The founder of the Stoics," said to have been assigned the Portico after his death -- distinguished in the same passage from the earlier, unrelated Zeno of Elea by his own city-epithet.

## Zeno of Elea · reference

Named, distinctly from Zeno of Citium the Stoic in the same passage, among the itinerant Greek teachers who "lived... from place to place" before schools of philosophy became settled institutions.

## Epicurus · reference

Cited for bequeathing his gardens to his own school, rather than to a public site, after his death.

## Thales · reference

Named, with Pythagoras, as one of "the two oldest Greek philosophers," whose schools were established outside of ancient Greece proper.

## Pythagoras · reference

Named with Thales as one of the two oldest Greek philosophers.

## Democritus · reference

Cited for a judgment on planting vineyards in his (largely lost) writing on husbandry, which Columella reports without disputing.

## Isocrates · reference

Quoted directly, reproaching contemporary teachers for charging only "four or five minae" for such grand promises; cited for his own much higher fee (ten minae per scholar) and his hundred students at Athens, and for his didactron reportedly totalling a thousand minae per course, per Plutarch.

## Protagoras · reference

Named, with Hippias, as an eminent teacher whose splendid way of living is described by Plato.

## Hippias · reference

Named with Protagoras as an eminent teacher of splendid, even ostentatious, style of living.

## Gorgias · reference

Cited for presenting his own statue in solid gold to the temple at Delphi, and named among the itinerant early Greek teachers.

## Lysias · reference

Named, with Isocrates, as an early example of the pattern -- found from their day down to Plutarch and Epictetus -- of teachers who also wrote for reputation.

## Carneades · reference

"Carneades the academic," sent by Athens, with Diogenes the Stoic, on a solemn embassy to Rome -- notable, Smith observes, because he was "a Babylonian by birth" yet still honoured by so exclusive a city.

## Diogenes the Stoic · reference

Sent, with Carneades the Academic, on Athens's embassy to Rome.

## Cato · reference

"Old Cato," quoted via Cicero for his maxim that feeding well was the first and most profitable thing in managing a private estate.

## Cicero · reference

Cited for Cato's maxim on estate management, and for the letters reporting that the "virtuous Brutus" lent money in Cyprus at forty-eight per cent.

## Marcus Brutus · reference

"The virtuous Brutus," cited via Cicero's letters for lending money in Cyprus at forty-eight per cent -- an example of the usury common in Roman provinces under proconsular administration.

## Julius Caesar · reference

His invasion of Britain is cited as the approximate point from which the value of silver is reckoned to have continually diminished until the American mines were discovered; his destruction of the Roman republic is compared to Cromwell's standing army turning out the Long Parliament.

## Augustus · reference

Cited for imposing the vicesima hereditatum, a twentieth-penny tax on inheritances among Roman citizens, and for the anecdote of Vidius Pollio ordering a slave's punishment in his presence.

## Servius Tullius · reference

The Roman king credited with first coining money at Rome, before whose time the Romans used unstamped bars of copper.

## Agrippina · reference

The empress for whom, per Pliny, a white nightingale was purchased as a gift at the price of six thousand sestertii.

## Pliny · reference

Cited via his Natural History for early Roman coinage under Servius Tullius, and for the anecdotes of extravagant purchases by Seius and Asinius Celer.

## Seius · reference

Cited by Pliny for purchasing a white nightingale as a gift for the empress Agrippina at six thousand sestertii.

## Asinius Celer · reference

Cited by Pliny for purchasing a surmullet at eight thousand sestertii.

## Varro · reference

The Roman agricultural writer, cited (with Columella) for recommending the fattening of small birds as a profitable practice among the ancient Romans.

## Columella · reference

The Roman agricultural writer, cited repeatedly for reporting and adjudicating debates among ancient Italian husbandmen (Democritus on vineyards, Varro and himself on kitchen gardens and fattening small birds).

## Palladius · reference

The Roman agricultural writer, cited for adopting Columella's opinion, itself recommended earlier by Varro.

## Suetonius · reference

Named, with Epictetus and Quintilian, in the list of authors illustrating that teachers of philosophy also wrote for reputation.

## Quintilian · reference

Named with Epictetus and Suetonius in the same list of philosopher-authors.

## Dion Cassius · reference

Cited (as printed, "Dion Cassius") for the vicesima hereditatum, the Roman inheritance tax imposed by Augustus.

## Dercyllidas · reference

Quoted for a remark on the treasure-hoarding court of Persia, which Smith applies to several Asiatic princes of his own time.

## Mithridates · reference

Cited for drawing his Scythian or Tartar militia from the countries north of the Euxine and Caspian seas.

## Hamilcar · reference

The first of "the great generals who succeeded one another in the command" of Carthage's forces in Spain -- father of Hannibal and father-in-law of Hasdrubal.

## Hasdrubal · reference

Hamilcar's son-in-law and successor in Spain, who led the whole or almost the whole Carthaginian army to oppose the Romans there.

## Hannibal · reference

Hamilcar's son (printed "Annibal"), whose superiority "grew every day less and less" as Hasdrubal drew off the army in Spain.

## Scipio · reference

"The great Scipio," who found only an inferior militia to oppose him in Spain once Hasdrubal had led the main Carthaginian force away.

## Homer · reference

Cited for the armour-prices of Diomede and Glaucus in the Iliad, for Agamemnon offering Achilles the sovereignty of seven cities, and for the state of property and government among the ancient Greeks before the Trojan war.

## Diomede · reference

The Homeric hero whose armour, per Homer, cost only nine oxen.

## Glaucus · reference

The Homeric hero whose armour, by contrast with Diomede's, cost a hundred oxen.

## Agamemnon · reference

Cited from Homer for offering Achilles the sovereignty of seven cities as an inducement to friendship.

## Achilles · reference

The Homeric hero to whom Agamemnon offers seven cities' sovereignty for his friendship.

## Hesiod · reference

Named among the early Greek didactic poets whose work circulated before formal schools of philosophy existed.

## Aesop · reference

Cited for the fables that, with the proverbs of Solomon and the verses of Theognis and Phocyllides, exemplify an early, informal mode of moral instruction.

## Theognis · reference

Named with Phocyllides for verses of moral instruction circulating before formal philosophy schools.

## Phocyllides · reference

Named with Theognis in the same context.

## Solomon · reference

Cited for his proverbs, grouped with Aesop's fables and Theognis and Phocyllides's verses as early moral instruction.

## Polybius · reference

Cited, with Dionysius of Halicarnassus, for direct testimony on Roman moral superiority over the Greeks in private life, and named with Plato and Aristotle as a respectable authority.

## Dionysius of Halicarnassus · reference

Cited with Polybius for testimony on Roman moral superiority over the Greeks, which Smith attributes partly to Roman education.

## Thucydides · reference

Cited for the judgment that Europe and Asia together could not resist a Scythian invasion, and for observing the Peloponnesians' seasonal pattern of campaigning.

## Lucian · reference

Cited for reporting the salary bestowed by "that philosophical emperor" (Marcus Antoninus) on a teacher of philosophy.

## Marcus Antoninus · reference

"That philosophical emperor," the first, per Smith, to endow a salaried teaching chair at Athens.

## Epictetus · reference

Named with Suetonius and Quintilian among later authors continuing the pattern of philosopher-teachers who also wrote.

## St. Dominic · reference

Founder, with St. Francis, of one of the two great mendicant orders whose establishment, per Machiavelli, revived declining religious credit in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

## St. Francis · reference

Founder of the second of the two great mendicant orders, with St. Dominic.

## Martin Luther · reference

Founder of the reformed tradition, with the Church of England, that Smith says preserved more of the ancient church's ranks and subordination than Calvin's followers did.

## Zwingli · reference

Named (as printed, "Zuinglius") as a reformer whose followers, more properly those of Calvin, bestowed a more level, egalitarian church constitution on each parish.

## Calvin · reference

The reformer whose followers, per Smith, more properly represent the egalitarian parish-level church constitution credited loosely to Zwingli.

## Thomas Becket · reference

Cited for his reputed magnificence in strewing his hall's floor with clean hay or rushes in winter, an anecdote on the state of domestic luxury in his time.

## Christian II of Denmark · reference

The tyrant (printed "Christiern II") whose oppression, with Archbishop Troll's, enabled Gustavus Vasa to expel them from Sweden; later deposed from the throne of Denmark himself.

## Archbishop Troll · reference

Archbishop of Uppsala (printed "Upsal"), whose tyranny, with Christian II's, enabled Gustavus Vasa's success in Sweden.

## Gustavus Vasa · reference

Expelled Christian II and Archbishop Troll from Sweden and established the Reformation there without difficulty.

## Frederick I of Holstein · reference

Mounted the Danish throne (printed "Frederic of Holstein") after Christian II, and, still favoured by the pope, followed Gustavus Vasa's example in his own dominions.

## William the Conqueror · reference

Introduced the custom of paying royal revenues in coined money rather than in kind, and set the pound-shilling-penny proportion that continued afterward among the English.

## William Rufus · reference

Cited for Westminster Hall reportedly being his dining-room -- an anecdote on the retinue and hospitality of great households in early feudal England.

## King John of England · reference

"King John of England," cited as the most liberal English king in granting charters of privilege to his boroughs.

## Edward I · reference

Cited for the weight and fineness of the pound sterling in his time, and for the Tower pound's introduction.

## Edward III · reference

Cited for the point at which gold began to be coined in England, and for a Statute of Labourers regulating clerical and mason's pay.

## Edward VI · reference

In whose reign "religious zeal prohibited all interest" -- a prohibition Smith says only increased the evil of usury.

## Henry III · reference

Cited for an ancient statute regulating the weight of wastel bread against the price of wheat.

## Henry VIII · reference

Cited for introducing the Troyes pound weight into the English mint, and for the statute capping legal interest at ten per cent, later revived under Elizabeth.

## Philip and Mary · reference

The joint English monarchs in whose reign the denomination of the English coin last underwent little alteration, and under whom the exportation of wheat was permitted with duty.

## Elizabeth I · reference

Cited repeatedly for statutes: reserving college-lease rents partly in corn, reviving Henry VIII's interest cap, and the Statute of Apprenticeship requiring a seven-year term.

## James I · reference

Cited for the introduction of copper coinage in Great Britain, and for restricting legal interest to eight per cent.

## Charles I · reference

Named with Charles II for the prices at which wheat was frequently sold in their reigns.

## Charles II · reference

Cited for Judge Hale's computation of labourers' family expense written in his time, for the statute fixing forty days' residence as a settlement, and for reducing legal interest to six per cent after the Restoration.

## James II · reference

Cited for the statute clarifying that the forty days' residence needed for a parish settlement should run only from written notice.

## William III · reference

Cited for the reformation of the silver coin in his reign, and for statutes on parish settlements.

## Queen Anne · reference

Cited for reducing legal interest to five per cent, and for statutes empowering bishops to set curates' stipends and clarifying settlement law.

## George II · reference

Cited for the statute establishing the assize of bread, later found impracticable in Scotland for want of the office it depended on.

## George III · reference

Cited for the statute regulating London tailors' wages, and for the later remedy of the assize-of-bread defect in Scotland.

## Richard II · reference

Cited for a fourteenth-century statute raising a particular duty to one shilling in the pound.

## Robert Bruce · reference

Cited as the endpoint of the period during which the Scots money pound held the same weight and fineness of silver as the English pound sterling.

## Sir Matthew Hale · reference

"Lord-chief-justice Hales," who computed the necessary weekly expense of a labourer's family in the time of Charles II.

## Chief Baron Gilbert · reference

Cited, with Dr. Blackstone, on the ancient status of English tenants as bailiffs rather than true tenants of their landlords.

## Dr. Blackstone · reference

Cited on the ancient status of English tenants, and on the proportion of college-lease rent arising from the corn portion reserved by the 18th of Elizabeth.

## Doctor Burn · reference

The historian of the poor laws, quoted repeatedly and at length on the law of parish settlements and certificates.

## Charlemagne · reference

Cited for the French livre's original weight standard in his time, set by the Troyes weight then used at the fair of Troyes in Champagne.

## King John of France · reference

"King John of France," cited (via Du Cange's Glossary) for adulterating his coin to pay his debts.

## Louis VI ("the Fat") · reference

Son of Philip I of France, known afterward as Louis the Fat (printed "Lewis the Fat"), who consulted the bishops of the royal demesnes, per Father Daniel, on restraining noble violence.

## Louis XIV · reference

"Lewis XIV," whose famous minister Colbert is described as a man of probity, industry, and knowledge of detail, though mistaken in overvaluing town industry relative to the country.

## Philip I of France · reference

Lost all effective authority over his barons; father of Louis the Fat.

## Robert II of France ("the Pious") · reference

"Robert, the second prince of the Capetian race," unjustly excommunicated by the court of Rome, whose own servants' reaction illustrates the era's clerical devotion.

## Colbert · reference

Louis XIV's famous minister, a man of probity and great ability who nonetheless, per Smith, overvalued the industry of towns above that of the country in his economic policy.

## the Duke of Choiseul · reference

Cited for a small experiment made about twelve years before writing, upon the parliament of Paris.

## Mr. Quesnay · reference

"The very ingenious and profound author" of the physiocratic economic system Smith examines and critiques at length; a physician by training.

## Mercier de la Riviere · reference

Author of a book expounding the physiocratic doctrine, once intendant of Martinico.

## the Marquis de Mirabeau · reference

"A very diligent and respectable author," quoted for calling writing, money, and the physiocrats' economic table three great inventions.

## Mr. de Voltaire · reference

Cited for the anecdote of father Porée, an unremarkable Jesuit professor whose students nonetheless all followed his literary advice.

## Father Porée · reference

The Jesuit professor of no great eminence whose students' uniform deference to his literary judgment Voltaire cites as remarkable.

## Father Daniel · reference

Cited as Smith's source for Louis the Fat's consultations with the bishops of the royal demesnes.

## Du Cange · reference

Cited (via his Glossary, s.v. Moneta) as the source for King John of France's coin adulteration.

## Columbus · reference

Concluded that the shorter way to the East Indies lay west, convinced Isabella of Castile of his project, and, per his own letters to Ferdinand and Isabella, insisted the lands he found were the Indies even against clear evidence -- a mistake that gave the region its lasting name.

## Vasco da Gama · reference

Sailed from Lisbon in 1497 (printed "Vasco de Gamo"), nearly five years after Columbus's own expedition set out.

## Ferdinand of Aragon · reference

Named with Isabella as the recipient of Columbus's letters insisting his discoveries were the Indies.

## Isabella of Castile · reference

Convinced by Columbus of his project's probability, and, with Ferdinand, the recipient of his letters on the supposed Indies.

## Cortes · reference

One of the adventurers whom "the sacred thirst of gold" carried to Mexico.

## Almagro · reference

Carried, with Pizarro, to Chile and Peru by the same motive of gold.

## Pizarro · reference

Carried, with Almagro, to Chile and Peru.

## Vasco Núñez de Balboa · reference

Carried, with Ovieda and Nicuessa (printed "Vasco Nugnes de Balboa"), to the Isthmus of Darien.

## Ovieda · reference

Named with Nicuessa and Balboa among those carried to Darien by the thirst for gold.

## Nicuessa · reference

Named with Ovieda and Balboa in the same expedition to Darien.

## Montezuma · reference

Cited for the population of Mexico City in his time, compared to its present state.

## Sir Walter Raleigh · reference

"The dream of Sir Waiter Raleigh" (a probable compositor misprint for Walter, corrected in the modern-en edition), concerning the golden city and country of El Dorado, and his patents to the London and Plymouth companies.

## Mr. Hobbes · reference

Quoted directly: "Wealth, as Mr Hobbes says, is power" -- though Smith immediately qualifies that a fortune's mere possession does not itself convey political power.

## Mr. Locke · reference

Cited three times: on the high price of silver bullion after the reformation of the coin under William III, alongside Law and Montesquieu on money and interest, and on the distinction between money and other moveable goods.

## Mr. Hume · reference

Cited for the observation that in Saxon times the fleece was valued at two-fifths of the whole sheep's worth.

## M. Montesquieu · reference

Named with Locke and Law on money and interest, and separately for accounting for high interest rates among Mahometan nations by legal prohibition rather than poverty.

## Mr. Law · reference

"The famous Mr Law," who believed a bank could increase a country's productive capital and established one on that principle, explained in his own published discourse on money and trade.

## Sir Josiah Child · reference

Represented the Hamburgh and other regulated companies as harmful exclusive monopolies; a regulated company was still established long after his time, in 1750.

## Mr. Mun · reference

Compared the operation of foreign trade to seed-time and harvest; his book, "England's Treasure in Foreign Trade," gave its title to a fundamental maxim of the mercantile system.

## Sir Matthew Decker · reference

"An excellent authority," cited repeatedly on the effects of taxes on the price of goods, and for his well-known proposal that all commodities be taxed by an annual licence rather than at the point of sale.

## Mr. Cantillon · reference

Computed that the lowest species of common labourers must earn at least double their own maintenance in order to rear children to working age.

## Mr. Anderson · reference

Compiler of the Diplomata Scotiae, to whose preface (by Ruddiman) Smith refers for the historical proportion of gold to silver in Scots coin.

## James Postlethwaite · reference

Author of the History of the Public Revenue, cited for figures on wartime exchequer discounts and on debt added during a war's continuance.

## Dr. Davenant · reference

Extolled the political-arithmetic skill of Mr. Gregory King, whose computation of labourers' income Smith cites.

## Mr. Lowndes · reference

Author of the Essay on the Silver Coin, cited for the market price of silver bullion around 1695.

## Mr. Dupré de St. Maur · reference

One of the "faithful, diligent, and laborious collectors of the prices of corn," cited repeatedly alongside Messance for French grain-price data.

## Mr. Messance · reference

"A French author of great knowledge and ingenuity," who showed that the poor produced more goods in cheap than in dear years across three French manufactures, and who collected French grain-price data with Dupré de St. Maur.

## Mr. Meggens · reference

Cited (via the Postscript to the Universal Merchant) for the estimated ratio of gold to silver annually imported into Europe.

## Mr. Ruddiman · reference

Author of the preface to Anderson's Diplomata Scotiae, cited on the historical proportion of gold to silver coin in Scotland.

## Bishop Fleetwood · reference

Collected historical wheat prices from 1202 to 1597 (in his Chronicon Preciosum), and acknowledged, on one occasion, having mistaken a conversion price for an actual market price.

## Mr. Gregory King · reference

"A man famous for his knowledge in matters of this kind," who in 1688 estimated the average produce of English land and the ordinary income of labourers and out-servants.

## Rev. John Smith · reference

Author of the Memoirs of Wool, cited for the historical price of English wool -- explicitly named as "the Reverend Mr John Smith," distinct from Adam Smith himself.

## Dr. Douglas · reference

Author of a Summary Smith cites, with some suspicion of misinformation, on plentiful herring catches.

## Dr. Arbuthnot · reference

Observed that there was much less variety of dress and household furniture in ancient than in modern times.

## Dr. Swift · reference

Author of the saying that in the arithmetic of the customs, two and two do not always make four but sometimes only one -- an illustration of how excessive duties can reduce revenue by encouraging smuggling.

## Ramazzini · reference

"An eminent Italian physician," author of a book on the occupational diseases peculiar to different trades.

## Mr. Kalm · reference

The Swedish traveller, cited on the state of husbandry in some of the English colonies he observed.

## Mr. Buffon · reference

Cited for the relative price of pork and beef in France.

## Frezier · reference

Cited, with Ulloa, on the taxation and rarity of profitable silver mining in Peru; visited Peru in 1713 and described Lima's population.

## Ulloa · reference

Cited, with Frezier, on Peruvian silver mining, and separately for cattle and horse prices at Buenos Aires; resided in the region between 1740 and 1746.

## Solórzano · reference

Cited for the reduction of Spain's registered silver coinage to a fifth of its former amount by 1504.

## Tavernier · reference

"A jeweller," who visited the diamond mines of Golconda and Visiapour and reported on their profitability.

## Rev. Mr. Borlase · reference

Vice-warden of the stannaries, cited on the productivity of some of the world's most fertile tin mines.

## Machiavelli · reference

Cited (as printed, "Machiavel") for the tyranny that banished Castruccio Castracani's silk-weaving families from Lucca, for the revival of religious credit by the mendicant orders, and for the trading methods of Lorenzo de' Medici's agents.

## Guicciardini · reference

Cited for the state of Italian land cultivation before Charles VIII's invasion.

## Castruccio Castracani · reference

"One of Machiavelli's heroes" (the subject of his biography), whose tyranny at Lucca banished nine hundred silk-weaving families in 1310.

## Lorenzo de' Medici · reference

"Not a prince of mean abilities," whose agents, per Machiavelli, carried on his trade -- an example of princely involvement in commerce.

## Sandi · reference

Cited (via his Istoria civile di Venezia) for the history of the Venetian silk trade.

## Pfeffel · reference

Cited on remarkable events under Frederick II and his successors of the House of Swabia, and on the early formidable rise of the Hanseatic league.

## Madox · reference

Author of Firma Burgi and a History of the Exchequer, cited repeatedly on medieval English town charters and taxation.

## Brady · reference

Author of a Historical Treatise of Cities and Boroughs, cited on medieval town taxation.

## Father Charlevoix · reference

Cited for an earlier population estimate of a region later found, by Smith's own account, to hold nearly double the number of inhabitants.

## Gemelli Careri · reference

"A pretended traveller, it is said," whose population estimate for a region Smith nonetheless notes agrees with another author's contemporary account.

## Bouchaud · reference

Cited (via his work on the French vingtième tax on inheritances) alongside Dion Cassius and Burman on Roman inheritance taxation.

## Burman · reference

Cited (via his work on Roman taxation) alongside Dion Cassius and Bouchaud on the vicesima hereditatum.

## the Abbé Expilly · reference

Cited (via his Collections) on evidence for how a particular order of French taxation fell unevenly on different groups.

## Mr. Barretti · reference

Reported on the weekly packet-boat trade in coin between Portugal and England.

## Cassendi · reference

"The famous Cassendi," who was, early in his life, a professor at a university -- an example against the claim that no eminent philosopher of the age held such a post.

## Serjeant Hawkins · reference

The legal writer who still considered a particular old statute in force, though Smith notes it had never been directly repealed.

## Mr. Drummond · reference

The banker whose promissory notes for twenty-five or fifty guineas are used to illustrate how a change in the gold-silver coin ratio would affect payment in one metal versus the other.

## Oliver Cromwell · reference

Whose standing army, formed during his government, eventually turned the Long Parliament out of doors -- compared to Julius Caesar's destruction of the Roman republic; his navy is also cited as superior to Holland's during the Anglo-Dutch war of his time.

## Mr. Pelham · reference

Prime minister in 1749, who observed to the House of Commons the exchequer discounts paid over the preceding three years.

## Sir Robert Walpole · reference

Author of the famous excise scheme intended to apply, to wine and tobacco, a system similar to that already used for other goods.

## the Earl of Chatham · reference

William Pitt the Elder, whose army accounts, balanced with Mr. Calcraft's, are cited among wartime savings figures.

## Mr. Calcraft · reference

Cited alongside the Earl of Chatham's accounts among wartime army savings figures.

## Mr. Cameron of Lochiel · reference

A gentleman of Lochaber who, without legal authority or even the rank of tenant-in-chief, exercised a criminal jurisdiction as a vassal of the Duke of Argyll -- cited as a recent (within thirty years) illustration of feudal-style authority persisting in the Scottish Highlands.

## the Duke of Argyll · reference

The feudal superior of whom Cameron of Lochiel was a vassal, cited in the same Highland jurisdiction anecdote.

## Peter the Great · reference

His improvements to the Russian empire, Smith argues, almost all resolve into the establishment of a well-regulated standing army.

## Abraham · reference

Cited (Genesis) for weighing out four hundred shekels of silver to Ephron for the field of Machpelah, an early example of metal exchanged by weight rather than by stamped coin.

## Ephron · reference

The seller to whom Abraham weighed out the four hundred shekels of silver for the field of Machpelah.

## Esau · reference

Cited for selling his birthright for a mess of pottage -- an image Smith uses (by explicit contrast) for Scottish landed gentlemen exchanging their feudal power for luxuries, but not under any such pressing necessity.
