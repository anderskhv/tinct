# Politics: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Socrates · major

As the speaker in Plato's Republic and Laws, the source of the community-of-women-and-property proposals that occupy most of Book 2's opening critique, and of the cyclical theory of constitutional change Aristotle rebuts at the close of Book 5.

## Plato · major

Author of the Republic and the Laws, whose proposed constitutions are examined at length in Book 2 and revisited in Books 4 and 7; cited by name as the author distinct from his speaker Socrates.

## Hesiod · reference

The early Greek poet, quoted for the line "First house and wife and an ox for the plough."

## Charondas · reference

The lawgiver of Catana, cited in Book 1 for a term he used for household members and, in Book 2, for the laws he gave his own and other Chalcidian cities in Italy and Sicily.

## Epimenides the Cretan · reference

Cited for a term he used for household members, alongside Charondas.

## Homer · reference

The poet, quoted repeatedly (Books 1, 3, 4) -- on the Cyclopes' households, on the "tribeless, lawless, hearthless" man outside the state, and on Agamemnon's power over Achilles.

## Solon · reference

The Athenian lawgiver, quoted in Book 1 on the limitlessness of wealth, and credited in Book 2 with laws against unlimited land ownership and with reshaping the Athenian constitution by opening the law courts to all citizens.

## Midas · reference

The legendary king whose golden touch is cited as an example of wealth that cannot feed a man.

## Theodectes · reference

The tragedian whose play Helen is quoted for a line about noble birth.

## Daedalus · reference

The legendary craftsman, whose self-moving statues are cited as an image of tools needing no servants to operate them.

## Hephaestus · reference

The god of craftsmanship, whose self-moving tripods are cited alongside the statues of Daedalus as an image of tools needing no servants.

## Amasis · reference

The Egyptian king, whose saying about his footpan is cited as an example of ceremony distinguishing a ruler from his subjects.

## Gorgias · reference

The rhetorician, cited in Book 1 for enumerating the virtues, and again in Book 3 (as "Gorgias of Leontini") for his ironic remark that citizens, like kettles, are manufactured by magistrates.

## Chares of Paros · reference

One of the writers on tillage and planting Aristotle cites. A different, unrelated "Chares" -- a political intermediary, not this agricultural writer -- appears later, in Book 5.

## Apollodorus of Lemnos · reference

One of the writers on tillage and planting Aristotle cites, alongside Chares of Paros.

## Thales · reference

The philosopher, cited for his olive-press monopoly, told as an anecdote about how philosophers could be rich if they chose. Book 2 also reports (and calls chronologically inconsistent) a tradition making a "Thales" the teacher of Lycurgus and Zaleucus -- most likely the same figure invoked by a strained legendary genealogy rather than a second, unnamed person.

## Cleisthenes of Athens · reference

The Athenian reformer who, after the expulsion of the tyrants, enrolled strangers, slaves, and resident aliens into new tribes (3:5) and later strengthened the democracy by similar measures (6:11). A different, unrelated Cleisthenes -- his maternal grandfather, the tyrant of Sicyon -- appears in Book 5.

## Pheidon the Corinthian · reference

An ancient legislator credited with the view that the number of citizen families should remain fixed. A different, unrelated Pheidon -- a king of Argos who became a tyrant -- appears in Book 5.

## Hippodamus · reference

The son of Euryphon, of Miletus: inventor of the grid-plan city and layout of the Piraeus, and the first non-statesman to write on the ideal constitution -- his own three-class model occupies most of Book 2's discussion of him.

## Lycurgus · reference

The traditional lawgiver of Sparta, credited with its constitution and common meals, said to have studied the Cretan constitution during King Charilaus's minority.

## Charilaus · reference

The Spartan king whose minority Lycurgus is said to have guarded before traveling to Crete (2:53). A later reference to "the tyranny of Charilaus at Lacedaemon" (5:78) is puzzling, since nothing elsewhere calls this legitimate king a tyrant; it is bound here as the same figure for lack of any textual signal of a second Charilaus, but the description does not obviously fit him.

## Minos · reference

The legendary king of Crete, credited with the laws still followed by its subject population and with Crete's naval empire; said to have died in Sicily. Cited again in Book 7 alongside Sesostris as an ancient founder of a warrior-versus-husbandmen class system.

## Ephialtes · reference

The Athenian statesman who, with Pericles, curtailed the power of the Areopagus.

## Pericles · reference

The Athenian statesman who, with Ephialtes, curtailed the power of the Areopagus and instituted payment for jury service.

## Zaleucus · reference

The lawgiver who gave laws to the Epizephyrian Locrians.

## Onomacritus · reference

A Locrian by birth trained in Crete, sometimes credited (on chronologically dubious authority) as the first legislator with any special skill, and placed by that same tradition as Thales's teacher.

## Philolaus the Corinthian · reference

A Theban legislator of the Corinthian Bacchiad family, who left Corinth for Thebes out of horror at his mother Halcyone's incestuous passion for him, and there gave the Thebans their "Laws of Adoption."

## Diocles · reference

The Olympic victor and lover of Philolaus, who left Corinth with him over the same scandal that drove Philolaus away. Original-en prints the OCR-mangled "Diodes"; both spellings are aliased and the error is left as printed.

## Halcyone · reference

Diocles's mother, whose incestuous passion for her son drove him and Philolaus from Corinth to Thebes.

## Draco · reference

The Athenian lawgiver, noted only for the severity of his penalties.

## Pittacus · reference

Elected leader (an aesymnetia, or elective tyranny) by the Mitylenaeans against exiles led by Antimenides and the poet Alcaeus; also cited as a lawgiver for a law doubling penalties for drunken violence.

## Antimenides · reference

One of the leaders of the exiles the Mitylenaeans elected Pittacus to oppose, named alongside the poet Alcaeus, his brother.

## Alcaeus · reference

The poet, one of the leaders of the exiles opposed to Pittacus, quoted denouncing his own city for making the low-born Pittacus tyrant.

## Androdamas of Rhegium · reference

Gave laws to the Chalcidians of Thrace, chiefly concerning homicide and heiresses.

## Phaleas of Chalcedon · reference

The first to propose that citizens of a state should have equal possessions; his equalization scheme and its limits occupy a long stretch of Book 2.

## Eubulus · reference

Talked Autophradates out of besieging Atarneus by pointing out the cost of the siege against the price of simply leaving.

## Autophradates · reference

The besieger of Atarneus whom Eubulus talked out of the siege.

## Diophantus · reference

Introduced the plan at Athens under which artisans engaged on public works are treated as public slaves.

## Jason · reference

Quoted for the remark that he "felt hungry when he was not a tyrant" -- unable to bear living as a private citizen.

## Euripides · reference

The tragedian, quoted on the special education of princes (Book 3), on living "according to one's fancy" as democratic license (Book 5), and elsewhere cited among those who scourged Decamnichus at Archelaus's court (Book 5).

## Lycophron · reference

The sophist, quoted for defining law as merely "a surety to one another of justice," a merely conventional guarantee rather than a maker of good citizens.

## Achilles · reference

The Homeric hero, cited complaining of Agamemnon's treating him "like some dishonoured stranger."

## Agamemnon · reference

The Homeric king, cited both for dishonouring Achilles and, elsewhere, for his prayer wishing for ten wise counsellors.

## Antisthenes · reference

The Cynic philosopher, whose fable of the lions and the hares -- "where are your claws?" -- is cited against demands for equality from the unequal.

## Heracles · reference

The legendary hero, said in myth to have been left behind by the Argonauts for fear he would overpower the rest of the crew -- an image for ostracism.

## Periander of Corinth · reference

The tyrant of Corinth, son of Cypselus, named in full at 5:67 as "the great master" of the arts of tyranny; identifiable elsewhere by the corn-field anecdote he shares with Thrasybulus of Miletus (3:54, 5:52) and by the Cypselid dynasty's reign lengths (5:75, 5:76, forty-four years, ending in 5:76's note that he was "a great soldier"). A different, unrelated Periander -- the tyrant of Ambracia -- is named in full elsewhere in Book 5.

## Thrasybulus of Miletus · reference

The tyrant of Miletus who received, via a herald, Periander of Corinth's wordless advice to cut down the tallest ears of corn -- meaning the leading citizens. A different, unrelated Thrasybulus -- Hiero's brother and successor as tyrant of Syracuse -- appears later in Book 5.

## Dionysius I · reference

The tyrant of Syracuse, cited repeatedly across Books 1, 3, and 5: asking the Syracusans for a bodyguard as his first step to power (3:70), rising by denouncing the rich (5:19), installed by Hipparinus (5:21), the Locrian marriage connection blamed for ruining that city (5:29), grouped among demagogues-turned-tyrant (5:49), and taxing his subjects into poverty within five years (5:67). His son and successor, Dionysius II, is a different, explicitly "younger" Dionysius named later in Book 5.

## Phocylides · reference

The gnomic poet, quoted praying for a middling competence.

## Telecles the Milesian · reference

Cited for a constitution in which citizens deliberate by turns rather than as a single body.

## Lysander · reference

The Spartan, said to have attempted to overthrow the Spartan monarchy.

## Pausanias (Spartan king) · reference

A king of Sparta who attempted to overthrow the ephoralty (5:2) and whom the Lacedaemonians later accused of trying to seize personal power in the state (7:57, both places naming him explicitly as "king"). Two different, unrelated men also named Pausanias appear elsewhere in Book 5: the regent who commanded at Plataea, and Philip of Macedon's assassin.

## Gelo · reference

The tyrant of Syracuse whose rise followed a period of democratic disorder there; his and his brother Hiero's dynasty is cited as the longest-lasting Syracusan tyranny, and its fall into a tyranny "distributed" among the family is discussed alongside Dionysius II's fall.

## Cleomenes · reference

The Lacedaemonian king whose defeat of the Argives in the "Battle of the Seventh Day" forced Argos to admit some of its subject population to citizenship.

## Heracleodorus · reference

His accession to office at Oreum overthrew the oligarchy there and changed it into a constitutional, democratic government.

## Paches · reference

The Athenian who took Mitylene in the war that followed a dispute there about heiresses.

## Timophanes (Mitylene) · reference

A wealthy citizen of Mitylene whose two daughters, sought by Doxander for his own sons and refused, occasioned a revolution. A different, unrelated Timophanes -- the Corinthian general who seized a tyranny -- appears later in Book 5.

## Doxander · reference

Wanted Timophanes's daughters for his sons; rejected, he stirred up revolution and, as Athenian proxenus, drew in Athenian interference at Mitylene.

## Mnaseas · reference

The father of Mnason, on one side of the Phocian heiress dispute that began the Sacred War.

## Mnason · reference

The son of Mnaseas, on one side of the Phocian heiress dispute that began the Sacred War.

## Euthycrates · reference

The father of Onomarchus, on the other side of the Phocian heiress dispute that began the Sacred War.

## Onomarchus · reference

The son of Euthycrates, on the other side of the Phocian heiress dispute that began the Sacred War.

## Phoxus · reference

The tyrant of Chalcis, killed by the people acting together with the notables, who then seized the government for themselves.

## Peisistratus · reference

The Athenian tyrant, who rose by leading a faction against the men of the plain; his rule and his sons' (the Peisistratidae) is cited repeatedly as an example of both tyranny's origins and its fall, and, in one anecdote, of a tyrant submitting to trial before the Areopagus.

## The Peisistratidae · reference

Peisistratus's family and dynasty at Athens, whose downfall -- provoked by the insult to Harmodius's sister -- and whose building projects (the temple of Olympian Zeus) are both cited.

## Theagenes · reference

The tyrant of Megara, who won popular favour by slaughtering the cattle of the wealthy.

## Daphnaeus · reference

One of the rich men whose denunciation by Dionysius I won him the people's confidence on his way to tyranny.

## Lygdamis · reference

A member of the oligarchy at Naxos who championed the oppressed people and afterward became tyrant himself.

## The Basilidae · reference

The family that ruled Erythrae well but was eventually deposed for the narrowness of its oligarchy.

## Charicles · reference

Led the dominant faction among the Thirty at Athens, an example of a demagogue operating within a small oligarchy.

## Phrynichus · reference

Led the dominant faction among the Four Hundred at Athens, an example of a demagogue operating within a small oligarchy.

## Simos · reference

Held the government at Larissa along with the Aleuadae family.

## The Aleuadae · reference

The ruling family of Larissa, who held the government there alongside Simos.

## Iphiades · reference

Associated with the political clubs that held power at Abydos.

## Cleotimus · reference

Introduced Chalcidian colonists at Amphipolis and then stirred them up against the rich.

## Hipparinus · reference

Installed Dionysius I as tyrant at Syracuse.

## Chares · reference

A political intermediary whose negotiation at Aegina became the occasion for an attempt to revolutionize the state there -- almost certainly the well-known Athenian general and mercenary commander, though the text gives no epithet to confirm it. A different, unrelated "Chares of Paros," an agricultural writer, is named in full in Book 1.

## Timophanes (Corinth) · reference

The Corinthian general who, given command of mercenaries, made himself tyrant -- the brother the celebrated liberator Timoleon later killed for it, though Aristotle does not name Timoleon here. A different, unrelated Timophanes -- an ordinary wealthy citizen of Mitylene -- appears earlier in Book 5.

## Diagoras · reference

Overturned the oligarchy of the knights at Eretria after being wronged in a marriage dispute.

## Eurytion · reference

Punished at Heraclea on a charge of adultery in a judgment executed in a spirit of party rather than justice, provoking revolution.

## Archias · reference

Punished at Thebes on a charge of adultery in a judgment executed in a spirit of party rather than justice, provoking revolution.

## The Partheniae · reference

The illegitimate sons of the Spartan peers, who attempted a revolution at Sparta and, once detected, were sent away to colonize Tarentum.

## Cinadon · reference

A brave man excluded from Sparta's honours, who conspired against the Spartans under Agesilaus.

## Agesilaus · reference

The Spartan king under whom Cinadon conspired against the Spartans.

## Tyrtaeus · reference

The poet whose "Good Order" is cited as evidence that citizens ruined by the Messenian War wanted a redistribution of land.

## Pausanias (the regent) · reference

Named as "general in the Persian War," cited as an example of an individual great enough to want to rule alone. Two different, unrelated men also named Pausanias appear elsewhere in Book 5: a Spartan king, and Philip of Macedon's assassin.

## Hanno · reference

A Carthaginian, cited alongside Pausanias as an example of an individual great enough to want to rule alone.

## Codrus · reference

Cited as a king who prevented his state from being enslaved in war, an example of royalty earned by benefiting one's people.

## Cyrus · reference

Cyrus the Great, cited as a king who gave his country freedom (5:50), and again as an example of a general who successfully attacked a monarch he despised -- his grandfather Astyages, whose power he judged worn out by soft living (5:59).

## Pheidon · reference

A king of Argos who became a tyrant, cited among rulers who extended hereditary power into outright tyranny. A different, unrelated Pheidon -- an ancient legislator -- is named in full ("Pheidon the Corinthian") in Book 2.

## Phalaris · reference

The tyrant of Acragas, cited as an example of a tyrant who rose from holding a great office rather than from being a demagogue.

## Panaetius · reference

Rose from demagogue to tyrant at Leontini; later cited again as an example of an oligarchy that changed directly into a tyranny.

## Cypselus · reference

The tyrant of Corinth and father of Periander, who rose as a demagogue, ruled without ever needing a bodyguard, and founded the Cypselid dynasty (thirty years' reign, then Periander's forty-four); his family's costly public offerings are cited as a device for keeping subjects poor and occupied.

## Harmodius · reference

The public dishonour of his sister, and the insult to himself, provoked the attack on the Peisistratidae that he and Aristogeiton carried out.

## Aristogeiton · reference

Joined Harmodius in the attack on the Peisistratidae, for Harmodius's sake.

## Periander of Ambracia · reference

The tyrant of Ambracia, expelled by the people acting with conspirators (5:15) and named in full ("Periander, the tyrant of Ambracia") as the target of a conspiracy provoked by an obscene question he asked a favourite youth (5:54). A different, unrelated Periander -- the tyrant of Corinth -- is Book 5's other, far more frequently cited Periander.

## Philip · reference

Philip II of Macedon, attacked by his own guard Pausanias after being permitted to suffer an insult from Attalus and his friends.

## Pausanias (assassin of Philip) · reference

Attacked Philip of Macedon after being permitted to suffer an insult from Attalus and his friends -- the historical assassin of Philip II, identified here by the surrounding narrative rather than an epithet. Two different, unrelated men also named Pausanias appear elsewhere in Book 5: a Spartan king, and the regent who commanded at Plataea.

## Attalus · reference

Insulted Pausanias, with the friends who joined him, provoking the attack on Philip of Macedon.

## Amyntas the Little · reference

Attacked by Derdas after boasting of having enjoyed Derdas's youth. A different, unrelated Amyntas -- Archelaus's own son -- is named later in the same passage.

## Derdas · reference

Attacked Amyntas the Little after being boasted of.

## Evagoras · reference

The king of Cyprus, slain by a eunuch avenging the eunuch's wife, whom Evagoras's own son had carried off.

## Crataeus · reference

Attacked and killed Archelaus, ostensibly over a broken marriage promise involving Archelaus's daughters, though Aristotle judges the real cause was Crataeus's disgust at their intimate connection.

## Archelaus · reference

The Macedonian king killed by Crataeus; the subject of several further conspiracies recounted in Book 5 (Hellanocrates, Decamnichus) arising from broken promises and personal insults.

## Sirrhas · reference

The enemy Archelaus was fighting when he married his elder daughter to the king of Elymaea for military support.

## Arrhibaeus · reference

Named alongside Sirrhas as an enemy Archelaus was fighting when he married his elder daughter to the king of Elymaea for military support.

## Amyntas (son of Archelaus) · reference

Archelaus's own son, married to Archelaus's younger daughter in hope of avoiding a quarrel with the son of Cleopatra. A different, unrelated Amyntas the Little is named earlier in the same passage.

## Cleopatra · reference

A Macedonian royal woman, mother of the son Archelaus hoped to keep from quarrelling with his own son Amyntas -- not the later, far better-known queen of Egypt.

## Hellanocrates of Larissa · reference

Archelaus's lover, who conspired against him after Archelaus failed to keep his promise of restoring him to his own country.

## Parrhon · reference

Killed Cotys with Heracleides of Aenos to avenge their father.

## Heracleides of Aenos · reference

Killed Cotys with Parrhon to avenge their father.

## Cotys · reference

The Thracian king killed by Parrhon and Heracleides of Aenos in revenge for their father, and mutilated Adamas as a child, provoking Adamas's own later revolt.

## Adamas · reference

Revolted from Cotys in revenge for being mutilated by him as a child.

## Megacles · reference

Attacked and killed the Penthalidae at Mitylene with his friends, after they had gone about the city assaulting people with clubs.

## The Penthalidae · reference

A ruling clan at Mitylene, killed by Megacles and his friends after going about assaulting people with clubs.

## Smerdis · reference

Beaten and torn from his wife by Penthilus, whom he later killed in revenge.

## Penthilus · reference

Beat Smerdis and tore him from his wife, and was later killed by him in revenge.

## Decamnichus · reference

Led the assassins in the conspiracy against Archelaus, enraged after Archelaus handed him over to Euripides to be scourged for a remark about the poet's bad breath.

## Artapanes · reference

Conspired against and killed Xerxes, fearing he would be blamed for hanging Darius without orders.

## Xerxes · reference

The Persian king, killed by Artapanes; also cited among rulers whose empires the Persian king (an earlier, unnamed reference) crushed rebellious former subjects.

## Darius · reference

Hanged, apparently on Artapanes's own initiative rather than Xerxes's orders -- probably Xerxes's own son of this name, distinct from Darius I "the Great."

## Sardanapalus · reference

The legendary Assyrian king, slain by a courtier who saw him carding wool among his women -- an example of contempt as a motive for conspiracy. Named only in modern-en; original-en's text is corrupted at this point and never supplies the name (see the package README).

## Dion · reference

Attacked and expelled his own relative Dionysius II out of contempt for his drunkenness, landing with only a small force on the principle that even a quick death would be an honourable risk.

## Dionysius II · reference

Dionysius I's son and successor as tyrant of Syracuse, explicitly called "the younger Dionysius," attacked out of contempt and then expelled by his own relative Dion.

## Astyages · reference

Cyrus's grandfather and the Median king Cyrus overthrew, his power judged worn out by an effeminate life.

## Seuthes · reference

A Thracian who conspired against Amadocus, the ruler whose general he was.

## Amadocus · reference

The Thracian ruler against whom his own general Seuthes conspired.

## Mithridates · reference

Conspired against Ariobarzanes out of a mix of contempt and love of gain.

## Ariobarzanes · reference

The ruler against whom his own subordinate Mithridates conspired.

## Theopompus · reference

The Spartan king who further limited royal power (chiefly by establishing the Ephoralty), reasoning that a reduced but more durable kingship was a better inheritance for his sons than a greater but less secure one.

## Hiero · reference

Gelo's brother and successor as tyrant of Syracuse, whose spies and informers are cited as a model of tyrannical surveillance; succeeded in turn by his own brother Thrasybulus.

## Thrasybulus (Syracuse) · reference

Hiero's brother, who flattered Gelo's son into excess in order to rule through him, provoking the family conspiracy that ended the dynasty (5:63); he later succeeded Hiero as tyrant in his own right, only to be driven out within eleven months (5:76). A different, unrelated Thrasybulus of Miletus is Book 5's other, far more frequently cited Thrasybulus.

## Myron · reference

The tyrant of Sicyon whose rule gave way to that of Cleisthenes of Sicyon.

## Cleisthenes of Sicyon · reference

The Orthagorid tyrant of Sicyon, respected for his military ability and said to have crowned the judge who ruled against him in the games. His rule followed Myron's (5:78) within the century-long Orthagorid tyranny (5:74). A different, unrelated Cleisthenes -- his own grandson, the Athenian democratic reformer -- appears in Books 3 and 6.

## Antileon · reference

His tyranny at Chalcis is cited as an example of a tyranny changing into an oligarchy.

## Cleander · reference

His tyranny at Gela is cited as an example of an oligarchy changing into a tyranny.

## Anaxilaus · reference

His tyranny at Rhegium is cited as an example of an oligarchy changing into a tyranny.

## Orthagoras · reference

Founder of the century-long Orthagorid tyranny at Sicyon, the longest-lasting of all -- attributed to the family's moderation and general observance of the laws.

## The Cypselidae · reference

The tyrant dynasty of Corinth founded by Cypselus, lasting seventy-three and a half years across Cypselus, Periander, and Psammetichus.

## Psammetichus · reference

Son of Gordius, the third and last of the Cypselid tyrants of Corinth, reigning three years after Periander.

## Oxylus · reference

Credited with an early law forbidding a man to borrow against a fixed portion of his property.

## Archilochus · reference

The poet, cited on the pleasures of leisure that follow the toil of war.

## Hippocrates · reference

The physician, cited as an example of a kind of "greatness" -- excellence in one's function -- that has nothing to do with physical size.

## Stentor · reference

The Homeric herald proverbial for a loud voice, invoked as a comparison for what statesmen wanting only more citizens would need.

## Sesostris · reference

The legendary Egyptian king credited with founding the warrior-versus-husbandmen class system later found in Egypt and Crete.

## Italus · reference

The legendary king of Oenotria after whom Italy is said to be named; credited with converting the Oenotrians from shepherds to husbandmen and founding their common meals.

## Scylax · reference

Cited for his claim that Indian kings hold no marked superiority over their subjects.

## Thibron · reference

Among the writers on the Spartan constitution who praised its legislator for the power hard training won the Lacedaemonians.

## Theodorus · reference

The tragic actor, who would not let any other actor -- however minor -- perform before him, since audiences grow fond of the first voice they hear.

## Odysseus · reference

The Homeric hero, quoted on the pleasure of a banquet with music.

## Musaeus · reference

The legendary poet, quoted on song as one of the pleasantest things.

## Olympus · reference

A musician (or composer) whose melodies are cited as proof that music can inspire real enthusiasm and moral feeling.

## Pauson · reference

A painter whose work young men should not be encouraged to look at, contrasted unfavourably with Polygnotus.

## Polygnotus · reference

A painter praised for expressing moral character in his work, contrasted favourably with Pauson.

## Archytas · reference

Credited with inventing the child's rattle -- a toy for the infant mind, as musical education is a toy for children of a larger growth.

## Philoxenus · reference

A dithyrambic poet who, having tried to compose his Tales in the Dorian mode, found it impossible and fell back into the more suitable Phrygian.

## Thrasippus · reference

Dedicated a tablet commemorating his sponsorship of a chorus for Ecphantides, cited as evidence of the flute's popularity at Athens.

## Ecphantides · reference

The Old Comedy poet for whom Thrasippus furnished a chorus.

## Athene · reference

The goddess, said in myth to have invented the flute and then thrown it away, on the reasoning (Aristotle's own gloss) that flute-playing contributes nothing to the mind.
