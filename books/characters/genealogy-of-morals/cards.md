# On the Genealogy of Morals: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Paul Rée · reference

German philosopher and physician whose 1877 book The Origin of the Moral Emotions provoked Nietzsche's own inquiry into the origin of morality.

## Arthur Schopenhauer · major

The philosopher of pessimism and the will, called Nietzsche's early teacher in the Preface and examined at length in the Third Essay as a philosopher who paid homage to the ascetic ideal.

## Plato · reference

The Greek philosopher, cited repeatedly as an early opponent of pity and, in the Third Essay, as art's greatest enemy and the antithesis of Homer.

## Baruch Spinoza · reference

The philosopher whose placement of good and evil in human imagination, and whose account of the sting of conscience, Nietzsche discusses at length.

## La Rochefoucauld · reference

The French moralist named among the philosophers who held pity in contempt before it became fashionable to praise it.

## Immanuel Kant · reference

The philosopher whose categorical imperative, definition of the beautiful, and critique of theological dogmatism Nietzsche repeatedly takes up and disputes.

## Zarathustra · reference

The prophet-figure of Nietzsche's own Thus Spoke Zarathustra, invoked at the close of the Second Essay as a voice reserved for what this book leaves unsaid.

## Herbert Spencer · reference

The English philosopher whose evolutionary account of 'good' as the useful, and of life as adaptation, Nietzsche disputes by name.

## Henry Thomas Buckle · reference

The English historian Nietzsche cites as the case in which modern 'plebeian' prejudice broke out most violently in historical writing.

## Theognis · reference

The Greek poet of Megara whom Nietzsche credits with first making 'the truthful' the nobility's own name for itself.

## Rudolf Virchow · reference

The German pathologist and anthropologist Nietzsche disputes on the racial ancestry of Germany's dark-haired population.

## Jesus of Nazareth · reference

Discussed as the culmination of the Jewish 'transvaluation of values' and, via a quoted early Christian text, as the crucified god whose blood believers claim.

## Peter · reference

The apostle, named among the figures before whom, Nietzsche says, 'almost half the world' now bows, and later mocked as an insufferably self-important New Testament voice.

## Paul · reference

The apostle, named among the figures before whom, Nietzsche says, 'almost half the world' now bows.

## Mary · reference

The mother of Jesus, named as the fourth of the figures before whom Nietzsche says 'almost half the world' now bows.

## Napoleon · reference

Napoleon Bonaparte, presented as the modern reappearance of the aristocratic ideal, 'that synthesis of Monster and Superman.'

## Pericles · reference

The Athenian statesman, quoted from his funeral oration as an example of aristocratic audacity and contempt for safety.

## Hesiod · reference

The Greek poet who divided the ages of man into gold, silver and bronze, which Nietzsche reads as a device for accommodating two incompatible views of the heroic age.

## Mirabeau · reference

The French Revolutionary orator, cited as a modern example of a strong nature who forgives only because he forgets.

## Homer · reference

The Greek poet, cited for his gods' amused view of human folly, as the creator of Achilles, and as the artist Nietzsche opposes to Plato.

## Dante · reference

The Italian poet, whose inscription over the gate of Hell in the Divine Comedy Nietzsche calls a crass mistake.

## Thomas of Aquinas · reference

The medieval theologian and saint, quoted on the blessed in heaven taking pleasure in witnessing the torments of the damned.

## Tertullian · reference

The early Christian writer, quoted at length in Latin from De Spectaculis, anticipating the torment of pagan emperors, philosophers, actors and poets after the Last Judgment.

## Don Quixote · reference

The hero of Cervantes' novel, invoked through the episode at the Duchess's court as a book its first readers could enjoy as pure comedy.

## Pope Innocent III · reference

The medieval pope quoted for his catalogue of the human body's indignities, from conception to decay.

## Martin Luther · reference

Discussed both as a plain-spoken, quarrelsome opponent of Church etiquette and, through the unrealized 'Luther's Wedding,' as a subject Wagner once considered setting to music.

## John Calvin · reference

Named alongside Luther as a source worth consulting on the idea of gods who delight in cruelty.

## Heracles · reference

The Greek hero, invoked as a figure of arduous labor performed under the gaze of witnesses: 'the Heracles of duty.'

## Kuno Fischer · reference

The German historian of philosophy whose commentary on Spinoza Nietzsche accuses of laboring to misunderstand him.

## Eugen Dühring · reference

The German philosopher, later a leading anti-Semitic agitator, whose theory that justice originates in resentment Nietzsche attacks by name in both the Second and Third Essays.

## Thomas Henry Huxley · reference

Cited for his reproach to Herbert Spencer over Spencer's 'administrative Nihilism.'

## Johann Wolfgang von Goethe · reference

Cited on the 'labyrinth of the breast,' as the creator of Faust, and for his claim that there are only thirty-six tragic situations.

## Faust · reference

The protagonist of Goethe's drama, invoked to argue that an artist need not himself be what he creates.

## Achilles · reference

The hero of Homer's Iliad, invoked as a figure of uncompromising boldness and, later, as an example of a character an author need not resemble.

## Aegisthus · reference

The figure Homer's Zeus cites as an example of mortals blaming the gods for disasters of their own making.

## Zeus · reference

The king of the Greek gods, quoted via Homer refusing to take the blame for mortal folly, and invoked as an alternative name for blind chance.

## Heraclitus · reference

The Greek philosopher of flux, cited for likening the world to a child’s game and for having withdrawn to the temple of Artemis at Ephesus.

## Adam · reference

The biblical first man, named as the ancestor on whom the curse of humanity's unpayable debt to God is fixed ('original sin').

## Richard Wagner · major

The composer, examined at length as an artist whose late turn to chastity and to the opera Parsifal exemplifies homage to the ascetic ideal.

## Ludwig Feuerbach · reference

The philosopher whose motto of 'healthy sensuality' Wagner followed enthusiastically in his youth.

## Georg Herwegh · reference

The poet credited with introducing Wagner to Schopenhauer's philosophy.

## Stendhal · reference

The French novelist, whose definition of the beautiful as 'a promise of happiness' Nietzsche sets against Kant's disinterested formula.

## Pygmalion · reference

The sculptor of Greek myth who fell in love with his own statue, cited as evidence against the claim that beauty is contemplated 'without interest.'

## Epicurus · reference

The Greek philosopher, quoted by Schopenhauer on the painless state as the highest good, and later compared to Indian doctrines of redemption through sleep.

## Ixion · reference

The mythical king condemned to an eternally turning wheel, invoked by Schopenhauer's own words as an image of the will's suffering.

## G. W. F. Hegel · reference

Named among the 'enemies' Nietzsche says Schopenhauer needed in order to keep going.

## René Descartes · reference

Listed among the great philosophers who never married.

## Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz · reference

Listed among the great philosophers who never married.

## Socrates · reference

The Athenian philosopher, cited as the one exception among philosophers who married, said to have done so, ironically, only to prove the rule.

## Buddha · reference

The founder of Buddhism, quoted on the birth of his son as a fetter, and on leaving the confinement of the household for freedom.

## King Vicvamitra · reference

The legendary Indian king who, through a thousand years of self-martyrdom, gained the power to build a new heaven.

## Shankara · reference

The Indian philosopher whose commentaries on redemption through unity with Brahman Nietzsche quotes, via Paul Deussen's translation.

## Paul Deussen · reference

Nietzsche's friend, described here as the first real European expert on Indian philosophy, whose translation of Shankara's commentaries Nietzsche quotes.

## Silas Weir Mitchell · reference

The American physician after whom the isolating 'rest cure,' invoked as a comparison for priestly asceticism, is named.

## Blaise Pascal · reference

Quoted for the ascetic maxim 'il faut s'abêtir' ('one must play the fool').

## Teresa of Ávila · reference

The Spanish mystic whose ecstasies Nietzsche cites as a symptom of ascetic self-starvation.

## Arnold Geulincx · reference

The Flemish philosopher whose phrase for self-contempt, 'despectus sui,' Nietzsche borrows.

## Lord Byron · reference

The English poet, said to have written a fully honest personal autobiography.

## Thomas Moore · reference

The Irish poet and Byron's literary executor, who burned his friend's papers rather than let the autobiography survive.

## Wilhelm von Gwinner · reference

Schopenhauer's literary executor, said to have likewise destroyed self-revealing papers.

## Alexander Wheelock Thayer · reference

The American biographer of Beethoven, said to have abandoned his life of the composer partway through rather than continue.

## Ludwig van Beethoven · reference

The composer, named as the subject of Thayer's unfinished biography.

## Johannes Janssen · reference

The Catholic historian whose history of the German Reformation Nietzsche calls harmless and square.

## Hippolyte Taine · reference

The French historian and critic cited as a model of fearless, unflinching biography.

## Leopold von Ranke · reference

The German historian Nietzsche calls the classic advocate of 'every causa fortior' — every accomplished fact — and 'the cleverest of all the clever opportunists.'

## Nicolaus Copernicus · reference

Named as the point from which Nietzsche traces an unbroken progress in man's self-diminishment, after displacing the earth from the center of the universe.

## Ximénès Doudan · reference

The French essayist quoted on the danger of admiring the unintelligible instead of resting content in the unknown.

## Leo Tolstoy · reference

Named for his late religious 'pity,' one of the few things Nietzsche says still grows in the barren landscape of modern historical writing.

## Ernest Renan · reference

The French historian of religion Nietzsche mocks, via the phrase 'Renan parfum,' as a scented, half-priest, half-satyr type among modern historians.

## Anacreon · reference

The Greek lyric poet, invoked mock-devotionally ('by St. Anacreon') as patron of kicking rather than merely running away.

## Charles the Bold · reference

Duke of Burgundy, whose defiant words against Louis XI ('I fight the universal spider') Nietzsche borrows for humanity's stance toward God.

## Louis XI · reference

King of France, Charles the Bold's adversary in the quoted line about fighting 'the universal spider.'
