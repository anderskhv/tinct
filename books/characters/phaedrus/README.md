# Phaedrus recognition cards

Six local sections and 408 paragraphs in each English edition. 89 original / 88 modern entries; 656 / 657 exact mentions. The modern text omits the translator's Cicero citation, so that Reference is original-only. Authoring-agent source review, not independent editorial approval; source editions unchanged and runtime registration pending.

Socrates and Phaedrus are Central. Lysias is Major because his written speech and its criticism structure much of the work, though he is absent from the conversation. Other named people and divine figures, along with the expressly imagined speech roles and allegories, are Reference. All cards remain single short recognition reminders; no argument-by-argument snapshots.

- Phaedrus is Socrates' companion and an admirer of speeches. He is not automatically described as a young man: the dialogue's fictional beautiful youths are distinct from him. The imagined speaker in Lysias's speech and the concealed lover in Socrates' invented story are separate roles.
- Acumenus is the physician and father of Eryximachus. Hippocrates is the physician, Herodicus the advocate of exercise, and Licymnius the rhetorical teacher; namesakes from mythology are not imported. Cephalus is Lysias and Polemarchus's father.
- Vain Man is the translation's playful rendering for Phaedrus's father, Pythocles. Godly Man and Euphemus name Stesichorus's father, not the Argonaut. Myrrhina and Himera/Desire are place-name wordplay, not additional people.
- Original Here means Hera only in the invocation at 1:32 and the followers-of-Here phrase at 4:21. Other Here occurrences remain ordinary words. Modern Hera binds at the same two positions.
- Eleatic Palamedes in 5:58 resolves to Zeno of Elea. The earlier Palamedes remains the mythical hero. Nestor and Odysseus in comparisons with Gorgias, Thrasymachus and Theodorus stay as references to the heroic figures, not aliases merging those rhetoricians. The Byzantian/Byzantine word-maker is Theodorus; the Chalcedonian giant is Thrasymachus.
- Theuth is the Egyptian god Thoth. Thamus and Ammon are one figure in this story. Their cards identify the roles without summarizing the verdict on writing. The bronze maiden is the speaking tomb statue; Midas remains the king named in the inscription.
- The charioteer and two horses are explicitly figures in the soul analogy. Generic lover/non-lover plurals do not all bind to the invented individual suitors. Cicadas/grasshoppers are the insect group involved in the Muses story, not unidentified human conversationalists.
- Eros binds explicit names and selected personified Love passages, not every occurrence of the abstract emotion. Anteros, Modesty and Destiny are conceptual language here, not automatically added deities. God is a general divine reference; the card does not force every philosophical use into Zeus or a Christian identity.
- Cratylus and Charmides occur as translator's cross-reference titles in the original and are not characters present in these passages. The abbreviated Cic. citation is Cicero. No claim is made to resolve every pronoun, generic philosophical example or anonymous collective.
