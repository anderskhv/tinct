# Phaedrus: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## Socrates · central

The Athenian philosopher discussing love, the soul and rhetoric with Phaedrus.

## Phaedrus · central

Socrates’ companion, an admirer of Lysias with a passion for speeches.

## Lysias · major

The Athenian speechwriter whose speech on love Phaedrus brings to the discussion.

## Cephalus · reference

Lysias and Polemarchus’s father.

## Acumenus · reference

The physician who is a friend of Socrates and Phaedrus, and Eryximachus’s father.

## Epicrates · reference

The Athenian hosting Lysias in the house associated with Morychus.

## Morychus · reference

The Athenian whose house is mentioned as the place where Lysias is staying.

## Pindar · reference

The Greek lyric poet quoted by Socrates.

## Herodicus · reference

The physician known for prescribing exercise, recalled for his walking regimen.

## Sappho · reference

The Greek lyric poet whom Socrates invokes as a source of inspiration about love.

## Anacreon · reference

The Greek lyric poet associated with love and song.

## Simmias of Thebes · reference

The friend of Socrates recalled as an unusually eager lover of argument.

## Ibycus · reference

The Greek lyric poet whom Socrates quotes.

## Homer · reference

The ancient Greek epic poet repeatedly cited in the conversation.

## Stesichorus · reference

The Greek lyric poet whose recantation about Helen inspires Socrates’ second speech.

## Pythocles · reference

Phaedrus’s father, playfully rendered as Vain Man in this translation.

## Euphemus · reference

Stesichorus’s father, rendered as Godly Man in the wordplay.

## Polemarchus · reference

Lysias’s brother, whom Socrates presents as interested in philosophy.

## Lycurgus · reference

The traditional Spartan lawgiver named in the discussion of enduring laws.

## Solon · reference

The Athenian lawgiver and poet.

## Darius I · reference

The Persian king named as an example of a lawgiver.

## Gorgias · reference

The celebrated Greek teacher of rhetoric, from Leontini.

## Thrasymachus · reference

The teacher of rhetoric from Chalcedon, also called the Chalcedonian giant.

## Theodorus of Byzantium · reference

The teacher of rhetoric whose elaborate divisions of a speech are discussed.

## Zeno of Elea · reference

The philosopher renowned for paradoxes, playfully called the Eleatic Palamedes.

## Evenus of Paros · reference

The poet and teacher of rhetoric credited with indirect praise and censure.

## Tisias · reference

The Sicilian teacher of rhetoric associated here with arguments from probability.

## Prodicus · reference

The sophist known for distinctions between words and their meanings.

## Hippias of Elis · reference

The sophist and teacher called the Elean stranger.

## Polus · reference

The teacher of rhetoric whose collection of technical terms is discussed.

## Licymnius · reference

The poet and teacher of rhetoric credited with supplying Polus’s terminology.

## Protagoras · reference

The sophist cited for rules of correct expression.

## Eryximachus · reference

The physician who is Phaedrus’s friend and Acumenus’s son.

## Sophocles · reference

The Athenian tragic playwright used as an example of mastery of a whole art.

## Euripides · reference

The Athenian tragic playwright named alongside Sophocles.

## Pericles · reference

The Athenian statesman and orator whose education Socrates praises.

## Anaxagoras · reference

The philosopher whose teaching about nature and mind influenced Pericles.

## Hippocrates · reference

The Greek physician whose approach to understanding the body is compared with rhetoric.

## Isocrates · reference

The orator whom Socrates names as a promising friend at the close of the dialogue.

## Cicero · reference

The Roman author cited in the translator’s note about the Tusculan Disputations.

## Zeus · reference

The chief Olympian god, who leads the divine procession in Socrates’ account of the soul.

## Boreas · reference

The Greek god of the north wind, linked with Orithyia in the local legend.

## Orithyia · reference

The Athenian princess carried away by Boreas in the legend discussed beside the stream.

## Artemis · reference

The Greek goddess whose nearby temple is mentioned.

## Pharmacia · reference

Orithyia’s companion in the version of the story Socrates considers.

## The centaurs · reference

The half-human, half-horse beings called Hippocentaurs in the discussion of myths.

## The chimeras · reference

The composite monsters mentioned among stories that resist rational explanation.

## The Gorgons · reference

The fearsome female monsters of Greek myth.

## Typhon · reference

The monstrous being called Typho, with whom Socrates compares his own unexplored nature.

## Hera · reference

The Olympian goddess and wife of Zeus, called Here in the original translation.

## Achelous · reference

The river god honored at the place where Socrates and Phaedrus rest.

## The Nymphs · reference

The nature divinities associated with the stream and the speakers’ inspiration.

## The Muses · reference

The goddesses of poetry, music and inspired learning.

## Eros · reference

The god of love, son of Aphrodite in the account discussed here.

## Aphrodite · reference

The Greek goddess of love and beauty.

## Helen · reference

Helen of Troy, the woman whom Stesichorus reviles and then praises in his recantation.

## The Sibyl · reference

The inspired prophetess cited as an example of divine madness.

## Hestia · reference

The goddess of the hearth, who remains at home in the divine procession.

## Ares · reference

The Greek god of war, whose followers Socrates describes.

## Apollo · reference

The Greek god associated with prophecy and inspiration.

## Ganymede · reference

The beautiful youth loved by Zeus in the myth recalled by Socrates.

## Odysseus · reference

The Homeric hero renowned for resourceful speech, used in comparisons with rhetoricians.

## Terpsichore · reference

The Muse associated here with dancers in the story of the cicadas.

## Erato · reference

The Muse associated here with lovers in the story of the cicadas.

## Calliope · reference

The eldest Muse, associated here with philosophy and elevated discourse.

## Urania · reference

The Muse associated with heaven and thought in Socrates’ account.

## Nestor · reference

The wise old king of Pylos in Homer, used as a model of eloquent speech.

## Palamedes · reference

The Greek hero famous for ingenuity, named in the comparison about rhetoric.

## Pan · reference

The rustic god invoked in Socrates’ closing prayer.

## Hermes · reference

The Greek messenger god invoked in the conversation.

## Midas · reference

The Phrygian king named in the quoted tomb inscription.

## Dionysus · reference

The Greek god associated with initiatory religious madness.

## Adrastus · reference

The legendary king of Argos praised for his honeyed eloquence.

## Theuth · reference

The Egyptian god, usually called Thoth, credited with inventing writing in Socrates’ story.

## Thamus · reference

The Egyptian king and god who judges Theuth’s inventions, identified here with Ammon.

## Adonis · reference

The mythological youth honored in the short-lived gardens used as Socrates’ comparison.

## God · reference

The divine nature or a divine patron invoked in Socrates’ account of souls.

## The Cypselids · reference

The ruling family of ancient Corinth, recalled for its lavish offerings at Olympia.

## The youth in Lysias’s speech · reference

The imagined young man addressed by the speaker in Lysias’s speech.

## The speaker in Lysias’s speech · reference

The imagined suitor who presents himself as a non-lover.

## The youth in Socrates’ speech · reference

The imagined young man addressed in Socrates’ speeches about love.

## The concealed lover · reference

The suitor in Socrates’ invented story who pretends not to love the youth.

## The Delphic priestess · reference

The prophetess of Apollo at Delphi, cited as an example of inspired speech.

## The priestesses of Dodona · reference

The women associated with the oracle at Dodona in the discussion of divine inspiration.

## The charioteer · reference

The driver in Socrates’ image of the soul, trying to guide its pair of winged horses.

## The noble horse · reference

The obedient horse in the image of the human soul, responsive to guidance and restraint.

## The unruly horse · reference

The resistant horse in the image of the human soul, pulling against the driver’s guidance.

## The cicadas · reference

The singing insects, called grasshoppers in the tale, whom Socrates connects with the Muses.

## The bronze maiden · reference

The speaking statue in the quoted inscription on Midas’s tomb.
