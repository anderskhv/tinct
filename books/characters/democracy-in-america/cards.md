# Democracy in America: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## George Washington · reference

President of the Constitutional Convention and first President of the United States, cited for his conduct and his Farewell Address's foreign-policy maxims; bare "Washington" binds here as the dominant sense (see the namesake note above for the one place-reference to the city that shares the surname).

## Thomas Jefferson · reference

Cited repeatedly as an authority in his own right (his "Notes on Virginia"), as the third President, and for his letters to Madison and Lafayette on the instability of laws and on the loyalty of the Eastern states.

## Alexander Hamilton · reference

Named among the fifty-five Constitutional Convention delegates, among the three authors (with Jay and Madison) of The Federalist, and, per a footnote, one of the Constitution's principal founders.

## James Madison · reference

Named among the Convention delegates and Federalist authors, and cited for a letter from Jefferson to him on the instability of American laws and on executive power.

## John Jay · reference

Named as one of the three authors, with Hamilton and Madison, of The Federalist.

## William Penn · reference

Received by the Lenape a hundred and fifty years before Tocqueville's writing, on the banks of a river -- cited in the chapter on the fate of Native American tribes.

## Benjamin Franklin · reference

Cited for his own attempt to establish a single legislative House, and for his Autobiography as a source.

## James Monroe · reference

Named as the fourth of the early Presidents (with Washington, Jefferson, and Madison) associated with the Union's founding generation.

## John Quincy Adams · reference

"Mr. Quincy Adams," cited for dismissing federal officeholders on entering office in 1825 -- one of only two such wholesale removals Tocqueville could find before Jefferson's own in 1801.

## Andrew Jackson · reference

"General Jackson," twice elected President, cited for his restrained use of the removal power compared to his predecessor, and for his conduct in a public affair Tocqueville criticizes as "that of a heartless despot."

## James Buchanan · reference

Cited for retaining office, after Lincoln's election, from November 1860 to March 1861 -- a period Tocqueville's argument suggests could have enabled a dangerous abuse of power.

## Abraham Lincoln · reference

His 1860 election is cited as the point after which Buchanan remained in office; a separate translator's note observes that his election "was the signal of civil war," a later historical event Tocqueville's own text anticipates but does not narrate.

## Andrew Johnson · reference

His 1868 impeachment is cited in a bracketed translator's note as an example -- occurring after Tocqueville's own death -- of the kind of political crisis his analysis anticipated.

## John C. Calhoun · reference

Vice-President and head of the Southern nullification party, quoted for a sentence summarizing that party's position before the Senate.

## Henry Clay · reference

"Mr. Clay," credited with bringing in the bill (the Missouri Compromise) that passed both Houses of Congress within four days.

## Robert E. Lee · reference

"General Lee," cited in a bracketed translator's note on 1862 events as holding that his primary allegiance was owed to Virginia rather than to the Union -- illustrating, after the fact, the sectional loyalty Tocqueville's chapter describes.

## Benedict Arnold · reference

"The treacherous Arnold," invoked by an opposition faction as an insulting comparison for Washington's administration, per Marshall.

## Edward Everett · reference

"Mr. Edward Everett," cited for an 1830 statement to the House of Representatives on Native American population figures.

## Mr. Richards · reference

Mayor of Philadelphia, thanked by Tocqueville for supplying thirteen years of city budget figures.

## Chancellor Kent · reference

Author of the "Commentaries" cited constantly, alongside Story, as a legal authority throughout the Federal Constitution chapter's footnotes.

## Justice Joseph Story · reference

"Judge of the Supreme Court of the United States," author of the "Commentaries on the Constitution" cited even more frequently than Kent as the chapter's principal legal authority.

## Thomas Sergeant · reference

Cited for a work on the constitution and jurisdiction of the United States courts.

## Henry Wheaton · reference

Named with Kent and Story as an authority whose truthfulness on a particular point Tocqueville vouches for.

## Blackstone · reference

Cited, with Delolme, on a point of English constitutional law.

## Delolme · reference

Cited, with Blackstone, on English constitutional law.

## William Stith · reference

Author of a "History of Virginia" cited for the character of early Virginia's adventurer-colonists.

## Robert Beverley · reference

Author of another "History of Virginia, from the Earliest Period," cited alongside Stith and Jefferson's own "Notes."

## Nathaniel Morton · reference

"The historian of the first years of the settlement," cited for his account of the Plymouth colonists landing on a barren coast.

## Thomas Hutchinson · reference

Author of a "History of the Colony of Massachusetts Bay," cited repeatedly on early Massachusetts law and governance.

## Timothy Pitkin · reference

Author of a "History" cited repeatedly on colonial charters and the founding of several colonies.

## George Chalmers · reference

Cited (as printed, "Chalmer") for early Virginia's importation of enslaved people.

## Ebenezer Hazard · reference

Author of a 1792 historical documents collection cited for colonial records.

## Cotton Mather · reference

Author of "Magnalia Christi Americana," cited for a speech made by Winthrop defending himself against a charge of arbitrary conduct.

## John Winthrop · reference

Massachusetts colonial governor, whose self-defense against a charge of arbitrary conduct is quoted via Mather.

## Captain John Smith · reference

Author of a "History of Virginia, from the First Settlements in the year 1624," cited among the early colonial historians.

## John Marshall · reference

Author of a "Life of Washington" cited repeatedly, including for Washington's own letters and for the opposition's comparison of his administration to Benedict Arnold.

## Isaac Goodwin · reference

Author of "The Town-Officer" (Worcester, 1827), cited on New England town governance.

## Malte-Brun · reference

The geographer, cited repeatedly (as printed, without a hyphen, "Malte Brun") on Caribbean waters, American population density, and European emigration rates.

## William Darby · reference

Author of "View of the United States," cited repeatedly on American population and territory statistics.

## Alexander von Humboldt · reference

Cited, with Malte-Brun and Fischer, on the unresolved question of Native American origins.

## Fischer · reference

Author of a "Conjecture sur l'Origine des Americains," cited alongside Humboldt and Adair on Native American origins.

## James Adair · reference

Author of a "History of the American Indians," cited on Native American origins.

## Le Page du Pratz · reference

Author of "Histoire de la Louisiane" (printed "Lepage Dupratz"), cited among the sources for Native American history.

## Charlevoix · reference

Author of "Histoire de la Nouvelle France," cited repeatedly for early French colonial and Native American history.

## John Heckewelder · reference

The Moravian missionary (cited as printed, "Rev. G. Hecwelder"), whose letters on Native Americans are cited as a source.

## Volney · reference

Author of "Tableau des États-Unis," cited repeatedly (and by name, "M. de Volney") on Native American population decline and customs, from whom Tocqueville says he borrows some details directly.

## John Tanner · reference

A European carried off by Native Americans at age six, whose memoirs Tocqueville cites at length as evidence of Native American decline and hardship; Tocqueville reports having met him personally at Lake Superior.

## William Clark · reference

Co-author (with Cass) of an 1829 report to Congress on Native American affairs, quoted repeatedly on tribal displacement and customs.

## Lewis Cass · reference

Co-author (with Clark) of the same 1829 report; identified as "now the Secretary of War" at the time of Tocqueville's writing.

## Mr. Bell · reference

Author of an 1829 report to the Committee on Indian Affairs, cited independently on the pace of Native American displacement.

## Metacom · reference

The Wampanoag leader (called "King Philip" by the English), who led the confederated tribes' 1675 war against the New England colonists.

## René Descartes · reference

Cited repeatedly on method in philosophy, grouped with Bacon, Luther, and Voltaire as thinkers who broke from received authority -- though Tocqueville notes Americans read his method more than his books.

## Blaise Pascal · reference

Quoted four times across widely separated chapters -- on truth fleeing the mind's grasp, on the stakes of religious belief, and on the advantages of noble birth.

## Francis Bacon · reference

Named alongside Descartes as a great seventeenth-century method-reformer, this time in the natural sciences.

## Voltaire · reference

Grouped with Luther and Descartes as sharing a common method of breaking from inherited authority.

## Martin Luther · reference

Grouped with Descartes and Voltaire as a method-reformer; separately, Tocqueville speculates how he might have fared "in an age of equality."

## Montesquieu · reference

Cited three times: on absolute power's tendency to destroy religion, on despondency under the Roman Caesars, and on the nature of authority in societies without an intervening class.

## Plutarch · reference

Quoted three times: on Archimedes's lofty contempt for practical invention, on martial prowess in his "Life of Coriolanus," and for his "Lives of Great Commanders" as evidence of ancient military familiarity between officers and men.

## Montaigne · reference

Quoted on following "the straight road" rather than a winding one.

## Socrates · reference

Cited for the uncertain fixity of his and his followers' philosophical opinions.

## Cicero · reference

Cited for declaiming against the cruelty of crucifying a Roman citizen -- in contrast, per Tocqueville, to indifference toward the same cruelty inflicted on slaves.

## Machiavelli · reference

Quoted from "The Prince" on the greater difficulty of conducting protracted warfare among neighboring, formidable states.

## Archimedes · reference

Quoted via Plutarch for his lofty disdain of the merely practical uses of his inventions.

## Tacitus · reference

Cited for his description of ancient Germanic tribal customs, compared by Tocqueville to Native American customs he has personally observed.

## Aesop · reference

Named, with Terence, among distinguished ancient authors who were or had been enslaved.

## Terence · reference

Named with Aesop as a distinguished ancient author who had been enslaved.

## Aulus Gellius · reference

Cited via a quotation in Racine's preface to "Britannicus" on a point of Roman religious custom.

## Cabanis · reference

Cited for the view that thought is a secretion of the brain, which Tocqueville treats as one extreme of materialist philosophy.

## La Bruyère · reference

Cited (as printed, "Labruyere") for satirizing the vices and follies of his times while living within Louis XIV's own palace.

## Molière · reference

Named alongside La Bruyère for criticizing courtiers in plays performed before those same courtiers.

## Jean Racine · reference

Cited for his humble preface to "Britannicus," apologizing for a detail about the Vestal Junia's age.

## William Shakespeare · reference

Cited for the presence of his plays even in a frontier log-house, and for Tocqueville's own memory of reading "Henry V" there.

## John Milton · reference

Credited with introducing more than six hundred new words into the English language -- evidence, for Tocqueville, of an era before democratic language change slowed such invention.

## Jacques-Louis David · reference

The Neoclassical painter, contrasted with Raphael as a superior anatomist who depicted models with exact fidelity rather than idealizing them -- not the biblical King David, who is not otherwise named in this book; see the namesake note above.

## Raphael · reference

The painter, contrasted with David's group as pursuing an ideal beauty beyond mere anatomical accuracy.

## Napoleon · reference

Cited for causing jurors to be returned to service by his own agents (echoing an English royal practice), and, separately, as the reputed inventor of a new, more decisive system of warfare.

## Sulla · reference

Named with Caesar as an exemplar of seizing supreme power by force -- a generic historical byword, not a narrative reference to his own specific acts.

## Philip of Macedon · reference

Bare "Philip," cited for undertaking to execute the decree of the Amphictyons in ancient Greece -- distinct from the Wampanoag leader Metacom, whom the English called "King Philip" but who is never named "Philip" in this book itself; see the namesake note above.

## Henry IV of France · reference

Named alongside Louis XIV as a monarch whose absolute style of rule some hoped, mistakenly per Tocqueville, could be revived.

## Henry VIII · reference

Named with Elizabeth as an English monarch under whom, absent the civil jury, English liberty would have perished.

## Elizabeth I · reference

Named with Henry VIII as a monarch under whom the civil jury preserved English liberty in reality, if not always in law.

## Louis XIV · reference

Named alongside Henry IV as a monarch whose absolutism some hoped, mistakenly, to revive; also the monarch in whose palace La Bruyère lived while composing his satirical chapter on the great.

## Madame de Sévigné · reference

The letter-writer, quoted at length (as printed, "Sevigne") describing, to her daughter, a brutal punishment she witnessed with what Tocqueville stresses was not personal cruelty but a failure to extend full human sympathy across class lines.

## the Marquis de Lafayette · reference

Recipient of a Jefferson letter on wartime state loyalties, quoted directly; separately cited for his own "Memoirs."

## Margaret Bedford · reference

Convicted of "loose conduct" by a 1643 New Haven court and sentenced to be whipped and to marry her accomplice, Nicholas Jemmings -- cited as an example of colonial New England's moral-legislation severity.

## Nicholas Jemmings · reference

Margaret Bedford's accomplice, whom the same 1643 court sentence ordered her to marry.
