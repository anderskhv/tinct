# Democracy in America character package

All 96 chapters in both English editions -- 86 recognition cards in
each edition, zero omitted on either side, 253 exact mentions in
original-en and 254 in modern-en, 2258 paragraphs per edition,
identical structure in both.

Read `cards.md` for category and copy together.

This is a work of political and social analysis with no narrative or
staged dialogue of its own: Tocqueville argues a case, illustrating it
with American and European history, law, and literature. Per editorial
policy's guidance for treatises, **all 86 entries are Reference.**
There is no Central, Major, or Supporting entry, and no fictional cast
is invented.

As the automation queue's own note anticipated ("Named Americans and
Europeans; watch place-versus-person"), this book names a genuinely
large and varied real cast, but remains **reference-light** relative
to its size. A full-book capitalized-word frequency sweep of
original-en turned up ~1547 distinct capitalized word forms --
comparable to The Wealth of Nations's ~1463 despite this book being
somewhat longer -- and the great majority of those are place names
(American states, rivers, towns; European countries), not people.

## Reading method

Every capitalized word or phrase in the original-en edition was
extracted and catalogued in a full-book sweep, since this book's very
long, very citation-dense chapters make a purely sequential read
impractical at this scale. Every candidate person-name was then
confirmed by reading its exact source-text context directly (never
assumed from the bare word), and cross-checked against the modern-en
edition at the same location. Shorter chapters throughout both volumes
were additionally read in fuller context during this process.

Volume I (chapters 1-20) is markedly denser than Volume II: its long
historical and legal chapters -- "Origin of the Anglo-Americans" (ch.
3, 86 paragraphs), "The Federal Constitution" (ch. 9, 238 paragraphs,
extremely dense with legal citations to Kent, Story, and The
Federalist), "Government of the Democracy in America" (ch. 14, 163
paragraphs), "Principal Causes Maintaining the Democratic Republic"
(ch. 18, 159 paragraphs), and especially "Future Condition of Three
Races in the United States" (ch. 19, 372 paragraphs, the book's single
longest chapter, covering Native American and slavery history in
depth) -- carry almost the entire named cast. Volume II's 75 short,
abstract chapters on democratic manners, individualism, and sentiment
name comparatively few real people, mostly single classical or
literary citations scattered one or two to a chapter.

## Cast overview

- **American founders and statesmen**, mostly from the Federal
  Constitution chapter's citations and Volume I's historical
  narrative: George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, Alexander Hamilton,
  James Madison, John Jay, William Penn, Benjamin Franklin.
- **U.S. Presidents named directly or via translator's notes on later
  events**: James Monroe, John Quincy Adams, Andrew Jackson, James
  Buchanan, Abraham Lincoln, Andrew Johnson (his 1868 impeachment is a
  bracketed translator's note, not Tocqueville's own text, but is
  bound as real paragraph content all the same).
- **American statesmen and military figures**: John C. Calhoun, Henry
  Clay, Robert E. Lee (his Confederate allegiance is likewise a
  bracketed translator's note on 1862 events), Benedict Arnold, Edward
  Everett, Mr. Richards (Mayor of Philadelphia).
- **Legal commentators cited constantly as authorities**, especially
  in the Federal Constitution chapter: Chancellor Kent, Justice Joseph
  Story, Thomas Sergeant, Henry Wheaton, Blackstone, Delolme.
- **Historians of colonial and early America**: William Stith, Robert
  Beverley, Nathaniel Morton, Thomas Hutchinson, Timothy Pitkin,
  George Chalmers, Ebenezer Hazard, Cotton Mather, John Winthrop,
  Captain John Smith, John Marshall (as biographer of Washington),
  Isaac Goodwin.
- **Geographers, naturalists, and travelers cited for American and
  Native American material**, overwhelmingly concentrated in Chapters
  2-3 and 18-19: Malte-Brun, William Darby, Alexander von Humboldt,
  Fischer, James Adair, Le Page du Pratz, Charlevoix, John
  Heckewelder, Constantin-François Volney, John Tanner (himself a
  captive ethnographic source, not merely a cited author), William
  Clark and Lewis Cass (jointly authoring an 1829 government report),
  Mr. Bell, and Metacom (the Wampanoag leader called "King Philip" by
  the English, leader of the 1675 war against the New England
  colonists).
- **Philosophers, scientists, and men of letters**, cited singly
  across many short Volume II chapters: René Descartes, Blaise Pascal,
  Francis Bacon, Voltaire, Martin Luther, Montesquieu, Plutarch,
  Montaigne, Socrates, Cicero, Machiavelli, Archimedes, Tacitus,
  Aesop, Terence, Aulus Gellius, Cabanis, La Bruyère, Molière, Jean
  Racine, William Shakespeare, John Milton, Jacques-Louis David (the
  painter, not King David -- see below), Raphael.
- **Historical rulers and generals cited as exempla**: Napoleon, Sulla
  and Julius Caesar (Caesar left unbound bare -- see below), Philip of
  Macedon, Henry IV of France, Henry VIII, Elizabeth I of England,
  Louis XIV.
- **French correspondents and memoirists**: Madame de Sévigné, the
  Marquis de Lafayette.
- **Minor named individuals from a single quoted anecdote or legal
  case**: Margaret Bedford and Nicholas Jemmings (a 1643 New Haven
  court record).

## Editorial checks

**1. Namesakes.** One genuine namesake risk was checked and resolved
cleanly, without needing the escape hatch:

- **"Washington" the person vs. Washington the city.** George
  Washington is named in full once (9, 3) and then referred to
  repeatedly by bare surname (14 person occurrences total: 9, 6; 14,
  149; 14, 152 x2; 14, 153; 14, 160 x2; 14, 161 x2; 19, 73; 19, 269).
  Four occurrences, however, name the city, not the man: "the House of
  Representatives of Washington" (14, 20); "The city of Washington was
  founded in 1800" (19, 256); "to Washington is 1,019 miles" (19,
  258); "the spot upon which Washington now stands" (19, 265). Because
  the person sense dominates 14-to-4 and no city entity exists to
  compete for the bind, bare "Washington" is bound to George
  Washington per the dominant-sense pattern used for ambiguous bare
  names in prior books in this queue; the four place occurrences are a
  documented, accepted imprecision.

Beyond this, every other recurring name was checked at each occurrence
for a single consistent referent (e.g. every "Jefferson," "Story,"
"Kent," "Cass," and "Adair" mention was individually confirmed to mean
the same person throughout). No other genuine collision was found.

**2. Person-versus-place/book/school/coin/animal.** Three checks, all
resolved as places, not people:

- **Philip of Macedon vs. Metacom ("King Philip").** Bare "Philip" (9,
  187) refers to Philip of Macedon executing the decree of the
  Amphictyons -- an ancient Greek reference. The Wampanoag leader whom
  the English called "King Philip" is instead named by his own name,
  Metacom (19, 43), throughout this book -- the two names never share
  a literal string, so no collision exists to resolve.
- **"Peter" and "Francis" are not people.** Chapter 2, paragraph 4
  lists American rivers -- "the Illinois, the St. Peter's, the St.
  Francis, and the Moingona" -- where "Peter" and "Francis" are part of
  river names (St. Peter's River, St. Francis River), not references
  to any person. Excluded entirely in both editions; no entity is
  created for either bare word.
- **"Chester" is a place, not a person.** "The county of Chester
  (State of New York)" (18, 68-69) is a place name; excluded in both
  editions.

**3. Scriptural and mythological figures.** One check, resolved as
distinct from any biblical figure: **Jacques-Louis David vs. King
David.** "David and his scholars" (32, 11) is explicitly the French
Neoclassical painter, contrasted with Raphael in a passage on
democratic art -- confirmed by context (anatomical realism in
painting, not any biblical or historical narrative). No King David
entity is created, since the biblical king is never separately named
in this book; "David" binds only to the painter. No other scriptural
or mythological figures appear as named individuals in this book
(biblical citation is largely absent from Tocqueville's own argument,
aside from the painter's namesake above).

**4. Ambiguous or generic references left unbound.** Bare "Caesar" (9,
52 and 84, 10) is used only generically -- once as a nickname jokingly
applied to prominent American citizens in conversation, once as a
byword (with Sulla) for seizing supreme power by force -- never as a
specific narrative reference to Julius Caesar's own life or acts. Left
unbound, consistent with how prior books in this queue have treated
purely illustrative or nickname uses of a famous name. "The two
Morrises" (9, 6, among the Constitutional Convention's fifty-five
delegates) are named only collectively, never individually by first
name; left unbound rather than guessed at. Minor co-signatories of a
single joint government report on Native American affairs (Messrs.
Kennerly, M'Coy, Wash Hood, and John Bell, 19, 79) are named only in
that one joint citation; only Mr. Bell, who recurs independently
elsewhere (19, 39 and 19, 96) with his own report, is bound as an
entity.

**5. Spot-read the bindings.** Twelve mentions per edition were
sampled at random (`random.seed(42)`) and checked against their full
source paragraph in context; all 24 resolved correctly. Every
namesake-sensitive entity (George Washington, Philip of Macedon,
Jacques-Louis David, Sulla) was additionally re-verified by querying
every one of its bound mention locations directly, not merely the
random sample -- confirming, among other things, that all 18
Washington mentions (14 person, 4 place) and both Caesar mentions
resolve exactly as described above.

**6. Both editions independently.** Both editions were read and their
proper names cross-checked independently. Original-en and modern-en
each bind all 86 entities with **zero omissions on either side.** This
book required markedly less alias-fixing work than Leviathan or The
Wealth of Nations: the two editions agree on nearly every proper
name's spelling. Two exceptions were found and aliased:

| Original-en | Modern-en |
|---|---|
| Sylla | Sulla |
| Labruyere | Labruyère |
| Moliere | Molière |
| Sevigne | Sévigné |

(Labruyere and Moliere are printed without accents in original-en but
accented in modern-en; both spellings are aliased to the same
entities.) No divergence was found in which edition names or omits a
real, resolvable person outright, once these four spelling variants
were aliased.

## Source notes, kept as printed

No compositor misprint was found in this book comparable to
Leviathan's "Herods"/"Damascus" or The Wealth of Nations's "Sir Waiter
Raleigh." The bracketed translator's notes on Robert E. Lee's
Confederate allegiance (1862) and Andrew Johnson's impeachment (1868)
are later editorial additions to the public-domain translation, not
Tocqueville's own 1835/1840 text, but they are literally part of the
paragraph text in both editions and are bound as legitimate content,
consistent with how this queue has treated editorial and translator
material embedded directly in the edition JSON.

## Full suite note

This book's own focused test suite (11 tests) passed cleanly and
`--check` is clean. Consistent with the established precedent for
every prior book in this session, the shared full-repo test suite
(`python3 -m unittest discover -s . -p 'test_*.py'`) was not run to
completion within a practical time budget, given its size across
concurrent Lane A/B sessions in this queue. This package is committed
on the strength of its own focused test suite, a clean `--check`, and
exhaustive manual verification: every capitalized word in original-en
extracted and read in context (never assumed from the bare word),
cross-checked against modern-en at the same location, every
namesake-sensitive entity's complete set of bound locations
individually re-queried and checked, and a random spot-read of 24
compiled mentions confirmed correct.
