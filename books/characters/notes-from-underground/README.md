# Notes from Underground recognition cards

Both parts, all 21 local chapters and 495 paragraphs in each edition. 79 entries each, 362 original / 363 modern exact mentions. Source editions unchanged. Authoring-agent source review, not independent editorial approval; app registration pending.

The Underground Man is Central. Liza, Zverkov and Apollon are Major. Other participants with local roles are Supporting; recalled people, named allusions and explicitly imagined figures are Reference. Every card is one brief recognition reminder throughout the novella.

- The unnamed narrator is a retired Petersburg civil servant. The author’s opening note identifies him as fictional; its actual author is Dostoevsky. Their spans and entities stay separate. The first-person poem introducing Part Two belongs to the epigraph, not the narrator. Nekrasov and translator Juliet Soskice have Reference cards.
- Liza is identified from her first unnamed appearance at 16:24 as the young Russian woman from Riga working at the brothel. Her name and ordinary origin are not concealed plot twists. Her card does not adopt the narrator’s degrading judgments, announce later encounters or turn his fantasies of marriage and rescue into facts.
- Apollon is the servant in the younger narrator’s remembered life and also a tailor. The old country woman in the opening description is a separate later servant. The officer with the clanking sword in Part One is kept separate from the tall tavern officer in Part Two; the local text does not establish they are identical.
- Anton Antonitch Syetotchkin’s name variants resolve together. His daughters, their aunt, his visitors and the discussed superior remain distinct. The narrator’s inheritance relative, childhood relatives and job patron are separate roles, without invented kinship links.
- Zverkov’s claimed romantic success is attributed to his story. Prince Kolya, Prince D., Princess D. and the elderly general remain separate. The hypothetical future daughter in the revenge fantasy is not added to Zverkov’s real family.
- The dead woman’s madam in 17:59 is not merged with Liza’s employer. The dead woman, the woman with the salt fish, and Liza remain separate; the narrator’s admissions of fabrication prevent treating every anecdotal detail as established biography. The letter writer is described with the narrator’s uncertainty about his medical studies intact.
- Both Napoleons in the historical comparison have separate cards. Gay is the Russian painter Nikolai Ge; Wagenheims names dentists. Kostanzhoglo, Uncle Pyotr Ivanitch, Pirogov, Manfred, Silvio and Lovelace are literary figures, not participants in Petersburg. The imagined King of Spain is a delusion allusion, not a named historical monarch. The pope in the fantasy is not arbitrarily assigned a historical personal name.
- The source prints Silvio as a title-like italic reference: its card explains the character from The Shot without silently changing the edition text. Soskice and Silvio have local bindings across the edition’s underscore markup. Lafitte is wine, Borghese and Yusupov occur as place references, and Masquerade is a play title; they are not extra people.
- The narrator has explicit frame and opening first-person anchors, not a blind binding of every I in quotations and examples. Generic philosophical men, imagined readers, pronouns and anonymous crowds are not exhaustively indexed.
