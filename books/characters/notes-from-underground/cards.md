# Notes from Underground: recognition cards

Editorial review copy; source excerpts and later identities may contain spoilers.

## The Underground Man · central

The unnamed narrator: a retired civil servant living in Petersburg.

## Liza · major

The young Russian woman from Riga working in the brothel the narrator visits.

## Apollon · major

The narrator’s servant during his younger years, who also works as a tailor.

## Zverkov · major

The narrator’s former schoolmate, now an army officer.

## Simonov · supporting

The former schoolmate with whom the narrator still occasionally associates.

## Ferfitchkin · supporting

The narrator’s former schoolmate and an admirer of Zverkov.

## Trudolyubov · supporting

The army officer among the former schoolmates, a distant relation of Zverkov.

## Anton Antonitch Syetotchkin · supporting

The narrator’s superior at the office and occasional social host.

## The officer at the government office · supporting

The officer whose clanking sword irritates the narrator during his government service.

## The officer from the tavern · supporting

The tall officer the narrator encounters at the billiard table and on Nevsky Prospect.

## The narrator’s later servant · supporting

The older country woman employed by the narrator when he writes his notes.

## The brothel’s madam · supporting

The woman running the brothel where Liza and Olympia work.

## Olympia · supporting

A woman at the brothel, known to Zverkov and the narrator.

## The sledge-driver · supporting

The driver hired by the narrator after the dinner.

## The restaurant waiters · supporting

The staff serving the farewell dinner at the Hôtel de Paris.

## The narrator’s benefactor · reference

The distant relative whose inheritance allows the narrator to retire.

## The narrator’s childhood relations · reference

The relatives responsible for sending the narrator to school.

## The narrator’s school friend · reference

The unnamed boy whom the narrator recalls befriending at school.

## The other clerk · reference

The office colleague whose appearance the young narrator compares with his own.

## The clerk in the dirty uniform · reference

The second office colleague described by the young narrator, remembered for his old uniform.

## The wine connoisseur · reference

The narrator’s acquaintance who prides himself on his knowledge of Lafitte wine.

## The man thrown from the tavern · reference

The man the narrator sees thrown out during a fight with billiard cues.

## The officer’s porter · reference

The porter who gives the narrator information about the tall officer’s lodgings.

## The narrator’s patron · reference

The influential person who recommended the narrator for his job.

## The countess · reference

The unnamed aristocratic woman whom the narrator imagines among Nevsky Prospect’s fashionable public.

## Prince D. · reference

The aristocrat mentioned among the fashionable people on Nevsky Prospect.

## Anton Antonitch’s daughters · reference

The two teenage daughters in Anton Antonitch’s household.

## The daughters’ aunt · reference

The aunt who pours tea at Anton Antonitch’s gatherings.

## Anton Antonitch’s visitors · reference

The officials who gather to discuss work, pay and promotion at Anton Antonitch’s home.

## His Excellency · reference

The senior official discussed by Anton Antonitch’s visitors.

## The elderly general · reference

The father of the young women whom Zverkov approaches at the theater.

## The general’s daughters · reference

The young women whom Zverkov approaches at the theater.

## Prince Kolya · reference

The wealthy hussar whom Zverkov describes as his friend.

## The woman in Zverkov’s story · reference

The unnamed woman whose affection Zverkov boasts of winning.

## Podharzhevsky · reference

The hussar whose large income the dinner guests discuss.

## Princess D. · reference

The princess whose beauty the dinner guests discuss without having met her.

## Liza’s parents · reference

The tradespeople in Riga with whom Liza grew up.

## The woman in the coffin · reference

The dead woman described by the narrator in his story to Liza.

## The other madam · reference

The brothel keeper in the narrator’s account of the dead woman’s debts.

## The affectionate father · reference

The father whose devotion to his daughter the narrator describes to Liza.

## The affectionate father’s daughter · reference

The daughter in the narrator’s example of paternal devotion.

## The woman with the salt fish · reference

The woman the narrator describes seeing outside a brothel on New Year’s Day.

## Liza’s correspondent · reference

The young man from Riga whose affectionate letter Liza keeps; the narrator thinks he is a medical student.

## Liza’s friend · reference

The friend who accompanies Liza to a dance and passes on the young man’s letter.

## The hosts of the dance · reference

The family at whose house Liza meets her childhood acquaintance again.

## Vanuha · reference

The gravedigger named in the narrator’s imagined burial scene.

## Fyodor Dostoevsky · reference

The author of the novella, distinct from the fictional writer of the notes.

## The Wagenheims · reference

The Petersburg dentists whose name the narrator invokes in his discussion of toothache.

## Nikolai Ge · reference

The Russian painter whose surname this translation renders as Gay.

## Mikhail Saltykov-Shchedrin · reference

The Russian satirist alluded to as the author of As You Will.

## Henry Thomas Buckle · reference

The English historian cited for his theory of civilization and violence.

## Napoleon I · reference

The French emperor referred to as Napoleon the Great.

## Napoleon III · reference

The later French emperor, called the present Napoleon in the narrator’s historical comparison.

## Attila · reference

The ruler of the Huns, invoked as an example of a violent conqueror.

## Stenka Razin · reference

The Cossack leader of a major rebellion in seventeenth-century Russia.

## Cleopatra · reference

The Egyptian queen cited in the narrator’s account of cruelty in history.

## Anaevsky · reference

The Russian writer mockingly cited on the Colossus of Rhodes.

## The Colossus of Rhodes · reference

The ancient giant statue at Rhodes mentioned in the narrator’s historical digression.

## Heinrich Heine · reference

The German poet cited on the difficulty of writing a truthful autobiography.

## Jean-Jacques Rousseau · reference

The author of the Confessions, discussed as an example of autobiographical self-presentation.

## Nikolai Nekrasov · reference

The Russian poet whose verse introduces the second part.

## Juliet Soskice · reference

The translator credited for the poem introducing the second part.

## Kostanzhoglo · reference

The efficient landowner in Gogol’s Dead Souls, invoked as a practical ideal.

## Uncle Pyotr Ivanitch · reference

The pragmatic uncle in Goncharov’s A Common Story, invoked as a model of worldly success.

## The imagined King of Spain · reference

The royal identity used as an example of delusion, recalling Gogol’s Diary of a Madman.

## Nikolai Gogol · reference

The Russian writer whose comic officer Pirogov is recalled.

## Lieutenant Pirogov · reference

The officer in Gogol’s Nevsky Prospect used as a comparison for the narrator’s adversary.

## Tchurkin · reference

The shopkeeper whose establishment supplies the narrator’s gloves and hat.

## Manfred · reference

Byron’s tormented dramatic hero, invoked as a model for the narrator’s fantasies.

## The pope · reference

The head of the Catholic Church as a figure in the narrator’s fantasy.

## William Shakespeare · reference

The English playwright whose lasting fame the dinner guests discuss.

## Alexander Pushkin · reference

The Russian writer whose fictional duelist Silvio supplies a model for the narrator’s fantasy.

## Silvio · reference

The duelist in Pushkin’s The Shot, recalled during the narrator’s revenge fantasy.

## Mikhail Lermontov · reference

The Russian writer of Masquerade, mentioned as another source of the narrator’s fantasy.

## Lovelace · reference

The seducer in Richardson’s Clarissa, used as a comparison in the narrator’s description of Zverkov.

## George Sand · reference

The French novelist invoked as a model for the narrator’s sentimental imaginings.

## Alexander the Great · reference

The Macedonian conqueror invoked in the narrator’s comparison of Apollon’s vanity.

## God · reference

The deity invoked in the narrator’s prayers, exclamations and moral language.

## The devil · reference

The supernatural figure invoked in the narrator’s exclamations.
