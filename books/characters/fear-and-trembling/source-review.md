## original-en · abraham · 2:0

Once upon a time there was a man who, as a child, had heard that beautiful tale of how God tempted Abraham, and how Abraham stood firm in the temptation, kept the faith, and a second time received a son against expectation. When he grew older, he read the same tale with still greater wonder; for life had divided what had been united in the child's pious simplicity. The older he grew, the more often his thought turned to that tale; his enthusiasm waxed stronger and stronger, and yet less and less could he understand the tale. At last he forgot all else for its sake; his soul had but one wish — to see Abraham — and one longing, to have been a witness of that event. His desire was not to behold the beautiful regions of the Orient, not the earthly glory of the promised land, not that god-fearing married couple whose old age God blessed, not the venerable figure of the aged patriarch, not the vigorous youth of Isaac whom God bestowed — he had nothing against its all taking place upon a barren heath. His craving was to accompany them on the three days' journey, when Abraham rode with sorrow before him and Isaac at his side. His wish was to be present in the hour when Abraham lifted up his eye and saw the mountain of Moriah in the distance, the hour when he left the asses behind and went on alone with Isaac up to the mountain; for what occupied him was not the artistic weaving of the imagination, but the shudder of thought.

## original-en · moses · 3:3

By faith Abraham went forth out of the land of his fathers, and became a stranger in the land of promise. One thing he left behind, one thing he took with him: he left his earthly understanding behind, and took faith with him; otherwise he would surely not have gone forth, but had thought, “It is unreasonable.” By faith he was a stranger in the land of promise, and there was nothing to remind him of what was dear; but everything, by its newness, tempted his soul to a wistful longing. And yet was he God's chosen, in whom the Lord had His pleasure! Yea, had he been an outcast, thrust away from God's grace, he might better have grasped it; as it was, it was as a mockery upon him and upon his faith. There was in the world also one who lived banished from the fatherland which he loved. He is not forgotten, neither his lamentations, when in wistfulness he sought and found what was lost. Of Abraham there is no lamentation. It is human to lament, human to weep with the weeper, but it is greater to believe, more blessed to behold the believer. By faith Abraham received the promise, that in his seed all the generations of the earth should be blessed. Time went on, the possibility was there; Abraham believed. Time went on, it became unreasonable; Abraham believed. There was, in the world, also one who had an expectation. Time went on, it drew toward evening; he was not so wretched as to have forgotten his expectation, therefore shall he not be forgotten either. Then he grieved, and grief did not deceive him as life had done; it did for him all that it could, and in the sweetness of grief he possessed his disappointed expectation. It is human to grieve, it is human to grieve with the griever; but it is greater to believe, more blessed to behold the believer. Of Abraham we have no song of mourning. He did not mournfully count the days as the times went on; he did not look upon Sarah with suspicious glances, lest she should grow old; he did not stop the course of the sun, that Sarah should not age and with her his expectation; he did not soothingly sing for Sarah his wistful lay. Abraham grew old, Sarah was mocked in the land; and yet was he God's chosen and heir of the promise, that in his seed all the generations of the earth should be blessed. Were it not better, then, that he had not been God's chosen? What is it to be God's chosen? Is it to be denied in youth the wish of youth, that with great trouble it may be fulfilled in old age? But Abraham believed, and held the promise fast. Had Abraham wavered, then had he given it up. He would have said unto God: “Then perhaps it is not Thy will after all that it should come to pass; then will I give up the wish; it was my only one, it was my blessedness. My soul is sincere; I harbor no secret resentment because Thou hast denied it.” He would not have been forgotten; he would have rescued many by his example, yet would not have become the father of faith. For it is great to give up one's wish, but it is greater to hold fast to it after having given it up; it is great to lay hold of the eternal, but it is greater to hold fast to the temporal after having given it up. — Then came the fullness of time. Had Abraham not believed, then perhaps Sarah had died of sorrow, and Abraham, dulled by grief, had not understood the fulfillment, but smiled at it as at a dream of youth. But Abraham believed, therefore was he young; for he who ever hopes for the best grows old, deceived by life, and he who is ever prepared for the worst grows old early; but he who believes preserves an eternal youth. Praised therefore be that tale! For Sarah, though stricken in years, was young enough to long for a mother's joy, and Abraham, though grey-haired, was young enough to wish to be a father. Outwardly, the wonder lies in this, that it came to pass according to their expectation; in a deeper sense, the wonder of faith lies in this, that Abraham and Sarah were young enough to wish, and that faith had preserved their wish, and therewith their youth. He received the fulfillment of the promise; he received it believing, and it came to pass according to the promise and according to faith — for Moses smote the rock with his staff, but he believed not.

## original-en · christ · 4:2

The story of Abraham has now this remarkable property, that it always remains glorious, however poorly one may understand it; yet here too it holds, that one must be willing to work and to be burdened. But men will not work, and yet they would understand the story. They speak in praise of Abraham, but how? They give the whole an altogether ordinary expression: “the great thing was that he so loved God that he was willing to sacrifice unto Him the best.” It is very true; but “the best” is an indefinite expression. In the rush of thought and tongue one quite securely identifies Isaac and the best, and the meditator may comfortably puff at his pipe during the meditation, and the hearer may comfortably stretch out his legs. Had that rich young man whom Christ met upon his way sold all his goods and given to the poor, then should we praise him, as we praise all that is great, and not even understand him without effort; yet he had not for that become an Abraham, although he sacrificed the best. What is left out of Abraham's history is the anguish; for to money I have no ethical obligation, but to the son the father has the highest and most sacred. Yet anguish is a perilous thing for the soft of heart, and so they forget it, and notwithstanding that they would speak of Abraham. They speak then, and in the course of speech the two expressions, Isaac and the best, change places; all goes excellently. Should it, however, fall out that there were among the hearers a man who suffered from sleeplessness, then the most dreadful, the deepest, the tragic and comic misunderstanding lies very near at hand. He went home, he would do as Abraham did; for the son is, after all, the best. If that orator came to know of it, he would perhaps go to him; he would gather all his clerical dignity and cry out: “abominable man, refuse of society, what devil hath so possessed thee that thou wouldst murder thy son?” And the priest, who had felt no warmth nor perspiration in preaching about Abraham, marveled at himself, at the wrath of earnestness with which he thundered down upon that wretched man; he was glad in himself, for he had never spoken with such pith and unction; he said unto himself and unto his wife: “I am an orator; what was lacking was the occasion. That there is, after all, no poet who could resolve to prefer such situations to the trumpery and tinsel with which men fill comedies and romances! ## original-en · herod · 4:28

Faith is therefore no aesthetic stirring, but somewhat far higher, precisely because it hath resignation before it; it is not the heart's immediate impulse, but the paradox of existence. Thus, when a young maiden, in spite of all difficulties, holdeth herself yet assured that her wish shall surely be fulfilled, this assurance is by no means that of faith, and that notwithstanding she be brought up by Christian parents, and have, perhaps, gone for a whole year to the priest. She is assured in all her childlike naïveté and innocence; this assurance, too, ennobleth her being, and giveth her a supernatural stature, so that, like a thaumaturge, she can adjure the finite powers of existence, and make stones themselves to weep, while, on the other hand, she can in her flurry just as well run to Herod as to Pilate, and move the whole world with her prayers. Not to go too far, I will only take what lies most immediately at hand. If Agamemnon himself, not Calchas, were to draw the knife against Iphigenia, then he would only have demeaned himself by, in the last moment, wanting to say a few words; for the meaning of his deed was, after all, evident to all; the procedure of piety, of pity, of feeling, of tears was completed; and besides, his life had no relation to spirit, i.e., he was no teacher or witness of spirit. If, on the other hand, the meaning of a hero's life is in the direction of spirit, then the lack of a line would weaken the impression of him. What he then has to say is not a few fitting words, a little declamation piece, but the meaning of his line is that he carries himself through in the decisive moment. Such an intellectual tragic hero ought to have, and ought to keep, the last word. One demands of him the same transfigured bearing that befits every tragic hero; but then one demands one more word. Insofar, then, as such an intellectual tragic hero culminates in a suffering (in death), then in this last word he becomes immortal before he dies — while, on the contrary, the ordinary tragic hero only becomes immortal after his death. One can use Socrates as an example. He was an intellectual tragic hero. His death sentence is announced to him. In that moment he dies; for he who does not understand that the whole power of the spirit is required to die, and that the hero always dies before he dies, will not get very far in his consideration of life. Now, as a hero, it is required of Socrates that he rest calmly in himself; but as an intellectual tragic hero, it is required of him that in this last moment he have spirit-strength enough to carry himself through. He cannot then, like the ordinary tragic hero, concentrate on holding himself up over against death, but he must so quickly make this movement that in the same moment with his consciousness he is past this struggle and asserts himself. Thus, if Socrates in death's crisis had become silent, he would have weakened the effect of his life, awakened a suspicion that the elasticity of irony in him was not a world-power, but a play whose resilience in the decisive moment would have to be used in inverse measure to hold him up pathetically *.
