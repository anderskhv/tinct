## original-en · katharine · 6:28

CHORUS. Thus with imagin’d wing our swift scene flies, In motion of no less celerity Than that of thought. Suppose that you have seen The well-appointed king at Hampton pier Embark his royalty, and his brave fleet With silken streamers the young Phoebus fanning. Play with your fancies; and in them behold Upon the hempen tackle ship-boys climbing; Hear the shrill whistle which doth order give To sounds confus’d; behold the threaden sails, Borne with the invisible and creeping wind, Draw the huge bottoms through the furrow’d sea, Breasting the lofty surge. O, do but think You stand upon the rivage and behold A city on the inconstant billows dancing; For so appears this fleet majestical, Holding due course to Harfleur. Follow, follow! Grapple your minds to sternage of this navy, And leave your England, as dead midnight still, Guarded with grandsires, babies, and old women, Either past or not arriv’d to pith and puissance. For who is he, whose chin is but enrich’d With one appearing hair, that will not follow These cull’d and choice-drawn cavaliers to France? Work, work your thoughts, and therein see a siege; Behold the ordnance on their carriages, With fatal mouths gaping on girded Harfleur. Suppose the ambassador from the French comes back, Tells Harry that the King doth offer him Katharine his daughter, and with her, to dowry, Some petty and unprofitable dukedoms. The offer likes not; and the nimble gunner With linstock now the devilish cannon touches,

The French king’s daughter, whom King Henry claims as part of his terms.

## original-en · orleans · 6:1

FRENCH KING. Thus comes the English with full power upon us, And more than carefully it us concerns To answer royally in our defences. Therefore the Dukes of Berry and of Brittany, Of Brabant and of Orleans, shall make forth, And you, Prince Dauphin, with all swift dispatch, To line and new repair our towns of war With men of courage and with means defendant; For England his approaches makes as fierce As waters to the sucking of a gulf. It fits us then to be as provident As fears may teach us out of late examples Left by the fatal and neglected English Upon our fields.

Charles, Duke of Orleans, a French nobleman and the king’s nephew.

## original-en · scroop · 2:39

CHORUS. Now all the youth of England are on fire, And silken dalliance in the wardrobe lies. Now thrive the armourers, and honour’s thought Reigns solely in the breast of every man. They sell the pasture now to buy the horse, Following the mirror of all Christian kings, With winged heels, as English Mercuries. For now sits Expectation in the air, And hides a sword from hilts unto the point With crowns imperial, crowns, and coronets, Promis’d to Harry and his followers. The French, advis’d by good intelligence Of this most dreadful preparation, Shake in their fear, and with pale policy Seek to divert the English purposes. O England! model to thy inward greatness, Like little body with a mighty heart, What mightst thou do, that honour would thee do, Were all thy children kind and natural! But see thy fault! France hath in thee found out A nest of hollow bosoms, which he fills With treacherous crowns; and three corrupted men, One, Richard Earl of Cambridge, and the second, Henry Lord Scroop of Masham, and the third, Sir Thomas Grey, knight of Northumberland, Have, for the gilt of France,—O guilt indeed!— Confirm’d conspiracy with fearful France; And by their hands this grace of kings must die, If hell and treason hold their promises, Ere he take ship for France, and in Southampton. Linger your patience on, and we’ll digest The abuse of distance, force a play. The sum is paid; the traitors are agreed; The King is set from London; and the scene Is now transported, gentles, to Southampton. There is the playhouse now, there must you sit; And thence to France shall we convey you safe, And bring you back, charming the narrow seas To give you gentle pass; for, if we may, We’ll not offend one stomach with our play. But, till the King come forth, and not till then, Unto Southampton do we shift our scene.

Henry, Lord Scroop of Masham, one of King Henry’s intimates.

## original-en · guichard-dauphin · 21:37

KING HENRY. This note doth tell me of ten thousand French That in the field lie slain; of princes, in this number, And nobles bearing banners, there lie dead One hundred twenty-six; added to these, Of knights, esquires, and gallant gentlemen, Eight thousand and four hundred; of the which, Five hundred were but yesterday dubb’d knights; So that, in these ten thousand they have lost, There are but sixteen hundred mercenaries; The rest are princes, barons, lords, knights, squires, And gentlemen of blood and quality. The names of those their nobles that lie dead: Charles Delabreth, High Constable of France; Jacques of Chatillon, Admiral of France; The master of the Crossbows, Lord Rambures; Great Master of France, the brave Sir Guichard Dauphin, John, Duke of Alençon, Anthony, Duke of Brabant, The brother to the Duke of Burgundy, And Edward, Duke of Bar; of lusty earls, Grandpré and Roussi, Fauconbridge and Foix, Beaumont and Marle, Vaudemont and Lestrale. Here was a royal fellowship of death! Where is the number of our English dead?

Great Master of France; a nobleman of that surname, not the king’s son.

## original-en · pharamond · 2:9

CANTERBURY. Then hear me, gracious sovereign, and you peers, That owe yourselves, your lives, and services To this imperial throne. ## modern-en · pharamond · 2:9

CANTERBURY. Then hear me, gracious sovereign—and you peers, who owe yourselves, your lives, and your service to this imperial throne. There is no obstacle to your Highness's claim to France except this, which they cite from Pharamond: _In terram Salicam mulieres ne succedant_—"No woman shall succeed in Salic land." This Salic land the French unjustly gloss as the realm of France, and Pharamond as the founder of the law and of this bar against females. Yet their own historians faithfully affirm that the Salic land is in Germany, between the rivers Sala and Elbe. There Charlemagne, having subdued the Saxons, left certain French settlers behind. Disdaining the German women for some loose habits, they established this law: no female should inherit in Salic land. That Salic, as I said, between the Elbe and the Sala, is today in Germany called Meissen. So it's clear the Salic law was not devised for the realm of France; nor did the French even possess the Salic land until four hundred and twenty-one years after the death of King Pharamond, who is supposed—idly—to have founded this law. He died in the year of our redemption 426; and Charlemagne subdued the Saxons and settled the French beyond the river Sala in the year 805. Besides, their own writers say that King Pepin, who deposed Childeric, made his claim and title to the crown of France as heir general, being descended from Blithild, daughter of King Clothair. Hugh Capet too, who usurped the crown from Charles Duke of Lorraine—sole male heir of the true line and stock of Charlemagne—to give his title some show of truth (though in pure fact it was corrupt and worthless), traced himself as heir to Lady Lingare, daughter of Charlemain, who was the son of Lewis the Emperor, and Lewis the son of Charlemagne. King Lewis the Tenth too, sole heir to the usurper Capet, could not keep his conscience quiet wearing the crown of France until he was satisfied that the fair Queen Isabel, his grandmother, was lineal descendant of Lady Ermengare, daughter to Charles, the said Duke of Lorraine. By this marriage the line of Charlemagne was reunited to the crown of France. So that, as clear as the summer sun: King Pepin's title, Hugh Capet's claim, King Lewis's satisfaction—all rest on right and title through the female line. So do the kings of France, even to this day; though they would hold up this Salic law to bar your Highness from claiming through the female, and would rather hide themselves in a net than openly defend their crooked titles, usurped from you and your forefathers.

The legendary Frankish king the French cite as the founder of the Salic law.
