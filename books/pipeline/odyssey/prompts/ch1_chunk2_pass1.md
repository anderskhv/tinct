Tell me, Muse, of that clever hero who wandered far and wide after he destroyed the famous city of Troy. He visited many cities and learned the customs of many peoples. He suffered greatly at sea while trying to save his own life and bring his men home safely. But no matter what he did, he could not save his companions—they died through their own reckless folly when they ate the cattle of the sun god Helios. So the god prevented them from ever reaching home. Tell me about all these things, daughter of Zeus, from whatever source you know them.

Now all those who had escaped death in battle or shipwreck had returned safely home—all except Odysseus. Though he longed to return to his wife and homeland, the goddess Calypso held him captive in her vast cave, wanting to marry him. But as the years passed, the time came when the gods decided he should return to Ithaca. Even then, when he was among his own people, his troubles would not be over. Nevertheless, all the gods had begun to pity him except Poseidon, who continued to persecute him relentlessly and would not let him reach home.

Now Poseidon had gone to the Ethiopians, who live at the world's edge and are divided in two groups, one facing west and the other east. He had gone there to receive an offering of a hundred sheep and oxen, and was enjoying himself at the feast. But the other gods gathered in the palace of Olympian Zeus, and the father of gods and men spoke first. At that moment he was thinking of Aegisthus, who had been killed by Agamemnon's son Orestes. So he said to the other gods:

"Look how mortals blame us gods for what is really nothing but their own foolishness. Take Aegisthus—he had to seduce Agamemnon's wife and then murder Agamemnon, even though he knew it would mean his death. I sent Hermes to warn him not to do either of these things, since Orestes would surely take revenge when he grew up and wanted to return home. Hermes gave him this warning with the best intentions, but Aegisthus wouldn't listen. Now he has paid the full price for everything."

Then Athena said, "Father, son of Cronos, king of kings, Aegisthus got what he deserved, and so would anyone else who acts as he did. But Aegisthus doesn't matter to me—it's Odysseus who breaks my heart when I think of his suffering on that lonely island surrounded by sea, far from all his friends. It's a forested island in the middle of the ocean, and a goddess lives there—the daughter of Atlas the magician, who watches over the ocean depths and holds up the great pillars that separate heaven and earth. This daughter of Atlas has trapped poor unhappy Odysseus and keeps trying with every kind of charm to make him forget his home. He's grown weary of life and thinks of nothing but seeing the smoke rising from his own chimneys once more. Yet you pay no attention to this. Didn't Odysseus honor you with many burnt offerings when he was at Troy? Why do you remain so angry with him?"

Zeus replied, "My child, what are you saying? How could I forget Odysseus? There's no mortal more capable than he, nor more generous in his offerings to the immortal gods who live in heaven. But remember that Poseidon is still furious with Odysseus for blinding the eye of Polyphemus, king of the Cyclopes. Polyphemus is Poseidon's son by the nymph Thoosa, daughter of the sea-king Phorcys. So while Poseidon won't kill Odysseus outright, he torments him by preventing him from reaching home. Still, let's put our heads together and figure out how to help him return. Then Poseidon will be appeased—if we're all in agreement, he can hardly stand against us alone."

Athena said, "Father, son of Cronos, king of kings, if the gods have now decided that Odysseus should return home, we should first send Hermes to the island of Ogygia to tell Calypso our decision—that Odysseus must be allowed to leave. Meanwhile, I'll go to Ithaca to encourage Odysseus's son Telemachus. I'll give him courage to call the Greeks to assembly and speak boldly to his mother Penelope's suitors, who keep devouring his flocks of sheep and herds of cattle. I'll also guide him to Sparta and Pylos to see if he can learn anything about his dear father's return—this will earn him a good reputation."

With that, she strapped on her gleaming golden sandals that carry her swift as the wind over land and sea. She grasped her mighty bronze-tipped spear, heavy and strong, with which she conquers ranks of warriors who displease her. Then she shot down from the heights of Olympus and appeared immediately in Ithaca, at the gateway of Odysseus's house. She disguised herself as a visitor—Mentes, chief of the Taphians—and held a bronze spear in her hand. There she found the arrogant suitors sitting on the hides of oxen they had slaughtered and eaten, playing board games in front of the house. Servants and attendants bustled about waiting on them—some mixing wine with water in the bowls, others wiping down tables with wet sponges and setting them again, still others carving great quantities of meat.

Telemachus spotted her long before anyone else did. He was sitting glumly among the suitors, thinking about his noble father and how he would drive them from the house if he were to return and reclaim his rightful place of honor. Lost in these thoughts among the suitors, he caught sight of Athena and went straight to the gate, annoyed that a stranger should be kept waiting for admission. He took her right hand in his and asked her to give him her spear. "Welcome to our house," he said, "and when you've shared our food, you can tell us what brings you here."

He led the way as he spoke, and Athena followed. Once inside, he took her spear and placed it in the spear rack against a sturdy pillar, alongside the many other spears belonging to his unfortunate father. He led her to a beautifully carved chair and spread a fine cloth beneath it. There was a footstool for her feet, and he placed another seat nearby for himself, away from the suitors, so she wouldn't be disturbed by their noise and rudeness while eating, and so he could ask her more freely about his father.

A maid brought them water in a beautiful golden pitcher and poured it into a silver basin for them to wash their hands. She set a polished table beside them. A housekeeper brought bread and offered them many delicious foods from the household stores. The carver served them portions of various meats and placed golden cups beside them, while a servant brought wine and poured it for them.

Then the suitors came in and took their places on the benches and chairs. Servants immediately poured water over their hands, maids distributed bread from baskets, and young men filled the mixing bowls with wine and water. Everyone reached for the good food set before them. When they had eaten and drunk their fill, they turned to music and dancing, which crown a feast. A servant brought a lyre to Phemius, whom they forced to sing for them. As soon as he touched his instrument and began to sing, Telemachus spoke quietly to Athena, leaning close so no one else could hear.

"I hope you won't be offended by what I'm about to say," he said. "These men find singing cheap entertainment since they don't pay for it—all this comes at the expense of a man whose bones are rotting in some wilderness or being ground to powder by the waves. If these men saw my father return to Ithaca, they'd pray for faster legs rather than more gold, because money wouldn't save them. But he has met with misfortune, and even when people sometimes say he's coming back, we no longer believe them. We'll never see him again. Now tell me truthfully who you are and where you come from. Tell me about your city and your parents, what kind of ship brought you here, how your crew got you to Ithaca, and what people they claim to be—you certainly couldn't have come on foot. Tell me honestly, because I want to know: are you a stranger to this house, or have you been here in my father's time? In the old days we had many visitors, for my father traveled widely himself."

Athena answered, "I'll tell you everything truthfully and completely. I am Mentes, son of Anchialus, and I rule the Taphians. I've come here with my ship and crew, sailing to foreign lands on a trading voyage to Temesa with a cargo of iron, and I'll bring back copper. My ship lies over there beyond the town in Rheithron harbor, beneath wooded Mount Neritum. Our fathers were friends before us, as old Laertes will tell you if you go ask him. But they say he no longer comes to town and lives alone in the countryside, struggling along with only an old woman to care for him and prepare his meals when he comes in exhausted from tending his vineyard. I came because they told me your father had returned home, but it seems the gods are still keeping him away. He's certainly not dead—not on the mainland. More likely he's trapped on some island surrounded by sea, held prisoner by savages against his will. I'm no prophet and know little about omens, but I speak as the thought comes to me from heaven: he won't be away much longer. He's so resourceful that even if he were bound in iron chains, he'd find some way to get home. But tell me honestly—can Odysseus really have such a handsome son? You look remarkably like him, especially around the head and eyes. We were close friends before he sailed for Troy with all the finest Greeks. Since then, neither of us has seen the other."

"My mother says I'm Odysseus's son," Telemachus replied, "but it's a wise child who knows his own father. I wish I were the son of someone who had grown old peacefully on his own estate. Since you ask, there's no man under heaven more unfortunate than the one they tell me is my father."

Athena said, "There's no danger of your family line dying out while Penelope has such a fine son as you. But tell me truly, what's the meaning of all this feasting? Who are these people? What's it all about? Is this some banquet, or is there a wedding in the family? No one seems to be bringing their own food. And the guests—how outrageously they're behaving! What a riot they're making throughout the house. It would disgust any decent person who came near them."

"Sir," Telemachus said, "regarding your question—as long as my father was here, all was well with us and our household. But the gods in their displeasure have willed otherwise and have hidden him away more completely than any mortal man has ever been hidden. I could bear it better even if he were dead—if he had fallen with his men at Troy, or died among friends when his fighting days were over. Then the Greeks would have built a tomb over his ashes, and I would have inherited his honor. But now the storm winds have swept him away to some unknown place. He's vanished without leaving a trace, and I inherit nothing but grief. Nor does my sorrow end simply with losing my father. Heaven has brought me troubles of another kind. All the nobles from our islands—Dulichium, Same, and wooded Zacynthus, as well as all the leading men of Ithaca itself—are consuming my household, pretending to court my mother. She neither refuses outright to marry nor brings the matter to an end. So they're destroying my inheritance, and soon they'll destroy me as well."

"Is that so?" exclaimed Athena. "Then you really do need Odysseus to come home. Give him his helmet, his shield, and a couple of spears, and if he's the man he was when I first knew him in our house, drinking and celebrating—he had just returned from Ephyra, where he'd gone to get poison for his arrows from Ilus, son of Mermerus. Ilus feared the eternal gods and wouldn't give him any, but my father provided some because he was very fond of Odysseus. If Odysseus is still that man, these suitors will have a quick end and a bitter wedding.

"But enough! It's up to heaven to decide whether he'll return and take revenge in his own house. However, I urge you to start planning how to get rid of these suitors immediately. Take my advice: call the Greek leaders to assembly tomorrow morning, present your case to them, and call on the gods to witness your words. Tell the suitors to go away, each to his own home. If your mother is determined to remarry, let her return to her father's house—he'll find her a husband and provide all the wedding gifts such a dear daughter deserves. As for yourself, let me persuade you to take the best ship you can get with a crew of twenty men and go searching for your long-missing father. Someone might tell you something, or you might hear some heaven-sent message—that's often how people learn things. First go to Pylos and question Nestor, then continue to Sparta to visit Menelaus, who returned home last of all the Greeks. If you hear that your father is alive and coming home, you can endure these suitors' waste for another year. But if you learn of his death, return home immediately, hold proper funeral rites, build a memorial mound, and see that your mother marries again. Then, having done all this, consider carefully how you might kill these suitors in your own house, whether by cunning or open force. You're too old to plead youth as an excuse any longer. Haven't you heard how people praise Orestes for killing Aegisthus, his father's murderer? You're a fine, handsome young man—show your courage and make a name for yourself. Now I must return to my ship and crew, who will be impatient if I keep them waiting longer. Think over what I've told you, and remember my words."

"Sir," Telemachus answered, "you've been very kind to speak to me as if I were your own son, and I'll do everything you've told me. I know you're eager to continue your voyage, but stay a little longer until you've bathed and refreshed yourself. Then I'll give you a gift—a beautiful and valuable keepsake, the kind that dear friends give each other—and you can go on your way rejoicing."

Athena replied, "Don't try to keep me—I need to leave at once. As for any gift you want to give me, save it until I return, and I'll take it home with me then. Give me a very fine one, and I'll give you one of equal value in return."

With these words she flew away like a bird into the air. But she had given Telemachus courage and made him think about his father more than ever. He felt the change, marveled at it, and knew that the stranger had been a god. So he went directly to where the suitors were sitting.

Phemius was still singing, and his audience sat in rapt silence as he told the sad story of the Greeks' return from Troy and the troubles Athena had brought upon them. Penelope, daughter of Icarius, heard his song from her room upstairs and came down the great staircase, not alone but accompanied by two of her maids. When she reached the suitors, she stood by one of the pillars supporting the roof of the hall, with a modest attendant on each side. She held a veil over her face and wept bitterly.

"Phemius," she cried, "you know many other tales of gods and heroes that poets love to sing. Sing one of those to the suitors and let them drink their wine in silence. But stop this sad song—it breaks my sorrowful heart and reminds me of my lost husband, whom I mourn constantly and whose name was renowned throughout Greece and central Argos."

"Mother," Telemachus answered, "let the singer sing whatever moves him. Singers don't create the troubles they sing about—Zeus does that, sending good fortune or bad to mortals as he pleases. This man means no harm by singing about the Greeks' ill-fated return from Troy. People always applaud the newest songs most enthusiastically. Steel yourself to bear it. Odysseus isn't the only man who never returned from Troy—many others died there too. Go back into the house and attend to your own work—your loom, your spinning, and managing the servants. Speech is men's business, and mine above all others, since I am master here."

She returned to the house in amazement, taking her son's words to heart. Going upstairs with her maids to her room, she mourned for her dear husband until Athena brought sweet sleep to her eyes. But the suitors grew rowdy throughout the covered halls, each praying that he might share her bed.

Then Telemachus spoke: "Shameless and arrogant suitors, let's feast peacefully now without fighting, for it's wonderful to hear a man sing with such a divine voice as Phemius has. But in the morning, meet me in full assembly so I can give you formal notice to leave and feast in each other's houses, taking turns at your own expense. But if you choose to keep devouring one man's livelihood, then heaven help me—Zeus will hold you accountable, and when you fall in my father's house, no one will avenge you."

The suitors bit their lips when they heard him and marveled at his bold speech. Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, said, "The gods seem to have taught you to boast and talk big. May Zeus never make you king in Ithaca as your father was before you."

Telemachus replied, "Antinous, don't be angry with me, but if god wills it, I'll gladly be king too. Do you think that's the worst fate you can imagine for me? It's no bad thing to be a king—it brings both wealth and honor. Still, now that Odysseus is dead, there are many great men in Ithaca, both old and young, and someone else might become their leader. Nevertheless, I will be master in my own house and rule over the people Odysseus won for me."

Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered, "It's up to heaven to decide who will be king among us, but you should be master of your own house and possessions. As long as there's a man left in Ithaca, no one will use force against you or rob you. Now, my good fellow, I want to know about this stranger. What country does he come from? What family is he from, and where are his lands? Did he bring you news about your father's return, or was he here on his own business? He seemed like a well-to-do man, but he left so suddenly—gone in a moment before we could get to know him."

"My father is dead and gone," Telemachus answered, "and even when some rumor reaches me, I no longer believe it. My mother does sometimes summon a prophet to question him, but I pay no attention to his predictions. As for the stranger, he was Mentes, son of Anchialus, chief of the Taphians, an old friend of my father's." But in his heart he knew it had been the goddess.

The suitors then returned to their singing and dancing until evening came. But when darkness fell on their revelry, they went home to bed, each to his own dwelling. Telemachus's bedroom was high up in a tower overlooking the courtyard. He went there, brooding and deep in thought. A loyal old woman, Eurycleia, daughter of Ops the son of Pisenor, went before him carrying two blazing torches. Laertes had bought her with his own money when she was quite young, paying the price of twenty oxen, and he honored her in his household as much as his own wife, though he never shared her bed for fear of his wife's anger. She was the one who now lit Telemachus's way to his room, and she loved him more than any of the other household women, for she had nursed him as a baby. He opened his bedroom door and sat on his bed. As he took off his soft tunic, he gave it to the good old woman, who folded it neatly and hung it on a peg beside his bed. Then she left the room, closed the door by its silver handle, and slid the bolt home with its leather strap. But Telemachus lay under his woolen blanket, thinking all night long about his planned journey and the advice Athena had given him.
