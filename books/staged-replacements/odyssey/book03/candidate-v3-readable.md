**Book 3 — Telemachus visits Nestor at Pylos**

But as the sun was rising out of the fair sea into the vault of heaven, to shed light on mortals and immortals, they reached Pylos, the city of Neleus. The people of Pylos were gathered on the seashore to offer a sacrifice of black bulls to Poseidon, lord of the Earthquake. There were nine companies with five hundred men in each, and nine bulls to each company. As they were eating the inner meats and burning the thigh bones on the embers in Poseidon’s name, Telemachus and his crew arrived, furled their sails, brought their ship to anchor, and went ashore.

**B03-P002**

Athena led the way and Telemachus followed her. Presently she said, “Telemachus, you must not be shy or nervous for a moment. You have made this voyage to try to find out where your father is buried and how he met his end, so go straight up to Nestor and let us see what he has to tell us. Ask him to speak the truth, and he will tell no lies, for he is an excellent man.”

**B03-P003**

“But how, Mentor,” Telemachus replied, “dare I go up to Nestor? And how am I to address him? I have never been used to holding long conversations with people, and I am ashamed to start questioning a man so much older than myself.”

**B03-P004**

“Some things, Telemachus,” Athena answered, “your own instinct will suggest to you, and heaven will prompt you further; for I am sure the gods have been with you from the day you were born until now.”

**B03-P005**

She then went quickly on, and Telemachus followed in her steps until they reached the place where the companies of the Pylian people were assembled. There they found Nestor sitting with his sons, while his companions around him were busy getting dinner ready, putting pieces of meat on the spits while other pieces were cooking. When they saw the strangers they crowded round them, took them by the hand, and asked them to take their places. Nestor’s son Pisistratus at once offered his hand to each of them, and seated them on some soft sheepskins that were lying on the sands near his father and his brother Thrasymedes. Then he gave them their portions of the inner meats and poured wine for them into a golden cup, handing it to Athena first, and greeting her as he did so.

**B03-P006**

“Say a prayer to King Poseidon, sir,” he said, “for it is his feast that you are joining. When you have duly prayed and made your drink offering, pass the cup to your friend so that he may do the same. I have no doubt that he too lifts his hands in prayer, for man cannot live without God in the world. Still, he is younger than you are, and much of an age with myself, so I will give you the precedence.”

**B03-P007**

As he spoke he handed her the cup. Athena thought it very right and proper of him to have given it to her first, so she began praying heartily to Poseidon. “You who encircle the earth,” she cried, “grant the prayers of your servants who call upon you. Above all, send down your grace on Nestor and on his sons; and after that make the rest of the Pylian people some handsome return for the fine sacrifice they are offering you. Last of all, grant Telemachus and myself a happy outcome in the matter that has brought us in our ship to Pylos.”

**B03-P008**

When she had finished praying, she handed the cup to Telemachus and he prayed in the same way. By and by, when the outer meats were roasted and had been taken off the spits, the carvers gave every man his portion and they all made an excellent dinner. As soon as they had had enough to eat and drink, Nestor, the horseman of Gerene, began to speak.

**B03-P009**

“Now that our guests have finished their dinner,” he said, “it will be best to ask them who they are. Who are you, then, strangers, and from what port have you sailed? Are you traders? Or do you sail the seas as raiders, with your hand against every man and every man’s hand against you?”

**B03-P010**

Telemachus answered boldly, for Athena had given him the courage to ask about his father and win himself a good name.

**B03-P011**

“Nestor, son of Neleus, honor to the Achaean name,” he said, “you ask where we come from, and I will tell you. We come from Ithaca under Neritum, and the matter I want to speak of is private, not public. I am seeking news of my unhappy father Odysseus, who is said to have sacked the town of Troy in your company. We know what fate befell each of the other heroes who fought at Troy, but where Odysseus is concerned heaven has hidden from us even the knowledge that he is dead at all, for no one can tell us for certain in what place he died, nor say whether he fell in battle on the mainland or was lost at sea among the waves of Amphitrite. So I come as a suppliant to your knees, in the hope that you may be willing to tell me of his wretched end, whether you saw it with your own eyes or heard it from some other traveler—for he was a man born to trouble. Do not soften things out of any pity for me, but tell me plainly and exactly what you saw. If my brave father Odysseus ever did you loyal service, by word or by deed, when you Achaeans were being harried among the Trojans, bear it in mind now in my favor and tell me truly all.”

**B03-P012**

“My friend,” Nestor answered, “you call to mind a time of much sorrow, for the brave Achaeans suffered a great deal, both at sea while raiding under Achilles, and in the fighting before King Priam’s great city. Our best men all fell there—Ajax, Achilles, Patroclus the equal of the gods in counsel, and my own dear son Antilochus, a man remarkably swift of foot and valiant in a fight. But we suffered much more than that; what mortal tongue indeed could tell the whole story? Though you were to stay here and question me for five years, or even six, I could not tell you all that the Achaeans suffered, and you would turn homeward weary of my tale before it ended. Nine long years we tried every kind of stratagem, but the hand of heaven was against us; and in all that time there was no one who could compare with your father in subtlety—if indeed you are his son. I can hardly believe my eyes—and you talk just like him too. No one would say that people of such different ages could speak so much alike. He and I never had a difference of any kind from first to last, in camp or in council, but with one heart and one purpose we advised the Argives how everything might be ordered for the best.

**B03-P013**

“When, however, we had sacked the city of Priam, and were setting sail in our ships as heaven had scattered us, then Zeus saw fit to make the Argives’ homeward voyage a hard one; for they had not all been either wise or understanding, and so many of them came to a bad end through the displeasure of Zeus’s daughter Athena, who brought about a quarrel between the two sons of Atreus.

**B03-P014**

“The sons of Atreus called a meeting, and it was not called as it should have been, for it was sunset and the Achaeans were heavy with wine. When they explained why they had called the people together, it appeared that Menelaus was for sailing homeward at once, and this displeased Agamemnon, who thought that we should wait until we had made great sacrifices to appease Athena’s anger. Fool that he was, he might have known that he would not prevail with her, for when the gods have made up their minds they do not change them lightly. So the two of them stood trading hard words, and then the Achaeans sprang to their feet with a cry that split the air, and were of two minds as to what they should do.

**B03-P015**

“That night we rested and nursed our anger, for Zeus was hatching trouble for us. But in the morning some of us drew our ships into the water and put our goods and our women on board, while the rest, about half in number, stayed behind with Agamemnon. We—the other half—embarked and sailed, and the ships went well, for heaven had smoothed the sea. When we reached Tenedos we offered sacrifices to the gods, for we were longing to get home; cruel Zeus, however, did not yet mean that we should, and raised a second quarrel, in the course of which some among us turned their ships back again and sailed away under Odysseus to make their peace with Agamemnon. But I, and all the ships that were with me, pressed forward, for I saw that trouble was brewing. The son of Tydeus went on with me too, and his crews with him. Later on Menelaus joined us at Lesbos, and found us making up our minds about our course—for we did not know whether to go outside Chios by the island of Psyra, keeping this to our left, or inside Chios, over against the stormy headland of Mimas. So we asked heaven for a sign, and were shown one to the effect that we should be out of danger soonest if we headed our ships across the open sea to Euboea. This we therefore did, and a fair wind sprang up which gave us a quick passage during the night to Geraestus, where we offered many sacrifices to Poseidon for having helped us so far on our way. Four days later Diomed and his men stationed their ships in Argos, but I held on for Pylos, and the wind never fell light from the day when heaven first made it fair for me.

**B03-P016**

“So, my dear young friend, I came home without hearing anything about the others. I do not know who got home safely and who was lost; but, as I am bound to do, I will give you without reserve the reports that have reached me since I have been here in my own house. They say the Myrmidons came home safely under Achilles’s son Neoptolemus, and so did the valiant son of Poias, Philoctetes. Idomeneus, again, lost no men at sea, and all his followers who escaped death in the field got home safe with him to Crete. However far out of the world you live, you will have heard of Agamemnon and the bad end he came to at the hands of Aegisthus—and Aegisthus paid a fearful reckoning for it before long. See what a good thing it is for a man to leave a son behind him to do as Orestes did, who killed false Aegisthus, the murderer of his noble father. You too, then—for you are a tall, smart-looking fellow—show your mettle and make yourself a name in story.”

**B03-P017**

“Nestor, son of Neleus,” Telemachus answered, “honor to the Achaean name, the Achaeans applaud Orestes, and his name will live through all time, for he avenged his father nobly. I wish heaven might grant me to take such vengeance on the insolence of the wicked suitors, who are ill-treating me and plotting my ruin; but the gods have no such happiness in store for me or for my father, so we must bear it as best we can.”

**B03-P018**

“My friend,” said Nestor, “now that you remind me, I remember hearing that your mother has many suitors, who are ill-disposed toward you and are making havoc of your estate. Do you submit to this tamely, or are public feeling and the voice of heaven against you? Who knows but that Odysseus may come back after all and pay these scoundrels in full, either single-handed or with a force of Achaeans behind him? If Athena were to take as great a liking to you as she took to Odysseus when we were fighting before Troy (for I never yet saw the gods so openly fond of anyone as Athena then was of your father), if she would take as good care of you as she took of him, some of these wooers would soon forget their wooing.”

**B03-P019**

Telemachus answered, “I can expect nothing of the kind; it would be far too much to hope for. I dare not let myself think of it. Even if the gods themselves willed it, no such good fortune could come to me.”

**B03-P020**

At this Athena said, “Telemachus, what are you talking about? Heaven has a long arm if it is minded to save a man; and if it were me, I should not care how much I suffered before getting home, provided I could be safe once I was there. I would rather that than get home quickly and then be killed in my own house, as Agamemnon was, by the treachery of Aegisthus and his wife. Still, death is certain, and when a man’s hour has come, not even the gods can save him, however fond of him they are.”

**B03-P021**

“Mentor,” Telemachus answered, “let us not talk about it any more. There is no chance of my father’s ever coming back; the gods long ago decreed his destruction. There is something else, though, that I should like to ask Nestor, for he knows much more than anyone else does. They say he has reigned for three generations, so that talking to him is like talking to an immortal. So tell me truly, Nestor: how did Agamemnon come to die in that way? What was Menelaus doing? And how did false Aegisthus come to kill a man so far better than himself? Was Menelaus away from Achaean Argos, voyaging somewhere else among mankind, so that Aegisthus took heart and killed Agamemnon?”

**B03-P022**

“I will tell you truly,” Nestor answered, “and indeed you have guessed for yourself how it all happened. If Menelaus, when he got back from Troy, had found Aegisthus still alive in his house, there would have been no mound heaped up for him, not even when he was dead; he would have been thrown outside the city for dogs and vultures, and not a woman would have mourned him, for he had done a deed of great wickedness. But we were over there, fighting hard at Troy, and Aegisthus, who was taking his ease quietly in the heart of Argos, cajoled Agamemnon’s wife Clytemnestra with unceasing flattery.

**B03-P023**

“At first she would have nothing to do with his wicked scheme, for she was of a good natural disposition; and besides, there was a bard with her, to whom Agamemnon had given strict orders on setting out for Troy that he was to keep guard over his wife. But when heaven had decreed her destruction, Aegisthus carried this bard off to a desert island and left him there for crows and seagulls to gorge on—after which she went willingly enough to the house of Aegisthus. Then he offered many burnt sacrifices to the gods, and decorated many temples with tapestries and gilding, for he had succeeded far beyond his expectations.

**B03-P024**

“Meanwhile Menelaus and I were on our way home from Troy, on good terms with one another. When we got to Sunium, which is the point of Athens, Apollo with his painless arrows killed Phrontis, the steersman of Menelaus’s ship (and no man ever knew better how to handle a vessel in rough weather), so that he died there and then with the helm in his hand; and Menelaus, though very anxious to press forward, had to wait in order to bury his comrade and give him his due funeral rites. Presently, when he too could put to sea again and had sailed as far as the Malean heads, Zeus decreed evil against him and made it blow hard until the waves ran mountains high. Here he divided his fleet and took the one half toward Crete, where the Cydonians live around the waters of the river Iardanus. There is a high headland in those parts stretching out into the sea from a place called Gortyn, and all along this part of the coast as far as Phaestus the sea runs high when there is a south wind blowing; but past Phaestus the coast is more sheltered, for a small headland can make a great shelter. Here this part of the fleet was driven onto the rocks and wrecked, but the crews just managed to save themselves. As for the other five ships, they were carried by winds and seas to Egypt, where Menelaus gathered much gold and substance among people of an alien speech. Meanwhile Aegisthus here at home was plotting his evil deed. For seven years after he had killed Agamemnon he ruled in Mycenae, and the people were obedient under him; but in the eighth year Orestes came back from Athens to be his ruin, and killed the murderer of his father. Then he celebrated the funeral rites of his mother and of false Aegisthus with a banquet for the people of Argos, and on that very day Menelaus came home, with as much treasure as his ships could carry.

**B03-P025**

“Take my advice, then, and do not go traveling about for long so far from home, nor leave your property with such dangerous people in your house; they will eat up everything you have among them, and you will have been on a fool’s errand. Still, I should advise you by all means to go and visit Menelaus, who has lately come back from a voyage among peoples so distant that no man could ever hope to return from them, once the winds had carried him so far out of his reckoning; even birds cannot fly that distance in a year, so vast and terrible are the seas they must cross. Go to him, therefore, by sea, and take your own men with you; or if you would rather travel by land, you can have a chariot, you can have horses, and here are my sons, who can escort you to Lacedaemon where Menelaus lives. Ask him to speak the truth, and he will tell you no lies, for he is an excellent man.”

**B03-P026**

As he spoke the sun set and darkness came on, and then Athena said, “Sir, all that you have said is well; but now have the tongues of the victims cut, and mix wine so that we may make drink offerings to Poseidon and the other immortals, and then go to bed, for it is bedtime. People should leave early and not keep late hours at a religious festival.”

**B03-P027**

So spoke the daughter of Zeus, and they did as she said. Manservants poured water over the hands of the guests, while pages filled the mixing-bowls with wine and water and handed it round, after giving every man his drink offering; then they threw the tongues of the victims into the fire, and stood up to make their drink offerings. When they had made their offerings and had drunk as much as each of them wanted, Athena and Telemachus were for going on board their ship, but Nestor caught them up at once and stopped them.

**B03-P028**

“Heaven and the immortal gods forbid,” he exclaimed, “that you should leave my house to go on board a ship. Do you think I am so poor and short of clothes, or that I have so few cloaks as to be unable to find comfortable beds both for myself and for my guests? Let me tell you, I have plenty of both rugs and cloaks, and I will not allow the son of my old friend Odysseus to camp down on the deck of a ship—not while I live—nor will my sons after me; they will keep open house as I have done.”

**B03-P029**

Then Athena answered, “Sir, you have spoken well, and it will be much better that Telemachus should do as you have said. He shall therefore go back with you and sleep at your house, but I must return to my crew to give them their orders and keep them in good heart. I am the only older person among them; the rest are all young men of Telemachus’s own age, who have made this voyage out of friendship, so I must go back to the ship and sleep there. Tomorrow, moreover, I must go to the Cauconians, where a large sum of money has long been owing to me. As for Telemachus, now that he is your guest, send him to Lacedaemon in a chariot, and let one of your sons go with him. Be so good as to provide him with your best and fastest horses as well.”

**B03-P030**

When she had said this she flew away in the form of an eagle, and everyone marveled as they watched her go. Nestor was astonished, and took Telemachus by the hand. “My friend,” he said, “I see that you are going to be a great hero some day, since the gods attend on you like this while you are still so young. This can have been none other of those who live in heaven than Zeus’s formidable daughter, the Trito-born, who showed such favor to your brave father among the Argives. Holy queen,” he went on, “grant your grace to me, to my good wife, and to my children. In return I will offer you in sacrifice a broad-browed heifer, a yearling, unbroken, and never yet brought under the yoke by any man. I will gild her horns, and offer her up to you in sacrifice.”

**B03-P031**

So he prayed, and Athena heard his prayer. He then led the way to his own house, followed by his sons and his sons-in-law. When they had got there and had taken their places on the benches and seats, he mixed them a bowl of sweet wine that was eleven years old when the housekeeper took the lid off the jar that held it. As he mixed the wine he prayed at length and made drink offerings to Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus. Then, when they had made their drink offerings and had drunk as much as each of them wanted, the others went home to bed, each to his own house; but Nestor put Telemachus to sleep in the room over the gateway, along with Pisistratus, who was the only unmarried son now left him. As for himself, he slept in an inner room of the house, with the queen his wife beside him.

**B03-P032**

When Dawn, the rosy-fingered child of morning, appeared, Nestor left his bed and took his seat on the benches of white polished marble that stood in front of his house. Neleus had sat here in former days, the equal of the gods in counsel, but he was dead now and had gone to the house of Hades; so Nestor sat in his seat, scepter in hand, as guardian of the public good. His sons gathered round him as they left their rooms—Echephron, Stratius, Perseus, Aretus and Thrasymedes; the sixth son was Pisistratus, and when Telemachus joined them they made him sit with them. Then Nestor spoke to them.

**B03-P033**

“My sons,” he said, “make haste to do as I tell you. First and foremost I wish to win the favor of the great goddess Athena, who showed herself to me visibly during yesterday’s festivities. Go, then, one or other of you, to the plain, tell the stockman to look me out a heifer, and come back here with it at once. Another must go to Telemachus’s ship and invite all the crew, leaving only two men in charge of the vessel. Someone else will run and fetch Laerceus the goldsmith to gild the heifer’s horns. The rest of you, stay where you are; tell the maids in the house to prepare an excellent dinner, and to fetch seats and logs of wood for a burnt offering. Tell them also to bring me some clear spring water.”

**B03-P034**

At this they hurried off on their several errands. The heifer was brought in from the plain, and Telemachus’s crew came from the ship; the goldsmith brought the anvil, hammer and tongs with which he worked his gold, and Athena herself came to accept the sacrifice. Nestor gave out the gold, and the smith gilded the horns of the heifer so that the goddess might take pleasure in their beauty. Then Stratius and Echephron brought her in by the horns; Aretus fetched water from the house in a ewer that had a flower pattern on it, and in his other hand he held a basket of barley meal; sturdy Thrasymedes stood by with a sharp axe, ready to strike the heifer, while Perseus held a bucket. Then Nestor began by washing his hands and sprinkling the barley meal, and he offered many a prayer to Athena as he threw a lock from the heifer’s head onto the fire.

**B03-P035**

When they had finished praying and sprinkling the barley meal, Thrasymedes dealt his blow, and brought the heifer down with a stroke that cut through the tendons at the base of her neck, and then the daughters and daughters-in-law of Nestor, and his venerable wife Eurydice (she was the eldest daughter of Clymenus), screamed with delight. Then they lifted the heifer’s head from the ground, and Pisistratus cut her throat. When she had finished bleeding and was quite dead, they cut her up. They cut out all the thigh bones in due order, wrapped them round in two layers of fat, and set some pieces of raw meat on top of them; then Nestor laid them on the wood fire and poured wine over them, while the young men stood near him with five-pronged spits in their hands. When the thighs were burned and they had tasted the inner meats, they cut the rest of the meat up small, put the pieces on the spits, and toasted them over the fire.

**B03-P036**

Meanwhile lovely Polycaste, Nestor’s youngest daughter, washed Telemachus. When she had washed him and anointed him with oil, she brought him a fine cloak and shirt, and he looked like a god as he came from the bath and took his seat beside Nestor. When the outer meats were done they drew them off the spits and sat down to dinner, waited on by some worthy attendants who kept pouring out their wine for them in cups of gold. As soon as they had had enough to eat and drink, Nestor said, “Sons, put Telemachus’s horses to the chariot so that he may start at once.”

**B03-P037**

So he spoke, and they did exactly as he had said, and yoked the fleet horses to the chariot. The housekeeper packed them a provision of bread, wine and sweetmeats fit for the sons of princes. Then Telemachus got into the chariot, while Pisistratus gathered up the reins and took his seat beside him. He lashed the horses on and they flew forward readily enough into the open country, leaving the high citadel of Pylos behind them. All that day they traveled, swaying the yoke upon their necks, until the sun went down and darkness lay over all the land. Then they reached Pherae, where Diocles lived, who was son to Ortilochus and grandson to Alpheus. Here they passed the night, and Diocles entertained them hospitably. When Dawn, the rosy-fingered child of morning, appeared, they yoked their horses again and drove out through the gateway under the echoing gatehouse. Pisistratus lashed the horses on and they flew forward readily enough; presently they came to the grain lands of the open country, and in the course of time completed their journey, so well did their horses carry them.
