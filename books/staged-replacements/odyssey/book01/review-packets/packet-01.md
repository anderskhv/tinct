## B01-P001 — ASSIGNED FOR REVIEW

**Source (Butler 1900):**

> Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide
after he had sacked the famous town of Troy. Many cities did he visit,
and many were the nations with whose manners and customs he was
acquainted; moreover he suffered much by sea while trying to save his
own life and bring his men safely home; but do what he might he could
not save his men, for they perished through their own sheer folly in
eating the cattle of the Sun-god Hyperion; so the god prevented them
from ever reaching home. Tell me, too, about all these things, oh
daughter of Jove, from whatsoever source you may know them.

**Candidate (modern English v1):**

> Tell me, Muse, about that resourceful man who wandered far and wide after he sacked the famous city of Troy. He saw the cities of many peoples and came to know their ways; and he suffered greatly at sea, struggling to save his own life and bring his men home. But he could not save his men, hard as he tried—they died through their own sheer folly, for they ate the cattle of the sun-god Hyperion, and the god kept them from ever reaching home. Tell me about all this too, daughter of Jove, whatever part of it you know.

## B01-P002 — ASSIGNED FOR REVIEW

**Source (Butler 1900):**

> So now all who escaped death in battle or by shipwreck had got safely
home except Ulysses, and he, though he was longing to return to his
wife and country, was detained by the goddess Calypso, who had got him
into a large cave and wanted to marry him. But as years went by, there
came a time when the gods settled that he should go back to Ithaca;
even then, however, when he was among his own people, his troubles were
not yet over; nevertheless all the gods had now begun to pity him
except Neptune, who still persecuted him without ceasing and would not
let him get home.

**Candidate (modern English v1):**

> By now, everyone else who had escaped death in battle or shipwreck had made it safely home—everyone except Ulysses. Though he longed to return to his wife and his country, the goddess Calypso was keeping him back, holding him in her great cave and wanting him for a husband. But as the years passed, a time came when the gods decided he should go home to Ithaca; even then, once he was among his own people, his troubles were not over. All the gods had come to pity him now except Neptune, who went on persecuting him without let-up and would not allow him to get home.

## B01-P004 — CONTEXT ONLY — reviewed in another packet

**Source (Butler 1900):**

> “See now, how men lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing
but their own folly. Look at Aegisthus; he must needs make love to
Agamemnon’s wife unrighteously and then kill Agamemnon, though he knew
it would be the death of him; for I sent Mercury to warn him not to do
either of these things, inasmuch as Orestes would be sure to take his
revenge when he grew up and wanted to return home. Mercury told him
this in all good will but he would not listen, and now he has paid for
everything in full.”

**Candidate (modern English v1):**

> “See how men blame us gods for their troubles, when it is really their own folly that brings them grief. Look at Aegisthus—he had to go and seduce Agamemnon's wife, and then kill Agamemnon, even though he knew it would be the death of him. I sent Mercury to warn him against doing either of these things, since Orestes was sure to take revenge once he grew up and wanted to come home. Mercury told him this in all good will, but he would not listen, and now he has paid for it all in full.”
