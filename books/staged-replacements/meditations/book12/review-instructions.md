## What to review

## Five flagged decisions, on which a ruling is specifically requested, and one point offered for confirmation

1. **XII.27's "Baiae" for PG's "Briae", and XII.29's "what" for PG's "that" — two departures from PG's letters in one book.** Every earlier book had at most one, and your predecessor called Book XI's "the best-evidenced in the package". Each here is argued on its own evidence, and each has Standard Ebooks against PG. *Briae* is not a place; *Baiae* is the Roman seaside resort, and it stands in a list of retreats beside "Tiberius at Capreae". *"That is its material"* makes a thing's matter be what the thing "is itself", which is the distinction the meditation exists to draw, and it breaks a three-member series ("what it is itself, what is its material, what the formal part") whose third member has already elided its verb; a `th`/`wh` slip is the ordinary explanation. Are both right? If only one, which?
2. **XII.3's "[to the god that is within thee]" dropped under D11.** The bracket is Long's own English gloss of "daemon", and it is the very phrase the glossary's *daimōn* row uses to render the word — the row cites **this section** as its authority. Applying the row gives "obedient to **the god within you**", after which the bracket is a second rendering of the same thing and D11 drops it. The result is textually indistinguishable from folding the bracket and deleting Long's word, which is uncomfortable: a reader cannot tell which rule produced it. Confirm the route, or rule that the **VII.17** treatment applies instead — keep Long's word and fold his gloss as an apposition, "obedient to your own daemon, the god that is within you", as the accepted Book VII does with "Eudaemonia, happiness".
3. **XII.17's "[For let thy efforts be—]" treated as a mark of textual doubt.** The **XI.26** ruling — the mark goes and the word stands, as with Long's daggers — was made for a single bracketed proper name and written into the ledger's **D13** row at Book XI acceptance. Here it is applied to a whole bracketed **clause**, which Long brackets because his Greek breaks off (his footnote: "There is something wrong here, or incomplete."). The alternatives are to drop the clause entirely as apparatus — which would leave XII.17 as two short lines and would hide a defect the reader is entitled to meet, against the V.29 / VII.58 / XI.18 precedent — or to keep the brackets, which no other bracket in the package does. Confirm, or rule otherwise.
4. **XII.4's "shall" beside "will" in one comparison.** "So much more respect do we have for what our neighbors **will** think of us than for what we **shall** think of ourselves." The rule keeps first-person "shall" (II.1, VIII.45, X.6 ×5, XI.18 ×2) and converts third-person plain futures, and applying it produces exactly this. It is ordinary English and it is the traditional distribution; but it is the first place in the package where the two stand in the same construction, and you may prefer "will" in both. Confirm, or rule for "will".
5. **XII.27's "[or Rufus at Velia]" dropped under D11.** D11 as worded reaches Long's bracketed **alternative renderings**. This bracket is not a second rendering of a Greek word but a second **construal** of the same Greek letters — one name, or a name and a place. The reasoning applied is that it is the same species of remark: Long addressing his reader about his own choices, not part of Marcus's sentence, and folding it would have Marcus name a fifth man in a list of four. Confirm that D11 reaches a bracketed alternative *construal*, or rule that it is the XII.17 / XI.26 textual-doubt class and that the words should stand.

Offered for confirmation rather than ruling: **"pancratiast" is kept untranslated at XII.9**, on the X.9 "Mimi" and XI.2 "pancratium" rulings — it is Long's text and not apparatus, the sentence glosses it by its own contrast with the gladiator (who drops the sword he uses, where the pancratiast always has his hand), and XI.2 already puts "pancratium" before the reader in an accepted book.

Two further points are put to you with reasons rather than flagged:

- **XII.23's two resumptive repairs.** "Nor he who has done this act, does he suffer any evil" becomes "nor **does he who has done this act suffer** any evil", and "nor he who has terminated this series at the proper time, has he been ill dealt with" becomes "nor **has he who has terminated this series at the proper time been** ill dealt with". Long is reproducing Greek word order with a resumptive pronoun that modern English does not have; no word is added or dropped, and the two commas that go were only holding the resumption open. But Long's words do move. Does this go further than the fidelity rule allows?
- **The five added commas**, listed above. Is each earned?
