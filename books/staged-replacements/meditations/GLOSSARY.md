# Glossary — stable renderings for the Meditations modern edition

Fixed before drafting Book II (2026-09-11) from a survey of George Long's usage
across all twelve books in `meditations-original-en.staged.json`. The left
column is Long's wording (the source); the middle column is the rendering used
in every candidate paragraph, in every book, unless a specific paragraph records
an exception in that book's `continuity.md`. The Greek is given only to show
which of Long's variants belong together; the candidate never uses the Greek.
**Exception, added at Book VII acceptance (2026-09-12):** that last clause
governs *this table's own Greek column*. Where **Long himself prints a Greek
word in the body of his translation**, it is his text and is kept — see "Long's
in-text Greek" under Voice and form below.

## Recurring concepts (from Anders's brief)

| Long (source) | Modern edition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| nature; according to nature; conformably to nature; contrary to nature | **nature**; **according to nature**; **against nature** | *physis*, *kata physin*. "According to nature" is kept: short, familiar, already modern. All of Long's variants ("conformably", "conformable to") collapse to it. |
| the nature of the whole; the nature of the universe; the nature of the universal | **the nature of the whole** | *hē tōn holōn physis*. One phrase for all of Long's variants. His third shape, "the nature of the universal" (VIII.5, VIII.6, VIII.35), is the same idea read through the row below that renders his bare noun "the universal" as "the whole"; row extended in Book VIII, before drafting. His adjective in "the universal nature" is a different phrase and keeps its own row. |
| the universal nature | **the universal nature** | Long's phrase for the same idea in later books; kept as its own phrase so the reader meets the same term Long uses there. |
| the common nature | **the common nature** | *koinē physis*, the nature all things share; Marcus contrasts it with "my own nature". Kept distinct. |
| the whole; the universe; the whole universe; the world (kosmos); the All; the universal (as a noun) | **the whole**; **the universe** | As Long has them. "Whole" is kept where Long has it because Marcus's argument is often about part and whole. Long's "the All" (IV.27, VII.75) is rendered "the whole". Added in Book IV. Long's bare noun "the universal" (VI.45 "for the interest of the universal") is the same idea and is rendered "the whole"; row extended in Book VI. His adjective in "the universal nature", "the universal substance" is untouched. |
| reason; the reason (of the universe, of our common nature) | **reason** | *logos*. Never "rationality" or "logic". |
| rational animal; reasonable animal; rational being | **rational being** | *zōon logikon*. "Animal" in Long means "living creature"; a modern reader hears something else. Long's "reasonable animal" (IV.5, IV.29, V.16, IX.8) is the same term. Added in Book IV. **Row corrected in Book XI, before drafting:** its left column formerly also listed "rational soul", which the edition has never in fact rendered "rational being" — the accepted Book VI keeps "a rational soul" at VI.14 (recorded in `book6/continuity.md`) and the accepted Book X keeps "an irrational soul" at X.33, which the Book X round-1 reviewer confirmed as "current English and Long's own". Where Long names **the soul itself** as the subject — "the properties of the rational soul" (XI.1), "held together by a rational soul" (VI.14), "governed by nature or an irrational soul" (X.33) — the phrase is his and is **kept**; the row governs his names for the **creature**. XI.1 is its third book, so the practice is promoted from the continuity sheets to this row, on the precedent of "beneficence" (Book VI) and "imagination" (Book VII). |
| social animal; social (acts, reason, life); political animal | **social being**; **social**; **political being** | *koinōnikos*, *politikon*. "Social act" and "social life" stay as they are. Long's "the rational animal is consequently also a political [social] animal" (X.2) belongs here: "animal" takes "being" as it does in every other compound, and the bracket is a second English rendering of one Greek word, so it is dropped under D11 and Long's primary word stands — "a political being". Not "political animal", which in current English is an idiom about a person who enjoys politics. Row extended in Book X, before drafting. |
| unsocial | **unsocial** | Not "antisocial" (a modern clinical sense) and not "unsociable" (shy). |
| the ruling faculty; the ruling part; the ruling principle; that which rules within; the leading principle | **the ruling part** | *to hēgemonikon*. One rendering for all five of Long's variants ("leading principle" V.3, VII.16, IX.18, IX.34; added in Book V). Where a first use in a book needs it, "the ruling part, the part of you that governs the rest" is the only permitted gloss, once. |
| the daemon (within); the deity which is (planted) in thee; the divinity which is planted in his breast; the divinity within thee; the god within; a good daemon | **the god within**; **a good god within** | *daimōn*. **Row corrected in Book XII, before drafting:** its left column named only Long's "deity" shape, but he uses "divinity" for the same thing and the edition has always rendered it the same way — the accepted Book III has "not to defile **the god within** his breast" at III.16 for Long's "the divinity which is planted in his breast". XII.1's "thy ruling faculty and **the divinity within thee**" is the same phrase in a fourth shape and takes the same rendering. A correction of a row that under-described the edition, not a change of practice; no accepted paragraph is affected. Long's *bare abstract* "the divinity" (XII.5 ×2, XII.14) is a different thing and belongs to the row below, which renders it "the divine". Long himself glosses "daemon" as "the god that is within thee" (XII.3). Not "spirit", "genius" or "inner self". His "a good daemon" — the *eudaimonia* pun — takes the same rendering: VII.17 "Eudaemonia [happiness] is a good daemon" is "Eudaemonia, happiness, is a good god within" in the accepted Book VII, and X.13's "fidelity, modesty, truth, law, a good daemon [happiness]" follows it, with Long's own bracketed gloss folded as an apposition. Row extended in Book X, before drafting. |
| the Deity; the divinity; a portion of the divinity | **the divine**; **a share of the divine** | Abstract references to the divine, as distinct from "the gods" and from "the god within". Long also uses "divinity" as a **count noun with a modifier**, naming the god rather than the quality — "impiety towards the highest divinity", "impiety to the same divinity" (IX.1). There "the divine" will not stand in ("the highest divine" is not English) and no substitution is needed: "the highest divinity" is current English for what Long means. So the row's rendering governs his **bare abstract** uses, and his modified count uses keep "divinity". Row extended in Book IX, before drafting. |
| the gods; god | **the gods**; **god** | As Long has them, including lowercase "god" where Marcus speaks of god in the singular. |
| providence | **providence** | *pronoia*. Not "fate", "destiny" or "a plan". |
| the common interest; the general interest; the common good; the common weal; the common advantage | **the common good** | *to koinon sympheron*. "Common weal" (I.16, V.35) rendered so in Book I; row extended in Book V. Long's "common advantage" is the same idea in a fourth English shape and was already rendered so in the accepted Book IV (IV.12, "as of what is just or **for the common good**"); XI.13 is its second book, and the variant is named here in Book XI, before drafting, so the row and the practice agree. Long's bare "advantage" where he speaks of the advantage of the whole or of a part (X.6 ×2) is a different phrase and is untouched. |
| a member of a civil community; political community; the world is a kind of state | **member of a community**; **political community**; **a kind of state** | As Long has them. |
| the most ancient city and polity; the highest city | **the oldest city and commonwealth**; **the highest city** | *polis*, *politeia*. "Commonwealth" for "polity". |
| opinion; all is opinion | **opinion** | *hypolēpsis*. Kept: "Everything is opinion" is clear and memorable. Never expanded into "how you judge things". |
| impressions; appearances (in the technical sense) | **impressions** | *phantasiai*. "Appearances" is kept only where Long means outward looks (I.15, I.16). |
| principles | **principles** | *dogmata*. |
| assent | **assent** | *synkatathesis*. |
| movement(s) (in the sense of impulse) | **impulse(s)** | *hormē*. Long's "movement" is misleading in modern English; where he means physical motion, "movement" stays. |
| things indifferent; indifferent | **indifferent** | Stoic technical sense. Long's text supplies the gloss itself where it matters (II.11: things "which make us neither better nor worse"). |
| intelligence; understanding; mind | **intelligence**; **understanding**; **mind** | Long keeps *nous*, *dianoia* and general "mind" apart; the candidate keeps his distinctions rather than merging them. |
| soul | **soul** | *psychē*. |
| flesh and breath; a little flesh and breath | **flesh and breath**; **a little flesh and breath** | Marcus's three-part self (flesh, breath, ruling part) is kept in his words. |
| fame; after fame; posthumous fame | **fame**; **fame after death** | *doxa*. |
| the present; the present time | **the present** | |
| the elements; dissolution (of the elements) | **the elements**; **dissolution** | Kept: the argument about death depends on these being the same words each time. |
| philosophy | **philosophy** | Never "wisdom" or "a philosophy". |
| felicity; happiness | **happiness** | |
| vexed; vexation; discontent(ed) | **resentful / resent**; **resentment**; **discontented** | "To be vexed at anything which happens" → "to resent anything that happens". The noun "vexation" (VI.20 "show any signs of vexation") → "resentment", and "vexed because" (VI.27) → "resentful because"; row extended in Book VI. Long's transitive verb "vex" (IV.44, V.23, "they vex him") is a different word, current, and stays. His reflexive "to vex ourselves at things" (VII.38) is not that transitive verb but his "be vexed at" in another shape, and takes "resent" with the rest; row extended in Book VII, which also has the plain "be vexed" at VII.58, VII.66 and VII.70. |
| tranquil; tranquillity | **calm** | |
| kinsman; akin to me; kinship | **kinsman**; **akin to me**; **kinship** | Kept: the family metaphor is the point. |
| dissatisfied; dissatisfaction | **discontented**; **discontent** | Long's word is neither "vexed" nor "discontent"; rendered with the "discontent" family for consistency (decided in Book II v2, II.2 and II.13). |
| in a manner | **in a way** | Long's hedge (II.10, III.2); "in a manner" now reads as "in a fashion". Decided in Book II v2. |
| daemons (plural, of spirits expelled by charlatans) | **spirits** | I.6. These are not Marcus's *daimōn*; the glossary's "the god within" does not apply. Decided in Book I. |
| perturbation(s); free from perturbation | **disturbance(s)**; **free from disturbance** | *tarachē*. IV.3, IV.37, VI.16, VII.16, IX.31 and others. "Perturbation" is dated and, in modern use, mild; "disturbance" is the plain word for the same state and is Long's own verb ("do not disturb thyself", IV.26). Added in Book IV. |
| seminal principle(s); seminal intelligence | **generative principle(s)**; **generative intelligence** | *logos spermatikos*, the seed-like reason from which things grow. IV.14, IV.21, VI.24. "Seminal" now means "highly influential", which is not the sense; "generative" says what "seminal" said (of seed, producing). Where Long has "seed" itself (IV.36) it stays "seed". Added in Book IV. |
| mutation; transmutation; transmuted | **change**; **transformation**; **transformed** | Long's own "change" and "transformation" are used elsewhere for the same idea (IV.3 "The universe is transformation", IV.36, IV.42); "mutation" now suggests genetics. IV.14, IV.21, IV.39, IX.19. Added in Book IV. |
| vulgar (notion, help, comfort); the vulgar | **commonplace**; **the common sort of men** | Long's "vulgar" means common or ordinary, not crude. "The common sort of men" is Long's own phrase (IV.3). IV.20, IV.36, IV.50, IX.3. Added in Book IV. |
| affects (my affects and disposition; those affects; the affects of the flesh) | **feelings** | *pathē*. V.20, V.26, VII.2, VII.66, VIII.1, XII.19. Long's noun "affects" is now psychological jargon and, to a general reader, a verb; "feelings" is the plain word for the same thing (the states that rise from the flesh to the mind, V.26) and imposes no modern reading. Not "emotions" (Long uses "emotions" himself, V.36, and it stays there) and not "passions". Added in Book V. |
| the formal; the material (as nouns: "composed of the formal and the material"); the causal | **form**; **matter**; **cause** | V.13, XII.18, XII.29. Long's own words for the same pair in IV.21 are "the cause of form" and "that which is material", which stay as they are; where he uses the bare adjectives as nouns, "form" and "matter" say the same in current English. Added in Book V. Long pairs "the material" with "the causal" as well (VII.10 "everything formal", VII.29 "the causal and the material", VIII.11), each time offering the other word in brackets; the bracketed word is a second rendering and is dropped under D11, and Long's primary word is rendered — "everything that is form" at VII.10, "cause and matter" at VII.29. Row extended in Book VII. |
| beneficence; benevolence; benevolent (acts, disposition); benevolently | **kindness**; **kind**; **kindly** | Long's one word-family kept as one family: I.3, I.7, I.9, I.15 (recorded in `book1/continuity.md` at Book I), V.5 "benevolence" → "kindness", VI.47 "a benevolent disposition" → "a kind disposition" (Long's own phrase at V.31). "Benevolent" now reads as institutional; "kind" is the plain word. Promoted from the Book I continuity sheet to a glossary row in Book VI, its third book. **Row extended in Book XII, before drafting:** the adverb, which occurs exactly once in the whole work — XII.5 "arranged all things well and **benevolently** for mankind" → "well and **kindly**" — takes the family's word, as the noun and the adjective do. |
| imagination (Long's word where he does not use "impressions") | **imagination** | *phantasia*, the same Greek word the "impressions" row covers, but Long keeps two English words for it and the candidate keeps his distinction rather than merging them (the "intelligence / understanding / mind" row follows the same principle). "Imagination" is current English and needs nothing. II.12 ("the things his imagination presents to him") and III.1 rendered so in Books II and III; VII.17 ("What then are you doing here, imagination?"), VII.29 ("Wipe out the imagination"), VII.64 ("if you add nothing to it in imagination"), VIII.29, IX.7. Promoted from the Book II and Book III candidates to a glossary row in Book VII, its third book, on the Book VI precedent for "beneficence". |
| a man; men (generic) | **a man**; **men** | Long's generic "man" is kept. Rewriting it would be a modern imposition, and Marcus's "as a Roman and a man" is his own. "Human being" is used only where Long uses it (V.1, VI.23). |

## Voice and form

- **Self-address.** Long's "thou / thee / thy / thyself" becomes "you / your /
  yourself". Marcus addresses himself; nothing is turned into advice for a
  reader. No "we" is introduced where Long has "thou".
- **Long's "shall / shalt" (added at Book VIII acceptance, 2026-09-12).** As a
  **plain future** — in a statement, or in a subordinate clause of condition or
  time — it is rendered "will", or the plain present where English uses the
  present for future time in such a clause ("if thou shalt live" → "if you
  live", "as soon as he shall choose" → "as soon as he chooses", "if thou shalt
  have no sensation" → "if you have no sensation"). "Shall" is **kept** only
  where it is current English in its own right: the first person ("I shall meet
  the busybody", II.1; "there I shall keep my divine part calm", VIII.45), the
  emphatic or volitional "shall" ("no man shall hinder me", V.29), the
  deliberative "shall" of a question, **direct or indirect**, in the first or
  third person ("How then shall a man do this?", VIII.1; "keeping to this main
  point, how the mind … shall be free from disturbances", IX.41), and the
  subjunctive of a negative consecutive clause ("so that each act shall not do
  its duty", VIII.32).
  Second-person "shall" in a question ("How then shall you possess…?") is not
  current and takes "will". Ruled at the Book VIII round-1 review (findings
  1.1, 55.1, 58.1, answered as one decision), and **widened at Book IX
  acceptance (2026-09-12, finding 41.1)** from "a direct question" to "a
  question, direct or indirect": IX.41 keeps Long's "how the mind … shall be
  free from disturbances", an indirect deliberative question inside Epicurus's
  reported speech, where "will be free" would turn a standing problem into a
  forecast. The rendering was right on the rule's spirit, the rule's letter did
  not reach it, and the wording is widened here — before Book X is drafted — so
  that a later drafter reading the rule literally does not convert it. Three
  third-person plain futures
  stand in already-accepted books (III.9, VII.8, VII.24); they are recorded in
  `00-progress-ledger.md` under "Open, not blocking" and are not reopened.
- **Imperatives stay imperatives.** "Remember", "Consider", "Throw away your
  books" are kept as the bare commands they are.
- **Numbering.** Every candidate paragraph begins with the same section number
  as its source paragraph ("1. ", "2. " …).
- **No expansion of short sections.** A one-line meditation stays one line.
- **No glosses beyond the glossary.** Where a term must be clarified, the
  clarification is the one recorded above or in the book's `continuity.md`, at
  most once per book, and never a modern psychological reading.
- **Long's square-bracket supplements** ("[only]", "[the same]", "[the world]")
  are folded into ordinary prose where they carry Long's plain sense; the
  brackets themselves are not reproduced. Long's parenthetical cross-references
  ("(vi. 28)") and his source citations after verse quotations are apparatus
  and are dropped from the candidate. Each such drop is listed in the book's
  `continuity.md`.
- **Manuscript notes** ("Among the Quadi at the Granua.", "This in Carnuntum.")
  are kept at the end of the section where Long prints them, as a separate
  final sentence: "Written among the Quadi, at the Granua." / "Written at
  Carnuntum."
- **Long's in-text Greek (exception to the Greek rule above).** Where Long
  prints a Greek word in the body of his translation — PG renders these
  `[Greek: …]` and the staged original `(Greek: …)` — the word is Long's text,
  not apparatus, and is **kept**, transliterated as the staged file has it and
  without the `Greek:` label: "I am a member (melos)… you are a part (meros)"
  (VII.13); "Eudaemonia, happiness, is a good god within" (VII.17). The two
  places in Book VII where this arises are arguments that do not exist without
  the Greek — a pun on two words differing by one letter, and an etymology —
  so dropping the word would delete the meditation rather than modernise it.
  The glossary's "the candidate never uses the Greek" was always a rule about
  this table's Greek column, which exists only to group Long's English
  variants; it was never a rule about words Long prints himself. Ruled by the
  Book VII round-1 reviewer and written here at the reviewer's suggestion so
  the question does not reopen at **VIII.57**, where PG has two more
  `[Greek: …]` spans. Long's own English gloss beside such a word (his
  bracketed "[happiness]") is folded as an apposition, as any supplement is.
- **Punctuation.** Straight apostrophes and quotation marks, em dashes without
  spaces, as in the staged original.
- **Spelling.** American, following the PG #15877 base text (honor, vapor).
