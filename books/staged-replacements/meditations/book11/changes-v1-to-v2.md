## The eight minor findings — all applied

The round-1 verdict was *Accept after corrections* with **0 substantive** findings: "nothing in the thirty-nine paragraphs is missing, added, softened, expanded, mistranslated or imported". Eight minor findings and three optional preferences were raised. **All eight minor findings are applied.** Four change the text (1.1, 18.1, 21.1, 26.1); three are record corrections that change no word (19.1, C1, C2); one adds a decision row to the ledger (C3 → **D14**).

**1.1 — applied.** XI.1's closing sentence read `Thus the right reason **differs not at all** from the reason of justice`, a finite negative without do-support and, as the reviewer found by searching the whole candidate, **the only one left standing in Book XI** — the identical shape eighteen sections later at XI.19 (`comes not from the real thoughts`) had already been modernised to `does not come from`. Two sentences of the same grammar decided two ways in one book is the defect, and the fix is the reviewer's: `Thus the right reason **does not differ at all** from the reason of justice.` PG's article is kept (D6, base-text ruling 8), the weight still falls on "not at all", and nothing else in the sentence moves. This is the last sentence of the book's opening meditation, which makes the archaism the more conspicuous.

**18.1 — applied.** XI.18's sixth rule read `consider when you are **greatly resentful** or grieved`. The reviewer is right that this is not idiomatic — the adverbs that take *resentful* are *deeply* and *bitterly* — and right about how it got there: the glossary gives "resent" for Long's "be vexed at", "much resentful" is impossible, and "greatly" was reached for. v2 reads `consider when you are **deeply resentful** or grieved`. Same register as Long's "much vexed", the pairing with "or grieved" intact, and **the glossary row is untouched**: the other three renderings in the same paragraph ("resentment", "angry and resentful", "resenting them") are idiomatic and unchanged.

**21.1 — applied, and the two sentences are now decided alike.** XI.21 removed the subject–verb comma in its last sentence (`For he who directs all his own efforts to this object will make all his acts alike`) and kept it in its first (`He who has not one and always the same object in life, cannot be one and the same all through his life`) — the same construction, a long `He who …` subject followed directly by its verb, in the same paragraph, and `continuity.md` listed the removal and did not mention the retention, so the inconsistency was not a recorded decision. **Removed in the first sentence too**, which is the direction the reviewer's confidence and the X-book precedent both point (X.6, X.20, X.33 ×2, all removals; and XI.10's removal in this book). Restoring both would have been internally consistent but would have broken with five removals already accepted. The punctuation tally now reads **three** subject–verb commas removed (XI.10, XI.21 ×2), and the point is recorded once at XI.21 rather than half-recorded.

**26.1 — applied, and the X.36.2 ruling is now bounded.** XI.26 read `constantly to think of **one** of the men of former times`, from Long's `some one of the men of former times`, and `continuity.md` filed it under the X.36.2 normalisation of 1862's two-word *some one* → *someone*. **That ruling does not reach this case, and the reviewer's class analysis is right.** At X.36 "some one" is the indefinite pronoun; here `some one of the men of former times` is a **partitive** — one or another of them, indefinite as to which — where the X.36 normalisation would have produced the ungrammatical "someone of the men". Seeing that, v1 deleted "some" instead; but deleting it makes an indefinite definite, turning "one or another of the men of former times" into "one of the men", which reads as one particular exemplar the precept has in mind. The Ephesian precept is the opposite: keep *some* exemplar or other constantly before you. v2 reads `constantly to think of **one or another** of the men of former times who practiced virtue.` The reviewer's alternative — keep Long's "some one of" — was weighed and declined: it is readable, but in a modern edition the two-word spelling reads as the pronoun *someone* mis-set, which is the very misreading the X.36.2 normalisation exists to prevent, and it would leave the book's one partitive looking like an unnormalised leftover. "One or another of" is plain current English, holds the indefiniteness exactly, and adds two words and no sense. **The bound on X.36.2 is now written into `continuity.md` and into the base-text note: the normalisation reaches the indefinite pronoun and not a partitive `some one of …`.**

**19.1 — applied as a record correction; no word changes.** XI.19's `the superior faculty` is **kept**, as the reviewer rules on the substance. `continuity.md` said of it "kept as Long's own phrase — see the flagged decisions", and the glossary-terms table repeated "flagged below", but **it is not in the flagged list**, which has five items and never included it; a reader following the cross-reference found nothing. Both places now carry the reason instead of the dangling pointer: "the superior faculty" is not one of the five variants the *hēgemonikon* row collects, the package's settled practice with Long's own phrases the row does not name is to keep them (VI.14 "a rational soul", X.33 "an irrational soul", X.8 and XI.20 "the intelligent part"), and the section's argument — the superior part overpowered by the perishable part — is about **superiority**, not about ruling, so "the ruling part" would be the wrong word as well as an imported one. That it stands eleven lines from XI.20's "the ruling part" is not a drift: they are two different phrases of Long's, kept apart.

## The three optional findings — one applied, two recorded and left

**15.1 — applied; the reviewer's word is better than the drafter's.** XI.15 read `just as he who is beloved **at once** reads everything in the eyes of lovers`. The *disposition* was upheld at review and stands: Long's "forthwith" is dead, and X.30's "immediately" is unavailable here because Long's own "immediately" stands nine words earlier, inside the dagger clause, so using it would manufacture an echo Long does not have and would make the dagger clause chime with the clause after it. But "at once" carries a second current sense — *simultaneously* — and it sits **immediately before "everything"**, so "at once reads everything" can be taken for "reads everything at the same time", which is not the sense: the point is the speed of the reading, not its scope. **`instantly`** is one word for one word, the same plain register, unambiguous, and it does not echo "immediately". There was no considered reason on record against it — `continuity.md` argued only against "immediately" — so D8 directs that the finding is applied. The dagger clause itself is untouched: it ends at "in his eyes", and this word falls outside it.

**12.1 — no change; logged in the ledger, which is the reviewer's own second route.** XI.12 keeps Long's `neither extended towards any object, nor contracted inwards, nor dispersed, **nor sinks down**` — three past participles and then a finite present verb. The reviewer offers two routes and asks only that one be taken: change to "nor sunk down", or record it in `../00-progress-ledger.md` under "Open, not blocking" beside the II.5 dangling relative. **The second is taken, and there is a reason for preferring it to the first.** "Sunk down" is a past participle in a series of *passives* ("is extended … contracted … dispersed"), so the parallel it completes would make the soul's sinking something **done to it**; Long's finite verb is intransitive and the sinking is the soul's own, which is the Stoic image (the sphere holds its figure of itself, or loses it of itself). Repairing the grammar would import an agent the sentence does not have. Nor is Long's construction strictly wrong: the negation can be read as scoping over the whole verb phrase — *neither is extended … nor sinks down* — which is loose but available. So this is the II.5 class exactly: a place where Long's syntax is awkward in modern English and the honest repair is worse than the awkwardness, recorded where a later reader can argue with it rather than fixed in silence. Logged in the ledger and recorded at XI.12 in `continuity.md`.

**34.1 — no change, record only, and the reason is now written out.** XI.34 keeps `"Tomorrow **perchance** you will die."` while XI.16 modernises the same word to "perhaps". The reviewer upholds the distinction and says plainly it would not change it, but asks that `continuity.md` say **why this counts as heightened speech** rather than only citing IX.3, because a Book XII drafter meeting a third case will have nothing else to go on. Fair, and the reason is now at XI.34: what makes this heightened is not who says it but **what kind of utterance it is** — a sentence composed to be said over and over as a discipline, framed twice as quoted speech (Epictetus reported by Marcus, quoting the father's whisper to himself), set off by quotation marks, and immediately treated by the interlocutor as a *form of words* ("But those are words of bad omen") rather than as information. A formula's solemnity is its whole function; "perhaps" would make it a casual aside and the objection that follows it unmotivated. XI.16's is the opposite case, Marcus's own running prose in an unquoted reported clause. **The test for a third case is therefore: is the word inside a quoted utterance whose form is itself the point?** Not: who is speaking. Recorded at XI.34 and in the XI.16 entry that cross-refers to it.

## The reviewer's rulings — every flagged decision settled, none left open

All five decisions flagged for the reviewer and the one point offered for confirmation were ruled, and the drafter is upheld on every one. Two further things put to the reviewer with reasons were confirmed. `continuity.md` and `provenance.json` move all eight out of the flagged section.

**1. XI.18 "flattering" for PG's "nattering" — UPHELD, "and it is not a close call".** "Natter" is a nineteenth-century dialect verb for chattering that does not enter standard written English until much later and makes no sense paired against "being vexed at them"; Long writes "flatter" of exactly this at XI.14 eleven sections earlier; the sentence's whole shape is flattery against resentment; Standard Ebooks reads "flattering"; and an `fl` ligature read as `n` is the ordinary class of slip. Same class as VIII.37 "Fergamus", IX.34 "pool souls" and X.15 "Let me see", each upheld at round 1 of its own book. It remains **the one departure from PG's letters in Book XI**.

**2. XI.18's nine-word bracket folded — UPHELD, and D13 must NOT acquire a size threshold.** The reviewer's first ground is stronger than the drafter's and is decisive: **the bracket carries the enumeration.** "[If any have offended against thee, consider first]" contains the word *first*, and the meditation then runs "Second, … Third, … Fourth, … Fifth, … Sixth, … Seventh, … Eighth, … Ninth," and closes "Remember these **nine rules**". Drop the bracket and the list has nine members and eight labels — an incoherence on the page. Folding is not merely the better reading; it is the only one that leaves Long's text consistent with itself. It also passes D13's own test cleanly: the bracket is in Marcus's voice and is about the occasion of the rules, not about Long's handling of the Greek. **And size is not the test.** X.23's dropped translator's note is *also nine words*, and it is dropped on its content; a size threshold would fail the very case D13 was written to get right. **The ledger's D13 row now says so explicitly**, so that a drafter meeting a long supplement does not invent a word limit. (The colon after the bracket is Long's own, outside the bracket, and the candidate keeps it — confirmed.)

**3. XI.10's "[things indifferent]" dropped under D11 — UPHELD, with the opacity answered rather than denied.** In shape it is X.2's "[social]" and X.15's "[political community]" exactly: a second English rendering of one Greek term beside the primary rendering Long has already given, so D11 fires and folding would make Marcus name two classes where he names one. The drafter's own objection — that here the primary word left standing is the *less* transparent of the two — is real, and each alternative is worse: folding contradicts the Book X ruling on identical shapes; extending the glossary's "indifferent" row to swallow "middle things" is folding by another name and overwrites Long's word with his gloss; a clarification breaches the no-glosses rule. And the cost is smaller than it looks — the clause supplies its own sense ("justice will not be observed, if we either care for middle things, or are easily deceived…"), where "middle things" is plainly the class one should *not* care about, and the accepted Book II already gives the reader Long's own gloss at II.11. **Ruling recorded in `continuity.md` so the question does not reopen at XII.**

**4. XI.26's "[Ephesians]" folded as a mark of textual doubt — CONFIRMED.** Long brackets the name because the manuscript reading is uncertain (his footnote reports Gataker's conjecture of *Epikoureion* for *Ephesion*), not because it is his supplement or his second English word for something. It is therefore a **textual mark**, and the package's settled practice with textual marks is that the mark goes and the word stands — which is exactly what it does with Long's daggers, three of them in this same book. D11 cannot reach it (there is no primary rendering for it to be an alternative to) and D13 cannot (it says nothing about Long's *handling*; it marks the state of the *Greek*). **This is the first bracket of its class in the package**, and the reviewer asks for a sentence in the ledger against the chance that Book XII contains another; that sentence is added to D13's row, where the three-way classification lives.

**5. XI.15's "at once" for "forthwith" — the reasoning UPHELD, the word replaced (finding 15.1 above).** The refusal of X.30's "immediately" is right, and "forthwith" could not stand: it is dead, and being inside the dagger *sentence* does not protect it, since the package's dagger practice protects the **clause**, which ends at "in his eyes". Only the replacement was questionable. v2 reads **"instantly"**.

**6. "Pancratium" kept untranslated at XI.2 — CONFIRMED.** Long's text, not apparatus; third in a list of three entertainments ("pleasing song and dancing and the pancratium"), where the reader takes it from its company; Long reuses it later in the same section, so a replacement would have to be made twice; and any gloss would import the footnote content the no-glosses rule exists to prevent. The X.9 "Mimi" case exactly.

**7. The four Epictetus references dropped — CONFIRMED.** They are Long's parenthetical citations of Arrian's *Discourses* by book, chapter and section, the same apparatus class as "(vi. 28)" and as the Hesiod citation dropped at V.33.
