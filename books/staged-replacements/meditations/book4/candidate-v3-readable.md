# Meditations, Book 4 — modern-English candidate v3

**Title:** Book 4

The accepted candidate v2 with the cross-book v3 pass applied (see `../README.md` and `changes-v2-to-v3.md`). Paragraph IDs are for reference only; the leading number is part of the text.

**[B4-P001]**

1. The ruling part, when it is according to nature, is so disposed toward the events that happen that it always adapts itself easily to what is possible and is presented to it. For it requires no definite material, but it moves toward its purpose, under certain conditions, however; and it makes a material for itself out of what opposes it, as fire lays hold of what falls into it, by which a small light would have been put out; but when the fire is strong, it soon makes its own the matter heaped on it, and consumes it, and rises higher by means of this very material.

**[B4-P002]**

2. Let no act be done without a purpose, nor otherwise than according to the perfect principles of art.

**[B4-P003]**

3. Men seek retreats for themselves, houses in the country, seashores, and mountains; and you too are in the habit of wanting such things very much. But this is altogether a mark of the most common sort of men, for it is in your power, whenever you choose, to retire into yourself. For nowhere does a man retire with more quiet or more freedom from trouble than into his own soul, particularly when he has within him such thoughts that by looking into them he is at once in perfect calm; and I affirm that calm is nothing else than the good ordering of the mind. Constantly, then, give yourself this retreat, and renew yourself; and let your principles be brief and fundamental, which, as soon as you turn to them, will be enough to cleanse the soul completely, and to send you back free from all discontent with the things you return to. For what are you discontented with? With the badness of men? Recall to your mind this conclusion, that rational beings exist for one another, and that to endure is a part of justice, and that men do wrong involuntarily; and consider how many already, after mutual enmity, suspicion, hatred, and fighting, have been stretched out dead, reduced to ashes; and be quiet at last.—But perhaps you are discontented with what is assigned to you out of the universe.—Recall to your mind this alternative: either there is providence or there are atoms, a chance concurrence of things; or remember the arguments by which it has been proved that the world is a kind of political community; and be quiet at last.—But perhaps bodily things will still fasten on you.—Consider, then, further that the mind does not mingle with the breath, whether it moves gently or violently, once it has drawn itself apart and discovered its own power; and think also of all that you have heard and assented to about pain and pleasure; and be quiet at last.—But perhaps the desire for the thing called fame will torment you.—See how soon everything is forgotten, and look at the chaos of infinite time on each side of the present, and the emptiness of applause, and the changeableness and lack of judgment in those who pretend to give praise, and the narrowness of the space within which it is confined; and be quiet at last. For the whole earth is a point, and how small a corner of it is this dwelling of yours, and how few are there in it, and what kind of people are they who will praise you. This, then, remains: remember to retire into this little territory of your own, and above all do not distract or strain yourself, but be free, and look at things as a man, as a human being, as a citizen, as a mortal. But among the things readiest to your hand to turn to, let there be these two. One is that things do not touch the soul, for they are external and remain immovable; but our disturbances come only from the opinion within. The other is that all these things you see change immediately and will no longer be; and constantly bear in mind how many of these changes you have already witnessed. The universe is transformation: life is opinion.

**[B4-P004]**

4. If our intellectual part is common, the reason also, in respect of which we are rational beings, is common: if this is so, common also is the reason that commands us what to do and what not to do; if this is so, there is a common law also; if this is so, we are fellow citizens; if this is so, we are members of some political community; if this is so, the world is in a way a state. For of what other common political community will anyone say that the whole human race are members? And from there, from this common political community, come also our very intellectual faculty and reasoning faculty and our capacity for law; or where else do they come from? For as my earthly part is a portion given to me from certain earth, and what is watery from another element, and what is hot and fiery from some peculiar source (for nothing comes out of what is nothing, as nothing also returns to non-existence), so also the intellectual part comes from some source.

**[B4-P005]**

5. Death is such as birth is, a mystery of nature; a composition out of the same elements, and a decomposition into the same; and altogether not a thing of which any man should be ashamed, for it is not against the nature of a rational being, and not against the reason of our constitution.

**[B4-P006]**

6. It is natural that these things should be done by such persons; it is a matter of necessity; and if a man will not have it so, he will not allow the fig tree to have juice. But by all means bear this in mind, that within a very short time both you and he will be dead; and soon not even your names will be left behind.

**[B4-P007]**

7. Take away your opinion, and then the complaint, "I have been harmed," is taken away. Take away the complaint, "I have been harmed," and the harm is taken away.

**[B4-P008]**

8. What does not make a man worse than he was also does not make his life worse, nor does it harm him either from without or from within.

**[B4-P009]**

9. The nature of what is universally useful has been compelled to do this.

**[B4-P010]**

10. Consider that everything that happens, happens justly, and if you observe carefully, you will find it to be so. I do not say only with respect to the continuity of the series of things, but with respect to what is just, and as if it were done by one who assigns to each thing its value. Observe, then, as you have begun; and whatever you do, do it together with this, being good, and in the sense in which a man is properly understood to be good. Keep to this in every action.

**[B4-P011]**

11. Do not have such an opinion of things as the man who does you wrong has, or such as he wishes you to have, but look at them as they are in truth.

**[B4-P012]**

12. A man should always have these two rules ready: one, to do only what the reason of the ruling and legislating part suggests for the use of men; the other, to change your opinion if there is anyone at hand who sets you right and moves you from any opinion. But this change of opinion must come only from a certain persuasion, as of what is just or for the common good, and the like, not because it appears pleasant or brings reputation.

**[B4-P013]**

13. Do you have reason? I have.—Why, then, do you not use it? For if this does its own work, what else do you want?

**[B4-P014]**

14. You have existed as a part. You will disappear into that which produced you; or rather, you will be received back into its generative principle by transformation.

**[B4-P015]**

15. Many grains of frankincense on the same altar: one falls before, another falls after; but it makes no difference.

**[B4-P016]**

16. Within ten days you will seem a god to those to whom you are now a beast and an ape, if you return to your principles and the worship of reason.

**[B4-P017]**

17. Do not act as if you were going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over you. While you live, while it is in your power, be good.

**[B4-P018]**

18. How much trouble he avoids who does not look to see what his neighbor says or does or thinks, but only to what he does himself, that it may be just and pure; or, as Agathon says, do not look round at the depraved morals of others, but run straight along the line without deviating from it.

**[B4-P019]**

19. He who has a vehement desire for fame after death does not consider that every one of those who remember him will himself also die very soon; then again also those who succeeded them, until the whole remembrance has been extinguished as it is passed on through men who foolishly admire and perish. But suppose that those who will remember are even immortal, and that the remembrance will be immortal, what then is this to you? And I do not say what is it to the dead, but what is it to the living? What is praise, except indeed so far as it has a certain utility? For you now unseasonably reject the gift of nature, clinging to something else....

**[B4-P020]**

20. Everything that is in any way beautiful is beautiful in itself, and ends in itself, not having praise as part of itself. A thing, then, is made neither worse nor better by being praised. I affirm this also of the things called beautiful by the common sort of men, for example, material things and works of art. What is really beautiful has no need of anything; no more than law, no more than truth, no more than kindness or modesty. Which of these things is beautiful because it is praised, or spoiled by being blamed? Is such a thing as an emerald made worse than it was if it is not praised? or gold, ivory, purple, a lyre, a little knife, a flower, a shrub?

**[B4-P021]**

21. If souls continue to exist, how does the air contain them from eternity?—But how does the earth contain the bodies of those who have been buried from time so remote? For as here the change of these bodies after a certain continuance, whatever it may be, and their dissolution, make room for other dead bodies, so the souls that are removed into the air, after existing for some time, are transformed and diffused, and take on a fiery nature by being received into the generative intelligence of the universe, and in this way make room for the fresh souls that come to dwell there. And this is the answer a man might give on the hypothesis that souls continue to exist. But we must think not only of the number of bodies that are buried in this way, but also of the number of animals that are eaten daily by us and the other animals. For what a number is consumed, and so in a way buried in the bodies of those who feed on them! And nevertheless this earth receives them by reason of the changes of these bodies into blood, and their transformations into the aerial or the fiery element. What is the investigation into the truth in this matter? The division into that which is material and that which is the cause of form.

**[B4-P022]**

22. Do not be whirled about, but in every impulse have regard to justice, and on the occasion of every impression maintain the faculty of comprehension.

**[B4-P023]**

23. Everything harmonizes with me that is in harmony with you, O Universe. Nothing is too early or too late for me that is in due time for you. Everything is fruit to me that your seasons bring, O Nature: from you are all things, in you are all things, to you all things return. The poet says, Dear city of Cecrops; and will you not say, Dear city of Zeus?

**[B4-P024]**

24. Occupy yourself with few things, says the philosopher, if you would be calm.—But consider whether it would not be better to say, Do what is necessary, and whatever the reason of the being that is naturally social requires, and as it requires. For this brings not only the calm that comes from doing well, but also the calm that comes from doing few things. For since the greatest part of what we say and do is unnecessary, if a man takes this away, he will have more leisure and less uneasiness. Accordingly, on every occasion a man should ask himself, Is this one of the unnecessary things? Now a man should take away not only unnecessary acts, but also unnecessary thoughts, for then superfluous acts will not follow after.

**[B4-P025]**

25. Try how the life of the good man suits you, the life of him who is satisfied with his portion out of the whole, and satisfied with his own just acts and kind disposition.

**[B4-P026]**

26. Have you seen those things? Look also at these. Do not disturb yourself. Make yourself all simplicity. Does anyone do wrong? It is to himself that he does the wrong. Has anything happened to you? Well; out of the universe from the beginning everything that happens has been apportioned and spun out to you. In a word, your life is short. You must turn the present to profit with the aid of reason and justice. Be sober in your relaxation.

**[B4-P027]**

27. Either it is a well-arranged universe or a chaos huddled together, but still a universe. But can a certain order exist in you, and disorder in the whole? And this too when all things are so separated and diffused and in sympathy with one another.

**[B4-P028]**

28. A black character, a womanish character, a stubborn character, bestial, childish, animal, stupid, counterfeit, scurrilous, fraudulent, tyrannical.

**[B4-P029]**

29. If he is a stranger to the universe who does not know what is in it, no less is he a stranger who does not know what is going on in it. He is a runaway who flees from social reason; he is blind who shuts the eyes of the understanding; he is poor who has need of another, and does not have from himself all that is useful for life. He is an abscess on the universe who withdraws and separates himself from the reason of our common nature through being displeased with the things that happen, for the same nature produces this, and has produced you too: he is a piece torn off from the state who tears his own soul away from that of rational beings, which is one.

**[B4-P030]**

30. The one is a philosopher without a tunic, and the other without a book: here is another half naked: I have no bread, he says, and I abide by reason—and I do not get the means of living out of my learning, and I abide by my reason.

**[B4-P031]**

31. Love the art, poor as it may be, that you have learned, and be content with it; and pass through the rest of life like one who has entrusted to the gods with his whole soul all that he has, making yourself neither the tyrant nor the slave of any man.

**[B4-P032]**

32. Consider, for example, the times of Vespasian. You will see all these things: people marrying, bringing up children, sick, dying, making war, feasting, trading, cultivating the ground, flattering, obstinately arrogant, suspecting, plotting, wishing for some to die, grumbling about the present, loving, heaping up treasure, desiring the consulship, kingly power. Well, then, that life of these people no longer exists at all. Again, move on to the times of Trajan. Again, all is the same. Their life too is gone. In the same way look also at the other epochs of time and of whole nations, and see how many, after great efforts, soon fell and were resolved into the elements. But chiefly you should think of those you have yourself known distracting themselves over idle things, neglecting to do what was in accordance with their proper constitution, and to hold firmly to this and be content with it. And here it is necessary to remember that the attention given to everything has its proper value and proportion. For then you will not be discontented, if you apply yourself to smaller matters no further than is fitting.

**[B4-P033]**

33. The words that were once familiar are now antiquated: so also the names of those who were famed of old are now in a way antiquated, Camillus, Caeso, Volesus, Leonnatus, and a little after also Scipio and Cato, then Augustus, then also Hadrianus and Antoninus. For all things soon pass away and become a mere tale, and complete oblivion soon buries them. And I say this of those who have shone in a wondrous way. For the rest, as soon as they have breathed out their breath they are gone, and no man speaks of them. And, to conclude the matter, what is even an eternal remembrance? A mere nothing. What, then, is that on which we ought to employ our serious pains? This one thing: thoughts just, and acts social, and words that never lie, and a disposition that gladly accepts all that happens, as necessary, as usual, as flowing from a principle and source of the same kind.

**[B4-P034]**

34. Willingly give yourself up to Clotho, one of the fates, allowing her to spin your thread into whatever things she pleases.

**[B4-P035]**

35. Everything is only for a day, both what remembers and what is remembered.

**[B4-P036]**

36. Observe constantly that all things take place by change, and accustom yourself to consider that the nature of the whole loves nothing so much as to change the things that are and to make new things like them. For everything that exists is in a way the seed of what will be. But you are thinking only of seeds that are cast into the earth or into a womb: but this is a very commonplace notion.

**[B4-P037]**

37. You will soon die, and you are not yet simple, nor free from disturbances, nor without suspicion of being hurt by external things, nor kindly disposed toward all; nor do you yet place wisdom only in acting justly.

**[B4-P038]**

38. Examine men's ruling parts, even those of the wise, what kind of things they avoid, and what kind they pursue.

**[B4-P039]**

39. What is evil to you does not exist in the ruling part of another; nor yet in any turning and change of your bodily covering. Where is it, then? It is in that part of you in which the power of forming opinions about evils exists. Let this power, then, not form such opinions, and all is well. And if what is nearest to it, the poor body, is cut, burnt, filled with matter and rottenness, nevertheless let the part that forms opinions about these things be quiet; that is, let it judge that nothing is either bad or good which can happen equally to the bad man and the good. For what happens equally to him who lives against nature and to him who lives according to nature is neither according to nature nor against nature.

**[B4-P040]**

40. Constantly regard the universe as one living being, having one substance and one soul; and observe how all things have reference to one perception, the perception of this one living being; and how all things act with one movement; and how all things are the cooperating causes of all things that exist; observe too the continuous spinning of the thread and the weave of the web.

**[B4-P041]**

41. You are a little soul carrying about a corpse, as Epictetus used to say.

**[B4-P042]**

42. It is no evil for things to undergo change, and no good for things to exist as a result of change.

**[B4-P043]**

43. Time is like a river made up of the events that happen, and a violent stream; for as soon as a thing has been seen, it is carried away, and another comes in its place, and this will be carried away too.

**[B4-P044]**

44. Everything that happens is as familiar and well known as the rose in spring and the fruit in summer; for such is disease, and death, and slander, and treachery, and whatever else delights fools or vexes them.

**[B4-P045]**

45. In the series of things, those that follow are always aptly fitted to those that have gone before: for this series is not like a mere enumeration of disjointed things, which has only a necessary sequence, but it is a rational connection: and as all existing things are arranged together harmoniously, so the things that come into existence show no mere succession, but a certain wonderful relationship.

**[B4-P046]**

46. Always remember the saying of Heraclitus, that the death of earth is to become water, and the death of water is to become air, and the death of air is to become fire, and the reverse. And think too of him who forgets where the way leads, and that men quarrel with that with which they are most constantly in communion, the reason that governs the universe; and the things they meet with daily seem to them strange: and consider that we ought not to act and speak as if we were asleep, for even in sleep we seem to act and speak; and that we ought not, like children who learn from their parents, simply to act and speak as we have been taught.

**[B4-P047]**

47. If any god told you that you will die tomorrow, or certainly on the day after tomorrow, you would not care much whether it was on the third day or tomorrow, unless you were mean-spirited in the highest degree; for how small the difference is! So think it no great thing to die after as many years as you can name rather than tomorrow.

**[B4-P048]**

48. Think continually how many physicians are dead after often contracting their eyebrows over the sick; and how many astrologers after predicting with great pretensions the deaths of others; and how many philosophers after endless discourses on death or immortality; how many heroes after killing thousands; and how many tyrants who have used their power over men's lives with terrible insolence, as if they were immortal; and how many cities are entirely dead, so to speak, Helice and Pompeii and Herculaneum, and others innumerable. Add to the reckoning all whom you have known, one after another. One man, after burying another, has been laid out dead, and another buries him; and all this in a short time. To conclude, always observe how ephemeral and worthless human things are, and what was yesterday a little mucus will tomorrow be a mummy or ashes. Pass, then, through this little space of time according to nature, and end your journey in content, as an olive falls off when it is ripe, blessing nature who produced it, and thanking the tree on which it grew.

**[B4-P049]**

49. Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it. Am I unhappy because this has happened to me? Not so; rather, I am happy, though this has happened to me, because I continue free from pain, neither crushed by the present nor fearing the future. For such a thing as this might have happened to every man; but not every man would have continued free from pain on such an occasion. Why, then, is that rather a misfortune than this a good fortune? And do you in all cases call that a man's misfortune which is not a deviation from man's nature? And does a thing seem to you to be a deviation from man's nature when it is not contrary to the will of man's nature? Well, you know the will of nature. Will, then, this that has happened prevent you from being just, magnanimous, temperate, prudent, secure against rash opinions and falsehood; will it prevent you from having modesty, freedom, and everything else by the presence of which man's nature obtains all that is its own? Remember too, on every occasion that leads you to resentment, to apply this principle: not that this is a misfortune, but that to bear it nobly is good fortune.

**[B4-P050]**

50. It is a commonplace, but still a useful help toward contempt of death, to pass in review those who have clung tenaciously to life. What more, then, have they gained than those who died early? Certainly they lie in their tombs somewhere at last, Cadicianus, Fabius, Julianus, Lepidus, or anyone else like them, who carried out many to be buried, and then were carried out themselves. Altogether the interval between birth and death is small; and consider with how much trouble, and in company with what sort of people, and in what a feeble body, this interval is laboriously passed. Do not then consider life a thing of any value. For look at the immensity of time behind you, and at the time before you, another boundless space. In this infinity, then, what is the difference between him who lives three days and him who lives three generations?

**[B4-P051]**

51. Always run by the short way; and the short way is the natural one: accordingly say and do everything in conformity with the soundest reason. For such a purpose frees a man from trouble, and warfare, and all artifice and ostentatious display.
