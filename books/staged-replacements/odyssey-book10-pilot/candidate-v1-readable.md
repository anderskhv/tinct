# Tinct Clear Edition — Odyssey, Book 10 (candidate v1)

**Title:** Book 10 — Aeolus, the Laestrygonians, Circe

Paragraph IDs are shown outside the prose, in the form `B10-Pxxx`, for review reference only. They are not part of the edition text.

**[B10-P001]**

From there we came to the floating island of Aeolus, son of Hippotas, a man dear to the immortal gods. It floats on the sea, so to speak, ringed by a wall of iron. Aeolus has six daughters and six strapping sons, and he married the sons to the daughters, so they all live together with their dear father and mother, feasting and enjoying every comfort life can offer. All day long the smell of roasting meat fills the house until it fairly groans with it, courtyard and all; but at night they sleep on well-made bedsteads, each with his own wife beside him under the blankets. These were the people we had now come among.

**[B10-P002]**

Aeolus kept me as his guest for a whole month, asking me endless questions about Troy, the Argive fleet, and the voyage home of the Achaeans. I told him exactly how it had all happened, and when I said I needed to leave and asked him to help me on my way, he raised no objection and set to work at once. He flayed a full-grown ox and made a hide bag to hold the paths of the blustering winds, for Zeus had made him keeper of the winds, with power to raise or calm each one as he pleased. He shut the winds inside the hide as if it were a sack, put the sack aboard my ship, and tied its mouth shut with a silver cord so tightly that not even a breath of wind could slip out from any direction. He let only the West Wind blow free, since that one was fair for us—but it all came to nothing, for our own foolishness destroyed us.

**[B10-P003]**

We sailed nine days and nine nights, and on the tenth day our own land came into view. We were close enough to see men tending the stubble fires, and I, worn out, fell into a light sleep—I had never let go of the rudder myself the whole way, wanting to get us home faster. While I slept, my men began talking among themselves, saying that I was bringing home gold and silver in the sack Aeolus had given me. ‘Just look,’ one would say to his neighbor, ‘how this man is honored and welcomed wherever he goes. See what fine prizes he’s carrying home from Troy, while the rest of us, who traveled just as far as he did, come back with empty hands—and now Aeolus has given him still more. Quick, let’s see what’s really in that sack he gave him, how much gold and silver.’

**[B10-P004]**

So they talked, and this bad idea won out. They opened the sack, and the winds burst out howling and raised a storm that swept us weeping out to sea, away from our own country. When I woke, I didn’t know whether to throw myself into the sea or to endure it and live; but I bore it, covered my face, and lay down in the ship, while the men wailed bitterly as the fierce winds carried our whole fleet back to the island of Aeolus.

**[B10-P005]**

When we reached it, we went ashore for fresh water, and the men ate their meal beside the ships. As soon as we had eaten, I took a herald and one of my men and went straight to the house of Aeolus, where I found him feasting with his wife and family; we sat down as suppliants on the threshold. They were astonished to see us. ‘Odysseus,’ they said, ‘what brings you back? What god has been mistreating you? We took great pains to send you safely on your way home to Ithaca, or wherever it was you wanted to go.’

**[B10-P006]**

That is what they said, but I answered sorrowfully, ‘My men have ruined me—they, and cruel sleep, have been my undoing. Set this wrong right for me, my friends; you can if you will.’

**[B10-P007]**

I pleaded as movingly as I knew how, but they said nothing, until at last their father answered, ‘Get out of this island at once, you vilest of men. I will not help a man the gods hate. Be off—you come here as someone loathed by heaven.’ And with these words he sent me from his door in sorrow.

**[B10-P008]**

From there we sailed on in low spirits, until the men were worn out from long, fruitless rowing, since there was no wind left to help them. We labored six days and nights, and on the seventh day we reached the rocky stronghold of Lamus, Telepylus, city of the Laestrygonians, where a shepherd driving his flock home to be milked greets another driving his flock out to pasture, and that man answers the greeting in turn. In that country, someone who never needed sleep could earn double wages, working first as a cattle herder and then as a shepherd, for the work goes on nearly the same by night as by day.

**[B10-P009]**

When we reached the harbor, we found it enclosed by steep cliffs on every side, with a narrow entrance between two headlands. My captains took all the other ships inside and made them fast close together, since there was never so much as a breath of wind in there—it was always dead calm. I kept my own ship outside and moored it to a rock at the very tip of the point; then I climbed a high rock to look around, but I could see no sign of man or cattle, only some smoke rising from the ground. So I sent two of my men, with an attendant under their command, to find out what kind of people lived there.

**[B10-P010]**

Once ashore, the men followed a level road that the people used to bring firewood down from the mountains into the town. Before long they met a young woman who had come out to fetch water—the daughter of a Laestrygonian named Antiphates. She was on her way to the spring Artacia, where the people drew their water, and when my men came up to her they asked who the king of that country was and what people he ruled. She pointed them to her father’s house, but when they got there they found his wife to be a giantess as huge as a mountain, and the sight of her horrified them.

**[B10-P011]**

She at once called her husband Antiphates from the assembly, and he set about killing my men without delay. He snatched up one of them and began making his dinner off him right there, at which the other two ran back to the ships as fast as they could. But Antiphates raised the alarm behind them, and thousands of sturdy Laestrygonians sprang up from every side—ogres, not men. They hurled boulders down at us from the cliffs as though they weighed nothing, and I heard the sickening sound of ships being crushed against one another, and the death cries of my men, as the Laestrygonians speared them like fish and carried them off to eat. While they were killing my men inside the harbor, I drew my sword, cut my own ship’s cable, and ordered my men to row for their lives if they didn’t want to meet the same end; they pulled with all their strength, and we were thankful enough when we broke into open water, out of reach of the rocks they were hurling at us. As for the rest of the fleet, not one man survived.

**[B10-P012]**

From there we sailed on in sorrow, glad to have escaped death but grieving for our lost comrades, and came to the island of Aeaea, where Circe lives—a formidable and cunning goddess, own sister to the sorcerer Aeetes, for both are children of the sun by Perse, daughter of Oceanus. We brought our ship into a safe harbor without a word, guided there by some god, and once we had landed we lay there for two days and two nights, worn out in body and spirit. When the morning of the third day came, I took my spear and my sword and went off from the ship to look around, hoping to find some sign of human work or hear the sound of voices. Climbing to the top of a high lookout point, I spotted the smoke of Circe’s house rising up out of a dense forest, and once I had seen it I wondered whether I should go on at once to learn more, but in the end I judged it best to go back to the ship, let the men eat first, and send some of them ahead instead of going myself.

**[B10-P013]**

As I was nearly back to the ship, some god took pity on my solitude and sent a fine antlered stag straight into my path. He was coming down from his mountain pasture to drink at the river, driven by the heat of the sun, and as he passed I struck him in the middle of the back; the bronze spearpoint went clean through him, and he fell groaning in the dust until the life went out of him. Then I set my foot on him, pulled my spear from the wound, and laid it down; I also gathered rough grass and rushes and twisted them into a stout rope about six feet long, with which I tied the creature’s four feet together. Then I slung him over my neck and walked back to the ship, leaning on my spear, for the stag was far too big for me to carry on one shoulder while steadying him with the other hand. When I threw him down in front of the ship, I called the men together and spoke encouragingly to each of them in turn. ‘Look here, my friends,’ I said, ‘we are not going to die before our time just yet, and at least we won’t starve as long as we have something to eat and drink on board.’ At this they bared their heads on the shore and admired the stag, for he really was a splendid animal. Once they had feasted their eyes on him enough, they washed their hands and began cooking him for dinner.

**[B10-P014]**

So we stayed there the whole day, until the sun went down, eating and drinking our fill, and when it grew dark we camped on the shore. When Dawn’s fingers reddened the sky, I called the men together and said, ‘My friends, we are in real trouble; listen to me. We have no idea where the sun rises or sets, so we don’t even know east from west. I see no way out of it, but we have to find one. We’re certainly on an island, for I climbed as high as I could this morning and saw the sea stretching all around it to the horizon. It lies low, but toward the middle I saw smoke rising out of a thick forest.’

**[B10-P015]**

Their hearts sank when they heard this, for they remembered how they had been treated by the Laestrygonian Antiphates and by the savage Cyclops Polyphemus. They wept bitterly in their distress, but crying would get them nothing, so I split them into two companies and set a captain over each—I gave one company to Eurylochus, and took command of the other myself. Then we cast lots in a helmet, and the lot fell to Eurylochus; so he set out with his twenty-two men, and they wept, as did we who stayed behind.

**[B10-P016]**

When they reached Circe’s house, they found it built of cut stone, on a site visible from far off, in the middle of the forest. Wild mountain wolves and lions prowled all around it—poor bewitched creatures she had tamed with her drugs and enchantments. They did not attack my men, but wagged their great tails, fawned on them, and rubbed their noses against them affectionately. Just as dogs crowd around their master when he comes home from dinner, knowing he will bring them something, so these wolves and lions, for all their great claws, fawned on my men—though the men were badly frightened at the sight of such strange creatures. Soon they reached the gates of the goddess’s house, and as they stood there they could hear Circe inside, singing beautifully as she worked at her loom, weaving a fabric so fine, so soft, and so brilliantly colored that only a goddess could have made it. At this, Polites—whom I valued and trusted more than any other of my men—said, ‘Someone in there is working at a loom and singing beautifully; the whole place rings with it. Let’s call out to her and see whether she’s a woman or a goddess.’

**[B10-P017]**

They called out, and she came down, unbarred the door, and invited them in. Suspecting nothing, they followed her—all except Eurylochus, who suspected a trap and stayed outside. Once she had them in her house, she seated them on benches and chairs and mixed them a dish of cheese, honey, barley meal, and Pramnian wine, but she laced it with wicked drugs to make them forget their homes. As soon as they had drunk it, she struck them with her wand and turned them into pigs, then shut them up in her pigsties. They had the heads, bodies, and bristles of pigs, and grunted just as pigs do, but their minds stayed the same as before, and they remembered everything.

**[B10-P018]**

So they were shut up there squealing, while Circe threw them acorns and beechnuts, the kind pigs eat. Meanwhile Eurylochus hurried back to tell me the grim fate of our comrades. He was so overcome that although he tried to speak, he could find no words; his eyes filled with tears, and he could only sob and sigh, until at last we drew the story out of him, and he told us what had happened to the others.

**[B10-P019]**

‘We went,’ he said, ‘just as you told us, through the forest, and in the middle of it we found a fine house built of cut stone, in a place visible from far off. There we found a woman—or perhaps a goddess—working at her loom and singing sweetly. The men called out and shouted to her, and she came down at once, opened the door, and invited us in. The others suspected no trap and followed her inside, but I stayed where I was, thinking there might be treachery. From that moment I saw no more of them, for not one of them ever came back out, though I sat there a long time watching for them.’

**[B10-P020]**

Then I took my bronze sword and slung it over my shoulder, took up my bow as well, and told Eurylochus to come back with me and show me the way. But he seized me with both hands and pleaded, ‘Sir, don’t force me to go with you—let me stay here. I know you won’t bring back a single one of them, and won’t come back alive yourself either. Let’s see instead whether we can escape with the few of us who are left, for we may yet save our lives.’

**[B10-P021]**

‘Stay here, then,’ I answered, ‘eating and drinking at the ship. But I must go, for I am bound to it and cannot refuse.’

**[B10-P022]**

With that I left the ship and went inland. When I had passed through the enchanted grove and was nearing the great house of the sorceress Circe, I met Hermes, with his golden wand, disguised as a young man in the first bloom of youth and beauty, the down just showing on his face. He came up to me, took my hand, and said, ‘Where are you going now, unlucky man, alone and not knowing your way, over this high ground? Your men are shut up in Circe’s pigsties like so many wild boars in their pens. Do you really think you can set them free? I tell you, you will never make it back yourself, but will have to stay there with the rest of them. But don’t worry, I will protect you and get you out of your trouble. Take this herb—it has great power—and keep it with you when you go to Circe’s house. It will guard you against every kind of harm she can work.

**[B10-P023]**

‘I’ll tell you all the wicked tricks Circe will try on you. She will mix you a dish to drink, and drug it with her potions, but she won’t be able to enchant you, because the herb I give you will keep her spells from working. Let me tell you exactly what will happen. When Circe strikes you with her wand, draw your sword and rush at her as though you meant to kill her. She will be frightened then, and will ask you to go to bed with her. Don’t refuse her outright, since you want her to free your men and to take good care of you as well, but make her swear a solemn oath by all the blessed gods that she will plot no further harm against you—or else, once she has you at her mercy, she will unman you and leave you good for nothing.’

**[B10-P024]**

As he spoke he pulled the herb out of the ground and showed it to me. Its root was black, and its flower as white as milk. The gods call it moly. It is hard for mortal men to dig up, but the gods can do anything.

**[B10-P025]**

Then Hermes went back to high Olympus, passing over the wooded island, while I went on to Circe’s house, my heart heavy with worry as I walked. When I reached the gates I stood and called out to the goddess, and as soon as she heard me she came down, opened the door, and asked me in; so I followed her, deeply troubled. She seated me on a richly decorated chair inlaid with silver, with a footstool under my feet, and mixed me a drink in a golden cup—but she had drugged it, meaning me harm. When she gave it to me and I drank it without being charmed, she struck me with her wand. ‘There,’ she cried, ‘off to the pigsty with you, and take your place among the rest.’

**[B10-P026]**

But I rushed at her with my sword drawn as though I meant to kill her, and she fell back with a loud scream, clasped my knees, and cried out piteously, ‘Who are you, and where are you from? What is your city, who are your people? How is it my drugs have no power over you? No man before you has ever withstood even a taste of that herb—you must be immune to spells. You can be no one but the bold hero Odysseus, whom Hermes always said would come here one day in his ship on his way home from Troy. So be it, then—sheathe your sword, and let us go to bed, so we may become friends and learn to trust each other.’

**[B10-P027]**

And I answered, ‘Circe, how can you expect me to be friendly with you, when you have just turned all my men into pigs? And now that you have me here, you want me to go to bed with you only so you can unman me and leave me fit for nothing. I will certainly not agree to go to bed with you unless you first swear a solemn oath to plot no further harm against me.’

**[B10-P028]**

So she swore at once, just as I told her to, and once she had completed her oath, I went to bed with her.

**[B10-P029]**

Meanwhile her four servants, her housemaids, set about their work. They are the children of the groves and springs, and of the sacred waters that flow down into the sea. One of them spread a fine purple cloth over a chair and laid a carpet under it. Another brought silver tables up beside the chairs and set them with golden baskets. A third mixed sweet wine with water in a silver bowl and set out golden cups on the tables, while the fourth brought in water and set it to boil in a great cauldron over a fire she had lit. When the water in the cauldron was boiling, she poured in cold water until it was just as I liked it, then set me in a bath and began washing me from the cauldron, pouring it over my head and shoulders, to work the weariness and stiffness out of my limbs. Once she had finished washing me and rubbing me with oil, she dressed me in a fine cloak and shirt and led me to a richly decorated chair inlaid with silver, with a footstool under my feet. A servant girl then brought me water in a beautiful golden pitcher and poured it into a silver basin so I could wash my hands, and she drew a clean table up beside me. A senior servant brought me bread and set out many of the good things in the house, and Circe urged me to eat—but I would not, and sat there paying no attention to what was in front of me, still troubled and wary.

**[B10-P030]**

When Circe saw me sitting there without eating, deep in grief, she came over to me and said, ‘Odysseus, why do you sit there like that, silent, gnawing at your own heart, refusing both food and drink? Are you still suspicious of me? You shouldn’t be—I have already sworn a solemn oath that I will not harm you.’

**[B10-P031]**

And I said, ‘Circe, no man with any decency could think of eating or drinking in your house until his friends have been set free and he has seen them with his own eyes. If you want me to eat and drink, you must free my men and bring them to me so I can see them for myself.’

**[B10-P032]**

When I said this, she went straight through the courtyard, wand in hand, and opened the pigsty doors. My men came out looking like fine hogs and stood there watching her, but she went among them and anointed each one with another drug. The bristles the first drug had given them fell away, and they became men again—younger than before, and taller and better looking. They knew me at once, seized me each by the hand, and wept for joy, until the whole house rang with their shouting; and Circe herself was so moved by their happiness that she came up to me and said, ‘Odysseus, noble son of Laertes, go back at once to the sea, to the ship you left there, and first haul it up onto dry land. Then hide all your gear and belongings in a cave, and come back here with your men.’

**[B10-P033]**

I agreed, so I went back to the shore and found the men at the ship, weeping and wailing most piteously. When they saw me, the foolish, tearful fellows crowded round me like calves let loose from their pens, frisking round their mothers when they come home to be milked after a day’s grazing, the whole barnyard ringing with their lowing. They seemed as glad to see me as if they had gotten safely back to their own rugged Ithaca, where they were born and raised. ‘Sir,’ said the affectionate fellows, ‘we are as glad to see you as if we had come safely home to Ithaca. But tell us what happened to our comrades.’

**[B10-P034]**

I spoke to them comfortingly and said, ‘We must haul our ship up onto the land and hide the gear and all our belongings in a cave. Then come with me, all of you, as quickly as you can to Circe’s house, where you will find your comrades eating and drinking amid great plenty.’

**[B10-P035]**

At this the men would have come with me at once, but Eurylochus tried to hold them back and said, ‘Poor wretches that we are, what will become of us? Don’t rush into ruin by going to Circe’s house—she will turn us all into pigs or wolves or lions, and we’ll be forced to stand guard over her house. Remember how the Cyclops treated us when our comrades went into his cave, Odysseus among them. It was his sheer recklessness that cost those men their lives.’

**[B10-P036]**

When I heard him, I was tempted to draw the sharp blade that hung at my sturdy thigh and cut off his head, close relation of mine though he was; but the men spoke up for him and said, ‘Sir, if it must be, let this man stay here to mind the ship, and take the rest of us with you to Circe’s house.’

**[B10-P037]**

So we all set off inland, and Eurylochus was not left behind after all, but came along too, frightened by the sharp rebuke I had given him.

**[B10-P038]**

Meanwhile Circe had been seeing to it that the men left behind were washed and rubbed with olive oil; she had also given them woollen cloaks and shirts, and when we arrived we found them all comfortably at dinner in her house. As soon as the men saw one another face to face and recognized each other, they wept for joy and cried out loud until the whole palace rang with it. Then Circe came up to me and said, ‘Odysseus, noble son of Laertes, tell your men to stop crying. I know how much you have all suffered at sea, and how badly you have fared among the cruel savages on the mainland, but that is over now. Stay here, and eat and drink until you are as strong and hearty as you were when you left Ithaca, for right now you are worn down in body and mind. You keep thinking about the hardships of your travels, so that you have no cheerfulness left in you.’

**[B10-P039]**

So she spoke, and we agreed. We stayed with Circe for a full year, feasting on more meat and wine than we could count. But when the year had passed, with the moons waning one after another and the long days come round again, my men took me aside and said, ‘Sir, it’s time you began thinking about going home, if you are ever to be spared to see your house and native land again.’

**[B10-P040]**

So they spoke, and I agreed. Then, through the whole day until sunset, we feasted our fill on meat and wine, and when the sun went down and darkness fell, the men lay down to sleep in the covered porches. I, however, once I had gone to bed with Circe, appealed to her by her knees, and the goddess listened to what I had to say. ‘Circe,’ I said, ‘please keep the promise you made me, to help me on my way home. I want to get back, and so do my men—they keep pestering me with their complaints the moment your back is turned.’

**[B10-P041]**

And the goddess answered, ‘Odysseus, noble son of Laertes, none of you shall stay here any longer than you wish—but there is another journey you must make before you can sail home. You must go to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the ghost of Teiresias, the blind Theban prophet, whose mind is still unclouded. To him alone has Persephone left his full understanding even in death; the other ghosts only flit about without sense.’

**[B10-P042]**

I was dismayed to hear this. I sat up in bed and wept, and would gladly have given up living to see the light of the sun again; but after a while, when I was worn out with weeping and tossing about, I said, ‘And who will guide me on this voyage? For the house of Hades is a harbor no ship can reach.’

**[B10-P043]**

‘You will need no guide,’ she answered. ‘Raise your mast, set your white sails, sit quite still, and the North Wind will carry you there on its own. Once your ship has crossed the waters of Oceanus, you will reach the fertile shore of Persephone’s country, with its groves of tall poplars and willows that shed their fruit before it ripens. Beach your ship there, on the shore of Oceanus, and go straight on to the dark house of Hades. You will find it near the place where the rivers Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus—a branch of the river Styx—flow into the Acheron, and you will see a rock there, just where the two roaring rivers meet.

**[B10-P044]**

‘When you reach that spot, as I am telling you now, dig a pit about a cubit—roughly eighteen inches—in length, width, and depth, and pour into it a drink-offering for all the dead: first honey mixed with milk, then wine, and third, water, sprinkling white barley meal over the whole. You must also offer many prayers to the poor, powerless ghosts, and promise them that when you get back to Ithaca you will sacrifice the best barren heifer you have to them, and pile the pyre with good things. Above all, you must promise that Teiresias will have a black sheep all to himself, the finest in your flocks.’

**[B10-P045]**

‘Once you have pleaded with the ghosts in this way, offer them a ram and a black ewe, turning their heads toward Erebus, but turn yourself away, toward the river, as if to go there. Then many ghosts of the dead will come to you, and you must tell your men to skin the two sheep you have just killed and burn them as a sacrifice, with prayers to Hades and Persephone. Then draw your sword and sit there, to keep any other poor ghost from coming near the spilled blood before Teiresias has answered your questions. The seer will soon come to you and tell you about your voyage—what stages lie ahead, and how you are to cross the sea to reach your home.’

**[B10-P046]**

By the time she had finished speaking, it was daybreak, so she dressed me in my shirt and cloak. She herself threw a light, gossamer robe over her shoulders, fastened it at the waist with a golden belt, and covered her head with a veil. Then I went through the house, from man to man, speaking kindly to each of them in turn: ‘You must not lie sleeping here any longer,’ I told them. ‘We have to leave, for Circe has told me everything.’ And they did as I told them.

**[B10-P047]**

Even so, we did not get away without mishap. We had with us a young man named Elpenor, not especially clever or brave, who had gotten drunk and was lying on the roof, apart from the rest of the men, sleeping off his liquor in the cool air. When he heard the noise of the men moving about, he jumped up suddenly and forgot all about the stairway, so he fell straight off the roof and broke his neck, and his soul went down to the house of Hades.

**[B10-P048]**

When I had gathered the men together, I said to them, ‘You think we’re about to set out for home, but Circe has told me that instead, we must go to the house of Hades and Persephone, to consult the ghost of the Theban prophet Teiresias.’

**[B10-P049]**

The men were heartbroken to hear it, and threw themselves on the ground, groaning and tearing their hair, but their crying changed nothing. When we reached the shore, weeping and mourning our fate, Circe brought the ram and the ewe and tied them fast beside the ship. She passed through the midst of us without our noticing, for who can see a god come and go, unless the god wishes to be seen?
