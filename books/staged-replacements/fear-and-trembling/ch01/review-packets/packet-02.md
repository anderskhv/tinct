## C01-P003 — CONTEXT ONLY — reviewed in another packet

**Source (Danish, 1843):**

> I vor Tid bliver Enhver ikke staaende ved Troen, men gaaer videre. Et Spørgsmaal om, hvor de komme hen, vilde maaskee være en Dumdristighed, derimod er det vel et Tegn paa Belevenhed og Dannelse, at jeg antager, at Enhver har Troen, da det ellers bliver en besynderlige Tale: at gaae videre. I hine gamle Dage var det anderledes, da var Troen en Opgave for hele Livet, fordi man antog, at den troende Færdighed ikke erhverves hverken i Dage eller Uger. Naar da den prøvede Olding nærmede sig sit Endeligt, havde stridt den gode Strid og bevaret Troen, da var hans Hjerte ungt nok til ikke at have glemt hiin Angst og Bævelse, der tugtede Ynglingen, som Manden vel beherskede, men som intet Menneske ganske voxer fra — uden forsaavidt det skulde lykkes ved saa tidlig som mulig at gaae videre. Hvor da hine ærværdige Skikkelser naaede hen, der begynder i vor Tid Enhver for at gaae videre.

**Candidate (modern English v1):**

> In our age, no one stops at faith, but everyone goes further. To ask where they arrive might perhaps be an impertinence; on the other hand, it is surely a sign of good breeding and culture that I assume everyone has faith, since otherwise it would be a strange thing to say—that he goes further. In those old days it was different: then faith was a task for an entire life, because it was assumed that the skill of believing is not acquired in days or in weeks. When, then, the tested old man drew near his end, having fought the good fight and kept the faith, his heart was still young enough not to have forgotten that fear and trembling which had disciplined his youth, which he as a grown man had mastered, but which no one wholly outgrows—except insofar as he manages to go further as early as possible. Where those venerable figures arrived, there, in our age, everyone begins, in order to go further.

## C01-P004 — ASSIGNED FOR REVIEW

**Source (Danish, 1843):**

> Nærværende Forfatter er ingenlunde Philosoph, han har ikke forstaaet Systemet, om det er til, om det er færdigt, han har allerede nok for sit svage Hoved i den Tanke, hvilket uhyre Hoved Enhver i vor Tid maa have, da Enhver har en saadan uhyre Tanke. Om man end var istand til at omsætte hele Troens Indhold i Begrebets Form, saa følger deraf ikke, at man har begrebet Troen, begrebet, hvorledes man kom ind i den, eller hvorledes "den kom ind i Een. Nærværende Forfatter er ingenlunde Philosoph, han er, poetice et eleganter, en Extra-Skriver, der hverken skriver Systemet eller Løfter om Systemet, der hverken forskriver sig paa Systemet. eller til Systemet. Han skriver, fordi det er ham en Luxus, der vinder i Behagelighed og Evidents, jo Færre der kjøbe og læse, hvad han skriver. Han forudseer let sin Skjæbne i en Tid, da man har slaaet en Streg over Lidenskaben for at tjene Videnskaben, i en Tid, da en Forfatter, der vil have Læsere, maa passe paa at skrive saaledes, at det beqvemt lader sig gjennemblade under Middagssøvnen, og passe paa at danne sin ydre Optræden i Lighed med hiin høflige Gartnersvends i Adresseavisen, der med Hatten i Haanden og gode Recommendationer fra det Sted, hvor han har tjent sidst, anbefaler sig til et høistæret Publikum. Han forudseer sin Skjæbne, at blive aldeles ignoreret, han aner det Forfærdelige, at den nidkjære Kritik vil lade ham flere Gange staae Skoleret; han gruer for det endnu Forfærdeligere, at en eller anden driftig Registrator, en Paragraphsluger, (der, for at frelse Videnskaben, altid er villig til at gjøre ved Andres Skrifter, hvad Trop for ,at redde Smagen" høimodigen gjorde ved »Menneskeslægtens Ødelæggelse — skal skære ham over i $8, og gjøre det med samme Ubøielighed som den Mand, der for at tjene Interpunctions-Videnskaben inddeelte sin Tale ved at tælle Ordene, saa der gik 50 Ord paa et Punktum og 35 paa et Semikolon. — Jeg falder i dybeste Underdanighed ned for enhver systematisk Posekigger: »Det er ikke Systemet, det har ikke det Mindste med Systemet at gjøre. Jeg nedbeder alt Godt over Systemet og over de danske Interessenter i denne Omnibus; thi et Taarn bliver det nok neppe. Jeg ønsker dem alle samtlige og hver især til Lykke og Velsignelse.”

**Candidate (modern English v1):**

> The present author is by no means a philosopher; he has not understood the System—whether it exists, whether it is complete. He already has enough to do for his weak head with the thought of what an enormous head everyone in our age must have, since everyone has such an enormous thought. Even if one were able to translate the entire content of faith into the form of the concept, it would not follow that one had grasped faith—grasped how one came into it, or how it came into one. The present author is by no means a philosopher; he is, poetice et eleganter, a scribe on the side, who neither writes the System nor promises of the System, who neither signs himself over to the System nor commits himself to it. He writes because it is a luxury for him, one that gains in pleasantness and self-evidence the fewer there are who buy and read what he writes. He readily foresees his fate in an age when people have drawn a line through passion in order to serve scholarship, in an age when an author who wants readers must take care to write in such a way that his book can conveniently be leafed through during the after-dinner nap, and take care to shape his outward bearing after that polite gardener’s apprentice in the Adresseavisen who, hat in hand and with good references from the place he last served, recommends himself to a most esteemed public. He foresees his fate: to be utterly ignored; he senses the more dreadful prospect, that zealous criticism will more than once make him stand at the school bench; he shudders at the still more dreadful prospect, that some enterprising registrar, some paragraph-glutton (who, in order to save scholarship, is always ready to do to the writings of others what Trop, “to save taste,” so magnanimously did to The Destruction of the Human Race)—will cut him up into §§, and do it with the same rigidity as that man who, in order to serve the science of punctuation, divided his speech by counting the words, so that fifty words made one full stop and thirty-five a semicolon. — I prostrate myself in the deepest submission before every systematic bag-inspector: “This is not the System; it has not the slightest thing to do with the System. I call down every blessing upon the System and upon the Danish shareholders in this omnibus—for a tower, indeed, it will scarcely become. I wish them all and each and every one good fortune and blessing.”
