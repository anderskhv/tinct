## Paragraph-level decisions

- **C01-P002** — "Nay, answer me. Stand and unfold yourself." → "No — you
  answer me. Stand and identify yourself." "Nay" is a plain "no"; "unfold
  yourself" (reveal who you are) → "identify yourself", the modern
  equivalent with no loss of sense (Francisco is demanding Barnardo prove
  who he is before Francisco answers him).
- **C01-P006** — "You come most carefully upon your hour." → "You've come
  right on the hour, as careful as ever." Kept both halves of Long's sense:
  punctual, and conscientious about it (not merely "on time").
- **C01-P008** — "For this relief much thanks." → "Many thanks for the
  relief." Plain modernization of the inverted word order; nothing added or
  dropped.
- **C01-P027** — "Horatio says 'tis but our fantasy, And will not let belief
  take hold of him Touching this dreaded sight, twice seen of us." → kept
  as one sentence with the same three steps (imagination; refusal to
  believe; the sight seen twice) rather than split or reordered.
- **C01-P028** — "Tush, tush, 'twill not appear." → "Nonsense, it won't
  appear." "Tush" has no modern equivalent word-for-word; "Nonsense"
  carries the same dismissive force. The doubled "Tush, tush" is not
  doubled in the candidate ("Nonsense" once) — recorded here as a place
  where a future review round should check whether the doubled dismissal
  is worth keeping (e.g. "Nonsense, nonsense") for Horatio's characteristic
  brusqueness; left as a single "Nonsense" in v1 because the doubling reads
  as filler rather than an image or argument step, but this is a judgment
  call, not a settled rule.
- **C01-P030** (lowest word ratio, 0.769) — "Well, sit we down, And let us
  hear Barnardo speak of this." → "Well, let's sit down, and hear Barnardo
  tell it." Short exchange, no content lost; "sit we down" and "let us
  hear" both collapse naturally into "let's" constructions in modern
  English, which is shorter by nature, not by omission.
- **C01-P031** — "Last night of all, When yond same star that's westward
  from the pole, Had made his course t'illume that part of heaven Where now
  it burns, Marcellus and myself, The bell then beating one—" → kept as one
  broken-off sentence (Barnardo is interrupted by Marcellus in the next
  paragraph, hence the em dash both in source and candidate). "Yond same
  star" → "that same star"; "t'illume" (to illuminate) → "to light up".
- **C01-P039 (index 38)** — "What art thou that usurp'st this time of
  night" → "What are you, that take on this hour of night". "Usurp'st"
  (seize without right) is a strong word; "take on" was chosen over
  "seize" to keep a natural spoken rhythm for a direct challenge, but
  "seize" is arguably closer to the source's sense of an illegitimate claim
  on the hour. Flagged for review as a candidate for "seize" instead of
  "take on".
- **C01-P052 (index 51)** — the long "shipwrights…Sunday…joint-labourer"
  passage: "impress of shipwrights" → "pressing of shipwrights" (impress =
  conscript into forced labor/service); "joint-labourer with the day" →
  "a fellow laborer with the day", keeping the personification of night
  working alongside day.
- **C01-P053 (index 52)** — Horatio's long history speech (Fortinbras/
  Hamlet combat). Kept every step: the elder Hamlet's combat with
  Fortinbras of Norway; the sealed, law-and-heraldry-ratified compact; the
  forfeiture of lands with life; the matching stake by the Danish king;
  what would have passed to Fortinbras's heirs; young Fortinbras's
  untested, hot nature; his mustering of lawless men along the Norwegian
  border for pay; the goal (recovering the lost lands by force); and the
  conclusion (this is the reason for the watch and the kingdom's
  preparations). "Shark'd up a list of lawless resolutes, For food and
  diet" → "gathered … a band of lawless men, hired for their keep" (food
  and diet = their keep, i.e. sustenance in exchange for service).
- **C01-P055 (index 54)** — the Rome/Julius Caesar comparison. "The graves
  stood tenantless" → "the graves stood empty"; "the sheeted dead" → "the
  shrouded dead" (a sheet used as a burial shroud, not bedding); "the moist
  star, Upon whose influence Neptune's empire stands" → "the moon, on whose
  influence the sea depends" (glossary: Neptune's empire → the sea; "moist
  star" = the moon, made explicit since "moist star" alone would be opaque
  to a modern reader without the accompanying explanation the source
  itself gives via "eclipse").
- **C01-P056 (index 55, "[Re-enter Ghost]")** and **C01-P057 (index 56,
  Horatio's address to the Ghost)** — "I'll cross it, though it blast me."
  → "I'll cross its path, though it blast me for it." Kept "blast" (to
  wither/strike with a curse), not modernized to "harm", since it is a
  specific period term for a specific kind of supernatural injury and
  remains intelligible.
- **C01-P067 (index 66)** — "Th'extravagant and erring spirit hies To his
  confine." → "the wandering, straying spirit hurries back to its own
  confines." "Extravagant" here means "wandering outside its proper
  bounds" (Latin extra + vagari), not the modern "excessive/lavish"; kept
  as "wandering" plus "straying" (a doublet covering both parts of the
  original word) rather than the false-friend modern sense.
- **C01-P068 (index 67)** — Marcellus's Christmas-season speech ("'gainst
  that season comes / Wherein our Saviour's birth is celebrated"). Kept
  intact: the folk belief that no spirit walks, no planet "strikes" (causes
  illness/misfortune by malign astrological influence), no fairy "takes"
  (afflicts by casting a spell), no witch can charm, during the season
  around Christmas.
- **C01-P069 (index 68)** — "the morn in russet mantle clad, Walks o'er the
  dew of yon high eastward hill" → "the morning, dressed in reddish-brown,
  walks over the dew on that high hill to the east" (glossary: russet
  mantle → dressed in reddish-brown). Personification of the morning kept
  ("walks").

## Nothing imported from other translations/modernizations

No wording was taken from No Fear Shakespeare or any other modern-English
version of Hamlet. Several lines in this scene are widely quoted ("Not a
mouse stirring", "'Tis bitter cold, / And I am sick at heart", "Something
is rotten" — not in this scene but in Scene 4) — each candidate line was
built directly from the served source text, not from memory of a familiar
modern version.
