# Accessibility Review — The Bacchae (`bacchae`), modern-en candidate

**Reviewer:** Claude Sonnet 5 (`claude-sonnet-5`), fresh blind pass — this
review was written before any comparison to `source.json`, per
`books/prompts/accessibility-review-prompt.md`.

**Coverage:** All 11 chapters, all 336 paragraphs, read in full, start to
finish, in document order (Prologue → Parodos → First Episode → First
Stasimon → Second Episode → Second Stasimon → Third Episode → Third
Stasimon → Fourth Episode → Fourth Stasimon → Exodos). No sampling. Verified
paragraph count by script: 3+9+26+4+53+19+69+4+30+8+111 = 336, matching the
candidate file exactly.

## Overall verdict: substantially accessible

The candidate is genuinely modern, idiomatic English — no archaic pronouns
(thee/thou/thy/doth/hast), no inverted "Ha! with women worshipping?"-style
period syntax left unconverted, and speech markers (DIONYSUS., PENTHEUS.,
CHORUS., LEADER., stage directions in brackets) are consistent and easy to
follow even with a large cast moving in and out of a bracket-heavy verse
source. A reader unfamiliar with Greek tragedy convention could follow the
plot, the turns of dialogue, and the choral odes without a glossary.

## Passages that read unusually well

- The stichomythia (rapid one-line exchanges) between Dionysus and Pentheus
  in the Second Episode (ch. 5) and the dressing scene in the Fourth
  Episode (ch. 9) read naturally, almost like a modern stage script — short
  clean lines, no padding.
- The Exodos messenger speech (ch. 11) sustains a difficult, very long
  single paragraph without losing the reader; sentence breaks land at
  natural narrative beats.

## Specific passages a general reader may stumble on

1. **Ch. 1, para 1 (Dionysus's opening monologue).** One extremely long
   sentence-chain: "Far behind me lie the golden lands of Lydia and
   Phrygia; far away the wide hot plains where Persian sunbeams play, the
   war-strongholds of Bactria, the storm-beaten clime of the Mede, Araby
   the Blest, and all of Asia that lies along the salt sea in proud
   embattled cities, woven motley-wise of Hellene and Barbarian." This is a
   geographic catalogue with several unfamiliar place names (Bactria, the
   Mede, Araby) strung together with no verb repetition to anchor a
   reader's parsing. A reader unfamiliar with ancient geography will likely
   re-read this once to find the sentence's structure (it's a long list of
   locations Dionysus is leaving behind). Not a translation defect — it is
   inherent to the catalogue form of the original speech — but worth
   flagging as genuinely hard going for a first-time reader.
2. **Ch. 3, para 18 (Tiresias's theological speech).** This is the
   chapter's single hardest passage: a sustained philosophical argument
   (Demeter/Earth vs. the wine-god as the "second" power, the nature of
   prophecy, the god's place in Ares' domain) delivered in one unbroken
   paragraph of many clauses. Example: "Prophecy clings to all frenzy, but
   above all to the frenzy of prayer. Then the God himself dwells in us,
   truly, and speaks the thing to come." The logical connectors ("Then...")
   are doing real work a reader can lose track of across such a long
   paragraph. A general reader will likely need to re-read this passage to
   hold the argument's shape, though no individual sentence is
   ungrammatical or archaic.
3. **Ch. 7, para 12 (the long cattle-and-Maenads messenger speech).** Very
   long, richly detailed, with many named minor figures introduced quickly
   ("Autonoe led one, Ino another, the third your own mother, Agave").
   Because three names arrive in one clause with no further description
   until later, a reader may have to look back to keep track of which
   sister is which through the rest of the speech — this is a structural
   density issue rather than a wording one.
4. **Ch. 9, para 2 ("And is this a Wild Bull I see, walking and waiting
   before me? There are horns upon your brow!").** Pentheus's madness
   here — seeing double, seeing horns on Dionysus — could momentarily read
   as a factual inconsistency (has Dionysus actually grown horns?) rather
   than a symptom of Pentheus's hallucination, if a reader isn't primed for
   the god's manipulation established a few paragraphs earlier. The text
   itself makes clear (via Dionysus's reply, "He hath unsealed your eyes to
   see what you should see") that this is subjective, but a reader moving
   fast could momentarily misread it as literal.
5. **Ch. 11, para 10 (the dismemberment/recognition messenger speech).**
   Structurally the hardest passage in the play: one very long paragraph
   compressing scene-setting, a supernatural stage effect (the bent pine
   tree), Pentheus's plea, and the killing itself. No single sentence is
   hard, but the sheer paragraph length and shift in tone partway through
   (from marvel to horror) could cause a reader to need to slow down. This
   is inherent to the source's own pacing (one uninterrupted messenger
   speech is the classical convention) and not something a paragraph split
   could fix without violating the locked paragraph count.

## Flow notes (not accessibility defects, but worth surfacing)

- The choral odes (Parodos, First/Second/Third/Fourth Stasimon) are dense
  with imagery and proper nouns (Cyprus, Paphos, the Nile, Olympus,
  Pieria, Axios, Lydias) delivered in rapid succession with minimal
  connective tissue between images. This reads as intentionally
  incantatory rather than confusing, and a reader will likely experience
  it as "lyrical" rather than "hard," but it is the least linear prose in
  the book.
- A few stage directions are folded directly into a speaking character's
  paragraph rather than set off (e.g., ch. 5 para 3: "DIONYSUS. There is no
  glory in it, nor any disgrace..." immediately preceded mid-paragraph by
  bracketed direction text). This is a formatting/convention matter
  inherited from the source's verse-drama layout, not a wording problem,
  but a reader skimming quickly could momentarily misattribute a stage
  direction to dialogue.

## Summary

The chapter reads as clear, natural modern English throughout. The
passages flagged above are hard chiefly because of the source's own
density (long catalogues, one uninterrupted messenger speech, several
names introduced at once) rather than because of leftover archaism or
poor modernization — none of the flagged passages contain unconverted
period vocabulary or syntax. No changes are required on accessibility
grounds; the items above are noted for completeness, not as blocking
defects.
