The Adventures of Pinocchio begins with a joke on the reader: once upon a time there was not a king but a piece of firewood. The wood can talk, and the puppet carved from it can run, lie, boast and argue before his maker has finished him. From then on Collodi keeps him moving through a Tuscany of country roads, inns, courtrooms, fishing villages and fairs, where almost everyone he meets is hungry or wants his money.

Pinocchio is never wicked. He is impatient, credulous and easily distracted, and he believes whatever he is told by anyone who flatters him. Every chapter offers him a choice between what he has promised and what looks more fun, and the book is honest about how often fun wins and how much it costs. The punishments are real and sometimes cruel, and the world that hands them out is not fair either: in one town the judge sends the victim of a robbery to prison.

What pulls him back are the few who love him without conditions. Geppetto goes hungry so the puppet can eat and sells his coat for a schoolbook. The Fairy with the blue hair forgives him again and again, and a talking cricket tells him the truth he does not want to hear.

Collodi wrote it in instalments for a weekly magazine in Rome from 1881, stopped it after fifteen chapters with the puppet hanging from an oak, and was persuaded to continue. The finished book appeared in Florence in 1883. Read it for its speed and its comedy, and watch how the puppet slowly learns to put someone else first.
