# Accessibility Review 1 — The Merchant of Venice (`merchant-of-venice`, modern-en)

**Reviewer:** Claude Sonnet 5, blind pass (candidate.json only, no source.json consulted).
**Coverage statement:** Read all 20 chapters (Act/Scene units) of `candidate.json`
in full, start to finish, paragraph by paragraph — all 779 paragraphs read
individually, none skimmed or sampled.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** This is a genuine modern-English rendering, not a
mechanical cleanup. Archaic syntax, inversions, and verb forms are consistently
rebuilt into ordinary contemporary sentence structure across all twenty scenes
("In sooth I know not why I am so sad" → "Honestly, I don't know why I'm so
sad"; "By my troth" → "Honestly"; Shylock's dense usury/Jacob argument in Act 1
Scene 3 reads as clear connected prose rather than chopped verse-lines).
Register, imagery, wordplay (Gratiano's and Launcelet's punning, the ring-plot
double entendres in Act 5) and rhetorical patterning (Shylock's "let him look
to his bond" refrain, Portia's "the quality of mercy" argument) all read
naturally as spoken English.

## Specific wording flagged for difficulty

1. **Ch3 ¶30 (Shylock's Jacob-and-Laban speech)** — a long, information-dense
   single paragraph explaining a Genesis story (streaked lambs, peeled wands,
   breeding ewes) to justify interest-taking. The candidate's sentence
   structure is clear, ordinary English throughout; the difficulty is the
   density of the biblical allusion itself, not the modernization. A reader
   unfamiliar with the Jacob/Laban story may need to slow down, but nothing in
   the wording is archaic or unclear.
2. **Ch13 ¶21 ("Hath not a Jew eyes?")** — reads powerfully and very clearly in
   modern English; flagging only because its emotional and rhetorical
   escalation (list → rhetorical questions → "the villainy you teach me I will
   execute") is dense enough that a reader could skim past the turn from
   self-defense to threat. This is the speech's own structure, not a
   modernization defect.
3. **Ch14 ¶13 (Bassanio's "outward shows" speech before choosing the casket)**
   — long single-paragraph argument (law, religion, cowardice, beauty, wigs)
   building toward the choice of lead. Each individual sentence is
   modernized and clear; a reader may need to track several stacked examples
   before the argument lands. Inherent to the speech's rhetorical build, not
   a wording problem.
4. **Ch10 ¶4 and Ch12 ¶6 (the Prince of Morocco's and the Prince of Aragon's
   casket-inscription reasoning)** — both are long single-paragraph internal
   monologues working through logical/rhetorical steps (Hyrcanian deserts,
   the angel-coin analogy; the "many men desire" vs. "as much as he
   deserves" distinction). Clear modern prose, but the density of the
   reasoning itself asks more of the reader than any individual sentence
   does.
5. **Ch20 ¶33 (Lorenzo's "music" speech — "the man that hath no music in
   himself")** — a sustained classical-allusion passage (Orpheus, "treasons,
   stratagems, and spoils," Erebus) rendered in clean modern syntax; flagging
   only because the string of allusions is dense for a reader unfamiliar
   with them, not because the language itself is unclear.

None of the above are vocabulary or archaic-construction defects — no sentence
in the candidate left an unresolved archaic verb form, an unglossed obsolete
word, or a mechanically chopped verse-line rendered as prose without
rebuilding its syntax. The five notes above are places where the *content*
(allusion density, argument structure, rhetorical build) asks more of a reader
than the wording does, which is a property of the play, not a modernization
failure.

## What reads unusually well

- The trial scene (Ch18, 152 paragraphs) — the longest and highest-stakes
  scene in the play — carries its legal argument, escalating tension, and the
  "quality of mercy" / "pound of flesh" reversal cleanly through modern
  syntax without losing the courtroom's formal register or its emotional
  turns.
- Launcelet Gobbo's comic scenes (Ch5, Ch17) preserve the malapropisms,
  wordplay, and deliberately garbled logic ("ergo," "frutify," the
  Scylla/Charybdis joke) as functioning jokes in modern English rather than
  smoothing them into plain sense.
- The Act 5 ring-plot dialogue (Ch20) keeps its comic double meaning intact
  while reading as natural conversational English.
- Shylock's dialogue throughout (Ch3, Ch6, Ch8, Ch13, Ch15, Ch18) preserves
  his register — formal, precise, repetitive-for-effect ("I'll have my bond")
  — without either flattening it into generic modern speech or leaving
  archaic scaffolding in place.

## Frank/period content (accessibility note, not a fidelity finding)

The play's antisemitic dialogue directed at Shylock (period slurs, "the
villain Jew," "currish Jew," "infidel," the other characters' casual cruelty)
and Shylock's own responses (including "Hath not a Jew eyes?") read as
clearly rendered, unsoftened period content — a blind reader encounters the
same frank hostility and the same force of Shylock's rebuttal that the
original carries. This reviewer flags it here only as context for the
fidelity pass, which checks it directly against source.

## Conclusion

No accessibility-blocking defects. The candidate is ready for the fidelity
review (step B).
