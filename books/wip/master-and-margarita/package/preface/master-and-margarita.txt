The Master and Margarita sends the devil to Moscow and lets him look around. What he finds is not grand wickedness but ordinary weakness: people who want a bigger apartment, a better dinner, a free dress or a safe job, and who will lie, inform or look away to get it. The comedy is quick and often wild, and its targets are entirely recognisable.

The visitors are not tempters in the usual sense. They mostly take people at their word, offering exactly what has been asked for and watching what happens next. An audience that wants money is given money; officials who want explanations are given them. The results expose more than any sermon could.

Alongside this runs a different book, written in a slow and heavy prose: the story of a Roman governor who must decide the fate of a gentle prisoner and who knows what the right decision is. His failure, and his long effort to live with it, make a quiet center for the noisy Moscow chapters. A writer's novel about that governor, and what it costs him to write it, joins the two worlds.

The love story that gives the book its title is fierce rather than sentimental. Margarita is willing to do something frightening for the sake of a man the world has written off, and the novel takes her courage seriously without making it simple. Loyalty, mercy and forgiveness are tested as hard as faith or reason.

Bulgakov wrote this book with little hope of seeing it published. Perhaps that is why it is so free. It mixes satire, fantasy, history and tenderness without apology, and it trusts that a story told truthfully may outlast the people who tried to silence it.
