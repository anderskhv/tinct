# Accessibility Review — Leviathan Ch. 17 pilot, Candidates X vs Y (final)

**Reviewer coverage:** Read both `candidate-X.json` and `candidate-Y.json` in
full, all 16 paragraphs (indices 0–15) of each, start to finish, then again
paragraph by paragraph. Did not read `source.json` or any other file in the
directory. Judged each purely as English prose for a general adult reader.

**Context given:** one chapter (Ch. 17, "Of the Causes, Generation, and
Definition of a Common-wealth") of a modern-English reading edition of a
17th-century political-philosophy treatise, general adult reader, not a
scholar. Both texts are second-round revisions that already went through a
pass for dense sentences, an ambiguous pronoun, unglossed idioms, and archaic
quoted formulas.

---

## Candidate X

### Stumble points

- **Para 1** — "The laws of nature ... go against our natural passions if
  nothing enforces them. Left to themselves, without some power to terrify us
  into observing them, they are contrary to the passions that drive us toward
  partiality, pride, revenge, and the like." The second sentence restates the
  first almost point-for-point (go against passions / contrary to passions)
  before adding anything new — reads as stitched-together rather than
  building. Mild, but a reader will feel the déjà vu.
- **Para 1** — "Even granting that, if no power has been set up..." — "that"
  has no single clear antecedent; the reader has to reconstruct what's being
  "granted" from two sentences back. A small hitch, not a big one.
- **Para 5** — "...which is why Aristotle counted them among the political
  creatures — and yet they have no guide..." "Political creatures" is left
  completely unglossed. A general reader today reads "political" and thinks
  of elections/government, not Aristotle's technical sense of "naturally
  social/organized." This is exactly the class of unglossed-idiom problem the
  prior revision round was supposed to catch, and it's still here.
- **Para 12** — "...who can reduce all their individual wills, by a
  plurality of voices, to a single will." "Plurality of voices" is left
  unglossed. A modern reader will not automatically know this means
  "majority vote." Same category of miss as above — this is the clause the
  brief specifically calls out as a known issue from the previous round.
- **Para 12** — "That is as much as to say: to appoint one man, or one
  assembly of men, to bear their person — that is, to act and speak as if he
  were the multitude itself — with every one of them owning and
  acknowledging himself the author of whatever the one who bears their
  person does, or causes to be done, in matters concerning the common peace
  and safety — and in that, to submit their wills, each to his will, and
  their judgments, to his judgment." The sentence sets up a list of parallel
  infinitives ("to appoint...") but then swaps to a gerund ("with every one
  of them owning...") and only returns to the infinitive pattern awkwardly
  at "and in that, to submit..." The grammatical backbone gets lost across
  the long "with ..." clause; a careful reader has to re-read to see how the
  final clause attaches. This is the single hardest sentence in X.
- **Para 12** — "...is called a COMMONWEALTH — in Latin, CIVITAS." Unlike the
  companion phrase two sentences later ("LEVIATHAN... mortal god"), CIVITAS
  is given with no gloss of what it means, only that it's the Latin term.
  Minor, but a reader unfamiliar with Latin is left with an untranslated
  word.
- **Para 13** — The formal definition quote ("One person, of whose acts a
  great multitude... have made themselves, every one, the author...") keeps
  Hobbes's original inverted, heavily embedded syntax nearly intact. This is
  a genuinely hard sentence to parse on first read — subject and main verb
  are separated by a long relative clause. (Y has the same problem in the
  same spot — see below — so this isn't a point of difference, but it is a
  real remaining stumble in X.)

### What reads well

- Paragraphs 0, 2, 3, and 4 are handled cleanly — Hobbes's often-tangled
  sentences are broken into two or three plain sentences each, with logical
  connectors ("So," "But," "Being at odds...") doing real work. Para 4 in
  particular ("But people want security that lasts their whole lives...
  Being governed and directed by one judgment only for that short a time is
  not enough.") is a good example of splitting a cleft sentence into two
  direct ones.
- Para 10's aside — "an injury — a wrong done to them —" — is a good, light
  in-line gloss that doesn't interrupt the sentence's momentum.
- The numbered "First... Second... Third..." answers (paras 6–11) read
  briskly and keep parallel structure without feeling mechanical.
- The closing definitional formula ("I authorize and give up my right of
  governing myself...") is rendered in clean, plain modern syntax — no
  archaism left in the actual quoted oath, which is the one place a reader
  most needs it to land clearly.

### Verdict: **needs targeted fixes**

The prose architecture is mostly sound — sentences are generally split and
sequenced well — but three concrete unglossed terms ("political creatures,"
"plurality of voices," "CIVITAS") and one genuinely tangled long sentence
(para 12, the "bear their person" sentence) remain. These are fixable in a
short follow-up pass, not a full rewrite.

## Candidate Y

### Stumble points

- **Para 0** — "The final cause, end, or design behind this restraint is
  that they foresee their own preservation..." "Final cause" is a specific
  Aristotelian/Scholastic technical term (as opposed to efficient or formal
  cause) and is not explained anywhere. A general reader will either skip
  over it without understanding why three synonyms are stacked, or stumble
  wondering what "final" is doing there. This is the clearest unglossed-term
  miss in Y, and it's in the opening sentence, where it does the most damage
  to a first read.
- **Para 1** — "For the laws of nature—justice, equity, modesty, mercy, and,
  in sum, doing to others as we would be done to—are in themselves, without
  the terror of some power to make them be observed, contrary to our natural
  passions..." The subject ("the laws of nature") and its verb ("are...
  contrary to") are separated by two stacked em-dash interruptions — first a
  five-item list, then a nine-word qualifying clause — before the sentence
  ever resolves. This is exactly the "sentence that's grammatically legal
  but overloaded" pattern the brief warns about; a reader has to hold the
  subject in memory across both interruptions.
- **Para 1** — "...by open force and by covert arts, for want of any other
  safeguard..." "Covert arts" (meaning "secret methods") is an unusual,
  slightly archaic collocation — "arts" in this sense (skills/techniques,
  not fine art) isn't how the word is used in ordinary modern English, so
  it reads as odd rather than clear.
- **Para 2** — "Nor is it the joining together of a small number of men that
  gives them this security." A cleft ("it is X that...") construction that's
  more formal/indirect than needed; not hard exactly, but less direct than
  a plain subject-verb sentence would be.
- **Para 3** — "And let the multitude be as great as you like: if their
  actions are directed by their own individual judgments..." The
  subjunctive "let X be Y" opening is a slightly archaic rhetorical move that
  a modern reader has to pause on to recognize as "no matter how great."
- **Para 4** — "Nor is it enough, for the security men want to last their
  whole lives, that they be governed and directed by a single judgment for a
  limited time, as in one battle or one war." This is a cleft sentence with
  a parenthetical inserted between "enough" and the "that" clause it
  belongs with ("Nor is it enough ... that they be governed..."). A reader
  has to hold "Nor is it enough" in mind across the inserted "for the
  security..." phrase before the sentence resolves. This is the single
  hardest sentence to parse in Y, and it sits in exactly the spot where X
  instead splits into two short, plain sentences.
- **Para 4** — "...when the man one party counts an enemy another party
  counts a friend..." A doubly embedded relative clause with both relative
  pronouns dropped ("the man [that] one party counts [as] an enemy [is the
  man] another party counts [as] a friend") — this requires a genuine
  re-read to parse correctly on a first pass. Combined with the cleft
  sentence just before it, para 4 is the weakest paragraph in Y for a
  general reader.
- **Para 10** — "...he loves to display his wisdom and to check the actions
  of those who govern the commonwealth." "Check" here means
  "restrain/criticize/rein in," but the dominant modern sense of "check" is
  "verify/look at." A reader is likely to misread this as "review the
  actions" rather than "challenge/oppose the actions," which actually
  inverts the intended meaning slightly.
- **Para 12** — "...he has the use of so much power and strength conferred
  on him that, by the terror of it, he is able to shape the wills of them
  all..." The result clause ("so much power... that he is able to...") is
  interrupted by "conferred on him," and "has the use of... power...
  conferred on him" is a roundabout way of saying "holds power." Not hard,
  but noticeably more convoluted than it needs to be.

### What reads well

- Para 5 glosses "political creatures" explicitly and well — "that is,
  creatures naturally suited to live in organized communities" — solving
  exactly the problem X leaves unaddressed in the same spot.
- Paras 6 and 8 are split into two clean sentences each ("Among men this
  competition breeds envy and hatred, and in the end war; among these
  creatures it does not.") — crisper than X's single run-on versions of the
  same content.
- Para 12's central "confer all their power" sentence is the strongest
  moment in either candidate: it keeps three parallel infinitive clauses
  genuinely parallel ("to appoint... and for every one... and in this to
  submit...") using semicolons to hold the structure together, where X's
  equivalent sentence loses its grammatical thread. It also glosses
  "plurality of voices" in-line ("that is, by whichever choice wins the most
  support") — directly fixing the unglossed-idiom problem the brief flags,
  and doing it better than X does anywhere.
- "COMMONWEALTH, or in Latin, CIVITAS — the Latin word for 'state'" gives
  the reader the actual translation, not just the Latin term left dangling.
- The quoted oath ("I authorize and give up my right...") is rendered as
  cleanly as in X — plain, modern, no residual archaism in the one place a
  reader most needs clarity.

### Verdict: **needs targeted fixes**

Y does noticeably better than X on glossing technical/Latin terms
(political creatures, plurality of voices, CIVITAS) — precisely the
"unglossed idioms" category the prior round was meant to fix — and its big
"confer all power" sentence is the most successfully modernized sentence in
either candidate. But it has two genuinely tangled sentences concentrated in
paragraphs 1 and 4 (cleft constructions with inserted clauses, plus one
doubly-embedded relative clause with dropped pronouns) that will cost a
general reader an actual re-read, which is a more disruptive kind of stumble
than an unglossed term a reader can skim past.

---

## Comparison and pick

Both candidates land at the same overall tier — **needs targeted fixes**,
not a broader pass. The base prose in both is generally short-to-medium
sentence, plain-verb, and clearly organized; the numbered "First... Second..."
section (paras 6–11) and the closing sovereignty paragraphs (13–15) are
essentially equally accessible in both. The difference is concentrated in a
handful of specific spots:

- **X's weak points** are mostly *missing glosses* on three terms
  ("political creatures," "plurality of voices," "CIVITAS") plus one
  structurally confused long sentence (para 12, the "bear their person"
  sentence).
- **Y's weak points** are mostly *tangled sentence construction* — a
  subject/verb pulled apart by nested interruptions (para 1), a cleft
  sentence with an inserted clause plus a doubly-embedded relative clause
  with dropped pronouns (para 4), and one ambiguous archaic verb sense
  ("check the actions," para 10) that can flip the intended meaning on a
  fast read. Y does out-glos X on the specific unglossed-idiom terms the
  brief calls out as a known prior-round issue.

Weighing the two: an unglossed technical term (X's failure mode) is a soft
stumble — a reader can usually infer meaning from context or read past it
without losing the sentence. A syntactically pulled-apart sentence or a
misleading archaic verb sense (Y's failure mode) is a harder stumble — it
forces an actual re-read or risks the reader coming away with the wrong
meaning. Y's problems are also concentrated back-to-back in one paragraph
(para 4), which compounds the effect for a reader moving through that
stretch.

**Pick: Candidate X reads slightly more accessibly overall**, on the
strength of its consistently plainer sentence architecture throughout the
chapter, even though it leaves three specific terms unglossed that a quick
targeted fix could close. Y has the better individual sentence in paragraph
12 and better term-glossing in general, but its two tangled sentences in
paragraphs 1 and 4 are more likely to actually stop a general reader mid-page
than any of X's gaps.
