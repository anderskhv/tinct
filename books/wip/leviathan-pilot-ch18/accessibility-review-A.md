# Accessibility Review — Reviewer A (blind, source not consulted)

**Book/chapter context given to reviewer:** one chapter of a modern-English reading
edition of a 17th-century political philosophy treatise; general adult reader,
not a scholar.

## Coverage

I read the full candidate file `candidate-A.json`, all 16 paragraphs (indices
0–15), in full, start to finish, then went back through paragraph by
paragraph for close analysis. I did not read any source text, notes, or other
candidate files.

## Per-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 1** (the long paragraph beginning "The laws of nature — justice,
equity, modesty, mercy...")
- "even granting the laws of nature — which a person keeps only when he is
  willing to keep them and can do so safely — if no power has been set up, or
  none great enough for our security, then every man will, and lawfully may,
  rely on his own strength and cunning as his guard against everyone else."
  This is a single sentence built from three stacked conditionals ("even
  granting X... if Y... then Z") with an embedded dash-clause interrupting
  the first clause. It's grammatically legal but overloaded — a reader is
  likely to lose the thread of "even granting the laws of nature" by the time
  "then every man will..." arrives, and have to re-read from the top.
- "so far from being considered contrary to the law of nature that the
  greater the plunder they won, the greater their honor." The "so far from
  X... that Y" correlative construction is a slightly archaic pattern not
  common in everyday contemporary prose; a reader may need a beat to parse
  it, though it's short enough to likely land on a single read.

**Paragraph 4** (bees and ants intro — "Nor is it enough, for the security
that people want to last their whole lives, that they be governed...")
- "Nor is it enough, for the security that people want to last their whole
  lives, that they be governed and directed by one judgment only for a
  limited time, as in a single battle or war." The core claim is "Nor is it
  enough that they be governed... only for a limited time," but a long
  parenthetical ("for the security that people want to last their whole
  lives") is wedged between "enough" and the "that..." clause it modifies.
  This is the kind of syntax that reads as grammatically fine on a slow
  parse but causes a stumble/re-read on a normal read-through.
- "afterward — once they either have no common enemy, or the one party
  regards as an enemy someone another party regards as a friend — the
  difference in their interests will inevitably pull them apart." The
  embedded dash-clause is itself a double-branched conditional ("either...
  or...") with a convoluted second branch ("the one party regards as an
  enemy someone another party regards as a friend" — object and subject are
  both unnamed parties, distinguished only by "the one"/"another"). A reader
  will likely need to re-read this clause to work out who is regarding whom
  as what.

**Paragraph 3** (multitude paragraph — "However great the multitude may
be...")
- "This leaves them easily subdued, not only by a small number who do agree
  among themselves, but also, when there is no common enemy, prone to making
  war on one another over their particular interests." The "not only... but
  also" pairing is set up to parallel two things happening to "them"
  (subdued / prone to making war), but the mid-sentence insertion "when
  there is no common enemy" sits awkwardly between "but also" and "prone,"
  breaking the parallel rhythm and making the sentence feel stitched
  together rather than flowing.
- "in which case there would be neither the need for, nor any such thing as,
  civil government or commonwealth at all, since there would be peace
  without anyone being subject to anyone else." The "neither X, nor Y" here
  has an interrupting comma-clause ("nor any such thing as,") before its
  object arrives ("civil government or commonwealth"), which is a
  double-negative construction general readers commonly misparse on first
  pass.

**Paragraph 10** (the "Fifth" item on irrational creatures)
- "that irrational creatures cannot distinguish between injury and mere
  damage" — "injury" is being used in its older, technical sense (a wrong
  done to someone) as distinct from "damage" (mere harm), a distinction
  central to the philosophical point but not how "injury" is used in everyday
  contemporary English (where injury usually just means physical harm). A
  general reader is likely to read "injury" and "damage" as near-synonyms
  and miss the intended contrast entirely, rather than stumbling on the
  sentence's grammar.

**Paragraph 12** (the central "common power" paragraph — by far the
densest in the chapter)
- "The only way to set up such a common power — one able to defend people
  from foreign invasion and from injuries done by one another, and so to
  secure them in such a way that, through their own industry and the fruits
  of the earth, they can feed themselves and live contentedly — is this: to
  confer all their power and strength upon one man..." The subject ("The
  only way... is this:") and its completion are separated by a very long
  dash-enclosed qualifier containing its own nested "in such a way that"
  clause. By the time the reader reaches "is this:" they have to mentally
  reload what the sentence was originally about. This is the single most
  overloaded sentence in the chapter.
- "to bear their person" / "the one who bears their person does, or causes
  to be done" — this phrase (repeated again in paragraph 14: "the one who
  bears this person") is a technical, archaic-sounding construction for
  "to represent them" or "to act as their representative." It is not
  glossed anywhere in the paragraph, and "bear a person" is not an idiom a
  contemporary reader will recognize; it reads as a literal (and slightly
  odd) image of physically carrying someone, which risks real
  misunderstanding rather than mere friction, especially since the whole
  paragraph's argument depends on this concept of representation.
- "For by this authority, given to him by every individual man in the
  commonwealth, he has conferred on him the use of so much power and
  strength that, through the terror it inspires, he is able to shape the
  wills of them all toward peace at home and mutual aid against their
  enemies abroad." This sentence has a genuine ambiguous-pronoun problem:
  "he has conferred on him" uses two different third-person pronouns ("he"
  as subject, "him" as object) that appear to refer to the same person (the
  sovereign), which doesn't parse — conferring is something the people do
  to him, not something he does to himself. As written, a reader has to
  stop and puzzle out who "he" and "him" are meant to be; this is the
  clearest single stumbling point in the whole chapter.

**Paragraph 14**
- "And the one who bears this person is called SOVEREIGN" — same "bear a
  person" phrasing as paragraph 12; flagged again because it recurs and
  compounds the earlier confusion rather than reinforcing a term the reader
  has now learned, since it was never actually explained in plain language
  the first time.

## Where the prose reads well

- **Paragraphs 6–11** (the numbered "First... Second... Third... Fourth...
  Fifth... Last" list explaining why humans can't cooperate like bees and
  ants) is the strongest stretch in the chapter. Each item is short,
  self-contained, and uses a consistent, easy-to-follow "that [creatures]...
  whereas [men]..." pattern. This reads naturally and is genuinely clear —
  a general reader will follow it without friction.
- **Paragraph 15** (natural force vs. institution) is clean and concrete —
  short sentences, clear cause-and-effect, and it lands its two new terms
  ("commonwealth by acquisition" / "commonwealth by institution") crisply
  right after using them, which makes them easy to retain.
- The closing quoted oath in paragraph 12 ("I authorize and give up my right
  of governing myself to this man...") is vivid and reads well as direct
  speech — it's the most memorable line in the chapter and needs no
  simplification.
- Paragraph 5 (bees and ants introduction) sets up the list well and reads
  smoothly despite mentioning Aristotle without further gloss (a minor,
  non-blocking reference).

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

## Summary

Most of this chapter reads as clear, natural contemporary prose — the
numbered list explaining why people aren't like bees and ants (paragraphs
6–11) and the closing paragraphs on sovereignty by force vs. institution
(paragraph 15) are genuinely strong and need no further accessibility work.
The trouble is concentrated in a handful of long, structurally overloaded
sentences, mostly in paragraphs 1, 3, 4, and especially 12 — the central
paragraph defining the commonwealth — where deeply embedded qualifying
clauses separate a sentence's subject from its main verb long enough that a
reader will likely lose the thread and have to re-read. The most serious
single issue is a genuine ambiguous-pronoun construction in paragraph 12
("he has conferred on him") that doesn't parse cleanly no matter how
carefully it's read, and the repeated unglossed idiom "bear their person"
(paragraphs 12 and 14) for "represent them," which risks real
misunderstanding rather than mere reading friction. None of this requires a
broader rewrite — the fixes are local, sentence-level untangling in a small
number of identified spots.
