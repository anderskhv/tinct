# Accessibility Review — Candidate Y (Reviewer A)

**Coverage:** Read all 16 paragraphs in full, start to finish, indices 0–15
(the complete `paragraphs` array of `candidate-Y.json`), then re-read each
paragraph individually. No other file was consulted.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 0**
- "The final cause, end, or design behind this restraint" — "final cause" is
  Aristotelian/scholastic terminology. It's immediately glossed by "end, or
  design" in the same breath, which mostly rescues it, but a reader unfamiliar
  with the phrase may still pause on "final cause" before reaching the gloss.
  Minor.

**Paragraph 5**
- "(which is why Aristotle counted them among the political creatures — that
  is, creatures naturally suited to live in organized communities — and yet
  they have no direction beyond their individual judgments and appetites, and
  no speech by which one of them can tell another what he thinks would serve
  the common benefit." — The opening parenthesis is never closed. The aside
  that starts with "(which is why Aristotle..." runs on through the rest of
  the sentence and into the next sentence ("So someone may perhaps want to
  know...") without a closing ")". A reader tracking the parenthetical frame
  will have to re-read to figure out where (or whether) the aside ends — this
  is a real stumbling point, distinct from vocabulary difficulty: it's a
  structural/punctuation break that costs a re-read.

**Paragraph 10**
- "creatures without reason cannot tell the difference between injury and
  damage" — the sentence trades on a specific legal-philosophical distinction
  (injury as a wrong done, damage as mere harm suffered) that isn't explained
  anywhere in the paragraph. A general reader will likely read "injury" and
  "damage" as near-synonyms and miss the point the sentence is making,
  without any signal that a technical distinction is intended.

**Paragraph 12** (the long definitional paragraph — flagging several distinct spots)
- "who can reduce all their wills, by a plurality of voices, to one will" —
  "by a plurality of voices" is a compressed, slightly archaic way of saying
  "by majority vote." It's parseable but will likely cause a brief stumble;
  a reader has to mentally substitute "voices" for "votes."
- "to appoint one man, or one assembly of men, to bear their Person; and for
  every one of them to own and acknowledge himself to be the Author of
  whatever the bearer of their Person shall do" — "Person" and "Author" are
  capitalized as technical terms but nothing in this chapter defines what
  "bearing a Person" or being its "Author" means. A reader coming to this
  chapter without the representation-theory setup from an earlier chapter
  will hit these capitalized words as unexplained jargon dropped into an
  otherwise plain sentence.
- "Once this is done, the multitude so united in one Person is called a
  COMMONWEALTH — in Latin, CIVITAS (Latin for "commonwealth," or "state")."
  — this says "Latin" twice and glosses the Latin word with an English
  translation that just repeats the word already given ("commonwealth").
  It reads as redundant/circular rather than clarifying — a reader has to
  do a double-take to confirm they didn't miss new information.

**Paragraph 13**
- "one person, whose acts a great multitude, by mutual covenants one with
  another, have made themselves every one the author of" — the syntax here
  is inverted and stacks a dangling preposition ("...the author of") after
  two intervening clauses ("by mutual covenants one with another") and an
  appositive ("every one"). It's grammatically legal but overloaded; a
  reader is likely to need a second pass to see that "of" attaches back to
  "whose acts."

## What reads well

- The numbered "Firstly / Secondly / Thirdly / Fourthly / Fifthly / Lastly"
  sequence (paragraphs 6–11) is genuinely clear and well-paced — short,
  parallel sentences that make Hobbes's argument-by-enumeration easy to
  follow, which is not a given with this kind of list.
- Paragraph 3's closing sentence ("For if we could suppose a great multitude
  of men agreeing to observe justice... there would be peace without
  subjection.") lands cleanly despite being a long, layered conditional —
  the logic is easy to trace in one pass.
- The famous authorization formula in paragraph 12 ("I authorize and give up
  my right of governing myself to this man...") and the "Mortal God" /
  "Leviathan" sentence that follows it both read naturally and with real
  rhetorical force — no stumbling there.
- Paragraph 15 (the two ways sovereignty is attained) is plain, well-ordered
  prose with no issues.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

## Summary

This chapter is, on the whole, genuinely readable modern prose — the
numbered list of reasons men aren't like bees and ants is a highlight, and
the famous set-piece sentences (the authorization formula, "Mortal God")
land with real clarity. The issues that remain are narrow and fixable rather
than pervasive: an unclosed parenthesis in paragraph 5 that will cost every
careful reader a re-read, a redundant double-translation of "commonwealth/
CIVITAS" in paragraph 12, a couple of unexplained technical terms
(capitalized "Person"/"Author" in paragraph 12, the injury/damage
distinction in paragraph 10) dropped in without a gloss, and one or two
compressed or inverted constructions ("by a plurality of voices," "have made
themselves every one the author of") that ask more of the reader than the
surrounding prose does. None of these require a rewrite of the chapter; they
're localized, identifiable fixes.
