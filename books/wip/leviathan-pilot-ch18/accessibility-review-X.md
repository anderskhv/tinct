# Accessibility Review — Reviewer A — candidate-X.json

**Coverage:** I read all 15 paragraphs of the chapter in full, start to finish, indices 0–14 (0-indexed as they appear in the `paragraphs` array), then re-read each paragraph individually for accessibility issues. No other file was consulted; this review judges the prose on its own, with no access to any source text.

## Per-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 0**
- "what ultimately causes them to do this — what their design and purpose amount to — is foresight" — the subject and verb ("what... is foresight") are separated by a second, restating appositive dash-clause. A reader has to hold the sentence open across two interruptions before the verb lands. Comprehensible, but likely to cause a re-read on first pass.

**Paragraph 1**
- "Even granting that, if no power has been set up — or none great enough for our security — then every man will, and lawfully may, rely on his own strength and skill as his guard against everyone else." — "Even granting that" as an opener, followed immediately by an "if... then" clause with its own interruption, stacks two conditional/concessive frames before the main clause arrives. Workable but dense.
- This paragraph overall is the longest in the chapter and carries several distinct claims (laws of nature are conditional; covenants without force are empty; small families plundered each other as an honor code; nations behave the same way at larger scale) run together with only semicolons and "and" to divide them. No single sentence is unreadable, but a reader has to do real work tracking which claim is which without paragraph breaks to lean on.

**Paragraph 3**
- "For if we could suppose a great multitude of men to agree in observing justice and the other laws of nature without any common power to keep them all in check, we might just as well suppose all mankind capable of the same thing — in which case there would be no need for civil government or commonwealth at all, and indeed no such thing would exist, since there would be peace without anyone being subject to anyone else." — one sentence carrying a hypothetical, a comparison ("just as well suppose"), a consequence, and a restatement of the consequence, chained with dashes and "since." This is the single most overloaded sentence in the chapter's first half; a general reader will likely need to re-read it to track what's being hypothesized versus what follows from it.

**Paragraph 4**
- "Being governed and directed by one judgment only for that short a time is not enough." — the sentence opens with a dangling-feeling gerund phrase ("Being governed and directed...") with no explicit subject stated until the reader infers it's "people" from the prior sentence. Reads slightly disconnected from its neighbors.
- "Afterward, once they either have no common enemy left, or one party comes to regard as an enemy someone another party regards as a friend, the difference in their interests will inevitably pull them apart" — "one party comes to regard as an enemy someone another party regards as a friend" nests a relative clause inside a direct object in a way that requires re-parsing to see who regards whom as what. This is the kind of grammatically-legal-but-overloaded construction worth flattening.

**Paragraph 5**
- "which is why Aristotle counted them among the political creatures" — a general adult reader may not recognize this as a pointer to Aristotle's concept of the "political animal" (zoon politikon); as written it just floats as an assertion. Minor, but a reader unfamiliar with the reference won't get the full weight of why this is being brought up.

**Paragraph 11** (the "common power" / commonwealth-formation paragraph)
- "to appoint one man, or one assembly of men, to bear their person — that is, to act and speak as if he were the multitude itself — with every one of them owning and acknowledging himself the author of whatever the one who bears their person does..." — "bear their person" (repeated twice in this sentence and again in paragraph 13) is an archaic, technical usage — a fusion of legal representation and theatrical "playing a part" — that a contemporary reader has no ready handle for. The text does gloss it once ("that is, to act and speak as if he were the multitude itself"), which helps, but the phrase itself will still read as opaque or oddly formal on first encounter, and it recurs without the gloss later.
- The sentence containing that clause is also extremely long — it runs from "That is as much as to say" through "their judgments, to his judgment," stacking an appositive, a parenthetical definition, a participial clause, and a final coordinated pair ("to submit their wills... and their judgments...") all under one main verb. This is the densest single sentence in the chapter and the one most likely to need a second pass.
- "or rather, to speak more reverently, of that mortal god to whom, under the immortal God, we owe our peace and defense" — "mortal god" and "under the immortal God" packed into one clause asks the reader to hold two different senses of "god" apart in quick succession. This is presumably intentional (it's the chapter's key image), but it is genuinely a stumbling point for a first-time reader, not just a stylistic flourish that reads smoothly.

**Paragraph 12**
- The quoted definition — "One person, of whose acts a great multitude, by mutual covenants with one another, have made themselves, every one, the author, so that he may use the strength and resources of them all, as he thinks fit, for their peace and common defense." — is presented as a formal, quotable definition, but its syntax is genuinely hard: "of whose acts a great multitude... have made themselves... the author" inverts normal subject-verb-object order and interposes a full prepositional clause ("by mutual covenants with one another") between "multitude" and its verb. Per the review instructions, being set off as a quoted/formal definition doesn't exempt it — this is likely the hardest sentence for a reader to parse in the whole chapter, requiring at least one re-read to see that "multitude" is the subject of "have made."

**Paragraph 13**
- "the one who bears this person" — same "bear... person" construction as paragraph 11, now used with no gloss at all. A reader who didn't fully absorb the explanation two paragraphs earlier will hit this cold.

**Paragraph 14**
- No significant issues. Clear throughout, including the two-part structure ("One is... The other is...") and the closing sentence, which cleanly sets up what's coming next.

## What reads well

Paragraphs 6–10 (the enumerated "First... Second... Third... Fourth... Fifth..." list contrasting men with bees and ants) are the strongest stretch in the chapter: short, parallel, concretely illustrated ("man's pleasure lies in comparing himself with other men, so he can enjoy nothing except what stands out above the rest"; "man is most troublesome precisely when he is most at ease"), and easy to follow point by point. The direct-address quotation in paragraph 11 ("I authorize and give up my right of governing myself to this man...") is also very effective — plain, vivid, and does real explanatory work instead of just restating the surrounding abstraction. Paragraph 14's two-way split (force vs. voluntary submission) closes the chapter cleanly.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

## Summary

The chapter is accessible for most of its length — the numbered bees-and-ants comparison in the middle is genuinely clear and well-paced, and several individual sentences elsewhere land well. But the chapter's structural core, where the "common power"/commonwealth/sovereign relationship is actually defined (paragraphs 11 through 13), is where the difficulty concentrates: a recurring unglossed technical phrase ("bear their person"), the single longest and most clause-stacked sentence in the chapter, and a formally quoted definition whose inverted syntax will send most general readers back to re-read it. Because these are exactly the paragraphs carrying the chapter's central idea, a targeted pass on that stretch — untangling the "bear their person" sentence, and restructuring the quoted definition into more natural word order — would meaningfully improve the chapter without requiring a full rewrite.
