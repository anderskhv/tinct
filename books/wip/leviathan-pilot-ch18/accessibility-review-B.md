# Accessibility Review — Reviewer A — Candidate B

**Coverage:** I read all 16 paragraphs of candidate-B.json in full, start to finish, in document order (indices 0–15). No source text, notes, or other candidates were consulted; this review judges the English prose on its own.

## Per-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 0**
- "*to keep them in awe and to bind them, through fear of punishment, to perform their covenants*" — "in awe" is used in its older sense (to keep in fear/check), but a contemporary reader's first association with "awe" is wonder/admiration, so this phrase risks a brief misread before the sentence's fear-of-punishment clause corrects it.
- "*the fourteenth and fifteenth chapters*" — a bare cross-reference to earlier chapters the reader may not have just read (or may not have easy access to while reading this one). Not a wording problem exactly, but a general reader will stumble on not knowing what's being pointed to.

**Paragraph 1**
- "*so far from being thought contrary to the law of nature that the greater the plunder they took, the greater their honor*" — the "so far from X that Y" correlative is a construction general readers rarely encounter in contemporary prose; it likely requires a re-read to parse as "not only was this not seen as wrong, the opposite was true."
- "*despite the laws of nature (which each man has kept only when he has the will to keep them and can do so safely), if no power is set up, or none great enough for our security, every man will rely...*" — the sentence's main clause ("despite the laws of nature... if no power is set up... every man will rely...") is interrupted by a dense parenthetical before the "if" clause even arrives. A reader has to hold "despite the laws of nature" in mind across the parenthesis and the conditional before reaching the payoff; this is the kind of overloaded-but-grammatical sentence the prompt flags, not just a long one.
- "*they enlarge their dominions on any pretext of danger or fear of invasion*" — "pretext" ordinarily signals a false or insincere excuse. Used here in a passage that otherwise reads as a neutral description of state behavior, it momentarily suggests irony or moral judgment that the surrounding sentence doesn't seem to intend, which could confuse a reader about the tone.

**Paragraph 5**
- "*which is why Aristotle counted them among the political creatures*" — "political creatures" applied to bees and ants will read as an odd or almost humorous phrase to a reader who doesn't already know Aristotle's "political animal" idea; the text doesn't gloss what's meant by "political" here (social/cooperative organization), so the reference may land as unclear rather than illuminating.

**Paragraph 12**
- "*I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner.*" — this is the chapter's sharpest accessibility problem. The surrounding prose is contemporary English, and then this sentence drops into archaic spelling ("my selfe"), thee/thou pronouns ("thou give up thy Right"), and mid-sentence capitalization of abstract nouns ("Right," "Man," "Assembly," "Actions") with no framing to tell the reader why the voice has suddenly shifted register. A general reader is likely to stop here, assuming either a typo or a quotation from elsewhere, and re-read the passage to figure out what's happening.
- "*the multitude so united in one Person is called a COMMONWEALTH, in Latin CIVITAS*" — "CIVITAS" is given with no gloss or translation. A reader who doesn't already know Latin will not get anything from this beyond "here is a Latin word," which is a genuine stumble even though it's clearly flagged as a foreign term.

**Paragraph 13**
- "*"One Person, of whose Acts a great Multitude, by mutuall Covenants one with another, have made themselves every one the Author, to the end he may use the strength and means of them all, as he shall think expedient, for their Peace and Common Defence."*" — like the quotation in paragraph 12, this entire sentence is left in 17th-century spelling and syntax ("mutuall," "Covenants," "the end he may use," "as he shall think expedient," capitalized "Author," "Peace and Common Defence") inside what otherwise reads as a modern rendering. This is the chapter's single most disruptive passage for a general reader: it is dense, archaically punctuated, and syntactically unmodernized, right at the point where the text is trying to deliver its key definition — exactly where clarity matters most. Readers will very likely need to re-read this sentence multiple times, and some may give up mid-sentence.

## What reads well

Paragraphs 6–11 (the six numbered reasons why humans, unlike bees and ants, need a sovereign) are the strongest stretch in the chapter: short, parallel, plainly worded sentences that build the argument cleanly one point at a time ("First, that men are continually in competition for honor and dignity..." / "Secondly, that among these creatures the common good does not differ from the private good..."). This section reads naturally and would give a general reader no trouble.

Paragraph 3 ("And let the multitude be as great as you like...") is also clear and well-paced despite discussing an abstract point (collective action failure without unified command) — it moves through cause and effect in plain, contemporary syntax.

Paragraph 15, the closing paragraph distinguishing commonwealth by institution vs. by acquisition, is concise and easy to follow.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

The chapter is largely accessible: the connective argument (why men submit to a sovereign, why bees/ants don't need one, how sovereignty is created) comes through clearly in plain modern English for most of its length, and the numbered-reasons section (paragraphs 6–11) is genuinely strong writing. The problems cluster narrowly: paragraph 1 has one overloaded, parenthetical-interrupted sentence and one archaic correlative construction; and paragraphs 12–13 contain two extended quotations left in unmodernized 17th-century spelling, capitalization, and syntax, dropped without warning into otherwise contemporary prose — these are the passages most likely to stop a general reader cold, precisely at the chapter's definitional climax. A targeted pass on those two quoted passages (plus light smoothing of the paragraph 1 sentence and a brief gloss for "CIVITAS") would resolve nearly everything flagged here.
