Both household and state should have unity, but only in certain respects. A state can reach a degree of unity at which it stops being a state. Or, while still existing, it can become a worse state, just as harmony becomes mere unison, or a rhythm is reduced to a single foot. As I said, the state is a plurality. Education should unite its members and form them into a community. It is strange that someone who devises an educational system that he thinks will make the state virtuous should expect these regulations to improve its citizens, instead of relying on philosophy, habits, and laws. Consider the laws about common meals in Sparta and Crete: through these the legislator has already made property common to a certain extent. We should not dismiss the experience of the ages. If these proposals were good, they would surely have become known over the course of so many years. Nearly everything has been discovered, although discoveries are sometimes left unconnected, and in other cases people fail to use the knowledge they possess. Watching such a constitution actually being established would clarify much. The legislator could not create any state without dividing its citizens into groups for common meals, and into phratries and tribes. Yet all this legislation finally amounts only to prohibiting the guardians from farming, a prohibition the Lacedaemonians already attempt to enforce. Again, Socrates has not explained, and it is not easy to determine, what the general constitution of such a community would be. The citizens outside the guardian class form the majority, but nothing has been settled about them. Must the farmers also own their property collectively? Or will each have private land in addition to the common land he cultivates? Will their wives and children belong to individual families or to everyone? If they share everything, like the guardians, how do they differ from the guardians, and what benefit do they gain from submitting to their rule? On what basis would they submit at all? Perhaps the rulers would adopt the Cretans' ingenious practice: the Cretans give their slaves institutions like their own, but forbid them physical training and the possession of weapons. If, instead, the lower classes have marriage and property arrangements like those of other cities, what kind of community will result? Will there not be two states within one, each hostile to the other? One class will consist of guardians, acting as watchmen; another will contain the farmers, alongside artisans and the remaining citizens. In that case, lawsuits, quarrels, and all the evils that Socrates identifies in other states will exist among them too. He does indeed say that such excellent education will spare citizens the need for many laws, including regulations for the city and the markets. But his education is confined to the guardians. Again, he lets farmers own the land on condition that they pay tribute. On those terms they will probably be far more difficult to control and more self-important than Helots, Penestae, or slaves generally. Socrates nowhere decides whether the lower class must share wives and property just as the upper class does. Nor does he settle the related questions of its education, government, and laws. It is difficult, though extremely important, to discover what character the lower classes must have if the guardians' common way of life is to be sustained.
