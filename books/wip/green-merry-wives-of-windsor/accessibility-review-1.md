# Accessibility Review 1 — The Merry Wives of Windsor (`merry-wives-of-windsor`, modern-en)

**What was read:** the full `candidate.json` (post-fix version, sha256
`cbe6b5d2fabd0fcd74c2d11afea6232c2dbe3d07244eba20a0771421dbdfd0f3`), all 23
chapters, all 1,155 paragraphs, in full and not sampled, with no reference to
`source.json` during this pass. Indexing: paragraph indices below are
**0-based** within each chapter (`[chapter.index]`, matching the array index
of `chapters[n]['paragraphs'][index]` — the same convention used throughout
this directory's working files).

## General impression

The candidate reads as a genuine modern-English staging text: clear sentence
structure, contemporary vocabulary, and stage directions normalized to
`[Enter X]` / `[Exit X]` bracket form throughout. Speaker tags are spelled out
in full caps (`FALSTAFF.`, `MISTRESS FORD.`) rather than abbreviated, which
is more accessible than source's `_Fal._` / `_Mrs Ford._` italic abbreviations.
Comic set pieces (the coat-of-arms/luce-louse joke, the Latin lesson, the
fairy-pinching finale) come through clearly to a reader with no Shakespeare
background.

## Genuinely difficult passages, with reasons

1. **Sir Hugh Evans's Welsh-accented English and Dr. Caius's French-accented
   English** (many lines throughout, esp. Ch1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14, 16,
   17, 22, 23). These are phonetically spelled ("Got" for God, "petter" for
   better, "'oman" for woman, "by gar" for by God, "vat"/"dat"/"de" for
   what/that/the) and take a beat of decoding on first encounter, exactly as
   in source. This is inherent to the characters' comic function — Evans and
   Caius are defined by their accents — and is not something a modern
   rendering can remove without destroying the joke (see Fidelity Review 1
   below for why this was actively restored, not merely left alone). Not a
   defect; flagging only because a first-time reader may want a note that
   this is intentional dialect, not typos.
2. **The Latin grammar-lesson scene (Ch13, William's examination)** is dense
   with real (if garbled) Latin declensions and only works if the reader
   knows this is a joke about words sounding like English obscenities
   ("hig, hag, hog" for "hic, haec, hoc"; "Jenny's case" for "genitive case").
   A brief editorial gloss would help a first-time reader but is outside the
   scope of paragraph-level translation and was not added, consistent with
   not inventing interpretation the source doesn't supply.
3. **Legal/heraldic jargon in Ch1** ("Custalorum," "Rato-lorum," "Star
   Chamber," "quarter the arms," "the dozen white luces") is dense but is
   itself the joke (Shallow and Slender garbling Latin legal titles); already
   rendered as clearly as English syntax allows without inventing glosses.
4. **Falstaff's and Ford's period-specific insults and oaths** ("Banbury
   cheese," "Mephostophilus," "latten-bilbo," "Cataian," "Castalion-King-
   Urinal") are left as vivid, specific period insults rather than flattened
   to generic ones — correctly, per this batch's standing rule against
   replacing specific named things with generic descriptors — but a reader
   unfamiliar with Elizabethan slang will not always catch the precise
   insult, only its general force. This is inherent to the material.
5. **The disguise/plot mechanics in Act 4–5** (buck-basket, Brentford witch,
   Brook alias, Herne's oak, fairy trick) involve several simultaneous
   deceptions tracked across many short scenes; the prose is clear
   sentence-by-sentence but a reader may want to re-read Ch18 (Fenton's
   exposition of the double elopement plot) once, as source's own single
   dense paragraph does the same.

## Not flagged as defects

Nothing else in the candidate reads as confusing, mistranslated, or
internally inconsistent from a cold read. Paragraph-to-paragraph flow is
natural; no paragraph reads as a list of disconnected clauses.
