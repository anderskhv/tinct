# Preliminary Expectoration

**[0]** An old saying, taken from the outward and visible world, says: “Only the one who works gets bread.” Strangely enough, the saying does not fit the world where it is most at home. For the outward world is subject to the law of imperfection, and there it happens again and again that the one who does not work also gets bread, and that the one who sleeps gets it more abundantly than the one who works. In the outward world everything belongs to whoever holds it. That world is in bondage to the law of indifference: the spirit of the ring obeys whoever has the ring, whether he is a Noureddin or an Aladdin, and whoever has the treasures of the world has them, however he came by them. In the world of spirit it is different. Here an eternal divine order reigns. Here it does not rain on the just and the unjust alike; here the sun does not shine on the good and the evil alike. Here it holds true that only the one who works gets bread; only whoever has been in anxiety finds rest; only whoever goes down into the underworld saves the beloved; only whoever draws the knife gets Isaac. The one who will not work does not get bread but is deceived, as the gods deceived Orpheus with an airy phantom in place of the beloved — deceived him because he was soft, not courageous; deceived him because he was a cithara player, not a man. Here it does no good to have Abraham for one’s father, or seventeen noble ancestors. What is written of the virgins of Israel fits the one who will not work: he brings forth wind. But whoever is willing to work brings forth his own father.

**[1]** There is a kind of knowledge that presumptuously wants to bring into the world of spirit the same law of indifference under which the outward world groans. It thinks that it is enough to know what is great, and that no other work is needed. But for that very reason it does not get bread: it perishes of hunger while everything turns to gold. And what does it know, anyway? In the Greece of that time there were many thousands, and in later generations countless people, who knew all of Miltiades’ triumphs; but there was only one who was made sleepless by them. There have been countless generations who knew the story of Abraham by heart, word for word — how many has it made sleepless?

**[2]** Now, the story of Abraham has the remarkable property that it always remains glorious, however poorly one understands it. Yet here again the question is whether one is willing to work and to be burdened. But people are not willing to work, and still they want to understand the story. They speak in Abraham’s honor — but how? They give the whole thing a perfectly commonplace expression: “What was great was that he loved God in such a way that he was willing to sacrifice the best to him.” That is very true; but “the best” is a vague expression. In the flow of thought and talk one quite confidently identifies Isaac with the best, and the one who meditates on it can smoke his pipe at his ease during the meditation, and the one who listens can stretch his legs out comfortably in front of him. If that rich young man whom Christ met on his way had sold all his goods and given them to the poor, we would praise him, as we praise everything great — though even him we would not understand without working. But he would still not have become an Abraham, even though he sacrificed the best. What is left out of Abraham’s story is the anxiety. For toward money I have no ethical obligation, but toward his son a father has the highest and holiest obligation of all. But anxiety is a dangerous business for the soft, and so it is forgotten; and nonetheless people want to talk about Abraham. Talk they do, and in the course of the talk the two expressions, Isaac and the best, are used interchangeably, and everything goes splendidly. Suppose, however, that among the listeners there happened to be a man who suffered from sleeplessness. Then the most terrible, the deepest misunderstanding, at once tragic and comic, lies very close at hand. He went home; he wanted to do just as Abraham did; for the son is, after all, the best. If that speaker got to hear of it, perhaps he went to the man, gathered all his clerical dignity and shouted: “You abominable man, you dregs of society, what devil has possessed you like this, that you would murder your son?” And the pastor, who had detected no warmth or sweat in himself when he preached about Abraham, was astonished at himself, at the earnest wrath with which he thundered down on that poor man. He was pleased with himself, for he had never spoken with such force and unction. He told himself and his wife: “I am an orator; all I lacked was the occasion. Last Sunday, when I spoke about Abraham, I was not moved in the least.” If this same speaker had some small surplus of understanding that he could afford to lose, I think he would lose it if the sinner answered calmly and with dignity: “But that is just what you yourself preached about last Sunday.” And how could the pastor ever have got such a thing into his head? Yet that is how it was, and the only mistake was that he had not known what he was saying. If only there were a poet who could make up his mind to prefer situations like this to the frippery and nonsense that comedies and novels are stuffed with! Here the comic and the tragic touch each other in absolute infinity. The pastor’s speech was perhaps ridiculous enough in and of itself, but it became infinitely ridiculous through its effect — and yet that effect was entirely natural. Or suppose the sinner, without raising any objection, really was converted by the pastor’s reprimand, and this zealous clergyman went home happy — happy in the awareness that he was effective not only from the pulpit but above all, with irresistible power, as a shepherd of souls: on Sunday he stirred the congregation to enthusiasm, while on Monday he stood like a cherub with a flaming sword before the man who by his deed wanted to put to shame that old saying that things in the world do not go as the pastor preaches. [* Note: In the old days people said: It is a pity that things in the world do not go as the pastor preaches. Perhaps the time will come, especially with the help of philosophy, when one can say: Fortunately things do not go as the pastor preaches; for in life there is at least a little meaning, but in his sermon there is none at all.]

**[3]** If, on the other hand, the sinner was not convinced, his situation is tragic enough. He was then presumably executed or sent to the madhouse — in short, he became unhappy in relation to so-called actuality. In another sense, I rather think, Abraham made him happy; for the one who works does not perish.

**[4]** How is one to explain a contradiction like that speaker’s? Is it because Abraham has acquired a prescriptive right to be a great man, so that whatever he does is great, while if someone else does the same it is sin, a sin that cries to heaven? In that case I do not wish to take part in such thoughtless glorification. If faith cannot make it a holy act to be willing to murder one’s son, then let the verdict on Abraham be the same as on anyone else. If people perhaps lack the courage to carry their thought through, and to say that Abraham was a murderer, then surely it is better to acquire that courage than to waste time on undeserved eulogies. The ethical expression for what Abraham did is that he was willing to murder Isaac; the religious expression is that he was willing to sacrifice Isaac. But precisely in this contradiction lies the anxiety that can indeed make a person sleepless; and yet without this anxiety Abraham is not the one he is. Or perhaps Abraham did not do at all what the story tells; perhaps, because of the conditions of those times, it was something quite different. Then let us forget him; for what is the point of remembering a past that cannot become a present? Or perhaps, just as he had ethically forgotten that Isaac was the son, that speaker had also forgotten something that corresponds to it. For when faith is taken away by being reduced to nil and nothing, all that remains is the raw fact that Abraham was willing to murder Isaac — and that is easy enough to imitate for anyone who does not have faith, that is, the faith that makes the imitating hard for him.

**[5]** I, for my part, am not short of courage to think a thought all the way through. Until now I have feared no thought. Should I ever come upon one, I hope I would at least have the honesty to say: This thought I fear; it sets something else in me in revolt, and therefore I will not think it. If I do wrong in this, the punishment will surely not fail to come. Had I recognized, as truth’s verdict, that Abraham was a murderer, I am not sure I could have silenced my reverence for him. But if I had thought it, I would presumably have kept silent about it; for one should not initiate others into thoughts like these. But Abraham is no illusion. He did not win his renown in his sleep, and he does not owe it to a whim of fate.

**[6]** Can one, then, speak about Abraham without reserve, and not run the risk that someone will go astray and do the same? If I do not dare, I will keep entirely silent about Abraham; and above all I will not belittle him in such a way that he becomes, precisely through that, a snare for the weak. For if one makes faith everything — that is, what it is — then I think one may speak of it without danger in our time, which hardly runs to excess in faith; and it is only by faith that one comes to resemble Abraham, not by murder. If one makes love a fleeting mood, a sensual stirring in a person, then one only lays snares for the weak when one wants to talk about the exploits of love. Everyone, no doubt, has a passing movement of feeling; but if, on the strength of that, everyone wanted to do the terrible deed that love has sanctified as an immortal exploit, then all is lost — both the exploit and the one who has gone astray.

**[7]** So one could, I suppose, speak about Abraham; for what is great can never do harm when it is grasped in its greatness. It is like a double-edged sword: it kills and it saves. If the lot fell to me to speak about it, I would start by showing how devout and God-fearing a man Abraham was, worthy to be called God’s elect. Only such a man is given such a test; but who is such a man? Next I would describe how Abraham loved Isaac. To that end I would ask all good spirits to stand by me, so that my speech might glow as a father’s love glows. I hope to portray it so that few fathers in all the King’s realms and lands would dare to claim that they loved like that. But if a father did not love as Abraham did, then any thought of sacrificing Isaac would, of course, be a spiritual trial — an inner assault on him. One could already speak about this for several Sundays; there is no need to hurry, after all. The result, if it was spoken of rightly, would be that a good many fathers would not ask to hear any more at all, but for the time being would be glad if they really managed to love as Abraham loved. If there was then one who, after hearing what is great but also what is terrible in Abraham’s deed, dared to set out on the road, I would saddle my horse and ride with him. At every stage, until we came to Mount Moriah, I would explain to him that he could still turn back: he could repent of the misunderstanding that he was called to be put to the proof in such a struggle, and could confess that he lacked the courage, so that if God wanted Isaac, God would have to take him himself. I am convinced that such a person is not rejected, that he can attain blessedness along with everyone else — though not here, in time. Would not even the most believing ages have passed that same judgment on such a person? I have known a person who once could have saved my life, if he had been magnanimous. He put it plainly: “I can see well enough what I could do; but I do not dare. I fear that later I will lack the strength, that I will come to regret it.” He was not magnanimous; yet who would stop loving him on that account?

**[8]** When I had spoken in this way, and had moved my listeners so that they had at least sensed the dialectical struggles of faith and its gigantic passion, I would not want to be to blame for an error on their part — that they should think: “He has faith to such a high degree; for us it is enough to hold on to his coat-tails.” For I would add: “I by no means have faith. I am by nature a shrewd head, and every such head always has great difficulty in making the movement of faith — though in and of itself I do not want to attach any value to that difficulty, as if overcoming it brought the shrewd head any further than the point that the simplest and most simple-hearted person reaches more easily.”

**[9]** Love, after all, has its priests in the poets, and now and then one hears a voice that knows how to keep it in honor. But of faith one hears not a word. Who speaks to honor this passion? Philosophy goes further. Theology sits painted at the window, courting philosophy’s favor and offering her own beauty for sale to philosophy. Understanding Hegel is said to be hard; but understanding Abraham, that is a trifle. Going beyond Hegel is a marvel; but getting beyond Abraham, that is the easiest thing in the world. For my part, I have spent a good deal of time trying to understand the Hegelian philosophy, and I believe I have understood it reasonably well. I am rash enough to think that when, in spite of all my efforts, I cannot understand him in certain places, it is probably because he himself was not entirely clear. All this I do lightly and naturally; it does my head no harm. But when I have to think about Abraham, I am as if annihilated. At every moment I catch sight of that monstrous paradox that is the content of Abraham’s life; at every moment I am thrust back, and for all its passion my thought cannot penetrate it, cannot get a hair’s breadth further. I strain every muscle to get a view of it — and in the same instant I am paralyzed.

**[10]** I am not unacquainted with what has been admired in the world as great and magnanimous. My soul feels its kinship with it and is assured, in all humility, that the cause the hero fought for was mine too. In the moment of contemplation I call out to myself: jam tua res agitur — now it is your own affair that is at stake. I can think myself into the hero; into Abraham I cannot think myself. When I have reached the summit, I fall, because what is offered me there is a paradox. Yet I by no means think that faith is something inferior on that account. On the contrary, I think it is the highest; and I think it is dishonest of philosophy to give out something else instead and to scoff at faith. Philosophy cannot give faith and is not supposed to; but it ought to understand itself, know what it is offering, and take nothing away — least of all cheat people out of something as though it were nothing. I am not unacquainted with the distress and dangers of life; I do not fear them, and I go to meet them boldly. I am no stranger to the terrible. My memory is a faithful wife, and my imagination is — what I myself am not — an industrious little girl who sits quietly at her work all day and in the evening knows how to chatter to me so prettily that I cannot help looking at her pictures, although what she paints is not always landscapes or flowers or Schäfer-Historier (shepherds’ idylls). I have looked the terrible in the eye; I do not flee from it in fear. But I know very well that even if I go to meet it with courage, my courage is still not the courage of faith, and is nothing to be compared with it. I cannot make the movement of faith. I cannot shut my eyes and plunge trustingly into the absurd; for me it is an impossibility — but I do not pride myself on that. I am convinced that God is love; this thought has for me a primal lyrical validity. When the thought is present to me, I am unspeakably blessed; when it is absent, I long for it more fiercely than a lover longs for the one he loves. But I do not have faith; this is the courage I lack. For me, God’s love is incommensurable — not measurable by any common standard — with the whole of actuality, and that in both a direct and an inverse sense. I am not cowardly enough to whimper and complain about that, but neither am I underhanded enough to deny that faith is something far higher. I can bear living in my own way well enough; I am glad and content. But my joy is not faith’s joy, and compared with that it is unhappy. I do not bother God with my small sorrows; particular things do not concern me; I keep my eyes on my love alone and keep its virginal flame clean and clear. Faith is convinced that God concerns himself with the smallest things. I am content in this life to be married with the left hand — in an unequal marriage; faith is humble enough to demand the right. For I do not deny, and never shall deny, that this is humility.

**[11]** Are all my contemporaries really capable of making the movements of faith? Unless I am much mistaken about them, they are inclined rather to take pride in doing what they probably do not even believe me capable of — namely, the imperfect. It goes against my soul to do what is so often done: to speak inhumanly about what is great, as if a few thousand years were an immense distance. I prefer to speak of it humanly, as if it had happened yesterday, and to let only greatness itself be the distance, a distance that either lifts up or judges. If, then (in the capacity of a tragic hero, for higher I cannot come), I had been summoned to such an extraordinary journey in the king’s service as the one to Mount Moriah, I know perfectly well what I would have done. I would not have been such a coward as to stay at home; I would not have dawdled along the highway either, or forgotten the knife to cause a little delay. I am fairly sure that I would have been there on the stroke of the hour, with everything in order — perhaps I would, if anything, have come too early, so that it could be over soon. But I also know what else I would have done. The moment I got up on the horse, I would have told myself: Now everything is lost. God demands Isaac; I sacrifice him, and all my joy with him — and yet God is love, and remains love for me. For in temporality God and I cannot talk with each other; we share no language. Perhaps someone or other in our time will be foolish enough, and envious enough of what is great, to want to persuade himself and me that had I really done this, I would have done something still greater than Abraham; for my enormous resignation was far more ideal and poetic than Abraham’s pettiness. And yet this is the greatest untruth; for my enormous resignation was the substitute for faith. Nor, then, could I do more than make the infinite movement, so as to find myself and to rest in myself again. Nor would I have loved Isaac as Abraham loved him. That I was resolute enough to make the movement might prove my courage, humanly speaking. That I loved him with all my soul is the premise without which the whole thing becomes a misdeed. But still I would not have loved as Abraham did; for if I had, I would have held back even at the very last minute — though without arriving at Mount Moriah too late on that account. Moreover, by my conduct I would have ruined the whole story; for had I received Isaac back, I would have been at a loss. What came easiest to Abraham would have been hard for me: to be glad in Isaac again! For the one who, with the whole infinity of his soul, proprio motu et propriis auspiciis — of his own motion and on his own authority — has made the infinite movement and can do no more, keeps Isaac only in pain.

**[12]** But what did Abraham do? He came neither too early nor too late. He mounted the donkey and rode slowly along the road. All that time he had faith: he believed that God would not require Isaac of him, while he was nevertheless willing to sacrifice him when it was demanded. He believed by virtue of the absurd; for human calculation was out of the question, and surely it was the absurd that God, who was requiring this of him, would take the requirement back the very next moment. He climbed the mountain, and even at the moment when the knife flashed, he believed — that God would not require Isaac. So he was no doubt surprised by the outcome; but through a double movement he had arrived at the state he was in at the start, and so he received Isaac more gladly than the first time. Let us go further. Let us suppose that Isaac really is sacrificed. Abraham had faith. He did not believe that he would one day be blessed in the world beyond, but that he would be blessed with happiness here in this world. God could give him a new Isaac, could call the sacrificed one back to life. He believed by virtue of the absurd; for all human calculation had long since come to an end. That grief can drive a person out of his mind — that one sees, and it is hard enough. That there is a strength of will that can sail so close to the wind that it saves the understanding, even if the person becomes a little strange — that one also sees, and I do not mean to belittle it. But to be able to lose one’s understanding — and with it the whole of finitude, since the understanding is the broker who handles all its exchanges — and then by virtue of the absurd to win back exactly the same finitude — that appalls my soul. Yet I do not say for that reason that it is something lowly; on the contrary, it is the only marvel. People generally suppose that faith turns out no work of art — that what it produces is rough, crude work, fit only for the clumsier natures. But the truth is quite otherwise. Of all things, the dialectic of faith is the most delicate and the most remarkable. It has an elevation I can certainly picture to myself, but no more. I can make the great springboard leap by which I pass over into infinity. I have a back like a rope-dancer’s, twisted when I was a child, so this comes easily to me: one, two, three, and I can walk on my head in existence. The next step, though, I cannot take; for the marvelous I cannot do — I can only stand amazed at it. Indeed, if Abraham, at the moment he swung his leg over the donkey’s back, had told himself, “Isaac is lost now; I could just as well sacrifice him here at home as travel all that long way to Moriah” — then I have no need of Abraham. As it is, I bow seven times to his name and seventy times to his deed. For he did not do this, as I can prove from the fact that he was glad to receive Isaac, heartily glad, and that he needed no preparation, no time to collect himself for finitude and its joy. If that had not been how things stood with Abraham, then perhaps he would have loved God, but he would not have had faith. For the one who loves God without faith turns his reflection on himself; the one who loves God in faith turns his reflection on God.

**[13]** Abraham stands at this summit. The last stage to vanish from his sight is infinite resignation — the movement by which one gives up the finite and finds one’s peace in the eternal. He really does go further, and comes to faith. For all these caricatures of faith — the miserable, lukewarm sloth that thinks, “There’s surely nothing to worry about; it isn’t worth grieving before the time comes,” the paltry hope that says, “One can’t know what will happen; it might just be possible” — these caricatures belong to the wretchedness of life, and infinite resignation has already infinitely despised them.

**[14]** I cannot understand Abraham; in a certain sense, I can learn nothing from him except to be astonished. If people imagine that by considering the outcome of that story they might let themselves be moved to have faith, they deceive themselves and want to cheat God out of the first movement of faith; they want to suck wisdom for living out of the paradox. Perhaps one or two may even manage this; for our age does not stop at faith, nor at faith’s miracle of making water into wine; it goes further and makes wine into water.

**[15]** Would it not be best, though, to stop at faith, and is it not outrageous that everyone wants to go further? When people in our time — and this is proclaimed in various ways — will not stop at love, where do they end up? In earthly shrewdness and petty calculation, in paltriness and wretchedness, in everything that can make a human being’s divine origin doubtful. Would it not be best to stop at faith, and for the one who stands to take care that he does not fall? For the movement of faith must constantly be made by virtue of the absurd — but, note carefully, in such a way that one does not lose finitude but gains it whole and entire. As for me, I can indeed describe the movements of faith, but I cannot make them. If you want to learn the movements of swimming, you can have yourself hung in a harness from the ceiling; you may well describe the movements, but you do not swim. Just so, I can describe the movements of faith; but when I am tossed into the water, I do swim, admittedly (for I am not one of the waders), yet my movements are different. They are the movements of infinity, while faith does the reverse: having made the movements of infinity, it makes those of finitude. Happy is the one who can make these movements! He does the marvelous, and I shall never tire of admiring him. Whether it is Abraham or the slave in Abraham’s household, a professor of philosophy or some poor maidservant, is absolutely indifferent to me; I look only at the movements. But at those I do look, and I let no one take me in — neither myself nor anyone else. The knights of infinite resignation are easy to recognize: their gait is gliding, bold. Those, on the other hand, who carry the jewel of faith are apt to deceive, because their outward appearance bears a striking resemblance to what both infinite resignation and faith deeply despise: philistinism, the outlook of the philistine — the narrow, comfortable townsman.

**[16]** I frankly confess that in my practice I have not come across a single reliable specimen — though I do not therefore mean to deny that perhaps every other person is such a specimen. All the same, I have been tracking one down for several years, in vain. People generally travel round the world to see rivers and mountains, new stars, gaudy birds, misshapen fish, ridiculous races of mankind; they give themselves over to the animal stupor that gawks at existence, and believe they have seen something. None of that engages me. If, however, I knew where such a knight of faith lived, I would make the journey to him on foot; for this marvel engages me absolutely. I would not let him out of my sight for an instant; every minute I would watch how he went about the movements. I would consider myself provided for in life, and divide my time between watching him and doing the exercises myself, and so spend all my time admiring him. As I said, I have not found anyone of the kind; but I can certainly conceive of him. Here he is. The introduction takes place; I am presented to him. The moment I first set eyes on him, in that same instant I thrust him away from me, make a leap backward myself, clap my hands together and say half aloud, “Good Lord! Is this the man? Is it really him? Why, he looks like a tax collector!” But it is him all the same. I draw a little closer to him and watch his slightest movement, to see whether some small, incongruous fragment of a signal might not show itself from the infinite — a glance, a look, a gesture, a touch of melancholy, a smile — that betrayed the infinite in its incongruity with the finite. No! I examine his figure from head to toe, to see whether there might not be a crack through which the infinite peeped out. No! He is solid through and through. His footing? It is sturdy, and belongs wholly to finitude; no solid citizen, dressed up for his Sunday-afternoon walk out to Frederiksberg, treads the ground more firmly. He belongs wholly to the world; no philistine could belong to it more. Nothing is to be detected of that strange and superior air by which one recognizes the knight of infinity. He takes delight in everything and takes part in everything; and every time one sees him take part in something in particular, he does it with a persistence that marks the earthly man whose soul is bound up with such things. He attends to his work. Seeing him at it, one would think he was a clerk who had lost his soul in Italian double-entry bookkeeping, so punctual is he. He takes Sundays off. He goes to church. No heavenly gaze, no sign of the incommensurable gives him away; without knowing him, one could not possibly single him out from the rest of the crowd, for his hearty, vigorous hymn-singing proves at most that he has a good pair of lungs. In the afternoon he walks out to the woods. He delights in everything he sees: in the bustle of people, the new omnibuses, the Sound. When one meets him on the Strandvej, one would think he was a petty shopkeeper out on the town for once — that is exactly how he enjoys himself; for he is no poet, and I have tried in vain to worm out of him the incommensurability of the poet. Toward evening he walks home, his gait as tireless as a postman’s. On the way he thinks that his wife is sure to have a special little hot dish waiting for him when he gets home — a roast lamb’s head with vegetables, for example. If he met a kindred spirit, he could go on all the way to Østerport talking with him about this dish, with a passion worthy of a restaurant keeper. As it happens, he does not have four skilling to his name, and yet he believes firmly and fully that his wife has that delicious dish waiting for him. If she has it, it would be an enviable sight for people of rank, and an inspiring one for ordinary folk, to watch him eat; for his appetite outdoes Esau’s. His wife has no such dish — and, oddly enough, he is exactly the same. Along the way he goes past a building lot and runs into another man. They chat for a moment; in a flash he raises a building, and he has every resource at his command to do it. The stranger leaves him thinking that this must surely be a capitalist, while my admired knight thinks, “Yes, if it came to that, I could easily get it.” He leans out of an open window and looks at the square where he lives, at everything that goes on there — a rat slipping in under the plank over the gutter, the children playing. Everything engages him, with a calm in existence as if he were a girl of sixteen. And yet he is no genius; for I have tried in vain to spy out the incommensurability of genius in him. Of an evening he smokes his pipe; to look at him, you would swear it was the grocer from across the street, vegetating in the twilight. He lets things take their course with an unconcern as if he were a feckless good-for-nothing; and yet at every moment he lives he buys up the opportune time at the dearest price, for he does not do even the smallest thing except by virtue of the absurd. And yet, and yet — indeed, I could become furious about it, if only out of envy — and yet this man has made the movement of infinity, and makes it at every moment. In infinite resignation he drains the deep melancholy of existence; the blessedness of infinity is known to him; he has experienced the pain of forsaking everything, whatever is dearest to one in the world. And yet finitude tastes every bit as good to him as to someone who never knew anything higher; for his remaining in finitude bore no trace of the cowed, anxious drill of a trained animal, and yet he has the confidence to delight in it as if it were the most certain thing of all. And yet, and yet, the whole earthly shape he brings forth is a new creation by virtue of the absurd. He resigned everything infinitely, and then he took hold of everything again by virtue of the absurd. He constantly makes the movement of infinity, but he makes it with such precision and assurance that he constantly gets finitude out of it, and there is not a second in which one suspects anything else. It is said to be the hardest task for a dancer to leap into a particular position in such a way that there is not a single second in which he is reaching for the position, but in the leap itself he stands in the position. Perhaps no dancer can do it — but that knight does. Most people live lost in worldly sorrow and joy; they are the ones who sit out and do not get into the dance. The knights of infinity are dancers and have elevation. They make the movement upward and come down again; and this too is a pastime not without its blessedness, and not unlovely to watch. But every time they come down, they cannot take up the position at once; they waver for a moment, and this wavering shows that they are strangers in the world after all. It is more or less conspicuous according to how much art they have; but not even the most artful of these knights can conceal the wavering. One need not see them in the air; one need only see them at the moment they touch the ground and have touched it — and one knows them. But to be able to come down in such a way that in the same second one looks as if one were standing and walking; to turn the leap in life into walking; to express the sublime absolutely in the pedestrian — that is what only that knight can do, and it is the only marvel.

**[17]** But this marvel can so easily deceive, so I will describe the movements in a particular case that can shed light on their relation to actuality; for that is what everything turns on. A young man falls in love with a princess, and the whole content of his life lies in this love; and yet the relationship is such that it is impossible for this love to be realized, impossible for it to be translated out of ideality into reality. The slaves of wretchedness, the frogs in life’s swamp, naturally shriek: “Such a love is folly; the rich brewer’s widow is every bit as good and solid a match.” Let them croak undisturbed in the swamp. That is not what the knight of infinite resignation does; he does not give up his love, not for all the splendor in the world. He is no simpleton. He first assures himself that it truly is his life’s content, and his soul is too sound and too proud to squander the least thing on an infatuation. He is not cowardly; he is not afraid to let the love steal into his most secret, his most remote thoughts, to let it wind itself in countless coils around every ligament of his consciousness — if the love becomes unhappy, he will never be able to tear himself free of it. He feels a blessed rapture in letting the love thrill through every nerve of his; and yet his soul is as solemn as that of someone who has drained the cup of poison and feels the juice seep through every drop of his blood — for this moment is life and death. When he has thus drawn the whole love into himself and immersed himself in it, he has courage enough to try everything and risk everything. He surveys the circumstances of his life; he summons the swift thoughts, which like well-trained doves obey his every signal; he swings his staff over them, and they scatter every way. But when they now all come back, all of them as messengers of sorrow, and explain to him that it is an impossibility, he grows quiet. He dismisses them, he is left alone, and then he undertakes the movement. If what I am saying here is to have any meaning, then everything depends on the movement taking place in the proper way. In the first place, then, the knight will have the strength to concentrate the whole content of life and the meaning of actuality in one single wish. If a person lacks this concentration, this self-containment — if his soul is scattered from the start among the many things — then he will never get as far as making the movement. He will act prudently in life, like the financiers who put their capital into all sorts of securities in order to gain on the one when they lose on the other: in short, he is no knight. In the second place, the knight will have the strength to concentrate the whole result of his thinking it through in one act of consciousness. If he lacks this self-containment, if his soul is scattered from the start among the many things, he will never find the time to make the movement. He will forever be running errands in life, never going into eternity; for even at the moment he is nearest to it, he will suddenly discover that he has forgotten something he has to go back for. In the next moment, he will think, it will be possible — and that is quite true, too. But by such considerations one never gets as far as making the movement; with their help one only sinks deeper and deeper into the mire. [* Note: It goes without saying that any other interest in which an individual has concentrated for himself the whole reality of what is actual can, when it proves unrealizable, give occasion for the movement of resignation. I have, however, chosen a case of falling in love to display the movements on, because this interest will be easier to understand, and so it spares me all preliminary considerations, which in a deeper sense could occupy only very few people.] [* Note: This requires passion. Every movement of infinity takes place through passion, and no reflection is able to produce a movement. It is this perpetual leap in existence that accounts for the movement, while mediation (Hegel’s term for reconciling opposites in a higher unity) is a chimera: in Hegel it is meant to account for everything, and at the same time it is the one thing he has never attempted to account for. Passion is needed even to draw the well-known Socratic distinction between what one understands and what one does not — and still more, of course, to make the true Socratic movement, that of ignorance. But what the age lacks is not reflection but passion. That is why, in a certain sense, the age actually clings to life too stubbornly to die; for to die is among the most curious of leaps. A little verse by a poet has always appealed to me greatly, because after five or six preceding lines in which he has wished himself good things in life, beautifully and simply, he ends like this: ein seliger Sprung in die Ewigkeit — a blessed leap into eternity.]

**[18]** So the knight makes the movement — but which movement? Will he forget the whole thing — for there is a kind of concentration in that too, is there not? No! For the knight does not contradict himself, and it is a contradiction to forget all that one’s life contained and still remain the same. He has no urge to become someone else, and he by no means considers that the great thing. Only the lower natures forget themselves and become something new. The butterfly, for instance, has entirely forgotten that it was a caterpillar; perhaps it can in turn forget so entirely that it was a butterfly that it can become a fish. The deeper natures never forget themselves; they never turn into anything but what they were. So the knight will remember everything; but this remembering is precisely the pain, and yet in infinite resignation he is reconciled with existence. For him, his love for that princess became the expression of an eternal love. It took on a religious character; it was transfigured into a love for the eternal being — who did, it is true, deny the fulfillment, but who nevertheless reconciled him again in the eternal consciousness that his love is valid in a form of eternity that no actuality can take from him. Fools and young people prattle that for a human being everything is possible. But this is a grave error. In terms of spirit, all things are possible; but in the world of finitude a great deal is not. Yet the knight makes this impossibility possible by expressing it spiritually — and he expresses it spiritually by renouncing it. The wish that would have led him out into actuality, but was wrecked on the impossibility, is now bent inward; yet that does not mean it is lost, nor is it forgotten. Sometimes it is the dim stirrings of the wish within him that awaken the memory; sometimes he awakens it himself. For he is too proud to want what was the whole content of his life to have been the affair of a fleeting moment. He keeps this love young, and it grows with him in years and in beauty. Yet he needs no finite occasion to make it grow. From the moment he has made the movement, the princess is lost. He has no need of those erotic tremblings of the nerves at the sight of the beloved, and so on. Nor does he need, in the finite sense, to be forever taking leave of her, because in the eternal sense he remembers her. And he knows very well that lovers who are so keen to see each other once more, in farewell, for the last time, are right to be keen, and right to think that it is the last time; for they are the ones who forget each other soonest. He has understood the deep secret that even in loving another human being one ought to be sufficient to oneself. He no longer takes any finite account of what the princess does, and that is exactly the proof that he has made the movement infinitely. This is where one can get to see whether the movement in the single individual is true or feigned. Someone else, too, believed he had made the movement; but, look, time went on, and the princess did something else — married a prince, for instance — and then his soul lost the elasticity of resignation. By this he showed that he had not made the movement correctly; for whoever has resigned infinitely is sufficient to himself. The knight does not cancel his resignation. He preserves his love as young as it was in its first moment; he never lets it slip from him, precisely because he has made the movement infinitely. Nothing the princess does can disturb him. Only the lower natures find the law for their actions in another human being — the premises of their actions outside themselves. If, on the other hand, the princess is of a like mind, then something beautiful will come of it. She will then introduce herself into that order of knighthood into which no one is admitted by ballot, but of which everyone is a member who has the courage to introduce himself — the order of knighthood that proves its immortality by making no distinction between man and woman. She, too, will preserve her love young and healthy; she, too, will have got over her anguish, even if she does not, as the old song has it, “lie every night at her lord’s side.” These two will then suit each other for all eternity, with a harmonia praestabilita — a pre-established harmony — so perfectly in time that if ever the moment came (a moment that does not, however, occupy them in the finite sense, for then they would grow old), if ever the moment came that allowed their love to be given its expression in time, they would be able to begin exactly where they would have begun if they had been united from the start. Anyone who understands this, man or woman, can never be deceived; for only the lower natures imagine that they have been deceived. No girl who is not this proud really knows how to love; but if she is this proud, then all the cunning and cleverness in the world cannot deceive her.

**[19]** In infinite resignation there is peace and rest. Every human being who wills it — who has not debased himself by holding himself in contempt, which is even more terrible than being too proud — can train himself to make this movement, which, in its pain, reconciles a person to existence. Infinite resignation is that shirt spoken of in an old folk tale. The thread is spun under tears, bleached with tears, the shirt sewn in tears; but then it also protects better than iron and steel. The imperfection in the folk tale is that a third party can make up this linen. The secret of life is that everyone has to sew it for himself; and the remarkable thing is that a man can sew it every bit as well as a woman. In infinite resignation there is peace and rest and consolation in pain — provided, that is, the movement is made normally. I could, however, easily write a whole book if I set out to go through the various misunderstandings, the back-to-front positions, the sloppy movements I have come across in my own small practice alone. People believe very little in spirit; and yet it is precisely spirit that matters in making this movement. What matters is that the movement not be the one-sided result of a dira necessitas — a harsh necessity — and the more that necessity is present, the more doubtful it always becomes whether the movement is normal. So if one means to hold that cold, barren necessity must necessarily be present in the movement, one is thereby saying that no one can experience death before he really dies — and that strikes me as crass materialism. But in our time people care less about making pure movements. Suppose someone who was to learn dancing said: “Generation upon generation has now spent centuries learning the positions; it is high time I turned this to my advantage and started straight in on the française.” People would probably laugh at him a little; but in the world of spirit this is found extremely plausible. What, then, is cultivation? I thought it was the course the single individual runs through in order to catch up with himself; and whoever will not go through this course is helped very little by having been born in the most enlightened age.

**[20]** Infinite resignation is the final stage preceding faith, so that whoever has not made this movement is without faith. For it is only in infinite resignation that I become clear to myself in my eternal validity, and not until then can there be talk of grasping existence by virtue of faith.

**[21]** Let us now have the knight of faith appear in the case described. He does precisely what the other knight does. He gives up, infinitely, the love that is his life’s content, and he is reconciled in the pain. But then the marvel occurs: he makes yet another movement, more wondrous than anything, for he says: “I believe nevertheless that I will get her — by virtue, that is, of the absurd, by virtue of this: that for God everything is possible.” The absurd does not belong among the distinctions that lie within the understanding’s own range. It is not the same thing as the improbable, the unexpected, the unlooked-for. At the moment the knight resigned, he assured himself of the impossibility, humanly speaking; this was the understanding’s result, and he had energy enough to think it. In the infinite sense, on the other hand, it was possible — namely, by resigning it. But to possess it in this way is, of course, at the same time to give it up; and yet for the understanding such possessing is no absurdity. For the understanding went on being right that in the world of wretchedness where it rules, it was and remained an impossibility. The knight of faith is just as clearly conscious of this. Accordingly, nothing can save him but the absurd, and this he grasps by faith. He acknowledges the impossibility, then, and in the same moment he believes the absurd. For if he wants to imagine that he has faith without acknowledging the impossibility with all the passion of his soul and with his whole heart, he deceives himself, and his testimony has no footing anywhere, since he has not even come as far as infinite resignation.

**[22]** Faith is therefore not an aesthetic emotion (the aesthetic being the sphere of immediate feeling) but something far higher, precisely because resignation comes before it. It is not the immediate impulse of the heart, but the paradox of existence. So when a young girl, in spite of all difficulties, nevertheless remains certain that her wish will indeed be fulfilled, this certainty is not at all the certainty of faith — even though she has been brought up by Christian parents and has perhaps gone to the pastor for instruction for a whole year. She is certain in all her childlike naivety and innocence. This certainty, too, ennobles her being and gives her a supernatural stature, so that like a thaumaturge — a worker of wonders — she can conjure the finite powers of existence and bring even stones to weep; while, on the other hand, in her fluster she is as likely to run to Herod as to Pilate, touching all the world with her pleading. Her certainty is most lovable, and one can learn much from her; but there is one thing one does not learn from her: one does not learn to make movements. For her certainty does not dare to look the impossibility in the eye, in the pain of resignation.

**[23]** I can understand, then, that making the infinite movement of resignation requires strength and energy and freedom of spirit; I can also understand that it is feasible. The next thing astonishes me; my brain turns over in my head. For after having made the movement of resignation, then by virtue of the absurd to get everything — to get the wish, whole and undiminished — that is beyond human powers; it is a marvel. But this I can understand: that the young girl’s certainty is mere light-mindedness compared with the unshakability of faith, even though faith’s unshakability has seen the impossibility. Every time I want to make this movement, everything goes black before my eyes; in the same moment I admire it absolutely, and in the same moment a tremendous anxiety seizes my soul — for what, then, is tempting God? And yet this movement is the movement of faith, and it goes on being so, even if philosophy, to confuse the concepts, would have us believe that it has faith, and even if theology wants to sell it off cheap.

**[24]** No faith is needed in order to resign. For what I win in resignation is my eternal consciousness; that is a purely philosophical movement, one I am confident I can make when it is demanded, and one I can discipline myself to make. For every time some bit of finitude threatens to get the better of me, I starve myself out until I make the movement; for my eternal consciousness is my love of God, and that ranks higher for me than anything. No faith is needed in order to resign; but faith is needed in order to get the very least thing more than my eternal consciousness, for this is what is paradoxical. The movements are often confused. People say that one needs faith in order to renounce everything. Indeed, one hears something stranger still: a person complains that he has lost his faith, and when one checks the scale to see where he stands on it, one sees, strangely enough, that he has only reached the point at which he ought to make the infinite movement of resignation. In resignation I renounce everything. I make this movement on my own; and if I fail to make it, that is because I am cowardly and effete and lacking in enthusiasm, and have no feeling for the significance of the lofty dignity allotted to every human being: that of being his own censor — a far more distinguished office than being censor general of the entire Roman Republic. I make this movement on my own, and what I thereby win is myself in my eternal consciousness, in blessed understanding with my love for the eternal being. By faith I renounce nothing; on the contrary, by faith I get everything — precisely in the sense in which it is said that whoever has faith as a grain of mustard seed can move mountains. A purely human courage is needed to renounce all of temporality in order to win eternity. But eternity I do win, and I can never in all eternity renounce it; that would be a self-contradiction. But a paradoxical and humble courage is needed to grasp, after that, the whole of temporality by virtue of the absurd — and this courage is faith’s. By faith Abraham did not give Isaac up; by faith Abraham got Isaac. By virtue of resignation that rich young man ought to have given everything away; but when he had done so, the knight of faith should have said to him: “By virtue of the absurd you will get every last penny back, provided you can believe it.” And these words ought by no means to be a matter of indifference to the formerly rich young man; for if he gave his possessions away because he was sick of them, then his resignation was in a sorry state.

**[25]** Temporality, finitude — that is what everything turns on. By my own powers I can resign everything, and then find peace and rest in pain. I can bear everything — even if that dreadful demon, more terrible than the skeleton Death who frightens people — even if madness held up the fool’s costume before my eyes and I understood from its look that it was I who was to put it on. I can still save my soul, as long as it matters more to me that my love for God should triumph in me than that I should have earthly happiness. Even in this last moment a person can gather his whole soul in one single glance toward the heaven from which every good gift comes; and this glance will be understood, by him and by the one it seeks, to mean that he stayed true to his love after all. Then he will calmly put on the costume. Anyone whose soul lacks this romance has sold his soul, whether he got a kingdom for it or a miserable piece of silver. But by my own powers I cannot get the least of what belongs to finitude; for I am constantly using my powers to resign everything. By my own powers I can give up the princess, and I will not become a sulker; I will find joy and peace and rest in my pain. But by my own powers I cannot get her back, for it is precisely to resign that I am using my powers. But by faith, says that wonderful knight — by faith you will get her, by virtue of the absurd.

**[26]** This movement, you see, I cannot make. As soon as I want to begin on it, everything turns upside down, and I flee back into the pain of resignation. I can swim in life, but for this mystical hovering I am too heavy. To exist so that at every moment my opposition to existence expresses itself as the most beautiful and secure harmony with it — that I cannot do. And yet it must be glorious to get the princess. I say so at every moment, and the knight of resignation who does not say so is a fraud: he has not had a single wish, and he has not kept the wish young in his pain. Perhaps there was one who found it convenient enough that the wish was no longer alive, that the arrow of pain had been blunted; but someone like that is no knight. A free-born soul who caught himself at it would despise himself and start over, and above all would not let his soul be deceived by itself. And yet it must be glorious to get the princess. And yet only the knight of faith is happy — the hereditary lord of the finite — while the knight of resignation is a stranger and a foreigner. It was conceivable too, of course, that the knight of resignation might get the princess; but his soul had seen through the impossibility of their future happiness. To get the princess in this way, to live happily and joyfully day in and day out with her; in this way to live happily and joyfully every moment by virtue of the absurd, at every moment to see the sword hovering above the head of the beloved, and yet to find not rest in the pain of resignation but joy by virtue of the absurd — that is wonderful. Whoever does it is great, the only great one. The thought of it moves my soul, which was never stingy in admiring what is great.

**[27]** Suppose, now, that everyone of my own time who will not stop at faith really is a man who has comprehended the horror of life, who has understood what Daub means when he says that a soldier standing alone at his post with a loaded rifle, by a powder magazine, on a stormy night, gets strange thoughts. Suppose that everyone who will not stop at faith really is a man who had the strength of soul to comprehend the thought that the wish was an impossibility, and who then took time to be alone with that thought. Suppose that everyone who will not stop at faith is a man who was reconciled in pain and reconciled by pain. Suppose that everyone who will not stop at faith is a man who then (and if he has not done all of the foregoing, he need not trouble himself when faith is under discussion) did the wonderful thing and grasped the whole of existence by virtue of the absurd. Then what I am writing is the highest eulogy on my contemporaries, by the least among them — one who could make only the movement of resignation. But in that case why will people not stop at faith? Why does one sometimes hear that people are embarrassed to confess that they have faith? This I cannot comprehend. If I ever get so far as to be able to make this movement, then from that day on I shall drive a coach and four.

**[28]** Is it really so? Is all the philistinism I see in life — which I let be judged not by my word but by my deed — really not what it seems? Is it the marvel? That is conceivable, after all; for that hero of faith did bear a striking resemblance to it — since that hero of faith was not even an ironist and humorist, but something still higher. There is a great deal of talk in our time about irony and humor, especially from people who have never managed to practice them, but who nonetheless know how to explain everything. I am not wholly a stranger to these two passions; I know somewhat more about them than is to be found in German and German-Danish compendiums. So I know that these two passions differ essentially from the passion of faith. Irony and humor also reflect on themselves, and therefore belong in the sphere of infinite resignation; their elasticity lies in the individual’s being incommensurable with actuality.

**[29]** The last movement, the paradoxical movement of faith, I cannot make — be it a duty or whatever it may be — although I would be only too glad to. Whether a person has the right to say this must be left to him; it becomes a matter between him and the eternal being who is the object of faith, whether he can come to an amicable settlement on this point. What every human being can do is make the movement of infinite resignation; and I for my part would not hesitate to call anyone a coward who wants to imagine that he cannot. With faith it is another matter. But what no human being has the right to do is to talk others into imagining that faith is a small thing, or an easy matter, when it is the greatest and the hardest thing of all.

**[30]** People take the story of Abraham another way. They praise God’s grace for granting him Isaac again — the whole thing was only a test. A test — this word can say a lot or a little, and yet the whole business is over the moment it is said. One climbs onto a winged horse; in the same instant one is on Mount Moriah; in the same instant one sees the ram. One forgets that Abraham rode only on a donkey, which goes slowly along the road; that he had a three-day journey; that he needed some time to split the firewood, to bind Isaac and to sharpen the knife.

**[31]** And yet people praise Abraham. The one who is to speak can quite well sleep until the last quarter of an hour before he has to speak; the listener can quite well fall asleep during the speech; for everything goes easily enough, without trouble on either side. If there were a man present who suffered from sleeplessness, then perhaps he went home, sat down in a corner and thought: “The whole thing is a matter of a moment. Just wait a minute and you will see the ram, and the test is over.” If the speaker came upon him in this state, I imagine he would plant himself before him in all his dignity and say: “You wretch, that you can let your soul sink into such foolishness! No miracle happens, and the whole of life is a test.” As the speaker went on with his outpouring, he got more and more worked up and grew more and more pleased with himself; and whereas he had noticed no rush of blood to the head while he was speaking about Abraham, he now felt the vein swelling in his forehead. Perhaps he would be struck dumb if the sinner answered calmly and with dignity: “But that is what you preached about last Sunday.”

**[32]** So let us either strike Abraham out altogether, or let us learn to be horrified at the tremendous paradox that is the meaning of his life, so that we may understand that our time, like every time, can be glad if it has faith. If Abraham is not a nullity, a phantom, a bauble one plays with to pass the time, then the error can never lie in the sinner’s wanting to do the same. What matters is to see how great a thing it was that Abraham did, so that the man can judge for himself whether he has the calling and the courage to be put to the proof in such a thing. The comic contradiction in the speaker’s conduct was that he made Abraham into something insignificant, and yet wanted to forbid the other man to behave in the same way.

**[33]** Should one, then, not dare to speak about Abraham? I rather think one should. If I had to speak about him, I would begin by portraying the pain of the test. For this purpose I would suck, leech-like, every bit of anxiety and distress and torment out of a father’s suffering, so that I could describe what Abraham suffered while through it all he nevertheless had faith. I would point out that the journey took three days and a good way into the fourth; indeed, those three and a half days should become infinitely longer than the couple of thousand years that separate me from Abraham. Then I would point out — for this is my opinion — that every human being still dares to turn around before he begins on such a thing, and can at any moment turn back in repentance. If this is done, I fear no danger, nor do I fear arousing in people a wish to be put to the proof as Abraham was. But if one wants to sell off a cut-price edition of Abraham, and yet forbid everyone to do the same, then it is ridiculous.

**[34]** It is now my intention, then, to draw out from the story of Abraham, in the form of problemata — questions set for debate — the dialectical element that lies in it, in order to see what a tremendous paradox faith is: a paradox capable of turning a murder into a holy act, pleasing to God; a paradox that returns Isaac to Abraham, which no thinking can master, because faith begins precisely where thinking leaves off.
