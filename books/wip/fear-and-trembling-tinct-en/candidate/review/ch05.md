# Problema I — Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?

**[0]** The ethical as such is the universal. As the universal, it applies to everyone — which can also be put another way: it applies at every moment. It rests immanently in itself: nothing outside it is its telos (its end: the purpose it exists for). Instead, it is itself the telos of everything outside it, and once the ethical has taken all of that into itself, it goes no further. Taken in his immediacy — as a being of senses and soul — the single individual is the individual who has his telos in the universal. His ethical task is to express himself in the universal continually: to cancel his particularity (whatever sets him apart as this one person) in order to become the universal. As soon as the single individual tries to assert himself in his particularity against the universal, he sins; the only way back to reconciliation with the universal is to acknowledge that he has sinned. Whenever the single individual, after having entered the universal, feels an urge to assert himself as the single individual, he is in a spiritual trial — an inner assault on him — and he can work his way out of it only by repenting and giving himself up, as the single individual, in the universal. If this is the highest thing that can be said about a human being and his existence, then the ethical has the same character as the eternal blessedness of a human being, which for all eternity and at every moment is his telos. For it would be a contradiction if eternal blessedness could be given up (that is, teleologically suspended). For the moment eternal blessedness was suspended, it would be forfeited — whereas whatever is teleologically suspended is not forfeited, but is preserved precisely in the higher thing that is its telos.

**[1]** If that is how things stand, then Hegel is right when, at the stage of "the Good and Conscience," he defines a human being only as the single individual. He is right to regard this condition as a "moral form of evil" (see especially his Philosophy of Right), one that must be cancelled in the teleology of ethical life — the ethics lived out in a people's shared customs and institutions. On this view, the single individual who remains at that stage either sins or is in a spiritual trial. Where Hegel is wrong, however, is in speaking of faith. He is wrong not to raise a loud and public protest when Abraham is honored and celebrated as a father of faith, whereas Abraham ought instead to be sent back to a lower court and expelled as a murderer.

**[2]** For faith is precisely this paradox: that the single individual is higher than the universal. But note carefully how this is so: the movement is repeated. Having first been in the universal, the single individual now isolates himself, as the single individual, as higher than the universal. If faith is not this, then Abraham is lost, and there has never been faith in the world — precisely because there has always been faith. For suppose that the ethical — that is, ethical life — is the highest, and that nothing incommensurable remains in a human being (nothing that cannot be measured by that common standard) except in the sense that this incommensurable element is evil, namely the particular, which ought to be expressed in the universal. In that case one needs no categories beyond those Greek philosophy had, or those that consistent thinking can derive from them. Hegel should not have concealed this; after all, he had studied the Greeks.

**[3]** One quite often hears men who, instead of losing themselves in study, lose themselves in phrases, announce that a light shines over the Christian world while darkness broods over paganism. This talk has always struck me as strange, since every more thorough thinker, every more serious artist, still renews himself in the eternal youth of the Greek people. Such a statement can be explained by the fact that people do not know what to say, only that they must say something. It is quite in order to say that paganism did not have faith. But if saying so is to mean anything, one must be a little clearer about what one understands by faith; otherwise one sinks back into phrases like these. It is easy to explain the whole of existence, faith included, without having any idea of what faith is. And the man who counts on being admired for such an explanation is not the worst calculator in life; for it is just as Boileau puts it: un sot trouve toujours un plus sot, qui l'admire — a fool always finds a bigger fool to admire him.

**[4]** Faith is precisely this paradox: that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal — is justified over against it, not subordinate to it but above it. But note carefully how this is so: it is the single individual who, after having been subordinate to the universal as the single individual, now, through the universal, becomes the single individual who as the single individual stands above it — that the single individual as the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute. This position cannot be mediated — that is, it cannot be brought under the universal — for all mediation takes place precisely by means of the universal. Now and forever it remains a paradox that thought cannot reach. And yet either faith is this paradox, or else — these are the consequences I ask the reader to keep in mind at every point, even though it would become too long-winded for me to write them out everywhere — or else there has never been faith, precisely because there has always been faith, or else Abraham is lost.

**[5]** It is true that the single individual can easily mistake this paradox for a spiritual trial, but that is no reason to conceal it. It is true that many people may be so constituted that the paradox repels them. But that is no reason to turn faith into something else so that one can have it too; better to admit that one does not have it. And those who do have faith should take care to set out some marks by which one could tell the paradox apart from a spiritual trial.

**[6]** Now, the story of Abraham contains just such a teleological suspension of the ethical. There has been no shortage of sharp minds and thorough scholars who have found analogies to it. Their wisdom comes down to the fine principle that basically everything is the same. If one looks a little more closely, I very much doubt that anyone will find a single analogy in the whole world — except a later one, which proves nothing if it stands firm that Abraham represents faith, and that in him faith is expressed as the norm — in a man whose life is not only the most paradoxical that thought can conceive, but so paradoxical that thought cannot conceive it at all. He acts by virtue of the absurd; for the absurd is precisely this: that he as the single individual is higher than the universal. This paradox cannot be mediated. For as soon as Abraham begins to mediate it, he has to admit that he was in a spiritual trial; and if so, he never gets as far as sacrificing Isaac — or, if he has already sacrificed Isaac, he must return in repentance to the universal. By virtue of the absurd he gets Isaac back. Abraham is therefore at no moment a tragic hero; he is something altogether different — either a murderer or a believer. The middle term that saves the tragic hero is something Abraham does not have. That is why I can understand a tragic hero but cannot understand Abraham, even though in a certain mad sense I admire him more than anyone else.

**[7]** Ethically expressed, Abraham's relation to Isaac is quite simply this: the father must love the son more than himself. But the ethical has several gradations within its own range. We shall see whether this story contains any higher expression of the ethical that could explain his conduct ethically — that could ethically justify him in suspending his ethical obligation to his son, without his thereby going beyond the teleology of the ethical, the ordering of purposes that lies within the ethical itself.

**[8]** When an undertaking that concerns a whole people is thwarted, when such an enterprise is halted by heaven's disfavor, when the god in his anger sends a windless calm that makes a mockery of all their labor, when the seer carries out his heavy task and announces that the god demands a young girl as a sacrifice — then the father must heroically make this sacrifice. Nobly he must hide his pain, though he might well wish to be "the lowly man who dares to weep" rather than the king whose part is to act royally. And though the pain enters his breast in solitude, and only three people in the nation share his secret, soon the whole nation will share in his pain — but will also share in his deed: that he was willing to offer her up for the common good — her, his daughter, the lovely young girl. "O breast! O fair cheeks, golden hair!" (line 687). The daughter's tears will move him; the father will look away; but it is the hero who will lift the knife. — And when word of it comes back to the land of their fathers, the beautiful girls of Greece will blush with enthusiasm; and if the daughter was a bride, her betrothed will not be angry but proud to share in the father's deed, because the girl belonged to him more tenderly than she belonged to her father.

**[9]** When the brave judge who saved Israel in its hour of need binds God and himself, in one breath, by the same vow, then he must heroically turn the young girl's jubilation, his beloved daughter's joy, into sorrow, and all Israel will mourn with her for her virgin youth. But every free-born man will understand Jephthah, every stout-hearted woman will admire him, and every virgin in Israel will wish to act as his daughter did. For what good would it have done that Jephthah won the victory through his vow, if he did not keep it? Would the victory not then be taken from the people again?

**[10]** When a son is forgetful of his duty, when the state puts the judge's sword into the father's hand, when the laws require that the penalty come from the father himself, then the father must heroically forget that the offender is his own son. Nobly he must hide his pain, but there will not be a single person in the nation, not even the son, who does not admire the father. And every time the laws of Rome are interpreted, it will be remembered that many interpreted them more learnedly, but none more gloriously, than Brutus.

**[11]** But suppose that Agamemnon, while a favorable wind was carrying the fleet under full sail toward its goal, had sent that messenger to fetch Iphigenia in order to sacrifice her. Suppose that Jephthah, without being bound by any vow that decided the fate of the people, had said to his daughter: "Mourn your short youth for two months now, and then I will sacrifice you." Suppose that Brutus had had a righteous son and had still called the lictors to execute him. Who would have understood them? And if these three men, asked why they did it, had answered, "It is a test in which we are being put to the proof" — would anyone have understood them better?

**[12]** When Agamemnon, Jephthah and Brutus, at the decisive moment, heroically overcome the pain, have heroically lost what they love, and have only to carry out the deed outwardly, then there will never be a noble soul in the world who does not shed tears of sympathy for their pain and tears of admiration for their deed. But suppose that at the decisive moment these three men were to add, to the heroism with which they bore the pain, these few little words: "But it will not happen." Who would understand them then? And if they explained, "We believe this by virtue of the absurd," would that make them any easier to understand? For anyone could readily understand that it was absurd — but who could understand that one could then believe it?

**[13]** The difference between the tragic hero and Abraham is plain to see. The tragic hero still stays within the ethical. He lets one expression of the ethical have its telos in a higher expression of the ethical. He reduces the ethical relation between father and son, or between daughter and father, to a feeling whose dialectic lies in its relation to the idea of ethical life. So here there can be no question of a teleological suspension of the ethical itself.

**[14]** With Abraham it is different. By his deed he overstepped the ethical altogether, and he had a higher telos outside it, in relation to which he suspended the ethical. For I would really like to know how anyone would bring Abraham's deed into relation with the universal — whether any connection whatever can be found between what Abraham did and the universal, other than that Abraham transgressed it. Abraham does not do it to save a nation, nor to uphold the idea of the state, nor to appease angry gods. If one could speak of the deity's anger at all, the anger was directed at Abraham alone, and Abraham's entire deed has no relation to the universal; it is a purely private undertaking. So while the tragic hero is great through the virtue of ethical life, Abraham is great through a purely personal virtue. In Abraham's life there is no higher expression of the ethical than this: the father must love the son. The ethical in the sense of ethical life is entirely out of the question here. Insofar as the universal was present at all, it was hidden in Isaac — concealed, so to speak, in Isaac's loins — and would have had to cry out with Isaac's mouth: "Do not do it! You are destroying everything."

**[15]** What, then, is Abraham's reason? He acts for God's sake and — which is wholly the same thing — for his own. For God's sake, because God requires this proof of his faith; for his own sake, so that he may furnish that proof. The unity of these two is expressed quite correctly in the phrase that has always been used for this situation: it is a test, a temptation. A temptation — but what does that mean? Normally a temptation is something that would keep a person from doing his duty. Here, though, the ethical itself is the temptation: it is what would keep him from doing the will of God. What, then, is duty? Duty is, after all, precisely the expression of God's will.

**[16]** Here it becomes clear that a new category is needed in order to understand Abraham. Paganism does not know such a relation to the deity. The tragic hero does not enter into any private relation to the deity; the ethical is the divine, and therefore whatever is paradoxical in his situation can be mediated in the universal.

**[17]** Abraham cannot be mediated; to say the same thing differently, he cannot speak. The moment I speak, I am expressing the universal; and if I do not speak, nobody can understand me. So as soon as Abraham wants to express himself in the universal, he has to say that his situation is a spiritual trial. For no higher expression of the universal — one that stands above the universal he is transgressing — is available to him.

**[18]** So Abraham fills me with admiration and at the same time with horror. Someone who denies himself and offers himself up to duty gives up the finite in order to grasp the infinite, and he stands on firm enough ground. The tragic hero gives up what is certain for what is still more certain, and the onlooker's eye rests on him with confidence. But the one who gives up the universal to grasp something still higher that is not the universal — what is he doing? Is it possible that this could be anything other than a spiritual trial? And if it is possible, but the single individual grasped wrongly, what salvation is there for him? He suffers all the pain of the tragic hero, he destroys his joy in the world, he renounces everything — and perhaps at the same moment he bars himself from the exalted joy that was so precious to him that he would have bought it at any price. The onlooker cannot understand him at all, nor let his eye rest on him with confidence. Perhaps what the believer intends cannot be done at all, since it is unthinkable. Or if it could be done, but the single individual had misunderstood the deity, what salvation would there be for him then? The tragic hero needs tears and demands tears. And where was the envious eye so barren that it could not weep with Agamemnon? But where was the person whose soul was so lost that he presumed to weep over Abraham? The tragic hero completes his deed at a definite moment in time; but as time goes on he does something no less: he visits the person whose soul is gripped by sorrow, whose chest cannot draw breath for stifled sighs, whose thoughts hang heavy over him, pregnant with tears. He appears to him, breaks the spell of sorrow, loosens the corset, and draws out the tear, as the sufferer forgets his own sufferings in the hero's. Over Abraham no one can weep. One approaches him with a horror religiosus — a holy dread — as Israel approached Mount Sinai. — Think, then, of the solitary man who climbs Mount Moriah, whose peak towers sky-high above the plains of Aulis. Suppose he is not a sleepwalker who walks safely over the abyss while the one who stands at the foot of the mountain, looking up, trembles with anxiety and, out of reverence and dread, does not even dare to call out to him. Suppose he becomes deranged in himself; suppose he had made a mistake! — Thanks, and thanks again, to the man who, when life's sorrows have assaulted someone and left him naked, holds out to him the expression, the fig leaf of the word, to hide his wretchedness! Thanks to you, great Shakespeare, who can put everything — everything, everything — into words exactly as it is — and yet why did you never give voice to this torment? Did you perhaps keep it for yourself, like the beloved whose name one cannot even bear to hear the world speak? For a poet buys this power of the word, the power to speak all the heavy secrets of others, at the price of one little secret he cannot speak; and a poet is no apostle: he drives out devils only with the devil's own power.

**[19]** But when the ethical has been teleologically suspended in this way, how does the single individual in whom it is suspended exist? He exists as the single individual in opposition to the universal. Does he sin, then? For, seen in terms of the idea (that is, in principle), this is the form of sin. Even a child, who does not sin because it is not conscious of its existence as such, has an existence that, seen in terms of the idea, is sin; and the ethical demands itself of the child at every moment. If one denies that this form — the single individual standing against the universal — can be repeated in such a way that it is not sin, then judgment has been passed on Abraham. How, then, did Abraham exist? He had faith. This is the paradox that keeps him at the summit, the paradox he cannot make clear to anyone else. For the paradox is that he, as the single individual, puts himself in an absolute relation to the absolute. Is he justified? His justification is, once again, something paradoxical; for if he is justified, he is so not by virtue of being something universal but by virtue of being the single individual.

**[20]** How, then, does the single individual make sure that he is justified? It is easy enough to flatten the whole of existence into the idea of the state or of society. Do that, and one can also mediate easily enough; for then one never arrives at the paradox that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal — which I can also express pointedly with a saying of Pythagoras: that the odd number is more perfect than the even. Now and then in our time one does hear an answer that points in the direction of the paradox. When one does, it usually runs like this: "It must be judged by the outcome." A hero who has become a skandalon — a stumbling block, an offense — to his contemporaries, conscious that he is a paradox who cannot make himself understood, calls out confidently to his age: "The outcome will surely prove that I was justified." In our time one hears this cry more rarely; for just as our age does not produce heroes, which is its failing, it has the advantage that it produces few caricatures either. So when in our time one hears the words "It must be judged by the outcome," one knows at once whom one has the honor of addressing. Those who talk this way are a numerous tribe, which I will call by the common name of the lecturers. In their thoughts they live secure in existence: they have a fixed position and safe prospects in a well-organized state; they have centuries, perhaps even millennia, between themselves and the upheavals of existence; they have no fear that such things could happen again — what would the police and the newspapers say? Their work in life is to judge the great men, and to judge them by the outcome. Such conduct toward greatness betrays a strange mixture of arrogance and wretchedness: arrogance, because they think themselves called to judge; wretchedness, because they do not feel their own lives to be even remotely related to those of the great. Anyone with even a little erectioris ingenii — a more elevated cast of mind — has not become an entirely cold and clammy mollusk; and as he approaches what is great, he can never forget that since the creation of the world it has been the custom for the outcome to come last, and that anyone who really means to learn from greatness must look precisely at how it began. Whoever is to act and wants to judge himself by the outcome will never begin. Even if the outcome delights the whole world, it cannot help the hero; for he learned the outcome only when the whole thing was over, and it was not by the outcome that he became a hero — he was one because he began.

**[21]** Besides, the outcome (insofar as it is finitude's answer to the infinite question) is, in its dialectic, entirely different in kind from the hero's existence. Or is the fact that Abraham received Isaac by a miracle supposed to prove that Abraham was justified in relating himself as the single individual to the universal? If Abraham had actually sacrificed Isaac, would he have been any less justified?

**[22]** But people are curious about the outcome, as they are about how a book ends. The anxiety, the distress, the paradox — of these they want to know nothing. They flirt aesthetically with the outcome; it arrives just as unexpectedly, but also just as easily, as a lottery prize; and once they have heard the outcome, they feel edified. And yet no temple robber laboring in chains and irons is so base a criminal as the one who plunders the holy in this way; and Judas himself, who sold his master for thirty pieces of silver, is no more despicable than the one who puts greatness up for sale like this.

**[23]** It goes against my soul to speak inhumanly about greatness — to let it loom dimly, in vague outline, at an enormous distance; to let it be great without bringing out what is human in it, and so it stops being great. What makes me great is not what befalls me but what I do; and surely no one thinks a man became great because he won the big prize in the lottery. Even if a person was born in humble circumstances, I still require of him that he not be so inhuman toward himself that he can think of the king's castle only from a distance, dreaming vaguely of its greatness, at once wanting to exalt it and to cancel it by exalting it in a servile way. I require of him that he be human enough to step forward there too, with confidence and dignity. He must not be so inhuman as to want to violate everything impudently by storming straight in from the street into the king's halls; by doing so he loses more than the king does. Rather, it should please him to keep every rule of good manners, with a happy, trusting eagerness that is exactly what will give him his freedom and boldness. This is only an image, for that difference in rank is a very imperfect expression of distance in the world of spirit. I require of every human being that he not think of himself so inhumanly as not to dare to go into those palaces where not only the memory of the chosen lives but where the chosen themselves live. He should not push forward impudently and force his kinship on them. He should be blessed every time he bows before them, but he should be bold and confident, and always something more than a charwoman — for if he refuses to be anything more, he will never get in. What will help him is exactly the anxiety and distress through which the great are put to the proof; for otherwise, if he has any backbone at all, the great would only arouse his justified envy. And whatever can be great only at a distance, whatever people try to make great with empty and hollow phrases — people themselves destroy it.

**[24]** Who in all the world was ever as great as that woman who found favor, the Mother of God, the Virgin Mary? Yet how do people talk about it? Being the favored one among women is not what makes her great. And if it did not so strangely happen that those who listen can think just as inhumanly as those who speak, then surely every young girl would ask: "Why was I not the favored one too?" And if I had nothing else to say, I would certainly not dismiss such a question as stupid; for when it comes to a favor, seen abstractly, every human being has an equal claim. What people leave out is the distress, the anxiety, the paradox. My thought is as pure as anyone's, and whoever can think such things will surely find his thought becoming pure; and if it does not, he has something terrible to expect. For whoever has once called up these images cannot get rid of them again. And if he sins against these images, they take revenge terribly, with a silent wrath more terrible than the loud clamor of ten ravening reviewers. Mary did bear the child miraculously, but it went with her as it goes with women, and that time is a time of anxiety, distress and paradox. The angel was a ministering spirit, certainly — but not an obliging spirit that went round to the other young girls in Israel saying, "Do not despise Mary; something extraordinary is happening to her." The angel came only to Mary, and no one could understand her. What woman was ever so wronged as Mary? And does it not hold here too that whom God blesses, he curses in the same breath? This is how the spirit understands Mary. And she is by no means — it outrages me to say it, but it outrages me even more that people have thoughtlessly and frivolously thought of her this way — she is by no means a fine lady sitting in state and playing with a divine child. When she nevertheless said, "Behold, I am the handmaid of the Lord," then she is great, and it should not be hard, I think, to explain why she became the Mother of God. She has no need of the world's admiration, just as Abraham has no need of tears; she was not a heroine, nor he a hero. Yet both became greater than heroes — not at all by being spared the distress, the torment and the paradox, but through them.

**[25]** There is greatness in a poet who sets his tragic hero before the public to be admired and ventures to say: "Weep for him, he is worthy of it." To be worthy of the tears of those who are worthy to weep is a great thing. There is greatness in the poet's daring to keep the crowd in its place and to take people to task, so that each one examines himself to see whether he is worthy to weep over the hero; for the waste water of blubberers degrades what is holy. — But greater than all this is that the knight of faith dares to say even to the noble person who wants to weep over him: "Do not weep for me, but weep for yourself."

**[26]** People are moved; they long to go back to those beautiful times; sweet, tender longings lead them to the goal of their wish — to see Christ walking about in the promised land. They forget the anxiety, the distress, the paradox. Was it such an easy matter not to be mistaken? Was it not terrifying that this human being walking among the others was God, terrifying to share a meal with him? Was becoming an apostle such a simple thing? But the outcome, eighteen centuries, helps — it helps with this shabby deception by which people deceive themselves and others. I do not feel courageous enough to wish I had been contemporary with such events; but for that reason I do not judge harshly those who were mistaken, nor think little of those who saw what was right.

**[27]** But I return to Abraham. In the time before the outcome, either Abraham was a murderer every minute, or we stand before the paradox that is higher than all mediations.

**[28]** The story of Abraham, then, contains a teleological suspension of the ethical. As the single individual, he has become higher than the universal. This is the paradox that cannot be mediated. It is just as inexplicable how he entered it as how he remains in it. If this is not how things stand with Abraham, then he is not even a tragic hero but a murderer. To go on calling him the father of faith, to talk about this to people who care about nothing but words, is thoughtless. A human being can become a tragic hero by his own strength, but not a knight of faith. When someone sets out on the tragic hero's road — hard, in a certain sense — many will be able to advise him; but the one who walks the narrow road of faith no one can advise, no one can understand. Faith is a miracle, and yet no human being is excluded from it; for what unites all human life is passion, and faith is a passion.
