# Problema III — Was it ethically defensible for Abraham to keep silent about his undertaking to Sarah, to Eliezer and to Isaac?

**[0]** The ethical as such is the universal, and, as the universal, it is in turn what is disclosed. Taken in his immediacy — as a being of senses and soul — the single individual is the concealed one. His ethical task, then, is to work himself free of his concealment — his state of being hidden — and to become disclosed in the universal. So every time he wants to remain in concealment, he sins and is in a spiritual trial, and he can come out of it only by disclosing himself.

**[1]** Here we stand once again at the same point. If there is no concealment that has its ground in this — that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal — then Abraham’s conduct cannot be defended; for he went over the heads of the intermediate ethical authorities. If, on the other hand, there is such a concealment, then we stand before the paradox, which cannot be mediated, since it rests precisely on the single individual’s being, as the single individual, higher than the universal — but the universal is precisely mediation. The Hegelian philosophy assumes no justified concealment, no justified incommensurability. It is therefore consistent with itself when it demands disclosure — that the individual bring what is hidden in him out into the open, and make himself known. But it is confused when it wants to regard Abraham as the father of faith and to talk about faith. For faith is not the first immediacy but a later one. The first immediacy is the aesthetic, and here the Hegelian philosophy may well be right. But faith is not the aesthetic — or else there has never been faith, because there has always been faith.

**[2]** The best course here will be to consider the whole matter purely aesthetically, and to that end to enter into an aesthetic deliberation. I would ask the reader to give himself over to it completely for the time being, while I, to do my part, will adapt my presentation to its subjects. The category I want to consider a little more closely is the interesting — what stands at a crisis, where things hang in the balance. It is a category that has gained great importance, especially in our time, precisely because the age lives in discrimine rerum — at a turning point in affairs; for it is really the category of the turning point. So one should not, as sometimes happens, first love it oneself pro virili — to the best of one’s powers — and then scorn it because it has grown too big for one. But neither should one be all too greedy for it. For this much is certain: to become interesting, or to have an interesting life, is not a task for artful industry but a fateful privilege, which can be bought only at the price of deep pain, as can every privilege in the world of spirit. Thus Socrates was the most interesting human being who has ever lived, and his life the most interesting ever led; but this existence was assigned to him by the god, and insofar as he had to acquire it himself, he was no stranger to toil and pain either. To want to take on such an existence out of vanity is unbecoming for anyone who thinks at all seriously about life; and yet in our time one not infrequently sees examples of such striving. The interesting, moreover, is a border category, a confinium — a borderland — between aesthetics and ethics. To that extent the deliberation must constantly stray over into the territory of ethics, while, if it is to have any significance, it must seize the problem with aesthetic inwardness and concupiscence. In our time ethics seldom engages in such things. The reason is said to be that there can be no room for them in the System. Well then, one could do it in monographs. And besides, if one does not want to be long-winded about it, one can do it briefly and still achieve the same thing — provided, that is, that one has command of the epithet; for one or two epithets can betray a whole world. Is there to be no room in the System for such little words either?

**[3]** Aristotle says in his immortal Poetics: δύο μὲν οὖν τοῦ μύθου μέρη, περὶ ταῦτ’ ἐστί, περιπέτεια καὶ ἀναγνώρισις — two parts of the plot, then, turn on these things: peripeteia, the reversal, and anagnorisis, the recognition (cf. chapter 11). Naturally it is only the second element that concerns me here: ἀναγνώρισις, recognition. Wherever there can be talk of a recognition, there is eo ipso — by that very fact — talk of a preceding concealment. Just as the recognition is what resolves, what releases the tension, so the concealment is what builds the suspense, the tension, in dramatic life. Earlier in the same chapter Aristotle develops the differing worth of tragedies according to how περιπέτεια and ἀναγνώρισις carom off each other, and discusses single and double recognition. I cannot take account of that here, however much it tempts by its inwardness and its quiet absorption — especially someone who has long enough been wearied by the superficial omniscience of the survey-writers. A more general remark must find its place here. In Greek tragedy, concealment (and, as a consequence, recognition) is an epic remnant. It has its ground in a fate: the dramatic action disappears into that fate, and that fate is also the dark, enigmatic source from which the action springs. That is why the effect a Greek tragedy produces resembles the impression a marble statue makes, lacking as it does the power of the eye. Greek tragedy is blind. That is why a certain abstraction is required in order to be properly affected by it. A son murders his father, but only afterward does he learn that it was his father. A sister is about to sacrifice her brother, but at the decisive moment she finds out. This kind of tragedy can have less hold on our reflective age. Modern drama has given up fate; it has emancipated itself dramatically; it is sighted; it gazes into itself and takes fate up into its dramatic consciousness. Concealment and disclosure are then the hero’s free act, for which he is responsible.

**[4]** In modern drama, too, recognition and concealment belong as an essential element. To cite examples of this would be too long-winded. I am polite enough to assume that anyone in our time who is so aesthetically voluptuous, so potent and so inflamed that he conceives as easily as the partridge, which, according to Aristotle, needs only to hear the cock’s voice or his flight over her head — I assume that anyone, merely on hearing the word “concealment,” will easily be able to shake ten or so novels and comedies out of his sleeve. I can therefore be brief and so straight away merely indicate a more general remark. If the one who plays hide-and-seek, and thereby brings dramatic ferment into the play, is hiding some nonsense, we get a comedy; if, however, he stands in relation to the idea, he can come close to being a tragic hero. Here is just one example of the comic. A man wears makeup and a wig. This same man would dearly like to make a conquest of the fair sex, and he is quite sure of victory with the help of the makeup and the wig, which render him unconditionally irresistible. He catches a girl and stands on the summit of happiness. Now comes the catch. Can he confess it? Will he not lose all his bewitching power when he shows himself as a plain, ordinary — indeed, even a bald — male person? Will he not thereby lose his beloved again? — His concealment is his free act, for which aesthetics, too, holds him responsible. This science is no friend of bald hypocrites; it hands him over to laughter. This must be enough merely to indicate what I mean; the comic cannot be the object of this investigation’s interest.

**[5]** The path I have to take is to carry concealment dialectically through aesthetics and ethics; for the point is that aesthetic concealment and the paradox should show themselves in their absolute difference.

**[6]** A couple of examples. A girl is secretly in love with someone, though the two of them have not definitively confessed their love to each other. Her parents compel her to marry another man (there may, besides, be a consideration of filial piety that decides her); she obeys her parents; she hides her love “so as not to make the other unhappy, and no one shall ever know what she suffers.” — A young man could, by a single word, come into possession of the object of his longings and his restless dreams. This little word, however, would compromise — indeed perhaps (who knows?) ruin — an entire family. He magnanimously resolves to remain in hiding: “The girl shall never know it, so that she may perhaps become happy at another man’s hand.” A pity that these two people, each of whom is hidden from his or her respective beloved, are also hidden from each other; otherwise a remarkable higher unity could have been brought about here. — Their concealment is a free act, for which they are responsible to aesthetics as well. Aesthetics, however, is a polite and sentimental science, which knows more ways out than any manager of the public pawnshop. So what does it do? It does everything possible for the lovers. By means of a coincidence, each of the two partners in the projected marriage gets a hint of the other’s magnanimous resolution; an explanation follows; they get each other, and along with that the rank of real heroes. For although they have not even had time to sleep on their heroic resolution, aesthetics nevertheless regards it as if they had bravely fought their purpose through for many years. Aesthetics, you see, does not care much about time; whether in jest or in earnest, time goes equally fast for it.

**[7]** But ethics knows nothing of that coincidence or of that sentimentality, nor does it have so quick a notion of time. This gives the matter a different look. Ethics is not good to argue with, since its categories are pure. It does not invoke experience — of all ridiculous things just about the most ridiculous — which, far from making a man wise, is more likely to make him mad if he knows nothing higher. Ethics has no coincidence, and so no explanation follows. It does not play games with ranks and honors. It lays an enormous responsibility on the frail heroes’ shoulders. It condemns as presumptuous the wish to play Providence by one’s deed — but it also condemns the wish to do so by one’s suffering. It bids one believe in actuality and have the courage to fight with all the tribulations of actuality, rather than with these bloodless sufferings one has taken upon oneself on one’s own responsibility. It warns against believing the clever calculations of the understanding, which are more faithless than the oracles of antiquity. It warns against every untimely magnanimity. Leave it to actuality: then it is time to show courage — but then ethics itself offers every possible assistance. If, however, it was something deeper that stirred in these two, if there was earnestness to see the task, earnestness to take it up, then something will no doubt come of them; but ethics cannot help them. It has taken offense; for they have a secret from it, a secret they have taken on at their own risk and on their own account.

**[8]** Aesthetics, then, demanded concealment and rewarded it; ethics demanded disclosure and punished concealment.

**[9]** Sometimes, however, aesthetics itself demands disclosure. When the hero, caught in the aesthetic illusion, thinks that by his silence he is saving another human being, aesthetics demands silence and rewards it. When, on the other hand, the hero by his deed intervenes disruptively in another human being’s life, it demands disclosure. Here I have arrived at the tragic hero. I want to consider Euripides’ Iphigenia in Aulis for a moment. Agamemnon is to sacrifice Iphigenia. Now, aesthetics demands silence of Agamemnon insofar as it would be unworthy of the hero to look for comfort from anyone else — just as he ought, out of consideration for the women, to hide it from them for as long as possible. On the other hand, precisely in order to be a hero, the hero must also be put to the proof in the terrible spiritual trial that the tears of Clytemnestra and Iphigenia will prepare for him. What does aesthetics do? It has a way out: it has an old servant ready, who discloses everything to Clytemnestra. Now everything is in order.

**[10]** Ethics, however, has no coincidence and no old servant at hand. The aesthetic idea contradicts itself as soon as it has to be carried out in actuality. Ethics therefore demands disclosure. The tragic hero shows his ethical courage precisely in this: that he, caught in no aesthetic illusion, is himself the one who announces her fate to Iphigenia. If he does this, then the tragic hero is the beloved son of ethics, in whom it is well pleased. Should he keep silent, that may be because he believes he makes it easier for others by doing so; but it may also be because he makes it easier for himself by doing so. But he knows that he is free of that motive. If he keeps silent, he takes on a responsibility as the single individual, insofar as he disregards an argument that may come from outside. This, as a tragic hero, he cannot do; for the very reason ethics loves him is that he always expresses the universal. His heroic deed demands courage, but it also belongs to this courage that he shirks no argument. Now it is certain that tears are a terrible argumentum ad hominem — an argument aimed at the man himself — and there has surely been someone whom nothing touched, yet whom tears moved. In the play Iphigenia is allowed to weep. In actuality she ought to be granted, like Jephthah’s daughter, two months to weep — not in solitude but at her father’s feet — and to use all the art she has, “which is only tears,” and to twine herself, instead of an olive branch, around his knees (cf. line 1224).

**[11]** Aesthetics demanded disclosure but made do with a coincidence; ethics demanded disclosure and found its satisfaction in the tragic hero.

**[12]** In spite of the strictness with which ethics demands disclosure, it cannot be denied that secrecy and silence are what really make a human being great, precisely because they are determinations of inwardness. When Amor leaves Psyche, he says to her: “You shall give birth to a child who will be divine if you keep silent, but a human being if you betray the secret.” The tragic hero, who is the favorite of ethics, is purely a human being; him I can indeed understand, and everything he undertakes also lies in what is disclosed, out in the open. If I go further, I keep coming up against the paradox — the divine and the demonic; for silence is both. Silence is the snare of the demon, and the more silence is kept, the more terrible the demon grows; but silence is also the deity’s bearing witness together with the single individual.

**[13]** Before I go on to the story of Abraham, however, I will call forth a couple of poetic figures. By the power of dialectic I keep them at the extreme point, and as I swing the scourge of despair over them, I will surely keep them from standing still, so that, if possible, in their anxiety they may discover one thing or another. There he stopped; in our time people go further, and explain more than they themselves have understood.]

**[14]** Aristotle, in his Politics, relates a story of political unrest in Delphi that arose from a question of marriage. The bridegroom, to whom the augurs foretell a misfortune that will spring from his marriage, abruptly alters his plan at the decisive moment, when he arrives to fetch the bride — he will not have the wedding. I need no more. In Delphi this event hardly passed off without tears; if a poet wanted to take it up, he could surely count on sympathy. Is it not terrible that love, which often enough has been an exile in life, is now robbed of heaven’s help as well? Is the old saying that marriages are made in heaven not put to shame here? At other times it is all the tribulations and difficulties of finitude that, like evil spirits, would separate the lovers; but love has heaven on its side, and that is why this holy alliance conquers all its enemies. Here it is heaven itself that separates what heaven itself, after all, has joined. And who would have suspected this? Least of all, surely, the young bride. Only a moment ago she was sitting in her room in all her beauty. The lovely girls had adorned her so conscientiously that they could answer for it before all the world, so that it gave them not only joy but envy — indeed, joy that they could not possibly grow more envious, because she could not possibly be more beautiful. Alone in her room she sat, transformed from beauty to beauty; for everything feminine art could do had been used to adorn worthily the one who was worthy. Yet something was still missing, something the young girls had not dreamed of: a veil finer, lighter and yet more concealing than the one in which the young girls had wrapped her; a bridal garment that no young girl knew about or could help her with — indeed, the bride herself did not have the understanding to help herself. It was an invisible power, a kindly power that takes its delight in adorning a bride, that wrapped her in it without her knowing anything about it; for she saw only how the bridegroom went past and went up to the temple. She saw the door close behind him, and she grew even calmer and more blessed, for she knew he was now hers more than ever. The temple door opened, he stepped out; but she lowered her eyes, as a maiden does, and so she did not see that his face was distraught. But he saw that heaven was surely envious of the bride’s loveliness and of his happiness. The temple door opened, and the young girls saw the bridegroom step out; but they did not see that his face was distraught, for they were busy fetching the bride. Then she came forth in all her maidenly humility, and yet like a sovereign lady, surrounded by her retinue of young girls, who curtsied to her as a young girl always curtsies to a bride. So she stood at the head of the lovely band and waited — it was only a moment, for the temple was right nearby — and the bridegroom came — but he went past her door. [** Note: According to Aristotle, the historical catastrophe was as follows: to take revenge, the family gets a temple vessel in among his household goods, and he is condemned as a temple robber. This, however, is of no importance; for the question is not whether the family is clever or stupid in taking its revenge. The family has ideal significance only insofar as it is drawn into the hero’s dialectic. Besides, it is fateful enough that, in trying to escape the danger by not marrying, he plunges into it, and that his life touches the divine in two ways: first through the augur’s pronouncement, then by being condemned as a temple robber.]

**[15]** But I break off here. I am no poet, and I go about things only dialectically. First, it should be noted that the hero receives this information at the decisive moment. He is therefore pure and has nothing to repent of; he has not bound himself frivolously to his beloved. Second, he has a divine pronouncement on his side — or rather against him — so he is not steered by his own cleverness, as those frail lovers, men and women alike, are. It further goes without saying that the pronouncement makes him just as unhappy as the bride — indeed a little more so, since he is, after all, the occasion of it. For it is true that the augurs foretold a misfortune only for him; but the question is whether this misfortune might not be such that, when it strikes him, it also strikes their happiness as husband and wife. What, then, is he to do? 1) Should he keep silent and hold the wedding, thinking: perhaps the misfortune will not come right away; at any rate I have stood up for love and have not been afraid of making myself unhappy — but I must keep silent, for otherwise even the short moment is thrown away? This seems plausible, but it is nothing of the kind; for in that case he has wronged the girl. By his silence he has in a way made the girl guilty; for had she known, she would presumably never have agreed to such a match. In the hour of distress, then, he will have to carry not merely the misfortune but also the responsibility for his having kept silent: her just anger that he kept silent. 2) Should he keep silent and refrain from holding the wedding? In that case he must enter into a mystification — a deliberate deception — by which he annihilates himself in his relation to her. Aesthetics might perhaps approve of this. The catastrophe could then be shaped like the actual one, except that at the very last moment there would be an explanation — one that came too late all the same, since, aesthetically considered, it becomes a necessity to let him die, unless this science should find itself able to cancel that ill-fated prophecy. Yet this conduct, however magnanimous it may be, contains an offense against the girl and the reality of her love. 3) Should he speak? One must not forget, of course, that our hero is a little too poetic for the renouncing of his love to mean no more to him than a failed business speculation. If he speaks, the whole thing becomes an unhappy love story in the same style as Axel and Valborg. The two become a pair that heaven itself parts. In the present case, though, this separation has to be thought of somewhat differently, since it also results from the free act of the individuals. For what is so very difficult about the dialectic of this case is that the misfortune is to strike only him. So they do not, like Axel and Valborg, get a common expression for their suffering, whereas heaven parts Axel and Valborg alike, since they stand equally near to each other. If that were the case here, a way out could be imagined. For since heaven uses no visible power to separate them but leaves it to the lovers themselves, it would surely be conceivable that they might resolve together to defy heaven, misfortune and all. [* Note: Incidentally, from this point one could set off in another direction of dialectical movements. Heaven foretells a misfortune for him from his marriage. So he could, of course, refrain from holding the wedding without therefore giving up the girl, and live with her in a romantic union that, for the lovers, would be more than reassuring. This, however, contains an offense against the girl, because he does not, in his love for her, express the universal. It would nevertheless be a task both for a poet and for an ethicist who wanted to defend marriage. In general, if poetry became attentive to the religious and to the inwardness of individuality, it would find far more significant tasks than those it busies itself with now. Over and over, after all, one hears this story in poetry: a man is tied to one girl he once loved — or perhaps never properly loved — because now he has seen another girl, who is the ideal. A man gets it wrong in life: the street was right, but it was the wrong house, for across the way, on the second floor, lives the ideal — this, people think, is a task for poetry. A lover has made a mistake: he saw his beloved by lamplight and thought she had dark hair, but look! on closer inspection it was fair — but her sister, she is the ideal. This, people think, is a task for poetry. My opinion is that every such man is a Laban (a boor) who may be unbearable enough in life but ought to be hissed off at once when he tries to make himself important in poetry. Only passion against passion makes a poetic collision, not this clatter of particularities within one and the same passion. If, for instance, a girl in the Middle Ages falls in love and then satisfies herself that earthly love is a sin, and prefers a heavenly love, then there is a poetic collision here, and the girl is poetic, because her life is in the idea.]

**[16]** Ethics, however, will require of him that he speak. His heroic courage must then essentially consist in giving up the aesthetic magnanimity — which in casu (in this case), it is true, could hardly be thought to contain any admixture of the vanity that lies in being concealed, since it must surely be clear to him that he is making the girl unhappy all the same. The reality of this heroic courage, however, depends on its having first had its presupposition and then cancelled it. For otherwise one could get heroes enough, especially in our time — our time, which has carried to an unrivalled virtuosity the forgery that achieves the highest by skipping over the middle term.

**[17]** But what is this sketch for, then, since I still get no further than the tragic hero? Because it was still possible that it might throw some light on the paradox. Everything depends, then, on how he stands in relation to the augurs’ pronouncement, which in one way or another becomes decisive for his life. Is this pronouncement publici juris — public property — or is it a privatissimum — a private lesson? The scene is Greece, and an augur’s pronouncement is intelligible to everyone. I do not mean only that the single individual can understand its content lexically; I mean that the single individual can understand that an augur is announcing heaven’s decision to the single individual. So the augur’s pronouncement is intelligible not only to the hero but to everyone, and no private relation to the deity results from it. He can do what he likes; what has been foretold will happen; and neither by acting nor by refraining does he come into a closer relation to the deity or become the object either of its grace or of its wrath. Any individual will be able to understand the outcome just as well as the hero, and there is no secret writing legible to the hero alone. Insofar as he wants to speak, then, he can do so perfectly well, for he can make himself understood. Insofar as he wants to keep silent, it is because, by virtue of being the single individual, he wants to be higher than the universal — wants to delude himself with all kinds of fantastic notions about how she will soon forget this business, and so on. If, on the other hand, heaven’s will had not been announced to him by an augur — if it had come to his knowledge entirely privately, if it had placed itself in an entirely private relation to him — then we are at the paradox, if it exists at all (for my deliberation proceeds by dilemma). In that case he could not speak, no matter how much he wanted to. He would then not be enjoying himself in his silence; he would be suffering the pain — but that pain would be precisely his assurance that he was justified. His silence would then not be grounded in his wanting, as the single individual, to place himself in an absolute relation to the universal, but in his having been placed, as the single individual, in an absolute relation to the absolute. He would then also be able to find rest in it, as far as I can imagine, whereas his magnanimous silence would be constantly troubled by the demands of the ethical. It is altogether to be wished that aesthetics would for once try to begin where for so many years it has ended: at illusory magnanimity. The moment it did that, it would be playing straight into the hands of the religious; for this power is the only one that can rescue the aesthetic from its struggle with the ethical. Queen Elizabeth sacrifices her love for Essex to the state by signing his death warrant. This was a heroic deed, even if a little personal offense was in play too — that he had not sent her the ring. As is well known, he had in fact sent it, but it had been held back through the malice of a lady at court. Elizabeth, so the story goes — ni fallor (unless I am mistaken) — received word of this; then she sat for ten days with one finger in her mouth, biting it without saying a single word, and then she died. That would be a task for a poet who knew how to wrench the mouth open; otherwise it is at most of use to a ballet master, with whom the poet in our time often enough confuses himself.

**[18]** Now I will follow this with a sketch in the direction of the demonic. For this I can use the legend of Agnete and the merman. The merman is a seducer who shoots up from his hiding place in the abyss and, in wild lust, seizes and breaks the innocent flower that stood in all her grace on the shore, pensively bowing her head to the murmur of the sea. This is how the poets have seen it up to now. Let us make a change. The merman was a seducer. He has called to Agnete; with his smooth talk he has coaxed out what was hidden in her; in the merman she has found what she was seeking, what she had been staring down at the bottom of the sea to find. Agnete wants to go with him. The merman has lifted her onto his arm; Agnete twines herself about his neck; trustingly, with her whole soul, she gives herself to the stronger one. He is already standing at the water’s edge; he is leaning out over the sea to plunge down with his prey — then Agnete looks at him once more: not fearfully, not doubtingly, not proud of her happiness, not drunk with desire, but in absolute faith, but in absolute humility, like the lowly flower she thought herself to be; with this look, in absolute trust, she confides her whole destiny to him. — And look! The sea no longer roars; its wild voice falls silent; the passion of nature, which is the merman’s strength, deserts him; the sea falls dead calm — and still Agnete looks at him in this way. Then the merman collapses. He cannot resist the power of innocence; his own element proves faithless to him; he cannot seduce Agnete. He leads her home again and explains to her that all he wanted was to show her how beautiful the sea was when calm; and Agnete believes him. — Then he goes back alone, and the sea rages, but the despair in the merman rages more wildly. He can seduce Agnete, he can seduce a hundred Agnetes, he can bewitch any girl — but Agnete has triumphed, and the merman has lost her. She can become his only as prey; faithfully he can belong to no girl, since he is, after all, only a merman. I have allowed myself a small change in the merman; basically I have changed Agnete a little too. For in the legend Agnete is not altogether without guilt — just as, in general, it is nonsense and gallantry and an offense against womankind to conceive of a seduction where the girl has no, no, no guilt at all. In the legend Agnete is — to put it in slightly more modern terms — a woman who demands the interesting, and any woman of that kind can always count on a merman being close by; for mermen spot that sort of thing with half an eye and make for it as the shark makes for its prey. It is therefore very stupid — or else it is a rumor that a merman has had put about — to say that so-called cultivation protects a girl against seduction. No, existence is more just and more even-handed than that: there is only one means, and that is innocence. [* Note: That legend could also be treated in another way. The merman does not want to seduce Agnete, even though he has seduced many before. He is no longer a merman — or, if you like, he is a poor wretch of a merman who has long been sitting on the bottom of the sea, grieving. Yet he knows, as the legend does teach, that an innocent girl’s love can save him. But his conscience is bad where girls are concerned, and he does not dare approach them. Then he sees Agnete. Many times already, hidden in the reeds, he has watched her walking along the shore. Her beauty, her quiet absorption in herself, hold him captive to her; but all is melancholy in his soul, and no wild lust stirs in it. And when the merman mingles his sigh with the whispering of the reeds, she turns her ear that way, stands still and sinks into dreams — lovelier than any woman, and yet beautiful as a saving angel who inspires trust in the merman. The merman takes courage; he approaches Agnete, he wins her love, he hopes for his salvation. But Agnete was no quiet girl. She was very fond of the roaring of the sea, and the melancholy sigh by the water pleased her only because it made the roaring within her the stronger. She wants to be off, off — to storm wildly out into the infinite with the merman she loves — then she inflames the merman. She scorned his humility; now pride awakes. And the sea roars, and the wave froths, and the merman clasps Agnete in his arms and hurls himself into the abyss with her. He had never been so wild, never so full of craving; for it was through this girl that he had hoped to be saved. He soon grew tired of Agnete; yet her body was never found, for she became a mermaid who tempted men with her songs.]

**[19]** We will now endow the merman with a human consciousness, and let the fact that he is a merman stand for a previous human existence, in whose consequences his life was entangled. Nothing prevents him from becoming a hero; for the step he now takes is a reconciling one. He is saved by Agnete; the seducer is crushed; he has bowed before the power of innocence; he can never seduce again. But in the same instant two powers contend for him: repentance; and Agnete and repentance. If repentance alone takes him, he is concealed; if Agnete and repentance take him, he is disclosed.

**[20]** Now, insofar as repentance seizes the merman and he remains concealed, he has certainly made Agnete unhappy. For Agnete loved him in all her innocence. She believed it was the truth that at that moment — when he seemed changed even to her, however well he hid it — he only wanted to show her the beautiful stillness of the sea. Yet the merman himself becomes, as regards passion, still more unhappy; for he loved Agnete with a great many passions, and he had, besides, a new guilt to bear. The demonic in repentance will now no doubt explain to him that just this is his punishment — and the more it tortures him, the better.

**[21]** If he gives himself over to this demonic element, he perhaps makes one more attempt to save Agnete, in the way that one can, in a certain sense, save a person with the help of evil. He knows that Agnete loves him. If he could wrest this love from Agnete, she would in a way be saved. But how? The merman is too sensible to count on an open-hearted confession arousing her disgust. So perhaps he will try to stir up all the dark passions in her — to scorn her, mock her, make her love ridiculous and, if possible, goad her pride. He will spare himself no torment; for this is the profound contradiction in the demonic — and in a certain sense a demonic person has infinitely more good dwelling in him than trivial people have. The more selfish Agnete is, the easier it will be to deceive her (for only very inexperienced people think that innocence is easy to deceive; existence is exceedingly profound, and the easiest thing for the clever is to fool the clever) — but the more terrible the merman’s sufferings will be. The more cunningly his deception is laid out, the less will Agnete’s modesty lead her to hide her suffering from him. She will use every means, and they will not be without effect either — not, that is, in shaking him, but in torturing him.

**[22]** By means of the demonic, then, the merman would be the single individual who as the single individual was higher than the universal. The demonic has the same property as the divine: the single individual can come into an absolute relation to it. Here we have the analogy, the counterpart, of that paradox we are discussing. It therefore has a certain likeness, and the likeness can deceive. Thus the merman apparently has this proof that his silence is justified: in it he suffers all his pain. Nevertheless there is no doubt that he can speak. So he can become a tragic hero — in my view a grandiose tragic hero — if he speaks. Only a few, perhaps, would comprehend where the grandeur lies. Then he will have the courage to wrest from himself every self-deception that he can make Agnete happy by his art, and the courage — in human terms — to crush Agnete. Incidentally, I will just make a psychological observation here. The more selfish Agnete’s development has been, the more dazzling the merman’s self-deception will be. Indeed, it is not unthinkable that it could happen in reality that a merman, by his demonic cunning, had — humanly speaking — not only saved an Agnete but drawn something extraordinary out of her. A demon, after all, knows how to wring strength out of even the weakest human being, and in his own way he can mean very well by a person. [* Note: Aesthetics sometimes treats something similar with its usual flirtatious trifling. The merman is saved by Agnete, and it all ends in a happy marriage. A happy marriage! Easy enough. Let ethics give the address at the wedding ceremony, though, and it will be another matter, I think. Aesthetics throws the cloak of love over the merman, and then everything is forgotten. It is also careless enough to think that a marriage goes the way an auction does, where everything is sold in the condition in which it is found when the hammer falls. It only makes sure that the lovers get each other; the rest it does not care about. It ought just to see what happens afterward; but it has no time for that — it is at once in full swing clapping a new pair of lovers together. Aesthetics is the most faithless of all the sciences. Everyone who has truly loved it becomes, in a certain sense, unhappy; but whoever has never loved it is and remains a pecus — a dull beast.]

**[23]** The merman stands at a dialectical extreme point. If he is saved out of the demonic in repentance, then two ways are possible. He can hold back and remain in concealment, but not rely on his cunning. In that case, as the single individual, he does not enter into an absolute relation to the demonic, but finds rest in the counter-paradox that the deity will save Agnete. (This is presumably how the Middle Ages would make the movement; for by its way of thinking the merman plainly falls by right to the monastery.) Or he can be saved by Agnete. This is not to be taken to mean that Agnete’s love would save him from turning seducer in the future (this is an aesthetic attempt at rescue, which always sidesteps the main point: the continuity of the merman’s life); for on that score he has been saved. He is saved insofar as he becomes disclosed. He then marries Agnete. Even so, he must have recourse to the paradox. For once his guilt has put the single individual outside the universal, the only way back into it is by virtue of his having entered, as the single individual, into an absolute relation to the absolute. Here I will now make a remark in which I say more than has been said anywhere in what has gone before. Sin is not the first immediacy; sin is a later immediacy. Already in sin, in the direction of the demonic paradox, the single individual is higher than the universal, because it is a contradiction on the universal’s part to want to demand itself of one who lacks the conditio sine qua non — the indispensable condition. If philosophy, among other things, also stopped to consider that a human being might take it into his head to want to act on its teaching, a strange comedy would come of it. An ethics that ignores sin is an altogether idle science; but if it makes sin count, then it is eo ipso — by that very fact — beyond itself. Philosophy teaches that the immediate is to be cancelled. This is true enough. What is not true, however, is that sin is straightforwardly the immediate — any more than faith is. [* Note: In the foregoing I have deliberately kept out any consideration of the question of sin and its reality. The whole is directed at Abraham, and him I can still reach in immediate categories — that is, to the extent that I can understand him. The moment sin appears, ethics perishes, and it perishes precisely on repentance. For repentance is the highest ethical expression, but precisely as such it is the deepest ethical self-contradiction.]

**[24]** As soon as I move in these spheres, everything goes easily. But what has been said here does not explain Abraham either. For Abraham did not become the single individual through sin; on the contrary, he was the righteous man, God’s chosen one. Only once the single individual has been put in a position to carry out the universal will the analogy to Abraham show itself — and now the paradox repeats itself.

**[25]** I can therefore understand the merman’s movements, but I cannot understand Abraham. For the merman, precisely by way of the paradox, comes to want to realize the universal. For if he remains concealed and dedicates himself to all the torments of repentance, then he becomes a demon, and as such he is annihilated. If he remains concealed, but does not cleverly suppose that by being tortured himself in the bondage of repentance he can work Agnete free, then he does indeed find peace, but he is lost to this world. If he becomes disclosed and lets himself be saved by Agnete, then he is the greatest human being I can conceive of. For it is only aesthetics that lightly imagines it glorifies love’s power by letting the lost one be saved through being loved by an innocent girl; only aesthetics sees wrongly and believes that the girl is the heroine, when it is the merman who is the hero. So the merman cannot belong to Agnete unless, after having made the infinite movement of repentance, he makes one movement more: the movement by virtue of the absurd. The movement of repentance he can make on his own strength, but it also takes absolutely all the strength he has; so it is impossible for him, on his own strength, to come back again and grasp actuality. When one does not have passion enough to make either the one movement or the other, when one muddles sloppily through life, repents a little and thinks the rest will surely take care of itself, then one has renounced, once and for all, living in the idea. And then one can very easily attain the highest, and help others to it too — that is: beguile oneself and others with the notion that in the world of spirit things go as in a game of gnav, a parlor game of luck, where everything goes along any old how. One can then amuse oneself by considering how strange it is, after all, that precisely in an age when, as we know, everyone can do the highest, doubt about the immortality of the soul can be so widespread; for whoever has really made even the movement of infinity hardly doubts. The inferences passion draws are the only reliable ones — that is, the only convincing ones. Happily, existence here is more loving, more faithful, than the wise men claim; for it excludes no human being, not even the lowliest, and it fools no one — for in the world of spirit only the one who fools himself is fooled. It is everyone’s opinion, and insofar as I dare presume to judge the matter it is my opinion too, that entering a monastery is not the highest. But it is by no means my opinion, for that reason, that in our age, when no one enters a monastery, everyone is greater than those deep and earnest souls who found their rest in a monastery. How many in our age have passion enough to think this, and then to judge themselves honestly? Consider merely the thought of answering in conscience for one’s time in this way: of giving conscience time to search out every secret thought in its sleepless tirelessness. Then, unless one makes the movement at every moment by virtue of what is noblest and holiest in a human being, one may discover with anxiety and horror the dark stirring that after all hides in every human life — and, if by nothing else, then by anxiety lure it out. When one lives in company with others, by contrast, one so easily forgets, so easily gets away from it; one is propped up in so many ways and gets the chance to begin afresh. This thought alone, conceived with due reverence, would, I should think, chasten many an individual in our age who already thinks he has arrived at the highest. But people care little about that in our age, which has arrived at the highest — even though no age has fallen so completely to the comic as this very one. And it is past understanding that the age has not already, by a generatio æquivoca — a spontaneous generation — given birth to its own hero: the demon who, sparing nothing, will stage the terrible play of setting the whole age laughing and bringing it to forget that it is laughing at itself. Or what is existence worth except to be laughed at, when one has already arrived at the highest in one’s twentieth year? And yet what higher movement has the age discovered since the time when people gave up entering the monastery? Is it not a pitiful worldly wisdom, prudence, faintheartedness, that sits in the place of honor — that cravenly makes people imagine they have done the highest, and slyly keeps them from even attempting the lesser? Whoever has made the monastery movement has only one movement left: that of the absurd. In our age, how many understand what the absurd is? In our age, how many live so that they have renounced everything or have gotten everything? How many are merely honest enough to know what they can and cannot do? And is it not true that, insofar as one finds such people, one is most likely to find them among the less educated, and in part among women? In a kind of clairvoyance the age discloses its failing, just as someone demonic always discloses himself without understanding himself; for again and again it demands the comic. Should that really be what the age needed? Should the theater perhaps need a new play in which it was made ridiculous that someone died of love? Or would it not rather be saving for the age if such a thing happened among us, if the age were witness to such an event — so that for once it might find the courage to believe in the power of spirit, the courage to stop cravenly stifling what is better in itself and enviously stifling it in others — by laughter? Should the age really need a ridiculous Erscheinung — apparition — of someone awakened, in order to get something to laugh at? Or should it not rather need such an inspired figure to remind it of what has been forgotten? [* Note: This is not believed in our earnest age; and yet it is remarkable enough that even in paganism — which is by its nature more frivolous and less thoroughly reflected — the two true representatives of the Greek view of existence — γνῶθι σαυτόν, “know yourself” — have each in his own way intimated that by going deep into oneself one discovers first and foremost the disposition to evil. That I am thinking of Pythagoras and Socrates I surely need not say.]

**[26]** Should anyone want a design in a similar vein that would nevertheless be more moving, since the passion of repentance is not set in movement in it, a story that occurs in the Book of Tobit would serve. The young Tobias wants to marry Sarah, the daughter of Raguel and Edna. There is a sad circumstance attached to this girl. She has been given to seven men, all of whom lost their lives in the bridal chamber. With regard to my design, this is a blemish on the story; for a comic effect is almost unavoidable when the thought is aroused of a girl’s seven fruitless attempts to get married, although she came very close to it — as close as a student who went up seven times for his degree examination and was failed. In the Book of Tobit the accent lies elsewhere, and therefore the high number matters there and is even, in a certain sense, a tragic contributing factor; for the young Tobias’s magnanimity is all the greater — partly insofar as he is his parents’ only son (6:15), partly because what might deter him presses itself on him all the more. So this must go. Sarah, then, is a girl who has never loved, who still keeps a young girl’s blessedness, her enormous prior claim on existence, her “Vollmachtbrief zum Glücke” — her letter of authority for happiness: to love a man with her whole heart. And yet no one is unhappier than she is, for she knows that on the wedding night the evil demon who loves her will kill the bridegroom. I have read about much sorrow, but I doubt that anywhere there is a sorrow as deep as the one that lies in this girl’s life. When unhappiness comes from outside, after all, there is still comfort to be found. If existence did not bring a person what could have made him happy, it is still a comfort that he could have received it. But the unfathomable sorrow that no time can dispel, no time can heal: to know that it would do no good if existence did everything! A Greek writer hides so infinitely much in his simple naivety when he says: πάντως γὰρ οὐδεὶς Ἔρωτα ἔφυγεν ἢ φεύξεται, μέχρι ἂν κάλλος ᾖ καὶ ὀφθαλμοὶ βλέπωσιν — for assuredly no one has ever escaped Eros, or ever will, as long as there is beauty and eyes can see (cf. Longus, Pastoralia). Many a girl grew unhappy in love, but at least she grew so; Sarah was so before she grew so. It is hard not to find someone to give oneself to, but it is unspeakably hard to be unable to give oneself. When a young girl gives herself, people say: Now she is not free any more. But Sarah was never free, yet she had never given herself. It is hard for a girl to give herself and be deceived; but Sarah had been deceived before she ever gave herself. Is there not a world of sorrow in what follows, when Tobias at last means to marry her? What wedding ceremonies, what preparations! No girl has been cheated as Sarah was; for she was cheated of the most blessed thing of all, the absolute wealth that even the poorest girl possesses — cheated of the secure, boundless, unbound, unbridled self-abandon of giving herself. For after all, first there had to be the fumigation, with the heart and liver of the fish laid on glowing embers. And how the mother must take leave of her daughter — the daughter who, just as she herself is cheated of everything, must, in continuity with this, cheat her mother of what is most beautiful! Just read the story. Edna prepared the chamber, and she led Sarah into it, and wept, and she accepted her daughter’s tears. And she said to her: Take courage, my child! May the Lord of heaven and earth give you joy for this sorrow of yours! Daughter, take courage. And now the moment of the wedding — read it, if you can for tears: But when the two of them were shut in together, Tobias rose from the bed and said: Get up, sister, and let us pray that the Lord may have mercy on us (8:4).

**[27]** If a poet read this story, if he wanted to use it, I would bet a hundred to one that he would stake everything on the young Tobias. This heroism of being willing to risk his life in so evident a danger — a danger the story reminds us of once more, for Raguel, the morning after the wedding, tells Edna: Send one of the maidservants to look and see whether he is alive; but if he is not, so that I may bury him and no one shall know of it (cf. 8:13) — this heroism would become the task. I take the liberty of proposing another. It is brave, stout-hearted and chivalrous of Tobias to act as he does; but any man who does not have the courage for it is a milksop who knows neither what love is, nor what it is to be a man, nor what is worth the trouble of living for. The little mystery — that giving is better than receiving — he has not even understood, and of the great one he has no notion: that receiving is far harder than giving; that is, once one has had the courage to go without and has not turned coward in the hour of distress. No, Sarah — she is the heroine. Her I want to approach as I have never approached any girl, nor felt tempted to approach in thought any girl I have read about. For what love of God is needed, to be willing to let oneself be healed when one has been bungled from the outset, guiltlessly — a botched specimen of humanity from the outset! What ethical maturity, to take upon oneself the responsibility of allowing the beloved such a daring venture! What humility before another human being! What faith in God, that she would not hate, the very next moment, the man to whom she owed everything!

**[28]** Let Sarah be a man, and the demonic lies close at hand. The proud and noble nature can endure anything; but there is one thing it cannot endure — it cannot endure pity. There lies in pity an injury that can be done to him only by a higher power; for through his own doing he can never become its object. If he has sinned, he can bear his punishment and not despair; but to be marked out, guiltless, from his mother’s womb as a victim offered to pity, a sweet smell in its nose — that is beyond his endurance. Pity has a strange dialectic: one moment it demands guilt, the next it will not have it. So to be predestined to pity grows ever more terrible, the more the individual’s misfortune tends toward the spiritual. But Sarah is without guilt. She is cast out as a prey to every suffering, and on top of that she is to be tortured by people’s pity; for not even I, who admire her more than Tobias loved her — not even I can utter her name without saying: the poor girl. Let a man be in Sarah’s place; let him know that if he wants to love a girl, a spirit from hell will come and murder the beloved on the wedding night. Then it might well be that he chose the demonic. He shut himself up within himself and said, in the way a demonic nature speaks in secret: “No, thank you — I am no friend of ceremonies and fuss; I do not in the least demand the pleasure of love; after all, I can become a Bluebeard who takes his delight in seeing the girls sink down on the wedding night.” As a rule one gets to know very little about the demonic, even though precisely in our age this territory has a valid claim to be explored, and even though the observer, provided he knows how to put himself a little in rapport with the demon, can make use of almost any human being, at least for a moment. In this respect Shakespeare is and always remains a hero. That dreadful demon, the most demonic figure Shakespeare has portrayed — but also portrayed incomparably — Gloucester (later Richard III): what made him a demon? Obviously this: that he could not bear the pity to which he had been exposed from childhood. His monologue in Act I of Richard III is worth more than whole systems of morality that have no inkling of the horrors of existence or of their explanation.

**[29]** … Ich, roh geprägt, und aller Reize baar.
Vor leicht sich dreh'nden Nymphen mich zu brüsten;
Ich, so verkürzt um schönes Ebenmass,
Geschändet von der tückischen Natur,
Entstellt, verwahrlost, vor der Zeit gesandt
In diese Welt des Athmens, halb kaum fertig
Gemacht, und zwar so lahm und ungeziemend,
Dass Hunde bellen, hink' ich wo vorbei.
[… I, that am rudely stamp’d, and want love’s majesty / To strut before a wanton ambling nymph; / I, that am curtail’d of this fair proportion, / Cheated of feature by dissembling nature, / Deform’d, unfinish’d, sent before my time / Into this breathing world, scarce half made up, / And that so lamely and unfashionable / That dogs bark at me as I halt by them.]

**[30]** Natures such as Gloucester’s cannot be saved by being mediated into an idea of society. Ethics really only makes fools of them — much as ethics would be mocking Sarah if it asked her: “Why don’t you express the universal and marry?” Such natures are in the paradox from the very ground up. They are by no means less perfect than other people; only, they are either lost in the demonic paradox or saved in the divine one. Now, time and again people have taken pleasure in the fact that witches, goblins, trolls and so on were misshapen creatures; and it is undeniable that every human being has an inclination, on seeing a misshapen creature, at once to attach to it the notion of a moral depravity. What a monstrous injustice! For the relation must rather be turned around: existence itself has corrupted them, the way a stepmother makes children turn out badly. To be placed outside the universal from the start, by nature or by historical circumstance — that is the beginning of the demonic, for which the individual himself, however, bears no guilt. In this way Cumberland’s Jew, too, is a demon, even though he does good. In this way the demonic can also show itself as contempt for people — a contempt, note carefully, that does not lead the demonic person himself to act contemptibly. On the contrary, his strength lies in knowing that he is better than all those who judge him. — About all such things the poet, of all people, ought to be the first to raise the alarm. God knows what books the younger versifiers now living read! Their study probably consists in learning rhymes by heart. God knows what their significance in existence is! At this moment I do not know that they are of any use except that they furnish an edifying proof of the immortality of the soul: one can confidently say of them to oneself what Baggesen says of the town poet Kildevalle — “if he becomes immortal, then we all will.” — What has been said here on the occasion of Sarah was said chiefly with a view to poetic production, and therefore on a fanciful presupposition. But it has its full significance if one wanted, with psychological interest, to go deeply into the meaning of the old saying: nullum unquam exstitit magnum ingenium sine aliqua dementia — there has never been a great genius without some touch of madness. For this dementia is the genius’s suffering in existence. It is the expression of — if I dare say so — the divine envy, while the genius in him is the expression of the divine partiality. So from the beginning the genius is disoriented in relation to the universal and brought into relation with the paradox. This holds whether, in despair over his limit — which in his eyes turns his omnipotence into impotence — he seeks a demonic reassurance, and therefore will not admit this, either before God or before people, or whether he reassures himself religiously in love for the deity. Here lie psychological tasks to which, it seems to me, one could gladly sacrifice a whole life; and yet a word about them is so rarely heard. What relation does madness bear to genius? Can the one be constructed out of the other? In what sense, and to what extent, is the genius in command of his madness? For it is obvious that up to a point he is in command of it — otherwise he really would be mad. For such observations, however, ingenuity is required in a high degree, and love as well; for to observe someone superior is very difficult. If one were to read through some of the most brilliant authors with attention to this, it might perhaps be possible, once in a great while and with much effort, to discover a little.

**[31]** I will imagine one more case: an individual who wants to save the universal by being concealed and by his silence. I can use the legend of Faust for this. Faust is a doubter, an apostate of the spirit who goes the way of the flesh. That is the poets’ view of him. And while it is said again and again that every age has its Faust, the age’s poets nonetheless go, one after another, undaunted down the same beaten track. Let us make a small change. Faust is the doubter κατ’ ἐξοχήν — the doubter above all others; but he is a sympathetic nature. Even in Goethe’s conception of Faust I miss a deeper psychological insight into doubt’s secret conversations with itself. In our time, when after all everyone has experienced doubt, no poet has yet taken a single step in that direction. So I think I could well offer them royal bonds to write on, so that they could write down all the many things they have experienced in this respect — they would hardly fill more than the margin on the front. [* Note: If one does not want to use a doubter, one could choose a similar figure — an ironist, for example, whose sharp eye has seen to the bottom of the ridiculousness of existence, and whom a secret understanding with the forces of life assures of what the patient wishes. He knows that he has the power of laughter if he wants to use it; he is sure of his victory — indeed, what is more, of his good fortune. He knows that a single voice will be raised to hold him back, but he knows that he is stronger. He knows that one can still, for a moment, get the men to look serious, but he also knows that in secret they long to laugh with him. He knows that one can still, for a moment, get the woman to hold her fan before her eyes when he speaks; but he knows she is laughing behind the fan; he knows the fan is not absolutely opaque; he knows one can write invisible writing on it; he knows that whenever a woman strikes at him with her fan, that is because she has understood him. He knows unerringly how laughter steals into a person and lives there hidden, and how, once it has taken up lodging, it sits lying in wait. Let us picture such an Aristophanes, such a Voltaire, slightly altered: for he is also a sympathetic nature. He loves existence, he loves people, and he knows that even if laughter’s condemnation may perhaps bring up a young generation that is saved, a great many people in the present generation will go under. So he keeps silent, and as far as possible forgets to laugh himself. But dare he keep silent? Perhaps there are quite a few who do not understand at all the difficulty I am speaking of. They probably think it was an admirable magnanimity to keep silent. That is not at all my view. For I think that any such nature, if he has not had the magnanimity to keep silent, is a traitor to existence. So I demand this magnanimity of him; but when he has it, dare he then keep silent? Ethics is a dangerous science, and it may well be that Aristophanes, determined on purely ethical grounds, resolved to let laughter pass judgment on the age that had gone astray. Aesthetic magnanimity cannot help here; for one does not venture such things on the strength of that. If he is to keep silent, he must go into the paradox. — I will hint at one more design: that a person, for example, possesses an explanation of a hero’s life that explains it in a lamentable way, and yet a whole contemporary age rests absolutely secure in this hero without suspecting anything of the kind.]

**[32]** Only when Faust is bent back into himself in this way — only then can doubt look poetic; only then, too, does he himself actually discover all its sufferings in actuality. He knows, then, that it is spirit that sustains existence. But he also knows that the security and joy in which people live are not grounded in the power of spirit, but are easily explained as an unreflective bliss. As a doubter — as the doubter — he is higher than all of this. And if anyone tries to deceive him by claiming to have gone through doubt himself, Faust easily sees through it. For whoever has made a movement in the world of spirit — that is, an infinite movement — can tell at once, just by listening to what someone says, whether the one speaking is a tried man or a Münchhausen. What a Tamerlane can do with his Huns, Faust knows he can do with his doubt: startle people up in terror, make existence sway under their feet, split people apart, make the cry of anxiety sound everywhere. And if he does it, he is still no Tamerlane; in a certain sense he is authorized — he has thought’s authorization. But Faust is a sympathetic nature. He loves existence; his soul knows no envy; he sees that he cannot stop the frenzy he may well be able to arouse; he craves no Herostratic fame. He keeps silent. He hides the doubt in his soul more carefully than a girl hides the fruit of a sinful love beneath her heart. He tries as well as he can to walk in step with other people; but what goes on inside him he consumes within himself, and in this way he offers himself as a sacrifice for the universal.

**[33]** Now and then one can hear people complain, when some eccentric head stirs up a whirlwind of doubt; then they say: “If only he had kept silent!” Faust makes this idea real. Whoever has a notion of what it means for a human being to live on spirit also knows what doubt’s hunger is, and that the doubter is as hungry for life’s daily bread as for the nourishment of spirit. Now, all the pain Faust suffers may be a pretty good argument that it is not pride that has possessed him. Even so, I will use a little precaution, which I can easily invent. For just as Gregory of Rimini was called tortor infantium — torturer of infants — because he held that little children are damned, so I could be tempted to call myself tortor heroum — torturer of heroes; for I am most inventive where tormenting heroes is concerned. Faust sees Margaret — not after he has chosen pleasure, for my Faust does not choose pleasure at all. He does not see Margaret in Mephistopheles’s concave mirror; he sees her in all her lovable innocence. And since his soul has kept its love for people, he is also quite capable of falling in love with her. But he is a doubter; his doubt has annihilated actuality for him. For my Faust is so ideal that he does not belong among those scholarly doubters who doubt for one hour each semester from the lectern but otherwise can do everything else — just as they do the doubting too — without being in a state of spirit or acting by virtue of spirit. He is a doubter, and the doubter is as hungry for joy’s daily bread as for the food of spirit. Still, he stays true to his resolve and keeps silent: he speaks to no one about his doubt, and to Margaret not about his love either.

**[34]** Naturally, Faust is too ideal a figure to rest content with the chatter that if he spoke he would set off a general discussion, or the whole thing would pass off without consequences, or perhaps, or perhaps. (Here, as any poet will easily see, the comic slumbers in the design: Faust could be brought into an ironic relation to those low-comic clowns who in our time run after doubt — who produce an outward argument that they really have doubted, a doctor’s certificate, for example; or swear they have doubted everything; or prove it by having met a doubter on their travels. These express messengers and sprinters in the world of spirit pick up, in the greatest haste, a little whiff of doubt from one person and of faith from another, and then wirthschaften — do their business — as best they can, according to whether the congregation wants fine sand or coarse sand.) Faust is too ideal a figure to shuffle around in slippers. Whoever does not have an infinite passion is not ideal; and whoever has an infinite passion has long since saved his soul out of such drivel. He keeps silent in order to sacrifice himself — or he speaks, conscious that he will throw everything into confusion.

**[35]** If he keeps silent, ethics condemns him. For it says: “You shall acknowledge the universal, and it is precisely by speaking that you acknowledge it; and you may not have pity on the universal.” This consideration ought not to be forgotten when one sometimes judges a doubter harshly because he speaks. I am not inclined to judge such conduct leniently; but here, as everywhere, what matters is that the movements happen as the norm requires. If it comes to the worst, a doubter — even if by speaking he brought every possible misfortune on the world — is still far preferable to these miserable sweet-tooths who taste a little of everything, who want to cure doubt without knowing it, and who are therefore, as a rule, the immediate occasion for doubt’s breaking out wildly and uncontrollably. — If he speaks, he throws everything into confusion. For only afterward does he learn whether the confusion comes about or not; and the outcome cannot help a person — neither in the moment of action nor as regards the responsibility.

**[36]** If he keeps silent on his own responsibility, he may well act magnanimously; but on top of his other pain he will feel a small spiritual trial. For the universal will keep tormenting him, saying: “You ought to have spoken. How will you ever be certain that it was not a hidden arrogance, after all, that guided your decision?”

**[37]** If, on the other hand, the doubter can become the single individual who as the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute, then his silence can receive an authorization. In that case he must turn his doubt into guilt. In that case he is in the paradox; but in that case his doubt is healed, even though he may come to have a different doubt.

**[38]** Such a silence would be acknowledged even by the New Testament. Indeed, the New Testament even contains passages that commend irony, provided only that it is used to hide what is better. This movement, however, belongs to irony just as much as to any other movement whose ground is that subjectivity is higher than actuality. Our age wants to know nothing of this. In general it wants to know no more about irony than what Hegel has said — and he, strangely enough, did not understand much about it and bore a grudge against it, a grudge our age has good reasons not to give up, for it had better simply beware of irony. The Sermon on the Mount says: “When you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, so that people will not see that you are fasting.” This passage testifies outright that subjectivity is incommensurable with actuality — indeed, that it is permitted to deceive. If only the people who in our time flit about with loose talk of the idea of the congregation would read the New Testament, they might perhaps come to think otherwise.

———

**[39]** But now Abraham — how did he act? For I have not forgotten, and the reader may now perhaps be good enough to remember, that it was in order to run up against this point that I entered into the whole preceding investigation. It was not so that Abraham would thereby become more understandable, but so that his incomprehensibility might become more disjointed. For, as I have said, Abraham I cannot understand; him I can only admire. It was also remarked that none of the stages described contained an analogy to Abraham. They were developed only so that, shown within their own sphere, each might, at the moment it deviated like a compass needle, point as it were to the boundary of the unknown land. Insofar as there could be any question of an analogy, it would have to be the paradox of sin. But that in turn lies in another sphere; it cannot explain Abraham, and is itself far easier to explain than Abraham.

**[40]** So Abraham did not speak. He did not speak to Sarah, not to Eliezer, not to Isaac; he passed over the three ethical authorities. For the ethical had no higher expression for Abraham than family life.

**[41]** Aesthetics permitted — indeed, demanded — silence of the single individual when by keeping silent he could save another. This alone is enough to show that Abraham does not fall within the range of aesthetics. His silence is by no means meant to save Isaac — just as, in general, his whole task, to sacrifice Isaac for his own sake and for God’s sake, is an offense to aesthetics. For aesthetics can certainly understand my sacrificing myself, but not my sacrificing someone else for my own sake. The aesthetic hero was silent. Ethics, however, condemned him, because he was silent by virtue of his accidental particularity. What determined him to keep silent was what he, as a human being, knew beforehand. This ethics cannot forgive: any such human knowledge is only an illusion. Ethics demands an infinite movement; it demands disclosure. So the aesthetic hero can speak, but will not.

**[42]** The real tragic hero sacrifices himself and everything that is his for the universal. Whatever he does, every emotion that stirs in him, belongs to the universal. He is disclosed, and in being disclosed he is ethics’ beloved son. That is not how it is with Abraham: he does nothing for the universal, and he is concealed.

**[43]** Here, then, we have reached the paradox. Either the single individual as the single individual is able to stand in an absolute relation to the absolute — in which case the ethical is not the highest — or else Abraham is lost: he is neither a tragic hero nor an aesthetic hero.

**[44]** To that extent it may again seem here that the paradox is the easiest and simplest thing of all. However, I must repeat that whoever holds himself convinced of this is not the knight of faith. For distress and anxiety are the only justification that can be thought of — even though it cannot be thought of in general terms, since then the paradox is cancelled.

**[45]** Abraham keeps silent — but he cannot speak; that is where the distress and the anxiety lie. For if, when I speak, I cannot make myself understood, then I do not speak, even if I spoke without a break, night and day. This is Abraham’s case. He can say everything; but one thing he cannot say — and yet, when he cannot say it, that is, say it in such a way that another person understands it, then he does not speak. Speech brings relief because it translates me into the universal. Abraham is quite able to say the finest things any language can come up with about the way he loves Isaac. But that is not what is on his mind; what is on his mind is the deeper thing: that he means to sacrifice Isaac because it is a test. This last thing no one can understand, and so everyone can only misunderstand the first. This distress the tragic hero does not know. In the first place, he has the comfort that every counterargument has had its due: that he has been able to give Clytemnestra, Iphigenia, Achilles, the chorus, every living being, every voice from the heart of humanity, every cunning thought, every anxiety-provoking, every accusing, every compassionate thought, the opportunity to rise up against him. He can be sure that everything that could be said against him has been said, ruthlessly, without mercy (and to fight against the whole world is a comfort, while to fight with oneself is terrible). He need not fear having overlooked something, so that one day, afterward, he might perhaps cry out as King Edward IV does at the news of Clarence’s murder:

**[46]** Wer bat für ihn? Wer kniet' in meinem Grimm
Zu Füssen mir und bat mich überlegen?
Wer sprach von Bruderpflicht? Wer sprach von Liebe. [Who sued to me for him? who, in my rage, / Kneel’d at my feet, and bade me be advis’d? / Who spake of brotherhood? who spake of love?] [* Note: Cf. Act 2, Scene 1.]

**[47]** The tragic hero does not know the terrible responsibility of solitude. Secondly, the tragic hero has this comfort: he is able to weep and lament together with Clytemnestra and Iphigenia — and while tears and cries relieve, sighs that cannot be uttered are torture. Agamemnon can quickly gather his soul into the certainty that he will act; that done, there is still time for him to comfort and encourage. Abraham cannot do this. When his heart is moved, when his words would hold a blessed comfort for the whole world, he dares not comfort; for would not Sarah, would not Eliezer, would not Isaac say to him, “Then why do you want to do it? You can just let it be, after all”? And if in his distress he then wanted to give vent to his feelings, wanted to embrace everything dear to him before he went on to the final act, he might perhaps bring about the terrible thing: that Sarah, that Eliezer, that Isaac would take offense at him and believe he was a hypocrite. Speak he cannot; he speaks no human language. Even if he himself understood all the tongues of the earth, even if his loved ones understood them too, he still cannot speak — he speaks in a divine tongue; he speaks in tongues.

**[48]** I can well understand this distress. I can admire Abraham. I have no fear that this story will tempt anyone to want, rashly, to be the single individual. But I also confess that I lack the courage for it, and that I would gladly renounce every prospect of getting any further, if only it were possible that I should ever, however late, get that far. At any moment Abraham can break off; he can repent the whole thing as a spiritual trial. Then he can speak, then everyone can understand him — but then he is no longer Abraham.

**[49]** Abraham cannot speak; for the thing that explains everything he cannot say (that is, say so that it is intelligible): that it is a test — note carefully, a test of such a kind that the ethical is the temptation. A person placed like this is an emigrant from the sphere of the universal. But the next thing he can say even less. Abraham, as has been sufficiently shown earlier, makes two movements. In the first — the infinite movement of resignation — he gives up Isaac, and no one can understand that, since it is a private undertaking. But next, at every moment, he makes the movement of faith. This is his comfort. For he says: “Yet it will not happen; or if it does happen, the Lord will give me a new Isaac — by virtue, that is, of the absurd.” With the tragic hero, the story does at least come to an end. Iphigenia submits to her father’s decision; she herself makes the infinite movement of resignation, and now the two of them understand each other. She can understand Agamemnon, because what he undertakes expresses the universal. If, by contrast, Agamemnon were to tell her, “Even though the god demands you as a sacrifice, it could still be that he did not demand it — by virtue, that is, of the absurd,” then in that same moment he would become unintelligible to Iphigenia. If he could say it by virtue of human calculation, then Iphigenia would surely understand him; but that would imply that Agamemnon had not made the infinite movement of resignation — and in that case he is no hero; then the seer’s pronouncement is a sailor’s yarn and the whole event a vaudeville.

**[50]** Abraham, then, did not speak. A single word from him is all that has come down to us: his one line to Isaac, which is itself sufficient proof that he had not spoken earlier. Isaac asks Abraham where the lamb for the burnt offering is. “And Abraham said: God will seek out for himself the lamb for the burnt offering, my son!”

**[51]** This last word of Abraham’s I shall consider here a little more closely. If this word were not there, the whole event would lack something; if it were different, everything would perhaps dissolve into confusion.

**[52]** I have often reflected on whether a tragic hero, whether he culminates in a suffering or in an action, ought to have a last line. As far as I can see, it depends on which sphere of life he belongs to: whether his life has intellectual significance, whether his suffering or action stands in relation to spirit.

**[53]** It goes without saying that the tragic hero, in his moment of culmination, can say a few words, like any other person who has not been deprived of speech — perhaps a few fitting words. But the question is whether it is fitting for him to say them. If what his life means lies in an outward deed, he has nothing to say: whatever he says is at bottom idle talk, and it only weakens the impression he makes. Tragic ceremony requires, rather, that he fulfill his task in silence, whether it consists in an action or in a suffering. So as not to go too far afield, I will simply take what lies closest to hand. Suppose it had been Agamemnon himself, rather than Calchas, who was to draw the knife on Iphigenia: wanting to say a few words at the last moment would only have demeaned him. For the significance of his deed was, after all, plain to everyone; the procedure of piety, of compassion, of feeling, of tears was complete; and apart from that, his life stood in no relation to spirit; he was, in other words, neither a teacher nor a witness to the spirit. If, on the other hand, the significance of a hero’s life lies in the direction of spirit, then the lack of a line would weaken the impression he makes. What he then has to say is not a few fitting words, a little piece of declamation. Rather, the significance of his line is that he carries himself through at the decisive moment. Such an intellectual tragic hero ought to have the last word, and ought to keep it. One demands of him the same transfigured bearing that befits every tragic hero, but then one demands one word more. Insofar, then, as such an intellectual tragic hero culminates in a suffering (in death), he becomes immortal in this last word before he dies, whereas the ordinary tragic hero becomes immortal only after his death.

**[54]** Socrates can serve as an example. He was an intellectual tragic hero. His death sentence is announced to him. In that moment he dies — for anyone who fails to see that dying takes the entire strength of the spirit, and that the hero always dies before he dies, will not get especially far in his contemplation of life. As a hero, Socrates is now required to rest calmly in himself; but as an intellectual tragic hero, he is required to have, in this last moment, strength of spirit enough to carry himself through. So he cannot, like the ordinary tragic hero, concentrate on holding his ground in the face of death. He must make this movement so quickly that in the same moment he is, in his consciousness, beyond this struggle and asserts himself. Had Socrates, then, fallen silent in the crisis of death, he would have weakened his life’s effect and raised the suspicion that, in him, irony’s elasticity was not a world-force but a game — a game whose springiness, at the decisive moment, had to be turned round and used the opposite way, to hold him up with pathos. [* Note: Which line of Socrates is to be regarded as the decisive one is a matter on which opinions may differ, since in so many ways Plato has made Socrates evaporate into poetry. I propose the following. The death sentence is announced to him; at that very moment he dies, and at that very moment he overcomes death and carries himself through with his famous retort: that he was surprised to have been condemned by a majority of three votes. No loose and idle talk in the marketplace, no foolish remark by an idiot, could he have joked about more ironically than about the death sentence that condemns him — himself — to death.]

**[55]** What has been briefly indicated here cannot, it is true, be applied to Abraham, if one thinks that by some analogy one could find a fitting word for Abraham to end with. It does apply, though, to the extent that one sees that it is necessary for Abraham to carry himself through at the last moment — that he must not draw the knife in silence but must have a word to say — for, as the father of faith, he is of absolute significance in the direction of spirit. What he is to say I can form no idea of beforehand. After he has said it, I can indeed understand — can indeed, in a certain sense, understand — Abraham in what he said, without thereby having come any closer to him than I have been in what went before. If no last line of Socrates had existed, I could have thought myself into him and formed one; and if I had not been able to do so, a poet would have managed it. But Abraham no poet can reach.

**[56]** Before going on to look more closely at Abraham’s last word, I must first point out how difficult it is for Abraham to come to say anything at all. The distress and anxiety in the paradox lay, as was developed above, precisely in the silence: Abraham cannot speak. To that extent, then, it is a self-contradiction to demand that he should speak — unless one wants to have him out of the paradox again, so that at the decisive moment he suspends it, and thereby ceases to be Abraham and annuls everything that went before. If, for example, Abraham were now, at the decisive moment, to say to Isaac, “It is you this is about,” that would be only a weakness. For if he could speak at all, he would have had to speak long ago; and the weakness would then lie in his not having had the maturity of spirit and the concentration to think the whole pain through in advance. He would instead have pushed something away from himself, so that the actual pain was something more than the pain he had thought. Moreover, by speaking like this he would fall out of the paradox; and if he really wanted to speak with Isaac, he would have to turn his condition into a spiritual trial — for otherwise he could say nothing at all — and if he does this, he is not even a tragic hero. [* Note: Insofar as there can be talk of any analogy, Pythagoras’s situation at his death offers one. For he had to carry through, in his last moment, the silence he had always maintained, and therefore he said: It is better to be killed than to speak. Cf. Diogenes, Book 8, § 39.]

**[57]** Nevertheless, a last word of Abraham’s has been preserved; and to the extent that I can understand the paradox, I can also understand how Abraham is totally present in this word. First and foremost, he says nothing, and it is in this form that he says what he has to say. His answer to Isaac is ironic in form; for it is always irony when I say something and yet do not say anything. Isaac asks Abraham on the assumption that Abraham knows. Now, if Abraham had answered, “I know nothing,” he would have said an untruth. To say something is beyond him; for what he knows, he cannot say. So he answers: “God will seek out for himself the lamb for the burnt offering, my son!” From this one sees the double movement in Abraham’s soul, as it has been described in what went before. Had Abraham merely given Isaac up in resignation and done no more, then he would have said an untruth; for he knows perfectly well that God demands Isaac as a sacrifice, and knows that he is himself ready, at this very moment, to sacrifice him. So at every moment, after making this movement, he has made the next one: he has made the movement of faith by virtue of the absurd. To that extent he says nothing untrue; for by virtue of the absurd it is, after all, possible that God could do something entirely different. So he says nothing untrue — but neither does he say anything; for he speaks in a foreign tongue. That becomes clearer still once we remember that the one who was to sacrifice Isaac was Abraham himself. Suppose the task had been different: suppose the Lord had ordered Abraham to take Isaac out to Mount Moriah, and had then let his own lightning strike Isaac and taken him as a sacrifice that way. In that case Abraham would, in a straightforward sense, have been right to speak as enigmatically as he does; for then he himself could not know what would happen. But as the task is set for Abraham, he himself must act. So at the decisive moment he must know what he himself will do, and so he must know that Isaac is to be sacrificed. If he has not been certain of this, he has not made the infinite movement of resignation; and in that case, though his word is certainly no untruth, he is also very far from being Abraham: he is of less account than a tragic hero; indeed, he is an irresolute man who can make up his mind neither to the one thing nor to the other, and who for that reason will always end up speaking in riddles. But such a hesitator is the very parody of the knight of faith.

**[58]** So it appears here again that one can indeed understand Abraham, but only understand him in the way one understands the paradox. For my part, I can indeed understand Abraham, but I also comprehend that I do not have the courage to speak like that, any more than I have the courage to act like Abraham. Yet I by no means say, on that account, that it is something slight; on the contrary, it is the one thing that is wondrous.

**[59]** And what, then, did his own age judge of the tragic hero? That he was great — and it admired him. And that honorable assembly of the noble, the jury that every generation sets up to judge the generation that has gone before, judged the same. But Abraham there was no one who could understand. And yet what did he attain? That he stayed true to his love. But a person who loves God needs neither tears nor admiration: he forgets the suffering in his love. Indeed, he has forgotten it so completely that afterward there would not be the slightest trace of a notion of his pain, were it not that God himself remembered it — for God sees in secret, and he knows the distress and counts the tears and forgets nothing.

**[60]** Either, then, there is a paradox — that the single individual as the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute — or Abraham is lost.
