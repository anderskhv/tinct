## ch4 ¶0 (text changed)

**before**

> An old saying, taken from the outward and visible world, says: “Only the one who works gets bread.” Strangely enough, the saying does not fit the world where it is most at home. For the outward world is subject to the law of imperfection, and there it happens again and again that the one who does not work also gets bread, and that the one who sleeps gets it more abundantly than the one who works. In the outward world everything belongs to whoever holds it. That world is in bondage to the law of indifference: the spirit of the ring obeys whoever has the ring, whether he is a Noureddin or an Aladdin, and whoever has the treasures of the world has them, however he came by them. In the world of spirit it is different. Here an eternal divine order reigns. Here it does not rain on the just and the unjust alike; here the sun does not shine on the good and the evil alike. Here the saying holds: only the one who works gets bread; only whoever has been in anxiety finds rest; only whoever goes down into the underworld saves the beloved; only whoever draws the knife gets Isaac. The one who will not work does not get bread but is deceived, as the gods deceived Orpheus with an airy phantom in place of the beloved — deceived him because he was soft, not courageous; deceived him because he was a cithara player, not a man. Here it does no good to have Abraham for one’s father, or seventeen noble ancestors. What is written of the virgins of Israel fits the one who will not work: he brings forth wind. But whoever is willing to work brings forth his own father.

> Now, the story of Abraham has the remarkable property that it always remains glorious, however poorly one understands it. Yet here, too, it holds true that everything depends on whether one is willing to work and to be burdened. But people are not willing to work, and still they want to understand the story. They speak in Abraham’s honor — but how? They give the whole thing a perfectly commonplace expression: “What was great was that he loved God in such a way that he was willing to sacrifice the best to him.” That is very true; but “the best” is a vague expression. In the flow of thought and talk one quite confidently identifies Isaac with the best, and the one who meditates on it can smoke his pipe at his ease during the meditation, and the one who listens can stretch his legs out comfortably in front of him. If that rich young man whom Christ met on his way had sold all his goods and given them to the poor, we would praise him, as we praise everything great — though even him we would not understand without working. But he would still not have become an Abraham, even though he sacrificed the best he had. What is left out of Abraham’s story is the anxiety. For toward money I have no ethical obligation, but toward his son a father has the highest and holiest obligation of all. But anxiety is a dangerous business for the soft, and so it is forgotten; and nonetheless people want to talk about Abraham. Talk they do, and in the course of the talk the two expressions, Isaac and the best, are used interchangeably, and everything goes splendidly. Suppose, however, that among the listeners there happened to be a man who suffered from sleeplessness. Then the most terrible, the deepest misunderstanding, at once tragic and comic, lies very close at hand. He went home; he wanted to do just as Abraham did; for the son is, after all, the best. If that speaker got to hear of it, perhaps he went to the man, gathered all his clerical dignity and shouted: “You abominable man, you dregs of society, what devil has possessed you like this, that you would murder your son?” And the pastor, who had detected no warmth or sweat in himself when he preached about Abraham, was astonished at himself, at the earnest wrath with which he thundered down on that poor man. He was pleased with himself, for he had never spoken with such force and unction. He told himself and his wife: “I am an orator; all I lacked was the occasion. Last Sunday, when I spoke about Abraham, I was not moved in the least.” If this same speaker had some small surplus of understanding that he could afford to lose, I think he would lose it if the sinner answered calmly and with dignity: “But that is just what you yourself preached about last Sunday.” And how could the pastor ever have got such a thing into his head? Yet that is how it was, and the only mistake was that he had not known what he was saying. If only there were a poet who could make up his mind to prefer situations like this to the frippery and nonsense that comedies and novels are stuffed with! Here the comic and the tragic touch each other in absolute infinity. The pastor’s speech was perhaps ridiculous enough in and of itself, but it became infinitely ridiculous through its effect — and yet that effect was entirely natural. Or suppose the sinner, without raising any objection, really was converted by the pastor’s reprimand, and this zealous clergyman went home happy — happy in the awareness that he was effective not only from the pulpit but above all, with irresistible power, as a shepherd of souls: on Sunday he stirred the congregation to enthusiasm, while on Monday he stood like a cherub with a flaming sword before the man who by his deed wanted to put to shame that old saying that things in the world do not go as the pastor preaches.

> How is one to explain a contradiction like that speaker’s? Is it because Abraham has acquired a prescriptive right to be a great man, so that whatever he does is great, while if someone else does the same it is sin, a sin that cries to heaven? In that case I do not wish to take part in such thoughtless praise. If faith cannot make the will to murder one’s son a holy act, then let the same sentence be passed on Abraham as on anyone else. If people perhaps lack the courage to carry their thought through, and to say that Abraham was a murderer, then surely it is better to acquire that courage than to waste time on undeserved eulogies. The ethical expression for what Abraham did is that he was willing to murder Isaac; the religious expression is that he was willing to sacrifice Isaac. But precisely in this contradiction lies the anxiety that can indeed make a person sleepless; and yet without this anxiety Abraham is not the one he is. Or perhaps Abraham did not do at all what the story tells; perhaps, because of the conditions of those times, it was something quite different. Then let us forget him; for what is the point of remembering a past that cannot become a present? Or perhaps that speaker had forgotten something that corresponds to the ethical forgetting of the fact that Isaac was the son. For when faith is taken away by being reduced to nil and nothing, all that remains is the raw fact that Abraham was willing to murder Isaac — and that is easy enough to imitate for anyone who does not have faith, that is, the faith that makes it hard for him.

> So one could, I suppose, speak about Abraham; for what is great can never do harm when it is grasped in its greatness. It is like a double-edged sword: it kills and it saves. If the lot fell to me to speak about it, I would start by showing how devout and God-fearing a man Abraham was, worthy to be called God’s elect. Only such a man is given such a test; but who is such a man? Next I would describe how Abraham loved Isaac. To that end I would ask all good spirits to stand by me, so that my speech might glow as a father’s love glows. I hope to portray it so that few fathers in all the King’s realms and lands would dare to claim that they loved like that. But if a father did not love as Abraham did, then surely any thought of sacrificing Isaac would be a spiritual trial — an inner assault on a person. One could already speak about this for several Sundays; there is no need to hurry, after all. The result, if it was spoken of rightly, would be that a good many fathers would not ask to hear any more at all, but for the time being would be glad if they really managed to love as Abraham loved. If there was then one who, after hearing what is great but also what is terrible in Abraham’s deed, dared to set out on the road, I would saddle my horse and ride with him. At every stage, until we came to Mount Moriah, I would explain to him that he could still turn back: he could repent of the misunderstanding that he was called to be put to the proof in such a struggle, and could confess that he lacked courage, so that God himself must take Isaac, if he would have him. I am convinced that such a person is not rejected, that he can attain blessedness along with everyone else — but not in time. Would people not, even in the most believing ages, judge such a person in this way? I once knew a person who could have saved my life, if he had been magnanimous. He said straight out: “I can see well enough what I could do; but I do not dare. I am afraid that later I will lack the strength, that I will come to regret it.” He was not magnanimous; yet who would stop loving him on that account?

> Love, after all, has its priests in the poets, and now and then one hears a voice that knows how to keep it in honor. But of faith one hears not a word. Who speaks to honor this passion? Philosophy goes further. Theology sits painted at the window, courting philosophy’s favor and offering her charms for sale to it. Understanding Hegel is said to be hard; but understanding Abraham, that is a trifle. Going beyond Hegel is a marvel; but getting beyond Abraham, that is the easiest thing in the world. For my part, I have spent a good deal of time trying to understand the Hegelian philosophy, and I believe I have understood it reasonably well. I am rash enough to think that when, in spite of all my efforts, I cannot understand him in certain places, it is probably because he himself was not entirely clear. All this I do lightly and naturally; it does my head no harm. But when I have to think about Abraham, I am as if annihilated. At every moment I catch sight of that monstrous paradox that is the content of Abraham’s life; at every moment I am thrust back, and for all its passion my thought cannot penetrate it, cannot get a hair’s breadth further. I strain every muscle to get a view of it — and in the same instant I am paralyzed.

> I am not unacquainted with what has been admired in the world as great and magnanimous. My soul feels its kinship with it and is assured, in all humility, that the cause the hero fought for was mine too. I am not unacquainted with the distress and dangers of life; I do not fear them, and I go to meet them boldly. I am no stranger to the terrible. My memory is a faithful wife, and my imagination is — what I myself am not — an industrious little girl who sits quietly at her work all day and in the evening knows how to chatter to me so prettily that I cannot help looking at it, although what she paints is not always landscapes or flowers or Schäfer-Historier (shepherds’ idylls). I am convinced that God is love; this thought has for me a primal lyrical validity. When the thought is present to me, I am unspeakably blessed; when it is absent, I long for it more fiercely than a lover longs for the one he loves. But I do not have faith; this is the courage I lack. God’s love is, for me, in both a direct and an inverse sense, incommensurable with the whole of actuality — not measurable by any common standard. I am not cowardly enough to whimper and complain about that, but neither am I underhanded enough to deny that faith is something far higher. I can bear living in my own way well enough; I am glad and content. But my joy is not faith’s joy, and compared with that it is unhappy. I do not bother God with my small sorrows; particular things do not concern me; I keep my eyes on my love alone and keep its virginal flame clean and clear. Faith is convinced that God concerns himself with the smallest things. I am content in this life to be married with the left hand; faith is humble enough to demand the right. For that this is humility I do not deny, and never shall.

**before**

> Is everyone in my time really capable of making the movements of faith? Unless I am much mistaken about my time, it tends rather to pride itself on doing what it presumably does not even think me capable of — namely, the imperfect. It goes against my soul to do what is so often done: to speak inhumanly about what is great, as if a few thousand years were an immense distance. I prefer to speak of it humanly, as if it had happened yesterday, and to let only greatness itself be the distance, a distance that either lifts up or judges. If, then (in the capacity of tragic hero, for higher I cannot come), I had been summoned for such an extraordinary royal journey as the one to Mount Moriah, I know perfectly well what I would have done. I would not have been such a coward as to stay at home; I would not have lain dawdling on the highway either, or forgotten the knife to cause a little delay. I am fairly sure that I would have been there on the stroke of the hour, with everything in order — perhaps I would even have come too early, so that it could be over soon. But I also know what else I would have done. The moment I got up on the horse, I would have told myself: Now everything is lost. God demands Isaac; I sacrifice him, and all my joy with him — and yet God is love, and remains love for me. For in temporality God and I cannot talk with each other; we share no language. Perhaps someone or other in our time will be foolish enough, and envious enough of what is great, to want to persuade himself and me that had I really done this, I would have done something still greater than Abraham; for my enormous resignation was far more ideal and poetic than Abraham’s pettiness. And yet this is the greatest untruth; for my enormous resignation was the substitute for faith. For I could do no more than make the infinite movement, so as to find myself and to rest in myself again. Nor would I have loved Isaac as Abraham loved him. That I was resolute enough to make the movement might prove my courage, humanly speaking. That I loved him with all my soul is the premise without which the whole thing becomes a misdeed. But still I would not have loved as Abraham did; for then I would have held back even at the very last minute — without thereby arriving at Mount Moriah too late. Moreover, by my conduct I would have ruined the whole story; for had I received Isaac back, I would have been at a loss. What came easiest to Abraham would have been hard for me: to be glad in Isaac again! For the one who, with the whole infinity of his soul, proprio motu et propriis auspiciis — of his own motion and on his own authority — has made the infinite movement and can do no more, keeps Isaac only in pain.
