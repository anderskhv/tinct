# Part B: before → after R2 fixes (for re-verification)

## ch3 ¶0 (text changed)

**before**

> Suppose there were no eternal consciousness in a human being; suppose that underneath everything there were nothing but a wild, fermenting power which, twisting in obscure passions, produced all things, the great and the trivial alike; suppose that a bottomless void, never sated, lay concealed beneath everything — then what would life be, if not despair? Suppose it were so: suppose no holy bond held humanity together; suppose one generation sprang up after another like leaves in a wood; suppose one generation replaced another like birdsong in a wood; suppose our kind went through the world the way a ship goes through the sea, the way wind goes through the desert — a thoughtless, barren business; suppose an everlasting forgetfulness lay in wait, forever hungry, for its prey, and no power were strong enough to tear the prey from it — how empty life would be then, and how bleak! But that is exactly why it is not so. As God created man and woman, in the same way he shaped the hero and the poet — or the orator. Of what the hero does, the poet can do nothing; all he can do is admire the hero, love him, take delight in him. And yet he too is happy — no less happy than the hero. For the hero is, as it were, his better being, with which he is in love — glad that it is not himself after all, glad that his love can be admiration. He is the presiding spirit of recollection. All he can do is call to mind what has been done; all he can do is admire what has been done. He takes nothing from what is his own; he is jealous, rather, of what was entrusted to him. He follows the choice of his heart; but when he has found what he was looking for, he goes round to every man’s door with his song and his speech, so that everyone may admire the hero as he does and take pride in the hero as he does. That is his feat, his humble task; that is his loyal service in the house of the hero. If in this way he keeps faith with his love, struggling night and day with the craft of forgetfulness, which wants to spirit the hero away from him, then his task is complete, and he is united with the hero, who has loved him with the same fidelity. For the poet is, as it were, the hero’s better being: without force, admittedly, as a memory is, but transfigured too, as a memory is. So no one who was great shall be forgotten. Even if it takes a long while, even if a cloud of misunderstanding should carry the hero off, his lover will come all the same; and the longer time went on, the more faithfully he clings to him.

## ch3 ¶7 (text changed)

**before**

> But Abraham had faith, and faith for this life. Indeed, had his faith concerned only a life to come, he would presumably have found it easier to throw everything away and hurry out of a world he did not belong to. But Abraham’s faith was not of that sort — if there is such a sort. For strictly speaking it is not faith, only faith’s remotest possibility, which dimly senses its object at the very edge of the horizon, yet is separated from it by a gaping chasm in which despair plays its game. But Abraham had faith precisely for this life: faith that he would live to old age in the land, held in honor among the people, blessed in his descendants, unforgettable in Isaac, the one dearest to him in life. He embraced Isaac with a love for which it was only a poor expression to say that he faithfully fulfilled a father’s duty to love his son — as indeed the summons itself also has it: “the son whom you love.” Jacob had twelve sons, and one he loved; Abraham had only one, whom he loved.

## ch3 ¶10 (text changed)

**before**

> We read in those holy scriptures: “And God tempted Abraham and said: Abraham, Abraham, where are you? But Abraham replied: Here I am.” You to whom my words are addressed — was it like that with you? When you saw the heavy visitations coming toward you from afar, did you not tell the mountains, “Hide me,” and the hills, “Fall on me”? Or were you stronger? Even so, did your foot not drag slowly along the road — did it not seem to yearn back toward the old tracks? When the call came to you, did you answer, or did you not — perhaps quietly, in a whisper? Not so Abraham. Glad, bold, full of trust, he answered in a loud voice: Here I am. We read on: “And Abraham rose early in the morning.” He hastened as though to a feast, and early in the morning he was at the place appointed, on Mount Moriah. He said nothing to Sarah, nothing to Eliezer — and who, after all, could have understood him? Had the temptation not, by its very nature, exacted from him a vow of silence? “He chopped the firewood, he bound Isaac, he set the pyre alight, he drew the knife.” My listener! There were many fathers who believed that in their child they were losing the dearest thing they had in the world, and being robbed of all hope for the future; yet there was, after all, no one who was the child of the promise in the sense that Isaac was for Abraham. There were many fathers who lost their child; but then it was God — the Almighty’s unchanging and unsearchable will — whose hand took it. Not so with Abraham. A heavier test had been kept for him: along with the knife, Isaac’s fate had been laid in Abraham’s hand. There he stood, that old man, with his only hope! Yet he did not doubt; he cast no anxious glances right or left; he did not challenge heaven by his prayers. He knew it was God Almighty who was testing him; he knew it was the heaviest sacrifice that could be demanded of him; but he knew too that no sacrifice was too heavy when God demanded it — and he drew the knife.

## ch3 ¶12 (text changed)

**before**

> If Abraham, standing on Mount Moriah, had doubted; if he had looked around him helplessly; if, before drawing the knife, he had happened to spot the ram; if God had allowed him to sacrifice it in Isaac’s place — then he would have traveled home, and everything would have been as it was: he would have Sarah, he would keep Isaac — and yet how altered it all would be! For his retreat would have been a flight, his rescue an accident, his reward disgrace, and his future, perhaps, perdition. Then he would have borne witness not to his faith, nor to God’s grace, but to how terrible a thing it is to go up Mount Moriah. Then Abraham would not have been forgotten, and nor would Mount Moriah. It would then be named not as Ararat is named, where the Ark came to rest, but named as a horror, because it was here that Abraham doubted.
