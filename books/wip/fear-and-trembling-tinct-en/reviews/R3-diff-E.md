# Part E: before → after R3 fixes (for re-verification)

## ch4 ¶24 (text changed)

**before**

> No faith is needed in order to resign. For what I win in resignation is my eternal consciousness; that is a purely philosophical movement, one I am confident I can make when it is demanded, and one I can discipline myself to make. For every time some bit of finitude threatens to get the better of me, I starve myself out until I make the movement; for my eternal consciousness is my love of God, and that ranks higher for me than anything. No faith is needed in order to resign; but faith is needed in order to get the very least thing more than my eternal consciousness, for this is what is paradoxical. The movements are often confused. People say that one needs faith in order to renounce everything. Indeed, one hears something stranger still: a person complains that he has lost his faith, and when one checks the scale to see where he stands on it, one sees, strangely enough, that he has only reached the point at which he ought to make the infinite movement of resignation. In resignation I renounce everything. I make this movement on my own; and if I fail to make it, that is because I am cowardly and effete and lacking in enthusiasm, and have no feeling for the significance of the lofty dignity allotted to every human being: that of being his own censor — a far more distinguished office than being censor general of the entire Roman Republic. I make this movement on my own, and what I thereby win is myself in my eternal consciousness, in blessed understanding with my love for the eternal being. By faith I renounce nothing; on the contrary, by faith I get everything — precisely in the sense in which it is said that whoever has faith as a grain of mustard seed can move mountains. A purely human courage is needed to renounce all of temporality in order to win eternity. But eternity I do win, and I can never in all eternity renounce it; that would be a self-contradiction. But a paradoxical and humble courage is needed to grasp, after that, the whole of temporality by virtue of the absurd — and this courage is faith’s. By faith Abraham did not give Isaac up; by faith Abraham got Isaac. By virtue of resignation that rich young man ought to have given everything away; but when he had done so, the knight of faith should have said to him: “By virtue of the absurd you will get every last penny back, provided you can believe it.” And these words ought by no means to be a matter of indifference to the formerly rich young man; for if he gave his possessions away because he was sick of them, then his resignation was in a sorry state.

## ch4 ¶28 (text changed)

**before**

> Is it really so? Is all the philistinism I see in life — which I let be judged not by my word but by my deed — really not what it seems? Is it the marvel? That is conceivable, after all; for that hero of faith did bear a striking resemblance to it — since that hero of faith was not even an ironist and humorist, but something still higher. There is a great deal of talk in our time about irony and humor, especially from people who have never managed to practice them, but who nonetheless know how to explain everything. I am not wholly a stranger to these two passions; I know somewhat more about them than is to be found in German and German-Danish compendiums. So I know that these two passions differ essentially from the passion of faith. Irony and humor also turn their reflection on themselves, and therefore belong in the sphere of infinite resignation; their elasticity lies in the individual’s being incommensurable with actuality.

## ch4 ¶30 (text changed)

**before**

> People take the story of Abraham another way. They praise God’s grace for granting him Isaac again — the whole thing was only a test. A test — this word can say a lot or a little, and yet the whole business is over the moment it is said. One climbs onto a winged horse; in the same instant one is on Mount Moriah; in the same instant one sees the ram. One forgets that Abraham rode only on a donkey, which goes slowly along the road; that he had a three-day journey; that he needed some time to split the firewood, to bind Isaac and to sharpen the knife.
