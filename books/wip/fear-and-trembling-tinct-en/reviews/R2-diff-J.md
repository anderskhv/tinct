## ch7 ¶45 (text changed)

**before**

> Abraham keeps silent — but he cannot speak; that is where the distress and the anxiety lie. For if, when I speak, I cannot make myself understood, then I do not speak, even if I spoke without a break, night and day. This is Abraham’s case. He can say everything; but one thing he cannot say — and yet, when he cannot say it, that is, say it in such a way that another person understands it, then he does not speak. Speech brings relief because it translates me into the universal. Abraham is quite able to say the finest things any language can come up with about the way he loves Isaac. But that is not what is on his mind; what is on his mind is the deeper thing: that he means to sacrifice Isaac because it is a test. This last thing no one can understand, and so everyone can only misunderstand the first. This distress the tragic hero does not know. ## ch7 ¶54 (text changed)

**before**

> Socrates can serve as an example. He was an intellectual tragic hero. His death sentence is announced to him. In that moment he dies — for anyone who fails to see that dying takes the entire strength of the spirit, and that the hero always dies before he dies, will not get especially far in his contemplation of life. As a hero, Socrates is now required to rest calmly in himself; but as an intellectual tragic hero, he is required to have, in this last moment, strength of spirit enough to carry himself through. So he cannot, like the ordinary tragic hero, concentrate on holding his ground in the face of death. He must make this movement so quickly that in the same moment he is, in his consciousness, beyond this struggle and asserts himself. Had Socrates, then, fallen silent in the crisis of death, he would have weakened his life’s effect and raised the suspicion that, in him, irony’s elasticity was not a world-force but a game, whose springiness at the decisive moment had to be used by the reverse measure — to hold him up with pathos.

Had Socrates, then, fallen silent in the crisis of death, he would have weakened his life’s effect and raised the suspicion that, in him, irony’s elasticity was not a world-force but a game — a game whose springiness, at the decisive moment, had to be turned round and used the opposite way, to hold him up with pathos.
