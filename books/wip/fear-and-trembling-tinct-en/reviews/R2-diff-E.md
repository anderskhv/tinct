## ch4 ¶19 (text changed)

**before**

> In infinite resignation there is peace and rest. Every human being who wills it — who has not debased himself by holding himself in contempt, which is even more terrible than being too proud — can train himself to make this movement, which, in its pain, reconciles a person to existence. Infinite resignation is that shirt spoken of in an old folk tale. The thread is spun under tears, bleached with tears, the shirt sewn in tears; but then it also protects better than iron and steel. The imperfection in the folk tale is that a third party can make up this linen. The secret of life is that everyone has to sew it for himself; and the remarkable thing is that a man can sew it every bit as well as a woman. In infinite resignation there is peace and rest and consolation in pain — provided, that is, the movement is made normally. I could, however, easily write a whole book if I set out to go through the various misunderstandings, the back-to-front positions, the sloppy movements I have come across in my own small practice alone. People believe very little in spirit; and yet it is precisely spirit that matters in making this movement. What matters is that the movement not be the one-sided result of a dira necessitas — a harsh necessity — and the more that necessity is present, the more doubtful it always becomes whether the movement is normal. So if one means to hold that cold, barren necessity must necessarily be present, one is saying that no one can experience death before he really dies — which strikes me as crass materialism. But in our time people care less about making pure movements. Suppose someone who was to learn dancing said: “Generation upon generation has now spent centuries learning the positions; it is high time I turned this to my advantage and started straight in on the française.” People would probably laugh at him a little; but in the world of spirit this is found extremely plausible. What, then, is cultivation? I thought it was the course the single individual runs through in order to catch up with himself; and whoever will not go through this course is helped very little by having been born in the most enlightened age.

## ch4 ¶21 (text changed)

**before**

> Let us now have the knight of faith appear in the case described. He does precisely what the other knight does. He gives up, infinitely, the love that is his life’s content, and he is reconciled in the pain. But then the marvel occurs: he makes yet another movement, more wondrous than anything, for he says: “I believe nevertheless that I will get her — by virtue, that is, of the absurd, by virtue of this: that for God everything is possible.” The absurd does not belong among the distinctions that lie within the understanding’s own range. It is not the same thing as the improbable, the unexpected, the unlooked-for. At the moment the knight resigned, he assured himself of the impossibility, humanly speaking; this was the understanding’s result, and he had energy enough to think it. In the infinite sense, on the other hand, it was possible — namely, by resigning it. But this possessing is, of course, at the same time a giving up; and yet for the understanding this possessing is no absurdity. For the understanding went on being right that in the world of wretchedness where it rules, it was and remained an impossibility. The knight of faith is just as clearly conscious of this. Accordingly, nothing can save him but the absurd, and this he grasps by faith. He acknowledges the impossibility, then, and in the same moment he believes the absurd. For if he wants to imagine that he has faith without acknowledging the impossibility with all the passion of his soul and with his whole heart, he deceives himself, and his testimony has no footing anywhere, since he has not even come as far as infinite resignation.

## ch4 ¶23 (text changed)

**before**

> I can understand, then, that making the infinite movement of resignation requires strength and energy and freedom of spirit; I can also understand that it is feasible. The next thing astonishes me; my brain turns over in my head. For after having made the movement of resignation, then by virtue of the absurd to get everything — to get the wish, whole and undiminished — that is beyond human powers; it is a marvel. But this I can understand: that the young girl’s certainty is mere light-mindedness compared with the unshakability of faith, even though faith’s unshakability has seen the impossibility. Every time I want to make this movement, everything goes black before my eyes; in the same moment I admire it absolutely, and in the same moment a tremendous anxiety seizes my soul — for what, then, is it to tempt God? And yet this movement is the movement of faith, and it goes on being so, even if philosophy, to confuse the concepts, would have us believe that it has faith, and even if theology wants to sell it off cheap.

## ch4 ¶24 (text changed)

**before**

> No faith is needed in order to resign. For what I win in resignation is my eternal consciousness; that is a purely philosophical movement, one I am confident I can make when it is demanded, and one I can discipline myself to make. For every time some bit of finitude threatens to get the better of me, I starve myself out until I make the movement; for my eternal consciousness is my love of God, and that ranks higher for me than anything. No faith is needed in order to resign; but faith is needed in order to get the very least thing more than my eternal consciousness, for this is what is paradoxical. The movements are often confused. People say that one needs faith in order to renounce everything. Indeed, one hears something stranger still: a person complains that he has lost his faith, and when one checks the scale to see where he stands, one sees, strangely enough, that he has only reached the point at which he ought to make the infinite movement of resignation. In resignation I renounce everything. I make this movement on my own; and if I fail to make it, that is because I am cowardly and effete and lacking in enthusiasm, and have no feeling for the significance of the lofty dignity allotted to every human being: that of being his own censor — a far more distinguished office than being censor general of the entire Roman Republic. I make this movement on my own, and what I thereby win is myself in my eternal consciousness, in blessed understanding with my love for the eternal being. By faith I renounce nothing; on the contrary, by faith I get everything — precisely in the sense in which it is said that whoever has faith as a grain of mustard seed can move mountains. A purely human courage is needed to renounce all of temporality in order to win eternity. But eternity I do win, and I can never in all eternity renounce it; that would be a self-contradiction. But a paradoxical and humble courage is needed to grasp, after that, the whole of temporality by virtue of the absurd — and this courage is faith’s. By faith Abraham did not give Isaac up; by faith Abraham got Isaac. By virtue of resignation that rich young man ought to have given everything away; but when he had done so, the knight of faith should have said to him: “By virtue of the absurd you will get every last penny back, provided you can believe it.” And these words ought by no means to be a matter of indifference to the formerly rich young man; for if he gave his possessions away because he was sick of them, then his resignation was in a sorry state.

> Temporality, finitude — that is what everything turns on. By my own powers I can resign everything, and then find peace and rest in pain. I can bear everything — even if that dreadful demon, more terrible than the skeleton Death who frightens people, even if madness held up the fool’s costume before my eyes and I understood from its look that it was I who was to put it on. I can still save my soul, as long as it matters more to me that my love for God should triumph in me than that I should have earthly happiness. Then he will calmly put on the costume. Anyone whose soul lacks this romance has sold his soul, whether he got a kingdom for it or a miserable piece of silver. But by my own powers I cannot get the least of what belongs to finitude; for I am constantly using my powers to resign everything. By my own powers I can give up the princess, and I will not turn into a sourpuss; I will find joy and peace and rest in my pain. But by my own powers I cannot get her back, for it is precisely to resign that I am using my powers. But by faith, says that wonderful knight — by faith you will get her, by virtue of the absurd.

## ch4 ¶26 (text changed)

**before**

> This movement, you see, I cannot make. As soon as I want to begin on it, everything turns upside down, and I flee back into the pain of resignation. I can swim in life, but for this mystical hovering I am too heavy. To exist so that at every moment my opposition to existence expresses itself as the most beautiful and secure harmony with it — that I cannot do. And yet it must be glorious to get the princess. I say so at every moment, and the knight of resignation who does not say so is a fraud: he has not had a single wish, and he has not kept the wish young in his pain. Perhaps there was one who found it convenient enough that the wish was no longer alive, that the arrow of pain had been blunted; but someone like that is no knight. A free-born soul who caught himself at it would despise himself and start over, and above all would not let his soul be deceived by itself. And yet it must be glorious to get the princess. And yet only the knight of faith is happy — the hereditary lord of the finite — while the knight of resignation is a stranger and a foreigner. To get the princess in this way, to live happily and joyfully day in and day out with her (for this too was conceivable, of course: that the knight of resignation might get the princess — but his soul had seen through the impossibility of their future happiness); in this way to live happily and joyfully every moment by virtue of the absurd, at every moment to see the sword hovering above the head of the beloved, and yet to find not rest in the pain of resignation but joy by virtue of the absurd — that is wonderful. Whoever does it is great, the only great one. The thought of it moves my soul, which was never stingy in admiring what is great.

That is conceivable, after all; for that hero of faith did bear a striking resemblance to it — since that hero of faith was not even an ironist and humorist, but something still higher. So I know that these two passions differ essentially from the passion of faith.
