# Part C: before → after R3 fixes (for re-verification)

## ch4 ¶7 (text changed)

**before**

> So one could, I suppose, speak about Abraham; for what is great can never do harm when it is understood in its greatness. It is like a double-edged sword: it kills and it saves. If the lot fell to me to speak about it, I would start by showing how devout and God-fearing a man Abraham was, worthy to be called God’s elect. Only such a man is given such a test; but who is such a man? Next I would describe how Abraham loved Isaac. To that end I would ask all good spirits to stand by me, so that my speech might glow as a father’s love glows. I hope to portray it so that few fathers in all the King’s realms and lands would dare to claim that they loved like that. But if a father did not love as Abraham did, then any thought of sacrificing Isaac would, of course, be a spiritual trial — an inner assault on him. One could already speak about this for several Sundays; there is no need to hurry, after all. The result, if it was spoken of rightly, would be that a good many fathers would not ask to hear any more at all, but for the time being would be glad if they really managed to love as Abraham loved. If there was then one who, after hearing what is great but also what is terrible in Abraham’s deed, dared to set out on the road, I would saddle my horse and ride with him. At every stage, until we came to Mount Moriah, I would explain to him that he could still turn back: he could repent of the misunderstanding that he was called to be put to the proof in such a struggle, and could confess that he lacked the courage, so that if God wanted Isaac, God would have to take him himself. I am convinced that such a person is not rejected, that he can become blessed along with everyone else — though he does not become so within time. Would not even the most believing ages have passed that same judgment on such a person? I have known a person who once could have saved my life, if he had been magnanimous. He put it plainly: “I can see well enough what I could do; but I do not dare. I fear that later I will lack the strength, that I will come to regret it.” He was not magnanimous; yet who would stop loving him on that account?
