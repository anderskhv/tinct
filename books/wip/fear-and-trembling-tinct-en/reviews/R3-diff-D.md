> Abraham stands at this summit. The last stage to vanish from his sight is infinite resignation — the movement by which one gives up the finite and finds one’s peace in the eternal. ## ch4 ¶15 (text changed)

**before**

> Would it not be best, though, to stop at faith, and is it not outrageous that everyone wants to go further? When people in our time — and this is proclaimed in various ways — will not stop at love, where do they end up? In earthly shrewdness and petty calculation, in paltriness and wretchedness, in everything that can make a human being’s divine origin doubtful. Would it not be best to stop at faith, and for the one who stands to take care that he does not fall? For the movement of faith must constantly be made by virtue of the absurd — but, note carefully, in such a way that one does not lose finitude but gains it whole and entire. As for me, I can indeed describe the movements of faith, but I cannot make them. If you want to learn the movements of swimming, you can have yourself hung in a harness from the ceiling; you may well describe the movements, but you do not swim. Just so, I can describe the movements of faith; but when I am tossed into the water, I do swim, admittedly (for I am not one of the waders), yet my movements are different. They are the movements of infinity, while faith does the reverse: having made the movements of infinity, it makes those of finitude. Happy is the one who can make these movements! He does the marvelous, and I shall never tire of admiring him. Whether it is Abraham or the slave in Abraham’s household, a professor of philosophy or some poor maidservant, is absolutely indifferent to me; I look only at the movements. But at those I do look, and I let no one take me in — neither myself nor anyone else. The knights of infinite resignation are easy to recognize: their gait is gliding, bold. Those, on the other hand, who carry the jewel of faith are apt to deceive, because their outward appearance bears a striking resemblance to what both infinite resignation and faith deeply despise: philistinism, the outlook of the philistine — the narrow, comfortable townsman.

## ch4 ¶16 (text changed)

**before**

> I frankly confess that in my practice I have not come across a single reliable specimen — though I do not therefore mean to deny that perhaps every other person is such a specimen. All the same, I have been tracking one down for several years, in vain. People generally travel round the world to see rivers and mountains, new stars, gaudy birds, misshapen fish, ridiculous races of mankind; they give themselves over to the animal stupor that gawks at existence, and believe they have seen something. None of that engages me. If, however, I knew where such a knight of faith lived, I would make the journey to him on foot; for this marvel engages me absolutely. I would not let him out of my sight for an instant; every minute I would watch how he went about the movements. I would consider myself provided for in life, and divide my time between watching him and doing the exercises myself, and so spend all my time admiring him. As I said, I have not found anyone of the kind; but I can certainly conceive of him. Here he is. The introduction takes place; I am presented to him. The moment I first set eyes on him, in that same instant I thrust him away from me, make a leap backward myself, clap my hands together and say half aloud, “Good Lord! Is this the man? Is it really him? Why, he looks like a tax collector!” But it is him all the same. I draw a little closer to him and watch his slightest movement, to see whether some small, incongruous fragment of a signal might not show itself from the infinite — a glance, a look, a gesture, a touch of melancholy, a smile — that betrayed the infinite in its incongruity with the finite. No! I examine his figure from head to toe, to see whether there might not be a crack through which the infinite peeped out. No! He is solid through and through. His footing? It is sturdy, and belongs wholly to finitude; no dressed-up citizen walking out to Frederiksberg on a Sunday afternoon treads the ground more firmly. He belongs wholly to the world; no philistine could belong to it more. Nothing is to be detected of that strange and superior air by which one recognizes the knight of infinity. He takes delight in everything and takes part in everything; and every time one sees him take part in something in particular, he does it with a persistence that marks the earthly man whose soul is bound up with such things. He attends to his work. Seeing him at it, one would think he was a clerk who had lost his soul in Italian double-entry bookkeeping, so punctual is he. He takes Sundays off. He goes to church. No heavenly gaze, no sign of the incommensurable gives him away; without knowing him, one could not possibly single him out from the rest of the crowd, for his hearty, vigorous hymn-singing proves at most that he has a good pair of lungs. In the afternoon he walks out to the woods. He delights in everything he sees: in the bustle of people, the new omnibuses, the Sound. When one meets him on the Strandvej, one would think he was a petty shopkeeper out on the town for once — that is exactly how he enjoys himself; for he is no poet, and I have tried in vain to worm out of him the incommensurability of the poet. Toward evening he walks home, his gait as tireless as a postman’s. On the way he thinks that his wife is sure to have a special little hot dish waiting for him when he gets home — a roast lamb’s head with vegetables, for example. If he met a kindred spirit, he could go on all the way to Østerport talking with him about this dish, with a passion worthy of a restaurant keeper. As it happens, he does not have four skilling to his name, and yet he believes firmly and fully that his wife has that delicious dish waiting for him. If she has it, it would be an enviable sight for people of rank, and an inspiring one for ordinary folk, to watch him eat; for his appetite outdoes Esau’s. His wife has no such dish — and, oddly enough, he is exactly the same. Along the way he goes past a building lot and runs into another man. They chat for a moment; in a flash he raises a building, and he has every resource at his command to do it. The stranger leaves him thinking that this must surely be a capitalist, while my admired knight thinks, “Yes, if it came to that, I could easily get it.” He leans out of an open window and looks at the square where he lives, at everything that goes on there — a rat slipping in under the plank over the gutter, the children playing. Everything engages him, with a calm in existence as if he were a girl of sixteen. And yet he is no genius; for I have tried in vain to spy out the incommensurability of genius in him. Of an evening he smokes his pipe; to look at him, you would swear it was the grocer from across the street, vegetating in the twilight. He lets things take their course with an unconcern as if he were a feckless good-for-nothing; and yet at every moment he lives he buys up the opportune time at the dearest price, for he does not do even the smallest thing except by virtue of the absurd. And yet, and yet — indeed, I could become furious about it, if only out of envy — and yet this man has made the movement of infinity, and makes it at every moment. In infinite resignation he drains the deep melancholy of existence; the blessedness of infinity is known to him; he has experienced the pain of forsaking everything, whatever is dearest to one in the world. And yet finitude tastes every bit as good to him as to someone who never knew anything higher; for his remaining in finitude bore no trace of the cowed, anxious drill of a trained animal, and yet he has the confidence to delight in it as if it were the most certain thing of all. And yet, and yet, the whole earthly shape he brings forth is a new creation by virtue of the absurd. He resigned everything infinitely, and then he took hold of everything again by virtue of the absurd. He constantly makes the movement of infinity, but he makes it with such precision and assurance that he constantly gets finitude out of it, and there is not a second in which one suspects anything else. It is said to be the hardest task for a dancer to leap into a particular position in such a way that there is not a single second in which he is reaching for the position, but in the leap itself he stands in the position. Perhaps no dancer can do it — but that knight does. Most people live lost in worldly sorrow and joy; they are the ones who sit out and do not get into the dance. The knights of infinity are dancers and have elevation. They make the movement upward and come down again; and this too is a pastime not without its blessedness, and not unlovely to watch. But every time they come down, they cannot take up the position at once; they waver for a moment, and this wavering shows that they are strangers in the world after all. It is more or less conspicuous according to how much art they have; but not even the most artful of these knights can conceal the wavering. One need not see them in the air; one need only see them at the moment they touch the ground and have touched it — and one knows them. But to be able to come down in such a way that in the same second one looks as if one were standing and walking; to turn the leap in life into walking; to express the sublime absolutely in the pedestrian — that is what only that knight can do, and it is the only marvel.
