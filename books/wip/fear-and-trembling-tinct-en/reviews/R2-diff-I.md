## ch7 ¶30 (text changed)

**before**

> Natures such as Gloucester’s cannot be saved by being mediated into an idea of society. Ethics really only makes fools of them — much as ethics would be mocking Sarah if it asked her: “Why don’t you express the universal and marry?” Such natures are in the paradox from the very ground up. They are by no means less perfect than other people; only, they are either lost in the demonic paradox or saved in the divine one. Now, time and again people have taken pleasure in the fact that witches, goblins, trolls and so on were misshapen creatures; and it is undeniable that every human being has an inclination, on seeing a misshapen creature, at once to attach to it the notion of a moral depravity. What a monstrous injustice! For the relation must rather be turned around: existence itself has corrupted them, the way a stepmother makes children turn out badly. To be placed outside the universal from the start, by nature or by historical circumstance — that is the beginning of the demonic, for which the individual himself, however, bears no guilt. In this way Cumberland’s Jew, too, is a demon, even though he does good. In this way the demonic can also show itself as contempt for people — a contempt, note carefully, that does not lead the demonic person himself to act contemptibly. On the contrary, his strength lies in knowing that he is better than all those who judge him. — About all such things the poet, of all people, ought to be the first to raise the alarm. God knows what books the younger versifiers now living read! Their study probably consists in learning rhymes by heart. God knows what their significance in existence is! At this moment I do not know that they are of any use except that they furnish an edifying proof of the immortality of the soul: one can confidently say of them to oneself what Baggesen says of the town poet Kildevalle — “if he becomes immortal, then we all will.” — What has been said here on the occasion of Sarah was said chiefly with a view to poetic production, and therefore on a fanciful presupposition. But it has its full significance if one wanted, with psychological interest, to go deeply into the meaning of the old saying: nullum unquam exstitit magnum ingenium sine aliqua dementia — there has never been a great genius without some touch of madness. For this dementia is the genius’s suffering in existence. It is the expression of — if I dare say so — the divine envy, while the genius in him is the expression of the divine partiality. So from the beginning the genius is disoriented in relation to the universal and brought into relation with the paradox. This holds whether, in despair over his limit — which in his eyes turns his omnipotence into impotence — he seeks a demonic reassurance, and therefore will not admit it either before God or before people, or whether he reassures himself religiously in love for the deity. Here lie psychological tasks to which, it seems to me, one could gladly sacrifice a whole life; and yet a word about them is so rarely heard. What relation does madness bear to genius? Can the one be constructed out of the other? In what sense, and to what extent, is the genius in command of his madness? For it is obvious that up to a point he is in command of it — otherwise he really would be mad. For such observations, however, ingenuity is required in a high degree, and love as well; for to observe someone superior is very difficult. If one were to read through some of the most brilliant authors with attention to this, it might perhaps be possible, once in a great while and with much effort, to discover a little.

## ch7 ¶31 (text changed)

**before**

> I will imagine one more case: an individual who wants to save the universal by being concealed and by his silence. I can use the legend of Faust for this. Faust is a doubter, an apostate of the spirit who goes the way of the flesh. That is the poets’ view of him. And while it is said again and again that every age has its Faust, the age’s poets nonetheless go, one after another, undaunted down the same beaten track. Let us make a small change. Faust is the doubter κατ’ ἐξοχήν — the doubter above all others; but he is a sympathetic nature. Even in Goethe’s conception of Faust I miss a deeper psychological insight into doubt’s secret conversations with itself. In our time, when after all everyone has experienced doubt, no poet has yet taken a single step in that direction. So I think I could well offer them royal bonds to write on, so that they could write down all the many things they have experienced in this respect — they would hardly write more than would fit in the front margin.

## ch7 ¶32 (text changed)

**before**

> Only when Faust is bent back into himself in this way — only then can doubt look poetic; only then, too, does he himself actually discover all its sufferings in actuality. He knows, then, that it is spirit that sustains existence. But he also knows that the security and joy in which people live are not grounded in the power of spirit, but are easily explained as an unreflective bliss. As a doubter — as the doubter — he is higher than all of this. And if anyone tries to deceive him by claiming to have gone through doubt himself, Faust easily sees through it. For whoever has made a movement in the world of spirit — that is, an infinite movement — can tell at once, just by listening to what someone says, whether the one speaking is a tried man or a Münchhausen. What a Tamerlane can do with his Huns, Faust knows he can do with his doubt: startle people up in terror, make existence sway under their feet, split people apart, make the cry of anxiety sound everywhere. And if he does it, he is still no Tamerlane; in a certain sense he is authorized — he has thought’s authorization. But Faust is a sympathetic nature. He loves existence; his soul knows no envy; he sees that he cannot stop the frenzy he may well be able to arouse; he craves no Herostratic fame. He keeps silent. He hides the doubt in his soul more carefully than a girl hides the fruit of a sinful love beneath her heart. He tries as well as he can to walk in step with other people; but what goes on inside him he consumes within himself, and in this way he brings himself as a sacrifice for the universal.

## ch7 ¶35 (text changed)

**before**

> If he keeps silent, ethics condemns him. For it says: “You shall acknowledge the universal, and it is precisely by speaking that you acknowledge it; and you may not have pity on the universal.” This consideration ought not to be forgotten when one sometimes judges a doubter harshly because he speaks. I am not inclined to judge such conduct leniently; but here, as everywhere, what matters is that the movements happen in the normal way. If it comes to the worst, a doubter — even if by speaking he brought every possible misfortune on the world — is still far preferable to these miserable sweet-tooths who taste a little of everything, who want to cure doubt without knowing it, and who are therefore, as a rule, the immediate occasion for doubt’s breaking out wildly and uncontrollably. — If he speaks, he throws everything into confusion. For only afterward does he learn whether it does not come to that after all; and the outcome cannot help a person — neither in the moment of action nor as regards the responsibility.

## ch7 ¶41 (text changed)

**before**

> Aesthetics permitted — indeed, demanded — silence of the single individual when by keeping silent he could save another. This alone is enough to show that Abraham does not fall within the range of aesthetics. His silence is by no means meant to save Isaac — just as, in general, his whole task, to sacrifice Isaac for his own sake and for God’s sake, is an offense to aesthetics. For aesthetics can certainly understand my sacrificing myself, but not my sacrificing someone else for my own sake. The aesthetic hero was silent. Ethics, however, condemned him, because he was silent by virtue of his accidental particularity. What determined him to keep silent was his human foreknowledge. This ethics cannot forgive: any such human knowledge is only an illusion. Ethics demands an infinite movement; it demands disclosure. So the aesthetic hero can speak, but will not.
