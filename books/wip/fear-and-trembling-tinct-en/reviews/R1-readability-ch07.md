## 1. Paragraph-by-paragraph comprehension

**[0]** Thesis: the ethical is the universal, and the universal is what is open to view. The individual is naturally hidden, and his ethical job is to reveal himself. Staying hidden is sin and "spiritual trial". *(Claim. This frames the whole chapter.)*

**[1]** Same fork as Problemata I and II. If there is no justified concealment, Abraham is indefensible, because he "passed over the intermediate ethical authorities". If there is one, we are at the paradox. Hegel is consistent in demanding disclosure, but he is confused when he also praises Abraham's faith. Faith is a "later immediacy", not the aesthetic "first immediacy". *(Claim plus a jab at Hegel. I followed the either/or but not the "immediacy" argument.)*

**[2]** Johannes says he will look at the question "purely aesthetically" for a while, starting with the category of "the interesting": the crisis, the turning point. Nobody can make himself interesting; it is a privilege paid for in pain, as in Socrates' case. Because the category lies on the border between aesthetics and ethics, the discussion will stray into ethics. He ends with a jab at "the System". *(Announces a digression and mocks the age and Hegel.)*

**[3]** Aristotle names recognition as a part of plot. Recognition presupposes earlier concealment, which creates the dramatic tension. In Greek tragedy, concealment comes from fate: the hero does not know, and so Greek tragedy is "blind". In modern drama, concealing and revealing are the hero's free acts, and he is responsible for them. *(Example plus historical claim. This moves concealment from fate to freedom.)*

**[4]** Modern drama is full of concealment. A joke about readers so "inflamed" that they can produce ten novels at the mere word. The rule: hiding nonsense makes comedy, and hiding something tied to "the idea" can make tragedy. Example: the bald man in a wig who has won a girl. Aesthetics laughs at him. The comic is set aside. *(Example and dismissal. The irony works.)*

**[5]** Method statement. He will trace concealment through aesthetics and then ethics, so that aesthetic concealment and the paradox show their "absolute difference". *(Signpost.)*

**[6]** Two lovers each hide their love out of noble self-sacrifice. Aesthetics, which is sentimental, uses a coincidence to reveal the secrets and crowns them heroes, with no regard for time. *(Example plus satire of aesthetics.)*

**[7]** Ethics has no coincidences and no sentimentality. It condemns playing Providence, whether by action or by suffering. It trusts reality, not clever calculation, and it "takes offense" at the lovers' secret. *(Counter-claim: the ethical verdict on the same case.)*

**[8]** Summary: aesthetics rewards concealment, and ethics punishes it. *(Recap.)*

**[9]** A qualification: sometimes aesthetics demands disclosure, namely when the hero's act interferes with another person's life. Agamemnon in Euripides: aesthetics wants him silent, yet it also wants him tested by the women's tears, so it has an old servant reveal everything. *(Concession/complication plus example. This bridges to the tragic hero.)*

**[10]** Ethics has no servant, so Agamemnon himself must tell Iphigenia. His silence might spare others or spare himself. As a tragic hero he must expose himself to every counter-argument, including tears, and Iphigenia should get her two months to weep, like Jephthah's daughter. *(Claim: the tragic hero discloses.)*

**[11]** Summary: aesthetics demanded disclosure but settled for coincidence, and ethics demanded disclosure and got it in the tragic hero. *(Recap.)*

**[12]** A concession against ethics: secrecy and silence do make a person great (Amor and Psyche). The tragic hero is merely human and understandable. Beyond him lies the paradox, which has two faces, the divine and the demonic, because silence belongs to both. *(Turn. This sets up the rest of the chapter's split between demonic and divine silence.)*

**[13]** He will "call forth" some poetic figures and push them to their extremes. The footnote thanks Lessing and jokes that people today explain more than they understand. *(Transition plus a digressive footnote.)*

**[14]** The Delphi story. The augurs predict disaster from the bridegroom's marriage, so he walks past the waiting bride. This is an extended lyrical scene of the unknowing bride. The footnote says the historical revenge plot does not matter. *(Example, told poetically.)*

**[15]** Dialectical analysis of the bridegroom's options: (1) keep silent and marry, which wrongs the girl; (2) keep silent and not marry, a "mystification" that aesthetics might like but that still offends her; (3) speak, which makes it an unhappy-love tragedy like Axel and Valborg, complicated because the misfortune is his alone. The long footnote: a "romantic union" without marriage, then a rant that poets waste time on trivial love mix-ups when "only passion against passion" makes a collision. *(Analysis of the example plus a digression.)*

**[16]** Ethics requires him to speak. His courage lies in giving up aesthetic magnanimity, but only if he genuinely felt its pull first. That is the jab at an age that skips the "middle term". *(Verdict on the example.)*

**[17]** Why the example matters: the augur's pronouncement is public. Everyone in Greece can understand it, so he can speak and make himself understood, and his silence would be self-indulgent. If heaven had spoken to him privately, he would be at the paradox: unable to speak, suffering, and finding rest. A wish that aesthetics would move toward the religious. Queen Elizabeth biting her finger for ten days is offered as a poet's task. *(This key paragraph sets public versus private revelation as the dividing line. It ends with a vivid side example.)*

**[18]** Agnete and the merman, retold. Agnete's innocent, trusting look disarms the seducer, who brings her home, and his despair rages. The legend's Agnete wanted "the interesting", and only innocence protects a girl. The footnote gives an alternative version in which a melancholy merman is inflamed by a wild Agnete and she becomes a mermaid. *(Example "in the direction of the demonic".)*

**[19]** The merman is given a human consciousness. Being a merman stands for a guilty past. Now two powers pull at him: repentance alone, which means concealment, or Agnete plus repentance, which means disclosure. *(Sets up the dilemma.)*

**[20]** If he stays hidden in repentance, Agnete is made unhappy and he suffers more. The "demonic in repentance" tells him the suffering is his punishment. *(First branch.)*

**[21]** The demonic merman might try to "save" Agnete by making her stop loving him through scorn and mockery, torturing himself in the process. Her pain will torment him. *(This develops the demonic branch. The psychology is dense.)*

**[22]** In the demonic, the merman becomes a single individual above the universal, which is a counterpart and counterfeit of the divine paradox. His suffering looks like proof that his silence is justified, yet he can speak, and if he does he is a grand tragic hero. The footnote mocks aesthetics' happy-ending marriages. *(Analogy plus a warning that it deceives.)*

**[23]** If repentance saves him, there are two routes. (a) He stays hidden and trusts God to save Agnete, which is the "counter-paradox" and the monastery. (b) He is saved by Agnete through disclosure and marries her, but this still needs the paradox, because guilt has put him outside the universal. There is a new remark on sin: sin is a "later immediacy", and ethics that takes sin seriously goes beyond itself. The footnote says repentance is the highest ethical expression and also ethics' self-contradiction. *(Deepens the argument. This is the hardest paragraph.)*

**[24]** None of this explains Abraham, who was righteous and not a sinner. The analogy would only appear after the sinner is restored to the universal. *(Return to Abraham, as a disclaimer.)*

**[25]** He understands the merman but not Abraham. The three outcomes are demon, monastery or greatest human. The merman needs a second movement "by virtue of the absurd" to return to reality after repentance. This becomes a long satire on the age: it thinks it has reached "the highest", scorns the monastery without surpassing it, doesn't know the absurd, and demands comedy. *(Conclusion of the merman plus a long satirical digression.)*

**[26]** Tobit's Sarah: seven bridegrooms killed on the wedding night by a demon who loves her. Johannes cuts the comic "seven" and presents Sarah as the saddest of women, unhappy before she ever could become so. The mother's farewell and the wedding-night prayer follow. *(New example, "more moving" because it involves no guilt or repentance.)*

**[27]** Poets would make Tobias the hero. Johannes says Sarah is the heroine: she must have the humility and faith to let herself be healed and to accept the risk to her beloved. *(Reverses the example, as with the merman: receiving is harder than giving.)*

**[28]** Make Sarah a man and the demonic appears. A proud nature cannot bear pity for a guiltless misfortune, and he might choose the demonic ("I can become a Bluebeard"). Shakespeare's Gloucester became a demon because he couldn't bear the pity he had lived with since childhood. *(Variation: innocent exclusion leading to the demonic.)*

**[29]** The Richard III monologue, in German with an English crib. *(Quotation.)*

**[30]** Such natures cannot be "mediated" into society. They are either lost in the demonic paradox or saved in the divine one. Misshapen beings are not morally worse; existence made them that way. Contempt can be demonic, as in Cumberland's Jew. A jab at young poets (Kildevalle), then genius and madness: genius is placed outside the universal from birth. *(Generalisation plus satire plus a psychological digression.)*

**[31]** The last case is someone who hides in order to save the universal: Faust, recast as a sympathetic doubter. Poets have not explored doubt. The footnote offers an alternative figure, the sympathetic ironist, who keeps silent to spare people but may not be allowed to. *(New example.)*

**[32]** Faust knows he could shake the world with doubt, like Tamerlane, but he loves people, so he hides his doubt and sacrifices himself for the universal. *(Develops the example.)*

**[33]** Johannes tests Faust further ("torturer of heroes"): Faust loves Margaret but tells neither his doubt nor his love. *(Raises the stakes.)*

**[34]** A parenthetical mocks shallow doubters. Faust is too ideal for chatter: he either keeps silent to sacrifice himself or speaks and causes chaos. *(Satirical aside, then restates the dilemma.)*

**[35]** Ethics condemns his silence: acknowledge the universal by speaking, and do not pity it. A real doubter who speaks is still better than dabblers. Speaking causes chaos, and the outcome can't justify it. *(Ethical verdict.)*

**[36]** Silent on his own responsibility, he may be magnanimous, but the universal torments him with the question of whether it was hidden pride. *(Consequence.)*

**[37]** Only if he stands as the single individual in absolute relation to the absolute is his silence authorised. Then his doubt must become guilt, and he is in the paradox. *(The divine-paradox branch, stated very briefly.)*

**[38]** The New Testament endorses such silence, and even irony that hides something better. Jab at Hegel's view of irony. The Sermon on the Mount on fasting shows subjectivity is incommensurable with actuality. *(Scriptural support plus satire.)*

**[39]** Back to Abraham. The preceding cases were not analogies. They were meant to show, where they deviate, the boundary of "the unknown land". Only the paradox of sin comes near, and it is easier than Abraham. *(Return and method statement.)*

**[40]** Abraham said nothing to Sarah, Eliezer or Isaac. These three are the family, the ethical's highest expression for him. *(Fact, which resolves the "intermediate authorities" of [1].)*

**[41]** Abraham is not aesthetic: his silence doesn't save anyone, and sacrificing another for one's own sake offends aesthetics. The aesthetic hero could speak but wouldn't. *(Elimination 1.)*

**[42]** He is not a tragic hero: he does nothing for the universal and is hidden. *(Elimination 2.)*

**[43]** So either an individual can stand in absolute relation to the absolute, in which case the ethical is not highest, or Abraham is lost. *(Either/or.)*

**[44]** This may make the paradox look easy. It isn't: distress and anxiety are its only possible justification. *(Warning.)*

**[45]** Abraham cannot speak, because he cannot make himself understood, and the "test" is unsayable. The tragic hero, by contrast, has heard every objection, which is a comfort, and won't cry out like Edward IV. *(Contrast.)*

**[46]** Edward IV's lines, in German with an English crib. *(Quotation.)*

**[47]** The tragic hero can weep with his family, but Abraham can't even comfort anyone, since they'd ask why he doesn't just stop, or think him a hypocrite. "He speaks in tongues." *(Pathos.)*

**[48]** Johannes understands the distress and admires Abraham, but lacks his courage. Abraham could stop at any moment by calling it a spiritual trial, and then he would no longer be Abraham. *(Confession.)*

**[49]** The unsayable thing is that the ethical is the temptation. Even less sayable is the movement of faith: "it will not happen, or God will give a new Isaac". Iphigenia can understand Agamemnon's resignation, but not a claim "by virtue of the absurd". *(Deepens the contrast by linking to the double movement.)*

**[50]** Abraham's single recorded word to Isaac: "God will seek out... the lamb." *(Text.)*

**[51]** This word is essential to the story. *(Signpost.)*

**[52]–[53]** When should a tragic hero have a last word? If his significance lies in outward deeds (Agamemnon), silence is fitting. If it lies "in the direction of spirit", he needs a last word to "carry himself through". *(General rule.)*

**[54]** Socrates is the example: his ironic last word after the verdict (surprise at the three-vote majority) is his victory over death. *(Example.)*

**[55]** Abraham, as father of faith, needs a last word too, but no one could have invented it in advance. *(Application.)*

**[56]** Yet speaking contradicts the paradox, and a disclosure like "It is you" would be weakness and a fall out of the paradox. *(Complication.)*

**[57]** Resolution: his answer is ironic, saying nothing while saying something. It expresses the double movement: he knows Isaac must die (resignation), yet by virtue of the absurd God might do otherwise (faith), so it is not a lie. If God were to do the killing, the vagueness would be trivial. Because Abraham must act, his words show both movements. *(Climax of the chapter.)*

**[58]** One can understand Abraham only as one understands the paradox, and Johannes lacks the courage to speak so. *(Confession.)*

**[59]** The tragic hero was admired. Abraham was understood by no one, but he stayed true to his love, and God remembers his tears. *(Pathos and consolation.)*

**[60]** Final either/or: the paradox, or Abraham is lost. *(Conclusion.)*

## 2. The argument as a chain

1. The ethical = the universal = what is disclosed. Hiddenness is the natural state of the individual, and ethically it must be overcome ([0]).
2. So either there is a justified concealment grounded in the individual being higher than the universal, or Abraham, who hid from his family, is indefensible ([1], [40]).
3. To locate that justified concealment, Johannes runs concealment through aesthetics and ethics to show that aesthetic concealment and the paradox are absolutely different ([2]–[5]).
4. **Aesthetics** loves concealment because it creates tension ("the interesting", recognition). It rewards magnanimous silence and solves things by coincidence ([3]–[6], [9]). **Ethics** demands disclosure, refuses coincidence and punishes concealment ([7]–[8], [10]–[11]).
5. The **tragic hero** (Agamemnon) discloses: he tells Iphigenia himself and exposes himself to every objection ([10]).
6. Silence can also be great. Beyond the tragic hero, silence is either demonic or divine, and these are the two faces of the paradox ([12]).
7. **Delphi bridegroom:** his warning is public (an augur), so he can make himself understood. Ethics requires him to speak. His silence would only be self-flattering magnanimity. If the warning had been private, he would be at the paradox: unable to speak and suffering. This separates *public* grounds for silence, which can be spoken, from *private* God-relation, which cannot ([14]–[17]).
8. **Agnete and the merman:** guilt (sin) puts the individual outside the universal. Demonic silence imitates the divine paradox (an absolute relation to the demonic) and deceives, because suffering seems to justify it. The merman *can* speak. Outcomes: demon (concealed repentance), monastery (concealed trust in God), or disclosure and marriage, which require the "absurd" movement to regain actuality ([18]–[25]).
9. Sin is a "later immediacy". Ethics that takes sin seriously exceeds itself. The paradox of sin is the nearest analogy to Abraham, but it doesn't fit, because Abraham is righteous ([23]–[24], [39]).
10. **Tobit's Sarah:** guiltless exclusion from the universal. In a woman, it calls for humility and faith to let herself be healed. **Gloucester/Richard III:** the same exclusion in a proud man, who cannot bear pity, becomes demonic. Such natures are either lost in the demonic paradox or saved in the divine ([26]–[30]).
11. **Faust (and the ironist):** someone who keeps silent *for* the universal, to spare others his doubt. Ethics still condemns him for pitying the universal. Only an absolute relation to the absolute could authorise his silence, turning doubt into guilt. The New Testament (fasting in secret) endorses such hidden inwardness ([31]–[38]).
12. None of these explain Abraham. They mark the border ([39]).
13. Abraham's silence saves no one, so it is not aesthetic. He does nothing for the universal, so he is not a tragic hero ([41]–[42]).
14. He *cannot* speak: what he would say (a test in which the ethical is the temptation, plus faith by virtue of the absurd) cannot be understood. That is his distress and anxiety ([44]–[49]).
15. Yet as a figure of spirit he needs a last word. His answer to Isaac is irony that says nothing false and nothing definite, and it holds both resignation and faith ([50]–[57]).
16. **Final either/or:** either the single individual can stand in an absolute relation to the absolute (the paradox), or Abraham is lost ([43], [60]).

### Terms as this text uses them

- **Concealment:** hiding something from others, whether a plan, a love or a fate. It can be a plot device (fate, in Greek tragedy), a free act (modern drama), aesthetic magnanimity, demonic self-enclosure, or the divine paradox.
- **Disclosure:** making oneself known to others, which puts oneself into the universal. It is ethically required.
- **Silence / speaking:** speaking means being *understood*, which brings relief and places one in the universal ([45]). Silence is ambiguous: noble, demonic or divine ([12]). Abraham "speaks" without speaking.
- **The aesthetic / aesthetics:** the world of poetry and drama and its standards. It loves suspense, magnanimity and coincidence. It is personified as a "polite and sentimental science". It is also the "first immediacy" ([1]).
- **Ethics / the ethical:** the universal standard that applies to everyone. It demands disclosure, accepts no coincidence, and for Abraham is expressed as family ([40]).
- **The interesting:** the category of the crisis or turning point. It sits on the border between aesthetics and ethics, and it is a privilege bought with pain. The text introduces it but barely uses it again (only Agnete "demands the interesting", [18]).
- **The demonic:** the individual's absolute relation to evil or self-enclosure. It is a counterfeit of the divine paradox, arising from guilt or from guiltless exclusion (deformity, pity).
- **Repentance:** the guilty person's infinite movement of remorse. It is "the highest ethical expression" and also ethics' self-contradiction. It can turn demonic.
- **The paradox:** the single individual as such, higher than the universal and in absolute relation to the absolute. It cannot be mediated or put into words. The text also speaks of a "demonic paradox", a "counter-paradox" and "the paradox of sin".
- **The universal:** what applies to and is understandable by all. It is equated with the ethical and with "mediation".
- **The single individual:** the person as a particular, set against the universal. Sometimes it is used loosely for just "any person" ([17]).
- **Irony:** saying something while saying nothing, which hides inwardness ([38], [57]). It is also Socrates' "world-force" ([54]).
- **Spiritual trial:** the temptation or ordeal of the ethical when it pulls at someone. It is used for the concealer's sin ([0]), for tears as an ordeal ([9]), for the universal's torment of the doubter ([36]), and for Abraham's possible retreat ([48], [56]).

## 3. Comprehension failures

### SEVERE

1. **[1] "For faith is not the first immediacy but a later one. The first immediacy is the aesthetic... But faith is not the aesthetic — or else there has never been faith, because there has always been faith."** "Immediacy" is never explained, and the closing riddle reads as a contradiction. I reconstructed the meaning ("if faith were just natural feeling, everyone would always have it, so it would not be faith") only by guessing. *IDEA plus WORDING (unglossed term).* This returns in [23] ("Sin is a later immediacy"; "the immediate is to be cancelled") and in the [23] footnote ("reach in immediate categories"), where it seems to contradict "faith is not the first immediacy". I relied on vague outside knowledge of Hegel.

2. **[23] "because it is a contradiction on the universal's part to want to demand itself of one who lacks the conditio sine qua non"** Which condition is meant: innocence, or the ability to fulfil it? What is the "demonic paradox"? And "If philosophy... gave a thought to the possibility that it might occur to a human being to want to act on its teaching, a strange comedy would come of it": I can't connect this joke to the sin point. *Mostly IDEA. The referent of "conditio sine qua non" is a WORDING gap.*

3. **[25] "Consider merely the thought of taking the time onto one's conscience in this way: of giving conscience time, in its sleepless tirelessness, to search out every secret thought, so that, if one does not make the movement at every moment by virtue of what is noblest and holiest in a human being, one may discover with anxiety and horror — and, if by nothing else, then by anxiety lure out — the dark stirring..."** This is garbled. "Taking the time onto one's conscience" is not idiomatic English, and the nested clauses lose the subject. I got the gist only from the monastery context: solitude lets conscience find the evil hidden in you. *WORDING.* Suggest splitting it, for example: "Consider what it means to give your conscience time: time, in its sleepless persistence, to search out every secret thought. Unless you make the movement at every moment by virtue of what is noblest in you, you will discover with anxiety and horror the dark stirring that hides in every human life, or at least lure it out by anxiety."

4. **[39] "It was not that Abraham would thereby become more understandable, but that his incomprehensibility might become more desultory (in the old sense: jumping from one thing to another)."** Even with the gloss, "desultory" makes no sense here. Why would incomprehensibility "jump"? *WORDING.* Suggest: "...but that his incomprehensibility might stand out more sharply" or "...stand out in sharper relief". The next sentence, "they could, at the moment of their deviation, like a compass needle's, as it were indicate the boundary", is also clumsy: "like a compass needle's" dangles. 5. **[54] "raised the suspicion that, in him, irony's elasticity was not a world-force but a game, whose springiness at the decisive moment had to be used by the reverse measure — to hold him up with pathos."** I could not parse the last clause. *WORDING.* Suggest: "...but a game, whose elasticity would, at the decisive moment, have had to be turned the other way, into pathos, to hold him up."

- **[41] "Ethics demands an infinite movement"**: earlier, "infinite movement" meant resignation, but here it means disclosure. **"What determined him to keep silent was his human foreknowledge."**: foreknowledge of what? *WORDING.* Suggest "his human calculation of what silence would achieve".
- **[44] "it may again seem here that the paradox is the easiest and simplest thing of all"**: why it would seem easy is not clear. *IDEA.*
- **[52]–[54] "intellectual significance", "intellectual tragic hero"**: "intellectual" suggests cleverness, but the text means "of spirit" ("in the direction of spirit"). The term is inconsistent within the passage. *WORDING.* Suggest "a tragic hero of the spirit".

### Term consistency notes
- "Spiritual trial" (four senses) vs "temptation" ([49]) vs "test" ([45], [49]). "Test" (Abraham's ordeal from God) and "spiritual trial" (the ethical as temptation) are kept distinct, which is good, but [49] then says "the ethical is the temptation", a third word for the "spiritual trial" idea.
- "The single individual": technical in most places, loose in [17].
- "Intellectual" vs "spirit" ([52]–[55]).
- "Ethics" and "the ethical", "aesthetics" and "the aesthetic" alternate. This is mostly harmless because aesthetics/ethics are personified as "sciences".
- "Cancelled" ([1], [16], [23], [44]) vs "annuls" ([56]) is acceptable.

### Voice
Humour lands well: the partridge, the bald man in a wig, aesthetics that "knows more ways out than any manager of the public pawnshop", the ballet master, Kildevalle, *tortor heroum*, Faust "in slippers", the scholarly doubters who doubt for one hour a semester. Pathos lands strongly in the Delphi bride, Sarah and her mother, and [45]–[47] and [59]. The satire of the age in [25] is weakened by the garbled sentence noted above. The sly irony of [2] ("predicate", "System") is mostly lost on a newcomer.

### Outside knowledge relied on
Hegel (mediation, immediacy, the System); Oedipus and Iphigenia in Tauris ([3] examples); Euripides' plot; Jephthah's daughter; Amor and Psyche; Axel and Valborg; Laban and Jacob; the Elizabeth and Essex ring story (partly given); the Agnete ballad; Tobit (the demon, the fish exorcism); Richard III; Cumberland's *The Jew*; Baggesen; Goethe's Faust (Margaret, the mirror); Tamerlane, Herostratus, Münchhausen; Socrates' trial; Diogenes Laertius; Genesis 22. None of these is essential except Tobit's smoke ritual and Cumberland, where the text gives too little.

## 4. Verdict

**Mostly yes, with a hard middle.** A newcomer can follow the chapter's spine from the text alone: the ethics-demands-disclosure framework, the aesthetic vs ethical treatment of concealment, the public/private test in the Delphi case, the demonic vs divine silence, Abraham's inability to speak, the irony of his last word, and the closing either/or. The examples are vivid, and the end of the chapter ([39]–[60]) is clear and moving.

Comprehension breaks down in the dense theoretical passages: "immediacy" ([1], [23]), the sin/counter-paradox material ([23]–[24]), the doubt-into-guilt step ([37]), and several sentences where the English itself is garbled ([25], [39], [54]). The idea-level obstacles are Kierkegaard's own. The wording obstacles (the five SEVERE items and roughly 15 MODERATE referent/idiom problems) are fixable, and fixing them would make the chapter readable end to end for a careful newcomer.
