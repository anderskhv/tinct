## ch4 ¶4 (text changed)

**before**

> How is one to explain a contradiction like that speaker’s? Is it because Abraham has acquired a prescriptive right to be a great man, so that whatever he does is great, while if someone else does the same it is sin, a sin that cries to heaven? In that case I do not wish to take part in such thoughtless praise. If faith cannot make it a holy act to be willing to murder one’s son, then let the verdict on Abraham be the same as on anyone else. If people perhaps lack the courage to carry their thought through, and to say that Abraham was a murderer, then surely it is better to acquire that courage than to waste time on undeserved eulogies. The ethical expression for what Abraham did is that he was willing to murder Isaac; the religious expression is that he was willing to sacrifice Isaac. But precisely in this contradiction lies the anxiety that can indeed make a person sleepless; and yet without this anxiety Abraham is not the one he is. Or perhaps Abraham did not do at all what the story tells; perhaps, because of the conditions of those times, it was something quite different. Then let us forget him; for what is the point of remembering a past that cannot become a present? Or perhaps that speaker had forgotten something that corresponds to the ethical forgetting of the fact that Isaac was the son. For when faith is taken away by being reduced to nil and nothing, all that remains is the raw fact that Abraham was willing to murder Isaac — and that is easy enough to imitate for anyone who does not have faith, that is, the faith that makes it hard for him.

## ch4 ¶6 (text changed)

**before**

> Can one, then, speak about Abraham without reserve, and not run the risk that someone will go astray and do the same? If I do not dare, I will keep entirely silent about Abraham; and above all I will not belittle him in such a way that he becomes, precisely through that, a snare for the weak. For if one makes faith everything — that is, what it is — then I think one dares to speak of it without danger in our time, which hardly runs to excess in faith; and it is only by faith that one comes to resemble Abraham, not by murder. If one makes love a fleeting mood, a sensual stirring in a person, then one only lays snares for the weak when one wants to talk about the exploits of love. Everyone, no doubt, has a passing movement of feeling; but if everyone therefore wanted to do the terrible thing that love has sanctified as an immortal exploit, then all is lost — both the exploit and the one who has gone astray.

## ch4 ¶7 (text changed)

**before**

> So one could, I suppose, speak about Abraham; for what is great can never do harm when it is understood in its greatness. It is like a double-edged sword: it kills and it saves. If the lot fell to me to speak about it, I would start by showing how devout and God-fearing a man Abraham was, worthy to be called God’s elect. Only such a man is given such a test; but who is such a man? Next I would describe how Abraham loved Isaac. To that end I would ask all good spirits to stand by me, so that my speech might glow as a father’s love glows. I hope to portray it so that few fathers in all the King’s realms and lands would dare to claim that they loved like that. But if a father did not love as Abraham did, then surely any thought of sacrificing Isaac would be a spiritual trial — an inner assault on a person. One could already speak about this for several Sundays; there is no need to hurry, after all. The result, if it was spoken of rightly, would be that a good many fathers would not ask to hear any more at all, but for the time being would be glad if they really managed to love as Abraham loved. If there was then one who, after hearing what is great but also what is terrible in Abraham’s deed, dared to set out on the road, I would saddle my horse and ride with him. At every stage, until we came to Mount Moriah, I would explain to him that he could still turn back: he could repent of the misunderstanding that he was called to be put to the proof in such a struggle, and could confess that he lacked courage, so that God himself must take Isaac, if he would have him. I am convinced that such a person is not rejected, that he can attain blessedness along with everyone else — but not in time. Would people not, even in the most believing ages, judge such a person in this way? I have known a person who once could have saved my life, if he had been magnanimous. He put it plainly: “I can see well enough what I could do; but I do not dare. I fear that later I will lack the strength, that I will come to regret it.” He was not magnanimous; yet who would stop loving him on that account?

## ch4 ¶10 (text changed)

**before**

> I am not unacquainted with what has been admired in the world as great and magnanimous. My soul feels its kinship with it and is assured, in all humility, that the cause the hero fought for was mine too. In the moment of contemplation I call out to myself: jam tua res agitur — now it is your own affair that is at stake. I can think myself into the hero; into Abraham I cannot think myself. When I have reached the summit, I fall, because what is offered me there is a paradox. Yet I by no means think that faith is something inferior on that account. On the contrary, I think it is the highest; and I think it is dishonest of philosophy to give out something else instead and to scoff at faith. Philosophy cannot give faith and is not supposed to; but it ought to understand itself, know what it is offering, and take nothing away — least of all cheat people out of something as though it were nothing. I am not unacquainted with the distress and dangers of life; I do not fear them, and I go to meet them boldly. I am no stranger to the terrible. My memory is a faithful wife, and my imagination is — what I myself am not — an industrious little girl who sits quietly at her work all day and in the evening knows how to chatter to me so prettily that I cannot help looking at it, although what she paints is not always landscapes or flowers or Schäfer-Historier (shepherds’ idylls). I have looked the terrible in the eye; I do not flee from it in fear. But I know very well that even if I go to meet it with courage, my courage is still not the courage of faith, and is nothing to be compared with it. I cannot make the movement of faith. I cannot shut my eyes and plunge trustingly into the absurd; for me it is an impossibility — but I do not pride myself on that. I am convinced that God is love; this thought has for me a primal lyrical validity. When the thought is present to me, I am unspeakably blessed; when it is absent, I long for it more fiercely than a lover longs for the one he loves. But I do not have faith; this is the courage I lack. God’s love is, for me, in both a direct and an inverse sense, incommensurable — not measurable by any common standard — with the whole of actuality. I am not cowardly enough to whimper and complain about that, but neither am I underhanded enough to deny that faith is something far higher. I can bear living in my own way well enough; I am glad and content. But my joy is not faith’s joy, and compared with that it is unhappy. I do not bother God with my small sorrows; particular things do not concern me; I keep my eyes on my love alone and keep its virginal flame clean and clear. Faith is convinced that God concerns himself with the smallest things. I am content in this life to be married with the left hand; faith is humble enough to demand the right. For that this is humility I do not deny, and never shall.

## ch4 ¶11 (text changed)

**before**

> Is everyone in my time really capable of making the movements of faith? Unless I am much mistaken about my time, it tends rather to pride itself on doing what it presumably does not even think me capable of — namely, the imperfect. It goes against my soul to do what is so often done: to speak inhumanly about what is great, as if a few thousand years were an immense distance. I prefer to speak of it humanly, as if it had happened yesterday, and to let only greatness itself be the distance, a distance that either lifts up or judges. If, then (in the capacity of a tragic hero, for higher I cannot come), I had been summoned to such an extraordinary journey in the king’s service as the one to Mount Moriah, I know perfectly well what I would have done. I would not have been such a coward as to stay at home; I would not have dawdled along the highway either, or forgotten the knife to cause a little delay. I am fairly sure that I would have been there on the stroke of the hour, with everything in order — perhaps I would, if anything, have come too early, so that it could be over soon. But I also know what else I would have done. The moment I got up on the horse, I would have told myself: Now everything is lost. God demands Isaac; I sacrifice him, and all my joy with him — and yet God is love, and remains love for me. For in temporality God and I cannot talk with each other; we share no language. Perhaps someone or other in our time will be foolish enough, and envious enough of what is great, to want to persuade himself and me that had I really done this, I would have done something still greater than Abraham; for my enormous resignation was far more ideal and poetic than Abraham’s pettiness. And yet this is the greatest untruth; for my enormous resignation was the substitute for faith. Nor, then, could I do more than make the infinite movement, so as to find myself and to rest in myself again. Nor would I have loved Isaac as Abraham loved him. That I was resolute enough to make the movement might prove my courage, humanly speaking. That I loved him with all my soul is the premise without which the whole thing becomes a misdeed. But still I would not have loved as Abraham did; for then I would have held back even at the very last minute — without thereby arriving at Mount Moriah too late. Moreover, by my conduct I would have ruined the whole story; for had I received Isaac back, I would have been at a loss. What came easiest to Abraham would have been hard for me: to be glad in Isaac again! For the one who, with the whole infinity of his soul, proprio motu et propriis auspiciis — of his own motion and on his own authority — has made the infinite movement and can do no more, keeps Isaac only in pain.
