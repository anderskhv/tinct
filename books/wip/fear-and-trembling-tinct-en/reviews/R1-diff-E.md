**before**

> So the knight makes the movement — but which movement? Will he forget it all? After all, there is a kind of concentration in that too. No! For the knight is never at odds with himself, and to forget everything his life contained and still stay the same person is a contradiction. He has no urge to become someone else, and he by no means considers that the great thing. Only the lower natures forget themselves and become something new. The butterfly, for instance, has entirely forgotten that it was a caterpillar; perhaps it can in turn forget so entirely that it was a butterfly that it can become a fish. The deeper natures never forget themselves; they never turn into anything but what they were. So the knight will remember everything; but this remembering is precisely the pain, and yet in infinite resignation he is reconciled with existence. For him, his love for that princess became the expression of an eternal love. It took on a religious character; it was transfigured into a love for the eternal being — who did, it is true, deny the fulfillment, but who nevertheless reconciled him again in the eternal consciousness that his love is valid in a form of eternity that no reality can take from him. Fools and young people prattle that for a human being everything is possible. But this is a grave error. In terms of spirit, all things are possible; but in the world of finitude a great deal is not. Yet the knight makes this impossibility possible by expressing it spiritually — and he expresses it spiritually by renouncing it. The wish that would have led him out into reality, but was wrecked on the impossibility, is now bent inward; yet that does not mean it is lost, nor is it forgotten. Sometimes it is the dim stirrings of the wish within him that awaken the memory; sometimes he awakens it himself. For he is too proud to want what was the whole content of his life to have been the affair of a fleeting moment. He keeps this love young, and as he ages it increases with him, in years and in beauty. Yet he needs no finite occasion to make it grow. From the moment he has made the movement, the princess is lost. He has no need of those erotic tremblings of the nerves at the sight of the beloved, and so on. Nor does he need, in the finite sense, to be forever taking leave of her, because in the eternal sense he remembers her. And he knows very well that lovers who are so keen to see each other once more, in farewell, for the last time, are right to be keen, and right to think that it is the last time; for they are the ones who forget each other soonest. He has grasped the deep secret that even in loving another human being one ought to be sufficient to oneself. He no longer takes any finite account of what the princess does, and that is exactly the proof that he has made the movement infinitely. This is where one can get to see whether the movement in the single individual is true or feigned. Someone else, too, believed he had made the movement; but, look, time went on, and the princess did something else — married a prince, for instance — and then his soul lost the elasticity of resignation. By this he showed that he had not made the movement correctly; for whoever has resigned infinitely is sufficient to himself. The knight does not cancel his resignation. He preserves his love as young as it was in its first moment; he never lets it slip from him, precisely because he has made the movement infinitely. Nothing the princess does can disturb him. Only the lower natures find the law for their actions in another human being — the premises of their actions outside themselves. If, on the other hand, the princess is of a like mind, then something beautiful will come of it. She will then introduce herself into that order of knighthood into which no one is admitted by ballot, but of which everyone is a member who has the courage to introduce himself — the order of knighthood that proves its immortality by making no distinction between man and woman. She, too, will preserve her love young and healthy; she, too, will have got over her anguish, even if she does not, as the old song has it, “lie every night at her lord’s side.” These two will then suit each other for all eternity, with a harmonia praestabilita — a pre-established harmony — so perfectly in time that if ever the moment came (a moment that does not, however, occupy them in the finite sense, for then they would grow old), if ever the moment came that allowed their love to be given its expression in time, they would be able to begin exactly where they would have begun if they had been united from the start. Anyone who understands this, man or woman, can never be deceived; for only the lower natures imagine that they have been deceived. No girl who is not this proud really knows how to love; but if she is this proud, then all the cunning and cleverness in the world cannot deceive her.

> In infinite resignation there is peace and rest. Every human being who wills it — who has not debased himself by holding himself in contempt, which is even more terrible than being too proud — can train himself to make this movement, which, in its pain, reconciles a person to existence. Infinite resignation is that shirt spoken of in an old folk tale. The thread is spun under tears, bleached with tears, the shirt sewn in tears; but then it also protects better than iron and steel. The imperfection in the folk tale is that a third party can make up this linen. The secret of life is that everyone has to sew it for himself; and the remarkable thing is that a man can sew it every bit as well as a woman. In infinite resignation there is peace and rest and consolation in pain — provided, that is, the movement is made normally. I could, however, easily write a whole book if I set out to go through the various misunderstandings, the back-to-front positions, the sloppy movements I have come across in my own small practice alone. People believe very little in spirit; and yet it is precisely spirit that matters in making this movement. What matters is that the movement not be the one-sided result of a dira necessitas — a harsh necessity — and the more that necessity is present, the more doubtful it always becomes whether the movement is normal. So if one means to hold that cold, barren necessity must necessarily be present, one is saying that no one can experience death before he actually dies — which strikes me as crass materialism. But in our time people care less about making pure movements. Suppose someone who was to learn dancing said: “Generation upon generation has now spent centuries learning the positions; it is high time I turned this to my advantage and started straight in on the française.” People would probably laugh at him a little; but in the world of spirit this is found extremely plausible. What, then, is education? I thought it was the course the single individual runs through in order to catch up with himself; and whoever will not go through this course is helped very little by having been born in the most enlightened age.

> Let us now have the knight of faith appear in the case described. He does precisely what the other knight does. He gives up, infinitely, the love that is his life’s content, and he is reconciled in the pain.
