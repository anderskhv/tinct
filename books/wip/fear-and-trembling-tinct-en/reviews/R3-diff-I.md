# Part I: before → after R3 fixes (for re-verification)

## ch7 ¶33 (text changed)

**before**

> Now and then one can hear people complain, when some eccentric head stirs up a whirlwind of doubt; then they say: “If only he had kept silent!” Faust makes this idea real. Whoever has a notion of what it means for a human being to live on spirit also knows what doubt’s hunger is, and that the doubter is as hungry for life’s daily bread as for the nourishment of spirit. Now, all the pain Faust suffers may be a pretty good argument that it is not pride that has possessed him. Even so, I will use a little precaution, which I can easily invent. For just as Gregory of Rimini was called tortor infantium — torturer of infants — because he held that little children are damned, so I could be tempted to call myself tortor heroum — torturer of heroes; for I am most inventive where tormenting heroes is concerned. Faust sees Margaret — not after he has chosen pleasure, for my Faust does not choose pleasure at all. He does not see Margaret in Mephistopheles’s concave mirror; he sees her in all her lovable innocence. And since his soul has kept its love for people, he is also quite capable of falling in love with her. But he is a doubter; his doubt has annihilated actuality for him. For my Faust is so ideal that he does not belong among those scholarly doubters who doubt for one hour each semester from the lectern but otherwise can do everything else — just as they do the doubting too — without being in a state of spirit or acting by virtue of spirit. He is a doubter, and the doubter is as hungry for joy’s daily bread as for the food of spirit. Still, he stays true to his resolve and keeps silent: he speaks to no one about his doubt, and to Margaret not about his love either.

**before**

> If he keeps silent on his own responsibility, he may well act magnanimously; but on top of his other pain he will feel a small spiritual trial. For the universal will keep tormenting him, saying: “You ought to have spoken. How will you ever be certain that it was not a hidden arrogance, after all, that guided your decision?”
