## 1. Paragraph-by-paragraph reading

**[0] Claim.** In the outward world rewards go to whoever happens to hold them: the idle often get bread and luck decides. In the world of spirit, what you get depends on what you go through: only the person who suffers anxiety finds rest, and "only whoever draws the knife gets Isaac." Having famous ancestors counts for nothing. This sets up the whole chapter: understanding Abraham has to be worked for.

**[1] Claim + example.** Some kinds of knowledge think it is enough to *know* about greatness and skip the work. Thousands knew about Miltiades' victories, but only one person lost sleep over them. Many know Abraham's story by heart, but hardly anyone is kept awake by it.

**[2] Example (satire).** Preachers turn Abraham into a cosy cliché: "he gave God the best." "The best" lets you forget that it was his *son*, and so forgets the anxiety. The narrator imagines a sleepless listener who goes home to copy Abraham. The same pastor who preached Abraham without feeling anything now thunders at him and is proud of his own eloquence, without noticing that the man was only acting on last Sunday's sermon. It is both comic and tragic. The footnote adds a joke about the old saying "things don't go as the pastor preaches."

**[3] Continuation.** If the sinner is not persuaded, he ends up executed or in a madhouse, which is tragic. In another sense, though, Abraham has made him "happy," because the one who works does not perish. This refers back to [0].

**[4] Objection / dilemma.** How can the pastor praise in Abraham what he condemns in the sinner? Either Abraham gets a special exemption (which the narrator rejects), or we should honestly call him a murderer, or the story is not what it seems (then forget it). Ethically he was willing to *murder* Isaac. Religiously he was willing to *sacrifice* him. The anxiety lies in that gap. Take faith away and all that is left is the "raw fact" of murder, which anyone can copy.

**[5] Confession.** Johannes says he is not afraid to think things through. If he ever concluded that Abraham was a murderer he would keep quiet about it. But Abraham is not an illusion: he earned his fame.

**[6] Concession + analogy.** Can one speak of Abraham safely? Yes, if faith is made "everything," because that is the only way to resemble Abraham, and the age is in no danger of too much faith. The analogy is love. Treat love as a passing mood and stories of great love become traps for the weak.

**[7] Hypothetical sermon.** How Johannes would preach Abraham. He would start with Abraham's piety and his love for Isaac. He would warn that without such love, the thought of sacrificing Isaac would be a "spiritual trial." He would ride beside anyone who dared set out, and tell him at every stage that he may turn back. Anyone who turns back is not rejected. A personal anecdote follows: a man who dared not save Johannes's life is still loved.

**[8] Confession.** He would also tell his listeners that he himself does *not* have faith. He is a "shrewd head," and for such a head the movement of faith is hard. The difficulty is not a merit, though.

**[9] Irony + confession.** Poets honour love, but nobody honours faith. Philosophy "goes further," and theology flirts with philosophy. Hegel is supposedly hard and Abraham easy, but for Johannes it is the reverse. Hegel he can manage. Faced with Abraham he is "annihilated" by a paradox that his thinking cannot get into.

**[10] Confession + claim.** He can identify with heroes but not with Abraham. That does not make faith lower: it is the highest thing, and philosophy is dishonest to pass off something else as faith. He is brave and has an active imagination, but his courage is not the courage of faith. He cannot "plunge into the absurd." He believes God is love, but that love has no connection to everyday actuality for him. So he does not trouble God with small things. Faith, by contrast, believes God cares about the smallest things. He is "married with the left hand"; faith humbly asks for the right.

**[11] Thought experiment (confession).** If Johannes had been sent to Moriah, as a "tragic hero," he would have gone promptly. But in his heart he would have given everything up on setting out ("Now everything is lost"). Some would call that greater than Abraham. In fact it is only resignation, a *substitute* for faith. And if he had got Isaac back he would have been at a loss, because someone who has only made the infinite movement "keeps Isaac only in pain."

**[12] Contrast: Abraham.** Abraham rode slowly and believed the whole time that God would *not* require Isaac, while still being willing to give him up. That is believing "by virtue of the absurd." Through a "double movement" he got back to where he started and received Isaac more gladly. Even if Isaac had died, Abraham would have believed he would get him back in *this* life. To lose everything finite and win the same finite back appals Johannes: it is "the only marvel." He can make the leap into infinity, but not the next step. The proof of Abraham's faith is that he was glad to get Isaac back. Loving God without faith reflects on oneself; loving God in faith reflects on God.

**[13] Definition.** Infinite resignation is the last stage before faith: giving up the finite and finding peace in the eternal. Abraham goes beyond it. Lazy optimism ("it'll probably be fine") is a mere caricature of faith, and resignation already despises it.

**[14] Irony.** You cannot get faith by looking at the happy ending. That skips the first movement. The age "goes further" and turns wine into water.

**[15] Claim + analogy.** We should stop at faith and not claim to go past it. Faith must keep being made by virtue of the absurd so that one *gains* finitude. The swimming analogy: in a harness you can describe the strokes without swimming. Johannes does swim, but his strokes are those of infinity. Faith makes the movements of infinity and then those of finitude. Knights of resignation are easy to spot. Knights of faith look like ordinary philistines.

**[16] Portrait.** Johannes has never found a knight of faith but can imagine one. He looks like a tax collector: a solid, cheerful Copenhagen citizen who enjoys church, walks, omnibuses and dreams of a lamb's head for dinner. He is unbothered if the dinner is not there. Nothing "infinite" shows through. Yet he has made the movement of infinity and makes it constantly, and gets finitude back "by virtue of the absurd." Dancer image: knights of infinity wobble on landing, but this knight lands straight into walking. "To express the sublime absolutely in the pedestrian."

**[17] Worked example: resignation.** A young man loves a princess and the love can never be realised. Philistines say "marry the brewer's widow." The knight of resignation keeps the love. He makes sure it really is his life's content, lets it fill him, tests every possibility, and when all say "impossible" he makes the movement. There are two conditions: concentrate life's whole content into one wish, and concentrate the result of one's thinking into one act of consciousness. The scattered, prudent person never gets there. Footnote 1: love is just the easiest example. Footnote 2: movements come from passion, not reflection. Hegel's "mediation" is a chimera, and the age lacks passion.

**[18] Continuation.** The knight does not forget. He remembers, and that is the pain. His love becomes a love of the eternal being, valid "in a form of eternity." He makes the impossible possible by expressing it spiritually, that is, by renouncing it. He needs nothing finite from the princess, and what she does (for example marrying a prince) cannot touch him. If she is like-minded, the two are in "pre-established harmony" and could begin at once if time ever allowed.

**[19] Claim + warnings.** Resignation brings peace and anyone can learn it. The comparison is the shirt from the folk tale, sewn in tears, which each person must sew for himself. There are many botched versions. The movement must not be the result of mere harsh necessity. People want to skip the basics, like a dance pupil who wants to go straight to the française. Cultivation is the course the single individual runs to "catch up with himself."

**[20] Definition.** Resignation is the final stage before faith. Without it there is no faith.

**[21] Worked example: faith.** The knight of faith does everything the knight of resignation does. Then he makes one more movement: "I believe nevertheless that I will get her, by virtue of the absurd, because for God everything is possible." The absurd is not just "improbable." He fully recognises the impossibility with his understanding and believes at the same moment. Without first acknowledging the impossibility he is deceiving himself.

**[22] Contrast.** Faith is not an immediate feeling. A naive girl's confidence that her wish will come true is lovely, but it is not faith, because it never looked impossibility in the eye.

**[23] Confession.** Johannes can understand resignation. Getting everything back after resigning is beyond him. It makes him dizzy and anxious ("what is it to tempt God?"). Still, it is faith, whatever philosophy and theology say.

**[24] Claim.** Resignation needs no faith. It is a "purely philosophical" movement that wins one's eternal consciousness (love of God), and a person can do it alone. Faith is needed to get anything *more* than that. People confuse the two. Resignation gives up everything and faith gets everything back (Abraham gets Isaac). The rich young man should resign everything and then be told he will get "every last penny back."

**[25] Claim.** It all turns on finitude. By his own power he can give everything up and keep his soul, even in madness. He cannot get back even the smallest finite thing, because his power is used up in giving it up. Only faith gets the princess back.

**[26] Confession.** He cannot make that movement: it is "mystical hovering," and he is too heavy. It must be glorious to get the princess. A knight of resignation who does not admit this is a fraud. Only the knight of faith is truly happy, "the hereditary lord of the finite."

**[27] Irony.** *Suppose* everyone who "goes further" really has gone through resignation and then faith. Then this book praises them all. But if so, why are people embarrassed to admit to faith?

**[28] Question + distinction.** Is the philistinism he sees really the marvel in disguise? Possibly, because the knight of faith looks like it. Irony and humour, which he knows well, are *not* faith. They reflect on the self and belong to resignation.

**[29] Claim.** Everyone can make the movement of resignation, and saying you can't is cowardice. Faith is another matter. No one may make faith sound easy.

**[30] Objection addressed.** Calling it "only a test" skips the three-day ride, splitting the wood and sharpening the knife.

**[31] Satire, reprise of [2].** The same sleepy sermon and the same angry pastor. This time the sleepless man thinks "it's just a moment until the ram," and the pastor scolds him with his own sermon.

**[32] Conclusion.** Either strike Abraham out or be horrified at his paradox. The pastor's comedy was making Abraham trivial while forbidding imitation.

**[33] Reprise of [7].** How to speak rightly: dwell on the pain and the length of the journey, and say you may always turn back.

**[34] Transition.** The following *problemata* will draw out the dialectic of the story. Faith is a paradox that can make murder holy, and "faith begins precisely where thinking leaves off."

## 2. The argument as a chain

1. In the spiritual world you only get what you work and suffer for ([0]–[1]).
2. The age praises Abraham cheaply by forgetting the anxiety, that is, forgetting that "the best" was his son ([2]–[3]).
3. Without faith, what Abraham did is simply willingness to murder. So either faith is a real and terrible thing that makes it holy, or Abraham is a murderer, or we should forget him ([4], [32]).
4. It is safe to speak about Abraham only if faith is presented as everything and as terrible, and the listener is told he can turn back ([6]–[7], [33]).
5. Johannes can understand heroes and Hegel, but not Abraham. He does not have faith ([8]–[10]).
6. Johannes can make infinite resignation: giving up the finite for peace in the eternal. That is what he would have done at Moriah. It is a substitute for faith, and it leaves him unable to rejoice if Isaac is returned ([11]).
7. Abraham does more. He resigns Isaac and at the same time believes, "by virtue of the absurd," that he will have Isaac in this life. That is the double movement ([12]).
8. Resignation is the last stage before faith and is required for it. Faith cannot be got by skipping to the happy ending ([13]–[14], [20]).
9. Faith makes the infinite movement and then the finite one. So the knight of faith looks like an ordinary philistine, and resignation shows in a detectable "wavering" ([15]–[16]).
10. The princess case shows resignation step by step: concentrate everything in one wish, see that it is impossible, renounce it, and keep the love eternally ([17]–[19]).
11. The knight of faith does the same, and then believes he will get her through the absurd, while fully knowing it is impossible ([21]). Naive confidence is not this ([22]).
12. Resignation is humanly possible and needs no faith. Getting back the finite needs faith and is paradoxical ([23]–[26], [29]).
13. Those who say they "go further" than faith have not even got there ([27]–[29]).
14. Hence the problemata: faith is a paradox that thought cannot master ([34]).

### Terms as this text uses them

- **Movement**: an inner act of the whole person, like a dancer's or swimmer's movement. It can be described from outside but has to be *done* ([15]). The word first appears unexplained in [8]. I could only fix its meaning once I reached the swimming image in [15].
- **Infinite resignation**: giving up the finite thing that is your whole life's content and finding peace, and your "eternal consciousness," in love of the eternal. The thing is kept as an eternal love rather than forgotten ([13], [18], [24]). Clear by [13]–[19].
- **Knight of infinite resignation**: the person who does this. He is recognisable by a bold, gliding walk and by a "wavering" when he comes back down into the world ([15]–[16]). He is a stranger in the world ([26]).
- **Faith / the movement of faith**: after resignation, believing that you will get the finite thing back *in this life* by virtue of the absurd, while fully acknowledging the impossibility ([12], [21], [24]). It is not a feeling ([22]).
- **Knight of faith**: the person who does both movements. He looks exactly like a contented philistine and is "the hereditary lord of the finite" ([16], [26]).
- **The absurd**: what faith relies on, "that for God everything is possible." It is explicitly *not* the improbable ([21]). I understood what it is *not* better than what it *is*, and the text says this is intended.
- **Paradox**: the content of Abraham's life that thinking cannot get into ([9]). Faith as "the paradox of existence" ([22]) can make murder holy ([34]). Getting "the very least thing more" than eternal consciousness is "what is paradoxical" ([24]).
- **Double movement**: resignation plus faith, so that one arrives back at one's first condition ([12]). The term is used once, before it is spelled out. It becomes clear retrospectively from [15] ("having made the movements of infinity, it makes those of finitude").
- **The leap**: the upward spring into infinity that Johannes can make ([12]). It is also the dancer's leap ([16]) and, in the footnote, the "perpetual leap in existence" that passion makes and reflection cannot ([17] n.). Clear enough as an image.
- **The finite / finitude**: the concrete, earthly world of this life: Isaac, the princess, a lamb's head for dinner ([12], [16], [25]). Clear.
- **The single individual**: the individual person, who has to make the movement for himself ([18], [19]). This read as ordinary English. I did not sense it was a technical term.
- **Spiritual trial**: glossed as "an inner assault on a person" ([7]). I understood it as something like a torment or temptation. Why it applies only to the father who loves *less* than Abraham was unclear (see failures).
- **Tragic hero**: someone who sacrifices out of duty to a higher cause, "in the king's service," and whom Johannes can identify with ([10]–[11]). He is "for higher I cannot come," which puts him below faith. The term is used rather than defined. I inferred it from [10]'s "hero."
- **Philistine**: the narrow, comfortable townsman who lives only in the finite ([15]). The knight of faith looks exactly like him ([16], [28]). Clear thanks to the gloss.
- **Actuality vs reality**: I could not find a distinction. "Actuality" is used for the real world of circumstances ([3], [10], [18], [28]). "Reality" appears in "out of ideality into reality" ([17]) and in the footnote phrase "the whole reality of actuality" ([17] n.). I read them as synonyms. The footnote phrase then looked redundant (see failures).

### Grammar and naturalness
There are no ungrammatical sentences. ### Voice
The irony and humour come through strongly: the pastor scenes [2]/[31], Hegel [9], "Philosophy goes further", the tax-collector knight [16], wine into water [14], and the coach and four [27]. The confessions ([8], [10], [26]) sound personal and moving. There are two places where the voice misfired. In [11], "the imperfect" irony failed because of the wording. In [11], "Abraham's pettiness" is irony that I only recognised on a second pass, because it sits inside a long sentence of reported opinion. A small cue would help, e.g. "Abraham's supposed pettiness", but only if the source supports it.

## 4. Verdict

**Yes. A newcomer can follow this chapter from the text alone, with moderate effort.** The spine is carried clearly by concrete images:
- the pastor satire
- the rule that "only whoever draws the knife gets Isaac"
- swimming in a harness
- the tax-collector knight and the dancer's landing
- the princess case

These images are enough to grasp the distinction between infinite resignation and faith, and why Johannes has one and not the other. The main friction is in [4], [7], [10]–[14] and [21]. There, unclear referents and a few knotted sentences cost rereads: "ethical forgetting", "held back", "this possessing", "loses sight of", "the imperfect". Several key terms ("movement", "double movement", "tragic hero") appear before the text makes them concrete. Most of these obstacles are WORDING and can be fixed. The residual difficulty of "the absurd" and "paradox" is IDEA-level and, by the text's own account, meant to resist understanding.
