## ch1 ¶0 (text changed)

**before**

> Our age is putting on ein wirklicher Ausverkauf — a genuine clearance sale — and not only in trade: in the realm of ideas too. Everything can be had at such a rock-bottom price that one begins to wonder whether in the end anyone will still want to bid. Every speculative billiard-marker who conscientiously keeps score of the momentous progress of modern philosophy, every private lecturer, tutor and student, every smallholder and lodger in philosophy, does not stop at doubting everything but goes further. It would perhaps be untimely and inopportune to ask them where they actually get to. But it is surely polite and modest to take it as settled that they have doubted everything; otherwise it would be a strange thing to say, that they went further. This preliminary movement, then, they have all made — and presumably so easily that they do not find it necessary to let fall a single word about how. For not even the person who anxiously and with concern looked for a little enlightenment found any: not a guiding hint, not a small dietary prescription for how one is to conduct oneself under this enormous task. “But surely Descartes did it?” Descartes, a venerable, humble and upright thinker whose writings no one, surely, can read without the deepest emotion, did as he said and said as he did. Alas! Alas! Alas! In our time that is a great rarity! Descartes, as he himself repeats often enough, did not doubt with regard to faith. (“Memores tamen, ut jam dictum est, huic lumini naturali tamdiu tantum esse credendum, quamdiu nihil contrarium a Deo ipso revelatur ..... Præter cætera autem, memoriæ nostræ pro summa regula est infigendum, ea quæ nobis a Deo revelata sunt, ut omnium certissima esse credenda; et quamvis forte lumen rationis, quam maxime clarum et evidens, aliud quid nobis suggerere videretur, soli tamen auctoritati divinæ potius quam proprio nostro judicio fidem esse adhibendam.” [Remembering, however, as has already been said, that this natural light is to be trusted only so long as nothing to the contrary is revealed by God himself ..... Above all, though, we must fix in our memory as the supreme rule that what God has revealed to us is to be believed as the most certain of all things; and even if perhaps the light of reason, however clear and evident, should seem to suggest something else to us, we must still put our faith in divine authority alone rather than in our own judgment.] Cf. Principia philosophiæ, part one, § 28 and § 76.) He did not cry “Fire!” and make it everyone’s duty to doubt; for Descartes was a quiet, solitary thinker, not a bellowing night watchman in the street. He modestly admitted that his method had significance only for himself, and that it arose in part from the muddle his earlier learning had been in. (“Ne quis igitur putet, me hic traditurum aliquam methodum, quam unusquisque sequi debeat ad recte regendam rationem; illam enim tantum, quam ipsemet secutus sum, exponere decrevi... Sed simul ac illud studiorum curriculum absolvi (sc. juventutis, quo decurso mos est in eruditorum numerum cooptari, plane aliud coepi cogitare. Tot enim me dubiis totque erroribus implicatum esse animadverti, ut omnes discendi conatus nihil aliud mihi profuisse judicarem, quam quod ignorantiam meam magis magisque detexissem.” [So let no one suppose that I mean to hand down here some method that everyone ought to follow in order to guide his reason rightly; for I have resolved to set out only the one I myself have followed... But as soon as I had finished that course of study (namely, of my youth), at the end of which it is the custom to be admitted to the ranks of the learned, I began to think something quite different. For I found myself entangled in so many doubts and so many errors that I judged that all my efforts to learn had done me no other good than to uncover my ignorance more and more.] Cf. Dissertatio de methodo, pp. 2 and 3.) — The ancient Greeks, who did after all know something about philosophy too, held doubting to be the task of a whole life, because the skill of doubting is not acquired in days or weeks. The old veteran who had served his time attained it: through every snare he had held doubt in equilibrium, had fearlessly denied the certainty of the senses and the certainty of thought, and had incorruptibly withstood the anxiety of self-love and the insinuations of fellow-feeling. What the Greeks took for a life’s task and the veteran attained — that is what everyone in our time begins with.

## ch1 ¶2 (text changed)

**before**

> The present author is no philosopher whatsoever. He has not understood the System — whether there is one, whether it is finished. His weak head already has enough to cope with in the thought of what an enormous head everyone in our time must have, since everyone has such an enormous thought. Even if one were able to translate the whole content of faith into the form of the concept, it does not follow that one has comprehended faith — comprehended how one came into it, or how it came into oneself. The present author is no philosopher whatsoever. Poetice et eleganter (poetically and elegantly put), he is an extra clerk, one who writes neither the System nor promises of the System, and who neither makes a slip of the pen on the System nor signs himself away to the System. He writes because for him it is a luxury, one that gains in pleasantness and obviousness the fewer people buy and read what he writes. He easily foresees his fate in an age that has struck a line through passion in order to serve scholarship — an age in which an author who wants readers must take care to write in such a way that it can be comfortably leafed through during the after-dinner nap, and take care to model his outward manner on that polite gardener’s boy in the Adresseavis who, hat in hand and with good references from his last place, recommends himself to a highly esteemed public. He foresees his fate: to be completely ignored. He has a presentiment of the terrible thing, that the zealous critics will make him run the gauntlet more than once. He dreads the still more terrible thing, that some enterprising abstractor or other, a gobbler of paragraphs (who, to save scholarship, is always willing to do to other people’s writings what Trop, “to save taste,” magnanimously did to “The Destruction of the Human Race”) — will cut him up into §§, and do it with the same inflexibility as the man who, to serve the science of punctuation, divided up his speech by counting the words, so that there were 50 words to a full stop and 35 to a semicolon. — I prostrate myself in deepest submission before every systematic customs-snooper: “This is not the System; it has not the slightest thing to do with the System. I call down all good things upon the System and upon the Danish partners in this omnibus, for a tower it is hardly likely to become. To each and every one of them, jointly and severally, I wish good luck and blessing.”

## ch2 ¶0 (text changed)

**before**

> There was once a man who had heard, as a child, that beautiful story of how God tempted Abraham, and how Abraham stood firm in the temptation, kept the faith, and, against all expectation, received a son a second time. As he grew older, he read that same story with even greater admiration; for life had separated what had been united in the child’s pious simplicity. The older he grew, the more often his thoughts turned to that story. His enthusiasm grew stronger and stronger, and yet he could understand the story less and less. At last he forgot everything else over it. His soul had only one wish — to see Abraham — and only one longing: to have been a witness to that event. What he craved was not to see the fair lands of the East, nor the earthly splendor of the land of promise, nor that God-fearing married couple whose old age God blessed, nor the patriarch’s venerable figure, full of years, nor the lush youth of Isaac, the gift of God — he would not have minded if the same thing had taken place on a barren heath. What he yearned for was to go along on the three-day journey on which Abraham rode with sorrow before him and Isaac at his side. His wish was to be there in the hour when Abraham raised his eyes and saw Mount Moriah far off, the hour when he left the donkeys behind and went up the mountain alone with Isaac; for what absorbed him was not the imagination’s artful weaving but the shudder of thought.

## ch8 ¶0 (text changed)

**before**

> Once, when the price of spices in Holland had grown somewhat sluggish, the merchants had a few cargoes sunk in the sea to drive the price up. That was a pardonable, perhaps a necessary, deception. Do we need something of the sort in the world of the spirit? Are we so utterly certain of having reached the highest that nothing remains but to talk ourselves piously into believing we have not come that far — merely so as to have something to occupy the time? Is it a self-deception of this kind that the present generation needs? Is it in virtuosity at this that it ought to be trained? Or is it not rather perfected enough already in the art of deceiving itself? Or is it not rather in need of an honest earnestness, which points out the tasks fearlessly and incorruptibly; an honest earnestness that keeps loving watch over the tasks, that does not make people anxious, so that they want to rush headlong at the highest, but keeps the tasks young and beautiful and lovely to look at and beckoning to all, and yet at the same time difficult and inspiring for the noble — for only what is difficult inspires the noble nature? Whatever one generation may learn from another, what is truly human no generation learns from the one before it. In this respect every generation begins from the very beginning. It has no task other than that of every previous generation, and it gets no further either — provided the previous generation did not betray the task and deceive itself. This truly human thing is passion, in which one generation also fully understands the other, and understands itself. Thus no generation has learned to love from another; no generation gets to begin at any point other than the beginning; no later generation has a shorter task than the one before. And if, unlike the previous generations, one does not want to stop at loving here, but to go further, that is nothing but idle and foolish talk.

> Faith is the highest passion in a human being. In every generation there are perhaps many who do not even come to it, but no one gets further. Whether there are also many in our time who do not discover it, I do not decide. I dare appeal only to myself — to one who does not hide that he has a long way to go, though he does not on that account wish to deceive himself, or what is great, by turning it into a trifle, a childhood illness one must wish to get over as soon as possible. But even for the one who does not so much as come to faith, life has tasks enough; and if he loves them honestly, his life will not be wasted either, even if it is never like the lives of those who sensed and grasped the highest. But the one who has come to faith (whether he is exceptionally gifted or a simple soul makes no difference) does not stop at faith. Indeed, he would be outraged if anyone said so to him, just as the lover would be indignant if people said that he stopped at love. For, he would reply, I am by no means stopping, since my life is in it. Yet he does not get further either, not to anything else; for if he discovers that, then he has another explanation.
