## ch7 ¶30 (text changed)

**before**

> Natures such as Gloucester’s are not to be saved by mediating them into an idea of society. Ethics really only makes fools of them — much as ethics would be mocking Sarah if it asked her: “Why don’t you express the universal and marry?” Such natures are in the paradox from the very ground up. They are by no means less perfect than other people; only, they are either lost in the demonic paradox or saved in the divine one. Now, time and again people have taken pleasure in the fact that witches, goblins, trolls and so on were misshapen creatures; and it is undeniable that every human being has an inclination, on seeing a misshapen creature, at once to attach to it the notion of a moral depravity. What a monstrous injustice! For the relation must rather be turned around: existence itself has corrupted them, the way a stepmother makes children turn out badly. To be placed outside the universal from the start, by nature or by historical circumstance — that is the beginning of the demonic, for which the individual himself, however, bears no guilt. In this way Cumberland’s Jew, too, is a demon, even though he does good. In this way the demonic can also show itself as contempt for people — a contempt, note carefully, that does not lead the demonic person himself to act contemptibly. On the contrary, his strength lies in knowing that he is better than all those who judge him. — About all such things the poets ought to be the very first to sound the alarm. God knows what books the younger versifiers now living read! Their study probably consists in learning rhymes by heart. God knows what their significance in existence is! At this moment I do not know that they are of any use except that they furnish an edifying proof of the immortality of the soul: one can confidently say of them to oneself what Baggesen says of the town poet Kildevalle — “if he becomes immortal, then we all will.” — What has been said here on the occasion of Sarah was said chiefly with a view to poetic production, and therefore with a fantastic presupposition. But it has its full significance if one wanted, with psychological interest, to go deeply into the meaning of the old saying: nullum unquam exstitit magnum ingenium sine aliqua dementia — there has never been a great genius without some touch of madness. For this dementia is the genius’s suffering in existence. It is the expression of — if I dare say so — the divine envy, while what is brilliant in him is the expression of divine favor. So from the beginning the genius is disoriented in relation to the universal and brought into relation with the paradox. This holds whether, in despair over his limit — which in his eyes turns his omnipotence into impotence — he seeks a demonic reassurance, and therefore will not admit it either before God or before people, or whether he reassures himself religiously in love for the deity. Here lie psychological tasks to which, it seems to me, one could gladly sacrifice a whole life; and yet a word about them is so rarely heard. What relation does madness bear to genius? Can the one be construed out of the other? In what sense, and to what extent, is the genius in command of his madness? For it is obvious that up to a point he is in command of it — otherwise he really would be mad. For such observations, however, ingenuity is required in a high degree, and love as well; for to observe someone superior is very difficult. If one were to read through some of the most brilliant authors with attention to this, it might perhaps be possible, once in a great while and with much effort, to discover a little.

> I will imagine one more case: a single individual who wants to save the universal by being concealed and by his silence. For this purpose the legend of Faust will serve. Faust is a doubter, an apostate of the spirit who goes the way of the flesh. That is the poets’ view of him. And while it is said again and again that every age has its Faust, the age’s poets nonetheless go, one after another, undaunted down the same beaten track. Let us make a small change. He knows unerringly how laughter steals into a person and lives there hidden, and how, once it has taken up lodging, it sits lying in wait. Let us picture such an Aristophanes, such a Voltaire, slightly altered: for he is also a sympathetic nature. He loves existence, he loves people, and he knows that even if the verdict of laughter may perhaps bring up a young generation that is saved, a great many people in the present generation will go under. So he keeps silent, and as far as possible forgets to laugh himself. But may he keep silent? Perhaps there are quite a few who do not understand at all the difficulty I am speaking of. They probably think it was an admirable magnanimity to keep silent. That is not at all my view. For I think that any such nature, if he has not had the magnanimity to keep silent, is a traitor to existence. So I demand this magnanimity of him; but when he has it, may he then keep silent? Ethics is a dangerous science, and it may well be that Aristophanes, determined on purely ethical grounds, resolved to let laughter pass judgment on the age that had gone astray. Aesthetic magnanimity cannot help here; for on that account one does not venture such things. If he is to keep silent, he must go into the paradox. — I will hint at one more design: that a person, for example, possesses an explanation of a hero’s life that explains it in a sorry way, and yet a whole contemporary age rests absolutely secure in this hero without suspecting anything of the kind. He hides the doubt in his soul more carefully than a girl hides the fruit of a sinful love beneath her heart. He tries as well as he can to walk in step with other people; but what goes on inside him he consumes within himself, and in this way he brings himself as a sacrifice for the universal.

For just as Gregory of Rimini was called tortor infantium — torturer of infants — because he held that little children are damned, so I could be tempted to call myself tortor heroum — torturer of heroes; for I am most inventive where tormenting heroes is concerned. Faust sees Margaret — not after he has chosen pleasure, for my Faust does not choose pleasure at all. He does not see Margaret in Mephistopheles’s concave mirror; he sees her in all her lovable innocence. For my Faust is so ideal that he does not belong among those scholarly doubters who doubt for one hour each semester from the lectern but otherwise can do everything else — the doubting included, which they too manage without being in the condition of spirit and without acting by virtue of spirit. He is a doubter, and the doubter is as hungry for joy’s daily bread as for the food of spirit. Still, he stays true to his resolve and keeps silent: he speaks to no one about his doubt, and to Margaret not about his love either.
