## ch5 ¶0 (text changed)

**before**

> The ethical as such is the universal. As the universal, it applies to everyone — which can also be put another way: it applies at every moment. It rests immanently in itself: nothing outside it is its telos (its end: the purpose it exists for). Instead, it is itself the telos of everything outside it, and once the ethical has taken all of that into itself, it goes no further. Taken in his immediacy — as a being of senses and soul — the single individual is the individual who has his telos in the universal. His ethical task is to express himself in the universal continually: to cancel his particularity (whatever sets him apart as this one person) in order to become the universal. As soon as the single individual tries to assert himself in his particularity against the universal, he sins; the only way back to reconciliation with the universal is to acknowledge that he has sinned. Whenever the single individual, after having entered the universal, feels an urge to assert himself as the single individual, he is in a spiritual trial — an inner assault on him — and he can work his way out of it only by repenting and giving himself up, as the single individual, in the universal. If this is the highest thing that can be said about a human being and his existence, then the ethical has the same character as the eternal blessedness of a human being, which for all eternity and at every moment is his telos. For it would be a contradiction if eternal blessedness could be given up (that is, teleologically suspended). For the moment eternal blessedness was suspended, it would be forfeited — whereas whatever is teleologically suspended is not forfeited, but is preserved precisely in the higher thing that is its telos.

## ch5 ¶20 (text changed)

**before**

> How, then, does the single individual make sure that he is justified? It is easy enough to flatten the whole of existence into the idea of the state or of society. Do that, and one can also mediate easily enough; for then one never arrives at the paradox that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal — which I can also express pointedly with a saying of Pythagoras: that the odd number is more perfect than the even. When, now and then in our time, one does hear an answer that points in the direction of the paradox, it usually runs like this: "It must be judged by the outcome." A hero who has become a skandalon — a stumbling block, an offense — to his contemporaries, conscious that he is a paradox who cannot make himself understood, calls out confidently to his age: "The outcome will surely prove that I was justified." In our time one hears this cry more rarely; for just as our age does not produce heroes, which is its failing, it has the advantage that it produces few caricatures either. So when in our time one hears the words "It must be judged by the outcome," one knows at once whom one has the honor of addressing. Those who talk this way are a numerous tribe, which I will call by the common name of the lecturers. In their thoughts they live secure in existence: they have a fixed position and safe prospects in a well-organized state; they have centuries, perhaps even millennia, between themselves and the upheavals of existence; they have no fear that such things could happen again — what would the police and the newspapers say? Their work in life is to judge the great men, and to judge them by the outcome. Such conduct toward greatness betrays a strange mixture of arrogance and wretchedness: arrogance, because they think themselves called to judge; wretchedness, because they do not feel their own lives to be even remotely related to those of the great. Anyone with even a little erectioris ingenii — a more elevated cast of mind — has not become an entirely cold and clammy mollusk; and as he approaches what is great, he can never forget that since the creation of the world it has been the custom for the outcome to come last, and that anyone who really means to learn from greatness must look precisely at how it began. Whoever is to act and wants to judge himself by the outcome will never begin.
