## ch7 ¶1 (text changed)

**before**

> Here we stand once again at the same point. If there is no concealment that has its ground in this — that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal — then Abraham’s conduct cannot be defended; for he passed over the intermediate ethical authorities. If, on the other hand, there is such a concealment, then we stand before the paradox, which cannot be mediated, since it rests precisely on the single individual’s being, as the single individual, higher than the universal — but the universal is precisely mediation. The Hegelian philosophy assumes no justified concealment, no justified incommensurability. It is therefore consistent with itself when it demands disclosure — that the individual bring what is hidden in him out into the open, and make himself known. But it is confused when it wants to regard Abraham as the father of faith and to talk about faith. For faith is not the first immediacy but a later one. The first immediacy is the aesthetic, and here the Hegelian philosophy may well be right. But faith is not the aesthetic — or else there has never been faith, because there has always been faith.

## ch7 ¶3 (text changed)

**before**

> Aristotle says in his immortal Poetics: δύο μὲν οὖν τοῦ μύθου μέρη, περὶ ταῦτ’ ἐστί, περιπέτεια καὶ ἀναγνώρισις — two parts of the plot, then, turn on these things: peripeteia, the reversal, and anagnorisis, the recognition (cf. chapter 11). Naturally it is only the second element that concerns me here: ἀναγνώρισις, recognition. Wherever there can be talk of a recognition, there is eo ipso — by that very fact — talk of a preceding concealment. Just as the recognition is what resolves, what releases the tension, so the concealment is what builds the suspense, the tension, in dramatic life. Earlier in the same chapter Aristotle develops the differing worth of tragedies according to how περιπέτεια and ἀναγνώρισις carom off each other, and discusses single and double recognition. I cannot take account of that here, however much it tempts by its inwardness and its quiet absorption — especially someone who has long enough been wearied by the superficial omniscience of the survey-writers. A more general remark must find its place here. In Greek tragedy, concealment (and, as a consequence, recognition) is an epic remnant. It has its ground in a fate in which the dramatic action vanishes, and from which that action has its dark, enigmatic origin. That is why the effect a Greek tragedy produces resembles the impression a marble statue makes, lacking as it does the power of the eye. Greek tragedy is blind. That is why a certain abstraction is required in order to be properly affected by it. A son murders his father, but only afterward does he learn that it was his father. A sister is about to sacrifice her brother, but at the decisive moment she finds out. This kind of tragedy can have less hold on our reflective age. Modern drama has given up fate; it has emancipated itself dramatically; it is sighted; it gazes into itself and takes fate up into its dramatic consciousness. Concealment and disclosure are then the hero’s free act, for which he is responsible.

## ch7 ¶6 (text changed)

**before**

> A couple of examples. A girl is secretly in love with someone, though the two of them have not definitively confessed their love to each other. Her parents compel her to marry another man (there may, besides, be a consideration of filial piety that decides her); she obeys her parents; she hides her love “so as not to make the other unhappy, and no one shall ever know what she suffers.” — A young man could, by a single word, come into possession of the object of his longings and his restless dreams. This little word, however, would compromise — indeed perhaps (who knows?) ruin — an entire family. He magnanimously resolves to remain in hiding: “The girl shall never know it, so that she may perhaps become happy at another man’s hand.” A pity that these two people, each of whom is hidden from his or her respective beloved, are also hidden from each other; otherwise a remarkable higher unity could have been brought about here. — Their concealment is a free act, for which they are responsible to aesthetics as well. Aesthetics, however, is a polite and sentimental science, which knows more ways out than any manager of the public pawnshop. So what does it do? It does everything possible for the lovers. By means of a coincidence, the respective partners in the projected marriage get a hint of the other party’s magnanimous resolution; an explanation follows; they get each other, and along with that the rank of real heroes. For although they have not even had time to sleep on their heroic resolution, aesthetics nevertheless regards it as if they had bravely fought their purpose through for many years. Aesthetics, you see, does not care much about time; whether in jest or in earnest, time goes equally fast for it.

## ch7 ¶10 (text changed)

**before**

> Ethics, however, has no coincidence and no old servant at hand. The aesthetic idea contradicts itself as soon as it has to be carried out in actuality. Ethics therefore demands disclosure. The tragic hero shows his ethical courage precisely in this: that he, caught in no aesthetic illusion, is himself the one who announces her fate to Iphigenia. If he does this, then the tragic hero is the beloved son of ethics, in whom it is well pleased. Should he keep silent, that may be because he believes he makes it easier for others by doing so; but it may also be because he makes it easier for himself by doing so. Of this, however, he knows himself to be free. If he keeps silent, he takes on a responsibility as the single individual, insofar as he disregards an argument that may come from outside. This, as a tragic hero, he cannot do; for the very reason ethics loves him is that he always expresses the universal. His heroic deed demands courage, but it also belongs to this courage that he shirks no argument. Now it is certain that tears are a terrible argumentum ad hominem — an argument aimed at the man himself — and there has surely been someone whom nothing touched, yet whom tears moved. In the play Iphigenia is allowed to weep. In actuality she ought to be granted, like Jephthah’s daughter, two months to weep — not in solitude but at her father’s feet — and to use all the art she has, “which is only tears,” and to twine herself, instead of an olive branch, around his knees (cf. line 1224).
