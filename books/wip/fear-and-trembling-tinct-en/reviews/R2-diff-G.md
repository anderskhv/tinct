> But I break off here. I am no poet, and I go about things only dialectically. First, it should be noted that the hero receives this information at the decisive moment. He is therefore pure and has nothing to repent of; he has not bound himself frivolously to his beloved. Second, he has a divine pronouncement on his side — or rather against him — so he is not steered by his own cleverness, as those slender lovers, men and women alike, are. It further goes without saying that the pronouncement makes him just as unhappy as the bride — indeed a little more so, since he is, after all, the occasion of it. For it is true that the augurs foretold a misfortune only for him; but the question is whether this misfortune might not be such that, when it strikes him, it also strikes their happiness as husband and wife. What, then, is he to do? 1) Should he keep silent and hold the wedding, thinking: perhaps the misfortune will not come right away; at any rate I have stood up for love and have not been afraid of making myself unhappy — but I must keep silent, for otherwise even the short moment is thrown away? This seems plausible, but it is nothing of the kind; for in that case he has wronged the girl. By his silence he has in a way made the girl guilty; for had she known, she would presumably never have agreed to such a match. In the hour of distress, then, he will have to carry not merely the misfortune but also the responsibility for his having kept silent: her just anger that he kept silent. 2) Should he keep silent and refrain from holding the wedding? In that case he must enter into a mystification, by which he annihilates himself in his relation to her. Aesthetics might perhaps approve of this. The catastrophe could then be shaped like the actual one, except that at the very last moment there would be an explanation — one that came too late all the same, since, aesthetically considered, it becomes a necessity to let him die, unless this science should find itself able to cancel that ill-fated prophecy. Yet this conduct, however magnanimous it may be, contains an offense against the girl and the reality of her love. 3) Should he speak? One must not forget, of course, that our hero is a little too poetic for the renouncing of his love to mean no more to him than a failed business speculation. If he speaks, the whole thing becomes an unhappy love story in the same style as Axel and Valborg. The two become a pair that heaven itself parts. In the present case, though, this separation has to be thought of somewhat differently, since it also results from the free act of the individuals. For what is so very difficult about the dialectic of this case is that the misfortune is to strike only him. So they do not, like Axel and Valborg, get a common expression for their suffering, whereas heaven parts Axel and Valborg alike, since they stand equally near to each other. If that were the case here, a way out could be imagined. For since heaven uses no visible power to separate them but leaves it to the lovers themselves, it would surely be conceivable that they might resolve together to defy heaven, misfortune and all.

> Incidentally, from this point one could set off in another direction of dialectical movements. Heaven foretells a misfortune for him from his marriage. So he could, of course, refrain from holding the wedding without therefore giving up the girl, and live with her in a romantic union that, for the lovers, would be more than reassuring. This, however, contains an offense against the girl, because he does not, in his love for her, express the universal. It would nevertheless be a task both for a poet and for an ethicist who wanted to defend marriage. In general, if poetry became attentive to the religious and to the inwardness of individuality, it would find far more significant tasks than those it busies itself with now. Over and over, after all, one hears this story in poetry: a man is tied to one girl he once loved — or perhaps never properly loved — because now he has seen another girl, who is the ideal. A man gets it wrong in life: the street was right, but it was the wrong house, for across the way, on the second floor, lives the ideal — this, people think, is a task for poetry. A lover has made a mistake: he saw his beloved by lamplight and thought she had dark hair, but look! on closer inspection it was fair — but her sister, she is the ideal. This, people think, is a task for poetry. My opinion is that every such man is a Laban (a boor) who may be unbearable enough in life but ought to be hissed off at once when he tries to make himself important in poetry. Only passion against passion makes a poetic collision, not this clatter of particularities within one and the same passion. If, for instance, a girl in the Middle Ages falls in love and then satisfies herself that earthly love is a sin, and prefers a heavenly love, then there is a poetic collision here, and the girl is poetic, because her life is in the idea. ## ch7 ¶17 (text changed)

**before**

> But what is this sketch for, then, since I still get no further than the tragic hero? Because it was still possible that it might throw some light on the paradox. Everything depends, then, on how he stands in relation to the augurs’ pronouncement, which in one way or another becomes decisive for his life. Is this pronouncement publici juris — public property — or is it a privatissimum — a private lesson? The scene is Greece, and an augur’s pronouncement is intelligible to everyone. I do not mean only that the single individual can understand its content lexically; I mean that the single individual can understand that an augur is announcing heaven’s decision to the single individual. So the augur’s pronouncement is intelligible not only to the hero but to everyone, and no private relation to the deity results from it. He can do what he likes; what has been foretold will happen; and neither by acting nor by refraining does he come into a closer relation to the deity or become the object either of its grace or of its wrath. Any individual will be able to understand the outcome just as well as the hero, and there is no secret writing legible to the hero alone. Insofar as he wants to speak, then, he can do so perfectly well, for he can make himself understood. Insofar as he wants to keep silent, it is because, by virtue of being the single individual, he wants to be higher than the universal — wants to delude himself with all kinds of fantastic notions about how she will soon forget this business, and so on. If, on the other hand, heaven’s will had not been announced to him by an augur — if it had come to his knowledge entirely privately, if it had placed itself in an entirely private relation to him — then we are at the paradox, if it exists at all (for my deliberation proceeds by dilemma). In that case he could not speak, no matter how much he wanted to. He would then not be enjoying himself in his silence; he would be suffering the pain — but that pain would be precisely his assurance that he was justified. His silence would then not be grounded in his wanting, as the single individual, to place himself in an absolute relation to the universal, but in his having been placed, as the single individual, in an absolute relation to the absolute. He would then also be able to find rest in it, as far as I can imagine, whereas his magnanimous silence would be constantly troubled by the demands of the ethical. It is altogether to be wished that aesthetics would for once try to begin where for so many years it has ended: with illusory magnanimity. The moment it did that, it would be playing straight into the hands of the religious; for this power is the only one that can rescue the aesthetic from its struggle with the ethical. Queen Elizabeth sacrifices her love for Essex to the state by signing his death warrant. This was a heroic deed, even if a little personal offense was in play too — that he had not sent her the ring. As is well known, he had in fact sent it, but it had been held back through the malice of a lady at court. Elizabeth, so the story goes — ni fallor (unless I am mistaken) — received word of this; then she sat for ten days with one finger in her mouth, biting it without saying a single word, and then she died. That would be a task for a poet who knew how to wrench the mouth open; otherwise it is at most of use to a ballet master, with whom the poet in our time often enough confuses himself.
