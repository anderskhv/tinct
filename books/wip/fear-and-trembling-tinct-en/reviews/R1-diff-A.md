> Our age is putting on ein wirklicher Ausverkauf — a genuine clearance sale — and not only in trade: in the realm of ideas too. Everything can be had at such a rock-bottom price that one begins to wonder whether in the end anyone will still want to bid. Every speculative billiard-marker who conscientiously keeps score of the momentous progress of modern philosophy, every private lecturer, tutor and student, every cottager and lodger on the estate of philosophy, does not stop at doubting everything but goes further. It would perhaps be untimely and ill-mannered to ask them where they are really going. But it is surely polite and modest to take it as settled that they have doubted everything; otherwise it would be a strange thing to say, that they went further. This preliminary movement, then, they have all made — and presumably so easily that they do not find it necessary to let fall a single word about how. For not even the person who anxiously and with concern looked for a little enlightenment found any: not a guiding hint, not a small dietary prescription for how one is to conduct oneself under this enormous task. “But surely Descartes did it?” Descartes, a venerable, humble and upright thinker whose writings no one, surely, can read without being deeply moved, did as he said and said as he did. Alas! Alas! Alas! In our time that is a great rarity! Descartes, as he himself repeats often enough, did not doubt with regard to faith. (“Memores tamen, ut jam dictum est, huic lumini naturali tamdiu tantum esse credendum, quamdiu nihil contrarium a Deo ipso revelatur ..... Præter cætera autem, memoriæ nostræ pro summa regula est infigendum, ea quæ nobis a Deo revelata sunt, ut omnium certissima esse credenda; et quamvis forte lumen rationis, quam maxime clarum et evidens, aliud quid nobis suggerere videretur, soli tamen auctoritati divinæ potius quam proprio nostro judicio fidem esse adhibendam.” [Remembering, however, as has already been said, that this natural light is to be trusted only so long as nothing to the contrary is revealed by God himself ..... Above all, though, we must fix in our memory as the supreme rule that what God has revealed to us is to be believed as the most certain of all things; and even if the light of reason, however clear and evident, should seem to suggest something else to us, we must still put our faith in divine authority alone rather than in our own judgment.] Cf. Principia philosophiae, part one, § 28 and § 76.) He did not cry “Fire!” and make it everyone’s duty to doubt; for Descartes was a quiet, solitary thinker, not a bellowing night watchman in the street. He modestly admitted that his method had significance only for himself, and that it arose in part from the muddle his earlier learning had been in. (“Ne quis igitur putet, me hic traditurum aliquam methodum, quam unusquisque sequi debeat ad recte regendam rationem; illam enim tantum, quam ipsemet secutus sum, exponere decrevi... Sed simul ac illud studiorum curriculum absolvi (sc. juventutis, quo decurso mos est in eruditorum numerum cooptari, plane aliud coepi cogitare. Tot enim me dubiis totque erroribus implicatum esse animadverti, ut omnes discendi conatus nihil aliud mihi profuisse judicarem, quam quod ignorantiam meam magis magisque detexissem.” [So let no one suppose that I mean to hand down here some method that everyone ought to follow in order to guide his reason rightly; for I have resolved to set out only the one I myself have followed... But as soon as I had finished that course of study (namely, of my youth), at the end of which it is the custom to be admitted to the ranks of the learned, I began to think something quite different. For I found myself entangled in so many doubts and so many errors that I judged that all my efforts to learn had done me no other good than to uncover my ignorance more and more.] Cf. Dissertatio de methodo, pp. 2 and 3.) — The ancient Greeks, who did after all know something about philosophy too, held doubting to be the task of a whole life, because the skill of doubting is not acquired in days or weeks. The old veteran who had served his time reached it at last: through every snare he had held doubt in equilibrium, had fearlessly refused both what the senses make certain and what thought makes certain, and had incorruptibly withstood the anxiety of self-love and the insinuations of fellow-feeling. What the Greeks took for a life’s task and the veteran achieved — that is where, in our time, everyone begins.

## ch1 ¶2 (text changed)

**before**

> The present author is no philosopher whatsoever. The System is beyond him: he does not know whether there is one, or whether it has been completed. His weak head already has enough to cope with in the thought of what an enormous head everyone in our time must have, since everyone has such an enormous thought. Even if one were able to translate the whole content of faith into the form of the concept, it does not follow that one has comprehended faith — comprehended how one came into it, or how it came into oneself. The present author is no philosopher whatsoever. Poetice et eleganter (poetically and elegantly put), he is an extra clerk, one who writes neither the System nor promises of it, and who neither makes a slip of the pen on the System’s account nor signs himself away to it. He writes because for him it is a luxury, one that gains in pleasantness and obviousness the fewer people buy and read what he writes. He easily foresees his fate in an age that has struck a line through passion in order to serve scholarship — an age in which an author who wants readers must take care to write in such a way that he can be comfortably leafed through during the after-dinner nap, and take care to model his outward manner on that polite gardener’s boy in the Adresseavis who, hat in hand and with good references from his last place, recommends himself to a highly esteemed public. He foresees his fate: to be completely ignored. He has a presentiment of the terrible thing, that the zealous critics will make him run the gauntlet more than once. He dreads the still more terrible thing, that some enterprising abstractor or other, a gobbler of paragraphs (who, to save scholarship, is always willing to do to other people’s writings what Trop, “to save taste,” magnanimously did to “The Destruction of the Human Race”) — will cut him up into §§, and do it with the same inflexibility as the man who, to serve the science of punctuation, divided up his speech by counting the words, so that there were 50 words to a full stop and 35 to a semicolon. — I prostrate myself in deepest submission before every systematic customs-snooper: “This is not the System, and has not the slightest connection with it. I call down all good things upon the System and upon the Danish partners in this omnibus — a tower it is hardly likely to become. To each and every one of them, jointly and severally, I wish good luck and blessing.”

## ch2 ¶0 (text changed)

Or is it not rather in need of an honest earnestness, which points out the tasks fearlessly and incorruptibly; an honest earnestness that keeps loving watch over the tasks, that does not make people anxious, so that they want to rush headlong at the highest, but keeps the tasks young and beautiful and lovely to look at and beckoning to all, and yet at the same time difficult and inspiring for the noble — for only what is difficult inspires the noble nature? Whatever one generation may learn from another, what is truly human no generation learns from the one before it. In this respect every generation begins from the very beginning. It has no task other than that of every previous generation, and it gets no further either — provided the previous generation did not betray the task and deceive itself. This truly human thing is passion, in which one generation also fully understands the other, and understands itself. Thus no generation has learned to love from another; no generation gets to begin at any point other than the beginning; no later generation has a shorter task than the one before. And if, unlike the previous generations, one does not want to stop at loving here, but to go further, that is nothing but idle and foolish talk.
