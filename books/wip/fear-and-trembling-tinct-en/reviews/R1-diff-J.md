## ch7 ¶49 (text changed)

**before**

> Abraham cannot speak; for the thing that explains everything he cannot say (that is, say so that it is intelligible): that it is a test — note carefully, a test of such a kind that the ethical is the temptation. A person placed like this is an émigré from the sphere of the universal. And the next thing is something he can say still less. Abraham, as has been sufficiently shown earlier, makes two movements. In the first — the infinite movement of resignation — he gives up Isaac, and no one can understand that, since it is a private undertaking. But next, at every moment, he makes the movement of faith. This is his comfort. For he says: “Yet it will not happen; or if it does happen, the Lord will give me a new Isaac — by virtue, that is, of the absurd.” With the tragic hero, the story does at least come to an end. Iphigenia submits to her father’s decision; she herself makes the infinite movement of resignation, and now the two of them understand each other. She can understand Agamemnon, because what he undertakes expresses the universal. If, by contrast, Agamemnon were to tell her, “Even though the god demands you as a sacrifice, it could still be that he did not demand it — by virtue, that is, of the absurd,” then in that same moment he would become unintelligible to Iphigenia. ## ch7 ¶53 (text changed)

**before**

> It goes without saying that the tragic hero, in his moment of culmination, can say a few words, like any other person who has not been deprived of speech — perhaps a few fitting words. But the question is whether it is fitting for him to say them. If what his life means lies in an outward deed, he has nothing to say: whatever he says is at bottom idle talk, and it only weakens the impression he makes. Tragic ceremony requires, rather, that he fulfill his task in silence, whether it consists in an action or in a suffering. So as not to go too far afield, I will simply take what lies closest to hand. Suppose it had been Agamemnon himself, rather than Calchas, who was to draw the knife on Iphigenia: wanting to say a few words at the last moment would only have demeaned him. For the significance of his deed was, after all, plain to everyone; the procedure of piety, of compassion, of feeling, of tears was complete; and apart from that, his life stood in no relation to spirit; he was, in other words, neither a teacher nor a witness to the spirit. If, on the other hand, the significance of a hero’s life lies in the direction of spirit, then the lack of a line would weaken the impression he makes. What he then has to say is not a few fitting words, a little piece of declamation. Rather, the significance of his line is that he carries himself through at the decisive moment. Such an intellectual tragic hero ought to have the last word, and ought to keep it. One requires of him the same transfigured bearing that befits every tragic hero, but then one requires one word more. Insofar, then, as such an intellectual tragic hero culminates in a suffering (in death), he becomes immortal in this last word before he dies, whereas the ordinary tragic hero becomes immortal only after his death.

## ch7 ¶54 (text changed)

**before**

> Socrates can serve as an example. He was an intellectual tragic hero. His death sentence is announced to him. In that moment he dies — for anyone who fails to see that dying takes the entire strength of the spirit, and that the hero always dies before his death, will not get especially far in his contemplation of life. As a hero, Socrates is now required to rest calmly in himself; but as an intellectual tragic hero, he is required to have, in this last moment, strength of spirit enough to carry himself through. So he cannot, like the ordinary tragic hero, concentrate on holding himself upright in the face of death. He must make this movement so quickly that in the same moment he is, in his consciousness, beyond this struggle and asserts himself. Had Socrates, then, fallen silent in the crisis of death, the effect of his life would have been weakened, and he would have given rise to the suspicion that, in him, irony’s elasticity was not a world-force but a game, whose springiness at the decisive moment had to be used by the reverse measure — to hold him up with pathos.

## ch7 ¶57 (text changed)

**before**

> Nevertheless, a last word of Abraham’s has been preserved; and to the extent that I can understand the paradox, I can also understand how Abraham is totally present in this word. First and foremost, he says nothing, and it is in this form that he says what he has to say. His answer to Isaac is ironic in form: to say something and yet not say anything is always irony. Isaac asks Abraham on the assumption that Abraham knows. Now, if Abraham had answered, “I know nothing,” he would have said an untruth. To say something is beyond him, since what he knows is what he cannot say. So he answers: “God will see to the lamb for the burnt offering himself, my son!” From this one sees the double movement in Abraham’s soul, as it has been described in what went before. Had Abraham merely given Isaac up in resignation and done no more, then he would have said an untruth; for he knows perfectly well that God requires Isaac as a sacrifice, and knows that he is himself ready, at this very moment, to sacrifice him. So at every moment, after making this movement, he has made the next one: he has made the movement of faith by virtue of the absurd. To that extent he says nothing untrue; for by virtue of the absurd it is, after all, possible that God could do something entirely different. So he says nothing untrue — but neither does he say anything; for he speaks in a foreign tongue. That becomes clearer still once we remember that the one who was to sacrifice Isaac was Abraham himself. Suppose the task had been different: suppose the Lord had ordered Abraham to take Isaac out to Mount Moriah, and had then let his own lightning strike Isaac and taken him as a sacrifice that way. In that case Abraham would, in a straightforward sense, have been right to speak as enigmatically as he does; for then he himself could not know what would happen. But as the task is set for Abraham, he himself must act. So at the decisive moment he must know what he himself will do, and so he must know that Isaac is to be sacrificed. If he has not been certain of this, he has not made the infinite movement of resignation; and in that case, though his word is certainly no untruth, he is also very far from being Abraham: he is of less account than a tragic hero; indeed, he is an irresolute man who can make up his mind neither to the one thing nor to the other, and who for that reason will always end up speaking in riddles. But such a hesitator is the very parody of the knight of faith.
