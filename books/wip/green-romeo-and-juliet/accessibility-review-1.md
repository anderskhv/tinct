# Accessibility Review 1 — Romeo and Juliet (candidate.json)

**Reviewer stance:** blind read of `candidate.json` only, no reference to
`source.json` or drafting notes. All 25 chapters (Prologue + Act 1 Scene 1
through Act 5 Scene 3), all 1062 paragraphs, read in full and in order — not
sampled.

**Indexing:** chapters are 1-based as stored (`chapters[0]` is chapter
number 1, the Prologue). Paragraphs are 0-based array indices within a
chapter (`paragraphs[0]` is the first paragraph of that chapter). All
paragraph references below use this 0-based paragraph indexing.

## Overall impression

The candidate reads as a genuine, fluent modern-English stage/reading text.
Sentence structure is rebuilt into ordinary contemporary syntax throughout;
long Shakespearean periodic sentences are broken into several shorter ones
without becoming choppy or list-like. Verse speeches (the Prologue, Queen
Mab, the balcony scene, Friar Lawrence's opening speech, Juliet's "Gallop
apace") keep their rhetorical shape and imagery while dropping archaic
diction ("doth," "hath," "'tis," thee/thou forms) in favor of natural
present-day phrasing. A new reader unfamiliar with the play would not need
a glossary to follow the plot, the relationships, or the emotional beats.

Comic/bawdy dialogue (the opening Sampson–Gregory exchange, Mercutio
throughout, the Nurse's digressions) keeps its joke structure — wordplay
that depends on a double meaning (e.g. "maidenheads," the Mercutio/Romeo
"pink"/"pump"/"goose" exchange, "single-soled jest") is rendered so the
joke still lands for a reader without footnotes, rather than being
flattened into plain description.

## Passages a new reader might still find hard, and why

None of these rise to a blocking defect — they are read-twice moments, not
comprehension failures — but a fresh reader would slow down here:

- **Ch5 p24 (Queen Mab speech)** — even in modern English this is a single
  very long cumulative sentence (Mercutio's "This is she—" trails into the
  cut-off at the end of the paragraph, correctly mirroring the source's own
  interruption by Romeo in the next paragraph). A reader will need to hold
  a lot of images in sequence, but this is inherent to the speech's
  structure, not a translation problem — shortening it would falsify the
  cumulative-momentum effect the scene depends on.
- **Ch7 p9 (Mercutio's "conjuring" speech)** — the classical/legendary
  references (Cupid, King Cophetua and the beggar-maid) are kept as proper
  nouns without gloss. A reader unfamiliar with the Cophetua legend won't
  get the specific allusion, but the surrounding sentence makes clear it's
  a love story, so the passage still tracks.
- **Ch10 (the "goose"/"pump"/"cheveril" banter between Romeo and Mercutio,
  p24–p39)** — this is intentionally a rapid-fire pun chain in the source;
  the modern rendering keeps the wordplay chain intact (pink → flower →
  pump → single-soled → goose → cheveril → broad) rather than paraphrasing
  it into exposition. A reader will recognize it as characters showing off
  wit for its own sake even if a couple of individual puns don't fully
  land — which is also true of a modern audience watching the original.
- **Ch18 p33 (Friar Lawrence's potion speech)** — long single-paragraph
  instructions with many clauses; modernized syntax throughout, but the
  paragraph is inherently dense because it's doing a lot of plot work in
  one breath. Not a fidelity or clarity defect, just long.

## Verdict

No blocking accessibility defects found on this blind pass. The prose is
accessible without being thinned out, and difficult passages are difficult
because the content itself is dense (Shakespeare's cumulative imagery,
rapid wordplay chains), not because the modern-English rendering obscures
meaning.
