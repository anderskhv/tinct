## Chapter 33, Paragraph 11 (Smerdyakov's sung verse)

**Source:** "What do I care for royal wealth If but my dear one be in
health? Lord have mercy / On her and on me! ×3"

**Corrected:** "What do I care for royal wealth, if only my dear one is in
health? Lord have mercy / On her and on me! ×3"

**Accessibility verdict:** Clear, natural modern English. No archaism, no
ambiguity. "If only ... is in health" reads as an ordinary modern
conditional wish. No notes.

**Fidelity verdict:** Faithful. Both content words of the couplet's rhyme
pair — "wealth" and "health" — are preserved exactly as in source. "If
but" → "if only" is a legitimate register-neutral paraphrase of the
conditional, no claim added or dropped. The refrain ("Lord have mercy / On
her and on me!" ×3) is untouched and matches source. No invented
rhyme-filler ("treasure," "leisure") survives.

**Recommendation: ACCEPT AS-IS.**

## Chapter 33, Paragraph 12 (dialogue quoting the remembered lyric)

**Source:** "'It was even better last time,' observed the woman's voice.
'You sang "If my darling be in health"; it sounded more tender. I suppose
you've forgotten to-day.'"

**Corrected:** "'It sounded even better last time,' the woman's voice
observed. 'You sang, "If my darling is in health" — it sounded more
tender. I suppose you've forgotten it today.'"

**Accessibility verdict:** Clear and natural. One very minor stylistic
note: the comma before the opening quote ("You sang, 'If my darling is in
health'") is slightly unusual phrasing but not confusing or archaic — a
general reader will parse it correctly on first read. Not a blocker.

**Fidelity verdict — checked jointly with P11, as required:**

Isolating exactly how source's two lines differ from each other:
- P11 (sung): "if **but** my **dear one** be in health"
- P12 (remembered/quoted): "If my **darling** be in health"

The *only* differences in source are (a) dropping "but" and (b) swapping
"dear one" → "darling." Notably, the verb phrase itself — "be in health"
— is **identical** in both source lines; the tenderness the dialogue
attributes to the remembered version comes from the more intimate
"darling," not from any wording change around "health."

Now the corrected pair:
- P11 (sung): "if **only** my **dear one** is in health"
- P12 (quoted): "If my **darling** is in health"

The corrected pair differs from each other in exactly the same two spots
— the conjunction ("only" vs. dropped) and the endearment ("dear one" vs.
"darling") — while "is in health" is identical across both, mirroring
source's identical "be in health" across both. This is the right shape:
not more divergence than source has, not less. No third, invented idea
(no "leisure," no "well and gay," nothing similarly rhyme-driven) has
been substituted in either line. Confirmed clean.

**Recommendation: ACCEPT AS-IS.**

## Chapter 36, Paragraph 1 (Grand Inquisitor epigraph)

**Source:** "No signs from heaven come to-day To add to what the heart
doth say."

**Corrected:** "No signs from heaven come today / To back up what the
heart says."

**Accessibility verdict:** Fully modern, no archaism remaining ("doth"
removed, verb now "says"). Reads naturally as an epigraph couplet.

**Fidelity verdict:** "Add to" → "back up" was already present in
`current_modern_en` (not introduced by this correction) and is a
reasonable paraphrase — "to add to what the heart says" and "to back up
what the heart says" both convey heaven's signs *confirming/reinforcing*
an internal conviction, not contradicting or replacing it. No meaning is
lost. The one actual change in this correction — "doth say" → "says" —
is a pure register fix with no content impact, and it resolves the
internal inconsistency the ledger flags (P2, one paragraph later, already
renders the identical phrase as "says"). Verified P2 is not part of this
staged batch and is out of scope here, consistent with the ledger's
claim.

**Recommendation: ACCEPT AS-IS.**

## Chapter 16, Paragraph 43 (Schiller "Ode to Joy," 18-line stanza)

**Source (relevant excerpt):** "Joy everlasting fostereth ... 'Tis at
her beck the grass hath turned Each blade towards the light ... Her
gifts to man are friends in need, The wreath, the foaming must, To
angels—vision of God's throne, To insects—sensual lust."

**Corrected changes:** "fostereth" → "fosters"; "'Tis at her beck the
grass hath turned" → "At her bidding the grass has turned." Everything
else, including "the foaming must," is left unchanged from source/current.

**Accessibility verdict:** The two changes made are correct, clean
modernizations with no loss of meaning. But the paragraph as a whole is
still heavily archaic outside those two spots: "kindly breast," "drink
Joy" (capitalized, personified), the em-dash constructions, and
especially **"the foaming must."** "Must" as a noun meaning
grape juice/new wine is genuinely obscure to a general modern reader —
most readers will either not parse "must" as a noun at all (expecting
the modal verb) or will simply not know what it means. This is a real,
flagged accessibility gap in the *unmodernized remainder* of the stanza,
not something this specific correction introduces or is expected to fix
(this is an isolated word-level pass, not the full-stanza modernization
that `inventory.md` explicitly defers).

**Fidelity verdict:** Both changes made are faithful, non-rhyme-driven,
meaning-preserving modernizations. "Fostereth" → "fosters" is a pure verb
inflection change. "'Tis at her beck ... hath turned" → "At her bidding
... has turned" preserves the same causal claim (Joy causes the grass to
turn toward light) with no image lost or added. Nothing else in the
paragraph was touched, so no new fidelity risk was introduced anywhere
in the remaining 14 lines.

**On the specific editorial call for "the foaming must":** I agree with
the reviewer's resolution, with a caveat. Rhyme structure in a quoted
poem is a real feature worth preserving, not decoration to be discarded
casually — "must" arrives at the end of its line specifically to rhyme
with "lust" two lines later, and the two words are thematically paired
(wine's intoxication vs. insects' "sensual lust" — Schiller's stanza is
explicitly building a hierarchy of what Joy gives to different orders of
being, and the wine/lust rhyme is part of that structure). No available
plain-English synonym for "must" (fermenting grape juice) rhymes with
"lust" without either sounding absurd or requiring restructuring the
whole quatrain — which would exceed the stated scope of this pass (a
targeted word-level correction, not a full-stanza rewrite). Within that
scope, leaving "must" alone and accepting the rhyme cost over the
plain-language cost is the right call.

Caveat: this is only the right call as long as it's understood to be a
*provisional* decision within a partial-modernization pass, not a
permanent judgment that "must" needs no further attention. When (per the
already-flagged next-batch item) this stanza gets a full modernization
pass, "must" should get a second look — either a footnote/gloss, or a
deliberate full-quatrain rewrite that finds a plain-language equivalent
without breaking the wine/lust pairing. For *this* correction, in
isolation, I would not hold it back over "must."

**Recommendation: ACCEPT AS-IS** for the two changes actually staged
("fostereth"→"fosters," "'Tis at her beck...hath turned"→"At her
bidding...has turned"), and agree with the editorial call to leave "the
foaming must" untouched for now. No fix required to this staged
correction. (Re-flag "must" for glossing/rewrite in the future full-pass
on this stanza — already effectively covered by the existing next-batch
item, no new ledger entry needed unless Anders wants "must" called out
by name.)
