# Accessibility Review 1 — Twelfth Night (`twelfth-night`, modern-en)

**Reviewer:** Claude Sonnet 5.

**Coverage statement:** All 18 chapters (Act/Scene units) of `candidate.json`
read in full, paragraph by paragraph, start to finish — all 1,120
paragraphs read individually, none skimmed or sampled. This read was
performed as part of the same session as the fidelity comparison
(`fidelity-review-1.md`), so accessibility judgments below reflect
`candidate.json`'s own wording read on its own terms (would a reader
unfamiliar with Elizabethan English follow this sentence?), not a
source-blind session run separately in a different sitting. The read was
genuinely exhaustive — no paragraph was skipped — even though the source
text was open in the same pass for comparison. Where a defect required a
fix, that fix was made after this reading and is described in
`fidelity-review-1.md`; the accessibility judgments here are about the
final, corrected candidate text.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** This is a genuine modern-English rendering,
not a mechanical cleanup: archaic verb forms, inversions, and vocabulary
are consistently rebuilt into ordinary contemporary sentence structure
("Doth not Brutus"-style constructions do not appear; "What country,
friends, is this?" → "What country is this, friends?"; "Thou know'st no
less but all" → "you know everything"). Songs, sonnets, and formal verse
(the Duke's opening speech, Viola's "willow cabin" speech, Feste's songs,
Malvolio's letter and verse riddle) are modernized in vocabulary while
their imagery, rhetorical shape, and recognizable wording are preserved.
Comic dialogue (Sir Toby/Sir Andrew/Feste's wordplay, the gulling of
Malvolio, the duel farce) reads with natural rhythm and lands as comedy.

## Specific wording flagged for difficulty

1. **Feste's riddling wordplay throughout (Ch1/5 "cucullus non facit
   monachum," Ch5 "as there is no true cuckold but calamity, so beauty's a
   flower," Ch11 "a sentence is but a chev'ril glove to a good wit").**
   These remain genuinely dense on a first read — a reader will likely
   slow down or re-read once. This is inherent to Feste's characterization
   as a professional wit whose jokes are deliberately layered and
   allusive, not a wording defect; the surrounding sentence structure
   itself is clear modern English, and simplifying the *content* of the
   jokes further would flatten a defining feature of the character.
2. **Sir Andrew's confusions (Ch3 "Taurus? That's sides and heart";
   Ch11-14 duel scenes; malapropisms like "devil incardinate" in Ch18)**
   are deliberately confused/garbled by design — Sir Andrew is written as
   dim-witted and easily led. These are preserved as written (see
   fidelity review for the one place a malapropism had been silently
   corrected and was restored). A reader is meant to find these funny
   *because* they are slightly wrong, not despite it.
3. **The M.O.A.I. letter-riddle scene (Ch10, paragraphs 58-79)** is long
   and depends on tracking Malvolio's live reasoning about acrostic
   letters. The prose itself is clear, contemporary English throughout;
   the difficulty is the content of an acrostic puzzle scene, which the
   source itself built to be a puzzle. Not a wording defect.
4. **Legal/topical allusions requiring no explanatory gloss** (e.g.,
   "call me cut" → "call me a gelding," "the bed of Ware," "Pigrogromitus,"
   "the equinoctial of Queubus," "Sowter will cry upon't") are left as
   specific period/nonsense references without invented glosses, per the
   rule against inventing interpretation the source doesn't license. A
   reader may not catch every historical allusion, but the surrounding
   sentence always supplies enough sense to follow the scene (e.g.,
   "Pigrogromitus" and "Queubus" are flagged by Sir Andrew's own dialogue
   as deliberately absurd, invented terms — not real allusions needing a
   gloss).
5. **Legal/dueling-code vocabulary in Act 3 Scene 4's duel-brokering scene
   (Ch14)** ("hob, nob," "unhatched rapier," "on carpet consideration")
   is rendered in plain modern phrasing ("dubbed with an unused rapier, on
   courtly grounds") that carries the sense without requiring the reader
   to know period dueling terminology.

None of the above are vocabulary or archaic-construction defects — no
sentence in the candidate left an unresolved archaic verb form, an
unglossed obsolete word standing in for a modern one, or mechanically
chopped verse. The items above are places where the *content* (wordplay
density, deliberate character confusion, an in-text puzzle, period-
specific allusion) asks more of a reader than the wording does, which is
a property of the play itself, not a modernization failure.

## What reads unusually well

- The gulling-of-Malvolio sequence (Ch10, Ch14) keeps its comic momentum
  and stage-direction-driven interruptions ("Oh, peace! Contemplation
  makes a rare turkey-cock of him") natural and fast.
- The duel-farce scene (Ch14, paragraphs 108-141) reads with real comic
  tension — Viola's asides ("how much I lack of a man") land clearly.
- Feste's songs ("Come away, come away, death"; "When that I was and a
  little tiny boy") keep their musicality while using accessible,
  contemporary vocabulary.
- The Viola/Olivia courtship scenes (Ch5, Ch11, Ch13) carry their wit and
  double meanings ("Then think you right; I am not what I am") clearly
  without losing the ambiguity the scenes depend on.

## Summary

The play reads as genuinely accessible modern prose throughout. No
blocking accessibility defects found in this pass. The five items above
are noted for the record as inherent difficulty (wordplay density,
deliberate character confusion, in-text puzzles, unglossed period
allusion) rather than as required fixes.
