=== BOOK 2: Book 2 — Moral Virtue ===

--- p0 ---
SRC: Well: human Excellence is of two kinds, Intellectual and Moral: now the Intellectual springs originally, and is increased subsequently, from teaching (for the most part that is), and needs therefore experience and time; whereas the Moral comes from custom, and so the Greek term denoting it is but a slight deflection from the term denoting custom in that language.
CAND: Human excellence, then, is of two kinds: intellectual and moral. Intellectual excellence is chiefly produced and increased by teaching, and so requires experience and time. Moral excellence, by contrast, comes from habit; the Greek word for it (ēthikē) is in fact only a slight variation on the word for custom (ethos).

--- p1 ---
SRC: From this fact it is plain that not one of the Moral Virtues comes to be in us merely by nature: because of such things as exist by nature, none can be changed by custom: a stone, for instance, by nature gravitating downwards, could never by custom be brought to ascend, not even if one were to try and accustom it by throwing it up ten thousand times; nor could file again be brought to descend, nor in fact could anything whose nature is in one way be brought by custom to be in another. The Virtues then come to be in us neither by nature, nor in despite of nature, but we are furnished by nature with a capacity for receiving themu and are perfected in them through custom.
CAND: From this it is plain that none of the moral virtues arises in us by nature. Nothing that exists by nature can be changed by habit. A stone, which by nature falls downward, cannot be trained by habit to rise, not even if you tossed it upward ten thousand times. Fire cannot be trained to descend. Nothing whose nature is in one way can by habit be brought to be in another. The virtues, then, do not come to us by nature, nor against nature; rather, we are equipped by nature with the capacity to receive them, and this capacity is perfected by habit.

--- p2 ---
SRC: Again, in whatever cases we get things by nature, we get the faculties first and perform the acts of working afterwards; an illustration of which is afforded by the case of our bodily senses, for it was not from having often seen or heard that we got these senses, but just the reverse: we had them and so exercised them, but did not have them because we had exercised them. But the Virtues we get by first performing single acts of working, which, again, is the case of other things, as the arts for instance; for what we have to make when we have learned how, these we learn how to make by making: men come to be builders, for instance, by building; harp-players, by playing on the harp: exactly so, by doing just actions we come to be just; by doing the actions of self-mastery we come to be perfected in self-mastery; and by doing brave actions brave.
CAND: Again, where we get capacities by nature, we possess the faculty first and perform the activities afterwards. The bodily senses are a clear example: it was not from having often seen or heard that we got these senses, but the reverse — we had them and then exercised them, not the other way around. But the virtues we get by first performing the actions. It is the same with the arts: what we have to learn to make, we learn to make by making. People become builders by building, harpists by playing the harp. In the same way, by doing just acts we become just, by doing acts of self-control we become self-controlled, and by doing brave acts we become brave.

--- p3 ---
SRC: And to the truth of this testimony is borne by what takes place in communities: because the law-givers make the individual members good men by habituation, and this is the intention certainly of every law-giver, and all who do not effect it well fail of their intent; and herein consists the difference between a good Constitution and a bad.
CAND: This is confirmed by what happens in communities. Lawgivers make their citizens good by habituating them, and this is what every lawgiver intends; those who do not do it well miss their aim. And this is what separates a good constitution from a bad one.

--- p4 ---
SRC: Again, every Virtue is either produced or destroyed from and by the very same circumstances: art too in like manner; I mean it is by playing the harp that both the good and the bad harp-players are formed: and similarly builders and all the rest; by building well men will become good builders; by doing it badly bad ones: in fact, if this had not been so, there would have been no need of instructors, but all men would have been at once good or bad in their several arts without them.
CAND: Every virtue is both produced and destroyed by the same circumstances. So is every art. It is by playing the harp that both good and bad harpists are made, and similarly with builders and all the rest: by building well men become good builders, by building badly, bad ones. If this were not so, there would be no need of teachers; everyone would be naturally good or bad at his craft.

--- p5 ---
SRC: So too then is it with the Virtues: for by acting in the various relations in which we are thrown with our fellow men, we come to be, some just, some unjust: and by acting in dangerous positions and being habituated to feel fear or confidence, we come to be, some brave, others cowards.
CAND: So too with the virtues. By our actions in dealings with other people, some of us become just, others unjust. By our actions in the face of danger, being habituated to feel fear or confidence, some become brave and others cowards.

--- p6 ---
SRC: Similarly is it also with respect to the occasions of lust and anger: for some men come to be perfected in self-mastery and mild, others destitute of all self-control and passionate; the one class by behaving in one way under them, the other by behaving in another. Or, in one word, the habits are produced from the acts of working like to them: and so what we have to do is to give a certain character to these particular acts, because the habits formed correspond to the differences of these.
CAND: It is the same with situations involving desire and anger. Some come to be self-controlled and even-tempered, others without self-control and hot-headed, according to how they behave in such situations. In a word, habits are produced from activities like them. Our task, then, is to give a certain character to these activities, since habits correspond to the differences in them.

--- p7 ---
SRC: So then, whether we are accustomed this way or that straight from childhood, makes not a small but an important difference, or rather I would say it makes all the difference.
CAND: Whether we are habituated in one way or another from childhood, then, makes not a small but a very great difference — or rather, it makes all the difference.

--- p8 ---
SRC: Since then the object of the present treatise is not mere speculation, as it is of some others (for we are enquiring not merely that we may know what virtue is but that we may become virtuous, else it would have been useless), we must consider as to the particular actions how we are to do them, because, as we have just said, the quality of the habits that shall be formed depends on these.
CAND: Since the aim of the present treatise is not mere speculation, as with some others (we are inquiring not just to know what virtue is but to become good; otherwise the study would be pointless), we must ask how to perform actions; for, as I just said, the quality of the resulting habits depends on this.

--- p9 ---
SRC: Now, that we are to act in accordance with Right Reason is a general maxim, and may for the present be taken for granted: we will speak of it hereafter, and say both what Right Reason is, and what are its relations to the other virtues.
CAND: That we should act in accordance with right reason is a general maxim, and may be granted for the present. We will speak of it later — what right reason is, and how it is related to the other virtues.

--- p10 ---
SRC: But let this point be first thoroughly understood between us, that all which can be said on moral action must be said in outline, as it were, and not exactly: for as we remarked at the commencement, such reasoning only must be required as the nature of the subject-matter admits of, and matters of moral action and expediency have no fixedness any more than matters of health. And if the subject in its general maxims is such, still less in its application to particular cases is exactness attainable: because these fall not under any art or system of rules, but it must be left in each instance to the individual agents to look to the exigencies of the particular case, as it is in the art of healing, or that of navigating a ship. Still, though the present subject is confessedly such, we must try and do what we can for it.
CAND: But let this be understood between us from the start: everything said about moral action must be in outline, not with precision. As I remarked at the beginning, only the precision the subject allows should be expected. Matters of moral action and expediency have no more fixedness than matters of health. And if general statements are imprecise, even less can we be exact in applying them to particular cases. Particular cases fall under no art or fixed system of rules; in each case agents must look at the particulars themselves, just as in medicine or navigation. So though our subject is confessedly such, we must do what we can with it.

--- p11 ---
SRC: First then this must be noted, that it is the nature of such things to be spoiled by defect and excess; as we see in the case of health and strength (since for the illustration of things which cannot be seen we must use those that can), for excessive training impairs the strength as well as deficient: meat and drink, in like manner, in too great or too small quantities, impair the health: while in due proportion they cause, increase, and preserve it.
CAND: First, note this: it is the nature of such things to be spoiled by deficiency and by excess. We see this with health and strength (we must use visible examples to illustrate the invisible). Too much training ruins strength, and so does too little. Food and drink in too great or too small quantities ruin health; in due proportion they produce, increase, and preserve it.

--- p12 ---
SRC: Thus it is therefore with the habits of perfected Self-Mastery and Courage and the rest of the Virtues: for the man who flies from and fears all things, and never stands up against anything, comes to be a coward; and he who fears nothing, but goes at everything, comes to be rash. In like manner too, he that tastes of every pleasure and abstains from none comes to lose all self-control; while he who avoids all, as do the dull and clownish, comes as it were to lose his faculties of perception: that is to say, the habits of perfected Self-Mastery and Courage are spoiled by the excess and defect, but by the mean state are preserved.
CAND: So it is with the habits of self-mastery, courage, and the other virtues. The man who flees from and fears everything, standing up to nothing, becomes a coward. The man who fears nothing and goes at everything becomes rash. In the same way, one who tastes every pleasure and refrains from none loses all self-control, while one who avoids all pleasure, like the dull and boorish, becomes insensible. Self-mastery and courage are spoiled by excess and deficiency, but preserved by the mean.

--- p13 ---
SRC: Furthermore, not only do the origination, growth, and marring of the habits come from and by the same circumstances, but also the acts of working after the habits are formed will be exercised on the same: for so it is also with those other things which are more directly matters of sight, strength for instance: for this comes by taking plenty of food and doing plenty of work, and the man who has attained strength is best able to do these: and so it is with the Virtues, for not only do we by abstaining from pleasures come to be perfected in Self-Mastery, but when we have come to be so we can best abstain from them: similarly too with Courage: for it is by accustoming ourselves to despise objects of fear and stand up against them that we come to be brave; and after we have come to be so we shall be best able to stand up against such objects.
CAND: Moreover, not only do habits originate, grow, and decay from and by the same circumstances; the activities they produce are also exercised on these same circumstances. So it is with strength, for instance: it comes by taking plenty of food and doing plenty of work, and the man who has attained strength is best able to do these very things. So with the virtues: by abstaining from pleasures we become self-controlled, and once we are so, we are best able to abstain from them. So with courage: by habituating ourselves to despise and face objects of fear we become brave, and once brave we are best able to face such objects.

--- p14 ---
SRC: And for a test of the formation of the habits we must take the pleasure or pain which succeeds the acts; for he is perfected in Self-Mastery who not only abstains from the bodily pleasures but is glad to do so; whereas he who abstains but is sorry to do it has not Self-Mastery: he again is brave who stands up against danger, either with positive pleasure or at least without any pain; whereas he who does it with pain is not brave.
CAND: As a test of whether the habits have been formed, we must take the pleasure or pain that follows the act. The self-controlled man not only abstains from bodily pleasures but is glad to do so; the man who abstains but is sorry to has no self-mastery. The brave man faces danger either with positive pleasure or at least without pain; the one who does it with pain is not brave.

--- p15 ---
SRC: For Moral Virtue has for its object-matter pleasures and pains, because by reason of pleasure we do what is bad, and by reason of pain decline doing what is right (for which cause, as Plato observes, men should have been trained straight from their childhood to receive pleasure and pain from proper objects, for this is the right education). Again: since Virtues have to do with actions and feelings, and on every feeling and every action pleasure and pain follow, here again is another proof that Virtue has for its object-matter pleasure and pain. The same is shown also by the fact that punishments are effected through the instrumentality of these; because they are of the nature of remedies, and it is the nature of remedies to be the contraries of the ills they cure. Again, to quote what we said before: every habit of the Soul by its very nature has relation to, and exerts itself upon, things of the same kind as those by which it is naturally deteriorated or improved: now such habits do come to be vicious by reason of pleasures and pains, that is, by men pursuing or avoiding respectively, either such as they ought not, or at wrong times, or in wrong manner, and so forth (for which reason, by the way, some people define the Virtues as certain states of impassibility and utter quietude, but they are wrong because they speak without modification, instead of adding “as they ought,” “as they ought not,” and “when,” and so on). Virtue then is assumed to be that habit which is such, in relation to pleasures and pains, as to effect the best results, and Vice the contrary.
CAND: Moral virtue is concerned with pleasures and pains. For because of pleasure we do what is bad, and because of pain we avoid what is right. This is why, as Plato observes, people should be trained from childhood to feel pleasure and pain at the proper objects; this is what right education is. Further: since the virtues have to do with actions and feelings, and every feeling and action is followed by pleasure or pain, here is another proof that virtue concerns pleasure and pain. The same is shown by the fact that punishments work through these; punishments are a kind of cure, and cures are by nature contraries of the ills they heal. To repeat what we said before: every habit of the soul, by its nature, bears on and exerts itself upon the same things by which it is naturally made better or worse. Such habits become vicious because of pleasures and pains — people pursuing or avoiding them when they should not, or at the wrong time, or in the wrong way. For this reason some define the virtues as states of impassivity and utter quietude; but they are wrong, because they speak without qualification, not adding "as one ought," "as one ought not," "when," and so on. Virtue, then, is a habit so related to pleasures and pains as to produce the best results, and vice is its contrary.

--- p16 ---
SRC: The following considerations may also serve to set this in a clear light. There are principally three things moving us to choice and three to avoidance, the honourable, the expedient, the pleasant; and their three contraries, the dishonourable, the hurtful, and the painful: now the good man is apt to go right, and the bad man wrong, with respect to all these of course, but most specially with respect to pleasure: because not only is this common to him with all animals but also it is a concomitant of all those things which move to choice, since both the honourable and the expedient give an impression of pleasure.
CAND: Consider the matter also in this way. There are principally three things that move us to choice and three to avoidance: the noble, the expedient, and the pleasant; and their three contraries: the base, the harmful, and the painful. The good man tends to go right, and the bad to go wrong, with respect to all of these — but especially with respect to pleasure. Pleasure is not only common to us and all animals; it is also a concomitant of everything that moves us to choice, since the noble and the expedient both give an impression of pleasure.

--- p17 ---
SRC: Again, it grows up with us all from infancy, and so it is a hard matter to remove from ourselves this feeling, engrained as it is into our very life.
CAND: Moreover, pleasure grows up with us from infancy, so it is difficult to remove this feeling, engrained as it is into our very life.

--- p18 ---
SRC: Again, we adopt pleasure and pain (some of us more, and some less) as the measure even of actions: for this cause then our whole business must be with them, since to receive right or wrong impressions of pleasure and pain is a thing of no little importance in respect of the actions. Once more; it is harder, as Heraclitus says, to fight against pleasure than against anger: now it is about that which is more than commonly difficult that art comes into being, and virtue too, because in that which is difficult the good is of a higher order: and so for this reason too both virtue and moral philosophy generally must wholly busy themselves respecting pleasures and pains, because he that uses these well will be good, he that does so ill will be bad.
CAND: We also adopt pleasure and pain (some of us more, some less) as the measure even of actions. All our work, therefore, must be with them; to receive right or wrong impressions of pleasure and pain is no small matter for conduct. Again, as Heraclitus says, it is harder to fight against pleasure than against anger. Art, and virtue too, arise in connection with what is more than usually difficult, since the good is of a higher order precisely where the task is hard. For this reason both virtue and moral philosophy in general must wholly occupy themselves with pleasures and pains; he who uses them well will be good, and he who uses them badly will be bad.

--- p19 ---
SRC: Let us then be understood to have stated, that Virtue has for its object-matter pleasures and pains, and that it is either increased or marred by the same circumstances (differently used) by which it is originally generated, and that it exerts itself on the same circumstances out of which it was generated.
CAND: Let this, then, be our position: that virtue concerns pleasures and pains, and that the same circumstances (used differently) by which it is originally produced, also increase or mar it, and that its activity is exercised on the very circumstances out of which it came to be.

--- p20 ---
SRC: Now I can conceive a person perplexed as to the meaning of our statement, that men must do just actions to become just, and those of self-mastery to acquire the habit of self-mastery; “for,” he would say, “if men are doing the actions they have the respective virtues already, just as men are grammarians or musicians when they do the actions of either art.” May we not reply by saying that it is not so even in the case of the arts referred to: because a man may produce something grammatical either by chance or the suggestion of another; but then only will he be a grammarian when he not only produces something grammatical but does so grammarian-wise, _i.e._ in virtue of the grammatical knowledge he himself possesses.
CAND: Now one might be perplexed by our claim that people become just by doing just acts and self-controlled by doing acts of self-control. One might say: if people are doing the actions, they must already have the virtues — just as people who do grammatical or musical acts are already grammarians or musicians. May we not reply that it is not quite so even in the case of those arts? A man may produce something grammatical either by chance or on someone else's suggestion. He will be a grammarian only when he not only produces something grammatical but does so in the manner of a grammarian — that is, in virtue of the grammatical knowledge he himself possesses.

--- p21 ---
SRC: Again, the cases of the arts and the virtues are not parallel: because those things which are produced by the arts have their excellence in themselves, and it is sufficient therefore that these when produced should be in a certain state: but those which are produced in the way of the virtues, are, strictly speaking, actions of a certain kind (say of Justice or perfected Self-Mastery), not merely if in themselves they are in a certain state but if also he who does them does them being himself in a certain state, first if knowing what he is doing, next if with deliberate preference, and with such preference for the things’ own sake; and thirdly if being himself stable and unapt to change. Now to constitute possession of the arts these requisites are not reckoned in, excepting the one point of knowledge: whereas for possession of the virtues knowledge avails little or nothing, but the other requisites avail not a little, but, in fact, are all in all, and these requisites as a matter of fact do come from oftentimes doing the actions of Justice and perfected Self-Mastery.
CAND: Besides, the cases of the arts and the virtues are not parallel. Things produced by the arts have their excellence in themselves; it is enough that they turn out a certain way. But in the case of the virtues, the actions are not just of a certain kind (of justice, say, or of self-mastery); they must also be done by an agent who is in a certain state — first, knowing what he is doing; second, choosing the acts deliberately, and choosing them for their own sakes; and third, doing them from a stable and unchanging character. For the possession of an art, these requirements do not count, except the knowledge. For the possession of the virtues, knowledge counts for little or nothing; the other requirements count for much — in fact, are all in all — and they come from repeatedly doing acts of justice and self-mastery.

--- p22 ---
SRC: The facts, it is true, are called by the names of these habits when they are such as the just or perfectly self-mastering man would do; but he is not in possession of the virtues who merely does these facts, but he who also so does them as the just and self-mastering do them.
CAND: The acts are called just or self-controlled when they are such as a just or self-controlled man would do. But a man does not possess the virtues merely by doing these acts; he must do them in the way the just and self-controlled do them.

--- p23 ---
SRC: We are right then in saying, that these virtues are formed in a man by his doing the actions; but no one, if he should leave them undone, would be even in the way to become a good man. Yet people in general do not perform these actions, but taking refuge in talk they flatter themselves they are philosophising, and that they will so be good men: acting in truth very like those sick people who listen to the doctor with great attention but do nothing that he tells them: just as these then cannot be well bodily under such a course of treatment, so neither can those be mentally by such philosophising.
CAND: We are right, then, to say that these virtues are formed by doing the actions, and that without doing them a man will not even be on the way to being good. But most people do not perform these actions. They take refuge in talk and flatter themselves that they are philosophizing and will in this way become good — behaving much like sick people who listen attentively to the doctor but do none of what he prescribes. Just as those cannot get well in body under such treatment, these cannot get well in soul by such philosophizing.

--- p24 ---
SRC: Next, we must examine what Virtue is. Well, since the things which come to be in the mind are, in all, of three kinds, Feelings, Capacities, States, Virtue of course must belong to one of the three classes.
CAND: Next, we must examine what virtue is. Since what comes to be in the soul is of three kinds — feelings, capacities, and states — virtue must belong to one of these three classes.

--- p25 ---
SRC: By Feelings, I mean such as lust, anger, fear, confidence, envy, joy, friendship, hatred, longing, emulation, compassion, in short all such as are followed by pleasure or pain: by Capacities, those in right of which we are said to be capable of these feelings; as by virtue of which we are able to have been made angry, or grieved, or to have compassionated; by States, those in right of which we are in a certain relation good or bad to the aforementioned feelings; to having been made angry, for instance, we are in a wrong relation if in our anger we were too violent or too slack, but if we were in the happy medium we are in a right relation to the feeling. And so on of the rest.
CAND: By feelings I mean such things as desire, anger, fear, confidence, envy, joy, love, hate, longing, emulation, pity — in short, all that are accompanied by pleasure or pain. By capacities I mean those in virtue of which we are said to be capable of these feelings: that is, of having become angry, or pained, or pitiful. By states I mean those in virtue of which we stand in some relation, well or badly, to the feelings mentioned. In relation to anger, for instance, we stand badly if we feel it too violently or too slackly, but well if we feel it in the mean. Similarly with the rest.

--- p26 ---
SRC: Now Feelings neither the virtues nor vices are, because in right of the Feelings we are not denominated either good or bad, but in right of the virtues and vices we are.
CAND: The virtues and vices are not feelings. We are not called good or bad in virtue of the feelings, but in virtue of the virtues and vices.

--- p27 ---
SRC: Again, in right of the Feelings we are neither praised nor blamed, (for a man is not commended for being afraid or being angry, nor blamed for being angry merely but for being so in a particular way), but in right of the virtues and vices we are.
CAND: Again, we are neither praised nor blamed in virtue of the feelings — a man is not commended for being afraid or angry, nor blamed for being angry simply, but for being angry in a particular way. But in virtue of the virtues and vices we are praised or blamed.

--- p28 ---
SRC: Again, both anger and fear we feel without moral choice, whereas the virtues are acts of moral choice, or at least certainly not independent of it.
CAND: Again, we feel anger and fear without deliberate choice, whereas the virtues are choices, or at any rate not independent of choice.

--- p29 ---
SRC: Moreover, in right of the Feelings we are said to be moved, but in right of the virtues and vices not to be moved, but disposed, in a certain way.
CAND: Moreover, with respect to the feelings we are said to be moved; with respect to the virtues and vices, not to be moved, but to be disposed in a certain way.

--- p30 ---
SRC: And for these same reasons they are not Capacities, for we are not called good or bad merely because we are able to feel, nor are we praised or blamed.
CAND: For these same reasons the virtues are not capacities either. We are not called good or bad simply because we are able to feel, nor are we praised or blamed for it.

--- p31 ---
SRC: And again, Capacities we have by nature, but we do not come to be good or bad by nature, as we have said before.
CAND: Moreover, we have the capacities by nature, but we do not become good or bad by nature, as we said earlier.

--- p32 ---
SRC: Since then the virtues are neither Feelings nor Capacities, it remains that they must be States.
CAND: Since the virtues are neither feelings nor capacities, it remains that they must be states.

--- p33 ---
SRC: Now what the genus of Virtue is has been said; but we must not merely speak of it thus, that it is a state but say also what kind of a state it is.
CAND: We have now said what the genus of virtue is. But we must not merely say it is a state; we must also say what kind of state.

--- p34 ---
SRC: We must observe then that all excellence makes that whereof it is the excellence both to be itself in a good state and to perform its work well. The excellence of the eye, for instance, makes both the eye good and its work also: for by the excellence of the eye we see well. So too the excellence of the horse makes a horse good, and good in speed, and in carrying his rider, and standing up against the enemy. If then this is universally the case, the excellence of Man, i.e. Virtue, must be a state whereby Man comes to be good and whereby he will perform well his proper work. Now how this shall be it is true we have said already, but still perhaps it may throw light on the subject to see what is its characteristic nature.
CAND: Observe, then, that every excellence both makes its possessor good and enables it to perform its function well. The excellence of the eye makes the eye good and makes it see well; the excellence of the horse makes the horse good — good at running, carrying its rider, and facing the enemy. If this holds universally, then the excellence of the human being — that is, virtue — must be a state that makes him good and enables him to perform his proper function well. How this happens has been said already, but it may also clarify things to consider the distinguishing nature of virtue.

--- p35 ---
SRC: In all quantity then, whether continuous or discrete, one may take the greater part, the less, or the exactly equal, and these either with reference to the thing itself, or relatively to us: and the exactly equal is a mean between excess and defect. Now by the mean of the thing, _i.e._ absolute mean, I denote that which is equidistant from either extreme (which of course is one and the same to all), and by the mean relatively to ourselves, that which is neither too much nor too little for the particular individual. This of course is not one nor the same to all: for instance, suppose ten is too much and two too little, people take six for the absolute mean; because it exceeds the smaller sum by exactly as much as it is itself exceeded by the larger, and this mean is according to arithmetical proportion.
CAND: In everything continuous or discrete, one can take a greater part, a smaller part, or an equal part — and these either with reference to the thing itself or relatively to us. The equal part is a mean between excess and deficiency. By the mean in the thing itself — the absolute mean — I mean what is equidistant from either extreme, which is one and the same for everyone. By the mean relative to us, I mean what is neither too much nor too little for the particular person — which is not one and the same for all. If ten is too much and two is too little, for example, people take six as the absolute mean; it exceeds the smaller by exactly as much as it is exceeded by the larger — an arithmetical proportion.

--- p36 ---
SRC: But the mean relatively to ourselves must not be so found ; for it does not follow, supposing ten minæ is too large a quantity to eat and two too small, that the trainer will order his man six; because for the person who is to take it this also may be too much or too little: for Milo it would be too little, but for a man just commencing his athletic exercises too much: similarly too of the exercises themselves, as running or wrestling.
CAND: But the mean relative to us is not to be found in this way. If ten pounds of food is too much for someone and two too little, it does not follow that a trainer will order six pounds. That too may be too much or too little for the particular eater. For Milo it would be too little; for a man just beginning his training, too much. Likewise with exercises such as running or wrestling.

--- p37 ---
SRC: So then it seems every one possessed of skill avoids excess and defect, but seeks for and chooses the mean, not the absolute but the relative.
CAND: Every skilled person, then, avoids excess and deficiency and seeks out the mean — not the absolute mean, but the one relative to us.

--- p38 ---
SRC: Now if all skill thus accomplishes well its work by keeping an eye on the mean, and bringing the works to this point (whence it is common enough to say of such works as are in a good state, “one cannot add to or take ought from them,” under the notion of excess or defect destroying goodness but the mean state preserving it), and good artisans, as we say, work with their eye on this, and excellence, like nature, is more exact and better than any art in the world, it must have an aptitude to aim at the mean.
CAND: If every skill does its work well by keeping its eye on the mean and bringing its product to this point (hence the common remark of well-made things: "one can neither add to them nor take away," since excess and deficiency destroy goodness while the mean preserves it), and if good craftsmen, as we say, work with their eye on this, and if excellence, like nature, is more exact and better than any art, then excellence must aim at the mean.

--- p39 ---
