=== BOOK 3: Book 3 — Moral Responsibility and Particular Virtues ===

--- p0 ---
SRC: Now since Virtue is concerned with the regulation of feelings and actions, and praise and blame arise upon such as are voluntary, while for the involuntary allowance is made, and sometimes compassion is excited, it is perhaps a necessary task for those who are investigating the nature of Virtue to draw out the distinction between what is voluntary and what involuntary; and it is certainly useful for legislators, with respect to the assigning of honours and punishments.
CAND: Since virtue is concerned with the regulation of feelings and actions, and since praise and blame apply to what is voluntary while allowance, and sometimes pity, is made for the involuntary, it is perhaps a necessary task for anyone investigating virtue to draw the distinction between the voluntary and the involuntary. It is also useful for legislators in assigning honors and punishments.

--- p1 ---
SRC: Involuntary actions then are thought to be of two kinds, being done either on compulsion, or by reason of ignorance. An action is, properly speaking, compulsory, when the origination is external to the agent, being such that in it the agent (perhaps we may more properly say the patient) contributes nothing; as if a wind were to convey you anywhere, or men having power over your person.
CAND: Involuntary actions are thought to be of two kinds: those done under compulsion, and those done through ignorance. An action is properly compulsory when its origin lies outside the agent — such that the agent (or, better, the patient) contributes nothing — as when a wind carries you somewhere, or men who have you in their power do.

--- p2 ---
SRC: But when actions are done, either from fear of greater evils, or from some honourable motive, as, for instance, if you were ordered to commit some base act by a despot who had your parents or children in his power, and they were to be saved upon your compliance or die upon your refusal, in such cases there is room for a question whether the actions are voluntary or involuntary.
CAND: But when actions are done either from fear of greater evils, or from some noble motive — for instance, if a tyrant holding your parents or children ordered you to commit some shameful act, and compliance would save them while refusal would kill them — in such cases there is room to ask whether the actions are voluntary or involuntary.

--- p3 ---
SRC: A similar question arises with respect to cases of throwing goods overboard in a storm: abstractedly no man throws away his property willingly, but with a view to his own and his shipmates’ safety any one would who had any sense.
CAND: A similar question arises with throwing goods overboard in a storm. In the abstract no one throws away his property willingly; but with a view to his own and his shipmates' safety, any sensible man would.

--- p4 ---
SRC: The truth is, such actions are of a mixed kind, but are most like voluntary actions; for they are choice-worthy at the time when they are being done, and the end or object of the action must be taken with reference to the actual occasion. Further, we must denominate an action voluntary or involuntary at the time of doing it: now in the given case the man acts voluntarily, because the originating of the motion of his limbs in such actions rests with himself; and where the origination is in himself it rests with himself to do or not to do.
CAND: The truth is that such actions are mixed, but they are more like voluntary ones. They are worth choosing at the moment they are done; and the end of an action must be taken with reference to the actual occasion. Further, we must call an action voluntary or involuntary at the time it is done. In the given case the man acts voluntarily, because the origin of the motion of his limbs rests with him; and where the origin is in himself, it rests with him to do or not to do.

--- p5 ---
SRC: Such actions then are voluntary, though in the abstract perhaps involuntary because no one would choose any of such things in and by itself.
CAND: Such actions are voluntary, then, though in the abstract perhaps involuntary, since no one would choose any of them by itself.

--- p6 ---
SRC: But for such actions men sometimes are even praised, as when they endure any disgrace or pain to secure great and honourable equivalents; if _vice versâ_, then they are blamed, because it shows a base mind to endure things very disgraceful for no honourable object, or for a trifling one.
CAND: For such actions men are sometimes even praised, when they endure some disgrace or pain for a great and noble equivalent. Conversely they are blamed; for it shows a base mind to endure things extremely disgraceful for no noble object, or for a trifling one.

--- p7 ---
SRC: For some again no praise is given, but allowance is made; as where a man does what he should not by reason of such things as overstrain the powers of human nature, or pass the limits of human endurance.
CAND: For some acts no praise is given, but allowance is made — when a man does what he should not from things that overstrain human nature or pass the limits of human endurance.

--- p8 ---
SRC: Some acts perhaps there are for which compulsion cannot be pleaded, but a man should rather suffer the worst and die; how absurd, for instance, are the pleas of compulsion with which Alcmaeon in Euripides’ play excuses his matricide!
CAND: Some acts, however, cannot be excused by compulsion; a man should rather suffer the worst and die. How absurd the pleas of compulsion with which Alcmaeon in Euripides' play excuses his matricide!

--- p9 ---
SRC: But it is difficult sometimes to decide what kind of thing should be chosen instead of what, or what endured in preference to what, and much moreso to abide by one’s decisions: for in general the alternatives are painful, and the actions required are base, and so praise or blame is awarded according as persons have been compelled or no.
CAND: It is sometimes difficult to decide what sort of thing should be chosen in place of what, or endured in preference to what — and still more difficult to abide by one's decisions. The alternatives are generally painful and the actions required base, so praise or blame is awarded according to whether a person has been compelled or not.

--- p10 ---
SRC: What kind of actions then are to be called compulsory? may we say, simply and abstractedly whenever the cause is external and the agent contributes nothing; and that where the acts are in themselves such as one would not wish but choice-worthy at the present time and in preference to such and such things, and where the origination rests with the agent, the actions are in themselves involuntary but at the given time and in preference to such and such things voluntary; and they are more like voluntary than involuntary, because the actions consist of little details, and these are voluntary.
CAND: What kind of actions, then, are to be called compulsory? We may say: simply and abstractedly, those whose cause is external and to which the agent contributes nothing. Where the acts in themselves are such as one would not wish but are worth choosing at the present moment and in preference to certain alternatives, and the origin rests with the agent, the actions are in themselves involuntary but, at the given time and in preference to those alternatives, voluntary. They are more like voluntary actions than involuntary, because actions consist in details, and these details are voluntary.

--- p11 ---
SRC: But what kind of things one ought to choose instead of what, it is not easy to settle, for there are many differences in particular instances.
CAND: But what sort of things one ought to choose instead of what is not easy to settle, since there are many differences in particular cases.

--- p12 ---
SRC: But suppose a person should say, things pleasant and honourable exert a compulsive force (for that they are external and do compel); at that rate every action is on compulsion, because these are universal motives of action.
CAND: Suppose someone said that pleasant and noble things exert a compelling force, since they are external and do compel us. At that rate every action would be on compulsion, since these are universal motives.

--- p13 ---
SRC: Again, they who act on compulsion and against their will do so with pain; but they who act by reason of what is pleasant or honourable act with pleasure.
CAND: Besides, those who act on compulsion and against their will do so with pain, while those who act for the sake of what is pleasant or noble act with pleasure.

--- p14 ---
SRC: It is truly absurd for a man to attribute his actions to external things instead of to his own capacity for being easily caught by them; or, again, to ascribe the honourable to himself, and the base ones to pleasure.
CAND: It is absurd to attribute one's actions to external things, rather than to one's own ready capacity to be captivated by them; or to take credit for one's noble actions while blaming pleasure for the base ones.

--- p15 ---
SRC: So then that seems to be compulsory “whose origination is from without, the party compelled contributing nothing.”
CAND: So what is compulsory seems to be whatever has its origin from outside, the compelled party contributing nothing.

--- p16 ---
SRC: Now every action of which ignorance is the cause is not-voluntary, but that only is involuntary which is attended with pain and remorse; for clearly the man who has done anything by reason of ignorance, but is not annoyed at his own action, cannot be said to have done it _with_ his will because he did not know he was doing it, nor again _against_ his will because he is not sorry for it.
CAND: Now every action done through ignorance is non-voluntary; but only that is involuntary which is attended with pain and regret. Someone who has done something through ignorance but is not distressed at his own action cannot be said to have done it with his will, because he did not know he was doing it; but neither against his will, because he is not sorry for it.

--- p17 ---
SRC: So then of the class “acting by reason of ignorance,” he who feels regret afterwards is thought to be an involuntary agent, and him that has no such feeling, since he certainly is different from the other, we will call a not-voluntary agent; for as there is a real difference it is better to have a proper name.
CAND: So within the class of those who act through ignorance, the one who feels regret afterwards is counted as an involuntary agent. The one who feels no regret we will call a non-voluntary agent, since he is genuinely different; where there is a real difference, it is better to have a proper name.

--- p18 ---
SRC: Again, there seems to be a difference between acting _because of_ ignorance and acting _with_ ignorance: for instance, we do not usually assign ignorance as the cause of the actions of the drunken or angry man, but either the drunkenness or the anger, yet they act not knowingly but with ignorance.
CAND: There also seems to be a difference between acting because of ignorance and acting with ignorance. We do not usually give ignorance as the cause of the actions of the drunken or angry man, but drunkenness or anger; yet he acts not knowingly, but with ignorance.

--- p19 ---
SRC: Again, every bad man is ignorant what he ought to do and what to leave undone, and by reason of such error men become unjust and wholly evil.
CAND: Moreover, every bad man is ignorant of what he ought to do and what to leave undone. Through such error men become unjust and generally evil.

--- p20 ---
SRC: Again, we do not usually apply the term involuntary when a man is ignorant of his own true interest; because ignorance which affects moral choice constitutes depravity but not involuntariness: nor does any ignorance of principle (because for this men are blamed) but ignorance in particular details, wherein consists the action and wherewith it is concerned, for in these there is both compassion and allowance, because he who acts in ignorance of any of them acts in a proper sense involuntarily.
CAND: We do not usually apply the term "involuntary" when a man is ignorant of his own true interest. Ignorance affecting moral choice constitutes depravity, not involuntariness. Nor does ignorance of principles count as involuntary (for men are blamed for such ignorance); it is ignorance of particular circumstances in which the action consists and with which it is concerned, where there is both pity and allowance, because he who acts in ignorance of these acts involuntarily in the proper sense.

--- p21 ---
SRC: It may be as well, therefore, to define these particular details; what they are, and how many; viz. who acts, what he is doing, with respect to what or in what, sometimes with what, as with what instrument, and with what result; as that of preservation, for instance, and how, as whether softly or violently.
CAND: It may be useful, then, to define these particular circumstances — what they are, and how many. Namely: who acts, what he is doing, with respect to what or in what, sometimes with what (as with what instrument), and with what result (for example, for preservation), and how (whether gently or violently).

--- p22 ---
SRC: All these particulars, in one and the same case, no man in his senses could be ignorant of; plainly not of the agent, being himself. But what he is doing a man may be ignorant, as men in speaking say a thing escaped them unawares; or as Aeschylus did with respect to the Mysteries, that he was not aware that it was unlawful to speak of them; or as in the case of that catapult accident the other day the man said he discharged it merely to display its operation. Or a person might suppose a son to be an enemy, as Merope did; or that the spear really pointed was rounded off; or that the stone was a pumice; or in striking with a view to save might kill; or might strike when merely wishing to show another, as people do in sham-fighting.
CAND: No sane man could be ignorant of all these particulars in one and the same case — and clearly not of the agent, being himself. But he may be ignorant of what he is doing. Speakers sometimes say a thing slipped out unawares; or Aeschylus was supposed not to have realized it was unlawful to speak of the Mysteries; or the man in that recent catapult accident said he fired it merely to demonstrate it. One might suppose one's son to be an enemy, as Merope did; take a sharpened spear for a practice one; think a real stone was pumice; strike to save and kill instead; or strike thinking to give a demonstration, as in sham fighting.

--- p23 ---
SRC: Now since ignorance is possible in respect to all these details in which the action consists, he that acted in ignorance of any of them is thought to have acted involuntarily, and he most so who was in ignorance as regards the most important, which are thought to be those in which the action consists, and the result.
CAND: Since ignorance is possible with respect to all these particulars that make up the action, one who acted in ignorance of any of them is thought to have acted involuntarily — most of all if he was ignorant of the most important, which are thought to be those in which the action consists, and the result.

--- p24 ---
SRC: Further, not only must the ignorance be of this kind, to constitute an action involuntary, but it must be also understood that the action is followed by pain and regret.
CAND: Further, to make an action involuntary, the ignorance must be of this kind; and it must also be understood that the action is followed by pain and regret.

--- p25 ---
SRC: Now since all involuntary action is either upon compulsion or by reason of ignorance, Voluntary Action would seem to be “that whose origination is in the agent, he being aware of the particular details in which the action consists.”
CAND: Since every involuntary action is either on compulsion or through ignorance, voluntary action would seem to be that whose origin is in the agent, the agent being aware of the particular circumstances in which the action consists.

--- p26 ---
SRC: For, it may be, men are not justified by calling those actions involuntary, which are done by reason of Anger or Lust.
CAND: It may not be right to call actions involuntary when they are done from anger or appetite.

--- p27 ---
SRC: Because, in the first place, if this be so no other animal but man, and not even children, can be said to act voluntarily. Next, is it meant that we never act voluntarily when we act from Lust or Anger, or that we act voluntarily in doing what is right and involuntarily in doing what is discreditable? The latter supposition is absurd, since the cause is one and the same. Then as to the former, it is a strange thing to maintain actions to be involuntary which we are bound to grasp at: now there are occasions on which anger is a duty, and there are things which we are bound to lust after, health, for instance, and learning.
CAND: In the first place, if it were so, no other animal besides man could act voluntarily — nor even children. Next, are we to say we never act voluntarily when acting from appetite or anger? Or that we act voluntarily in doing what is right and involuntarily in doing what is disgraceful? The latter is absurd, since the cause is one and the same. As for the former, it is strange to call involuntary actions we are bound to reach for; there are occasions when anger is a duty, and things we are bound to desire (health, for instance, and learning).

--- p28 ---
SRC: Again, whereas actions strictly involuntary are thought to be attended with pain, those which are done to gratify lust are thought to be pleasant.
CAND: Again, strictly involuntary actions are thought to be attended with pain, but actions done to gratify appetite are thought to be pleasant.

--- p29 ---
SRC: Again: how does the involuntariness make any difference between wrong actions done from deliberate calculation, and those done by reason of anger? for both ought to be avoided, and the irrational feelings are thought to be just as natural to man as reason, and so of course must be such actions of the individual as are done from Anger and Lust. It is absurd then to class these actions among the involuntary.
CAND: Further: what difference is there, in point of involuntariness, between wrong acts done from deliberate calculation and those done from anger? Both should be avoided. And the irrational feelings are thought no less natural to man than reason; so actions done from anger and appetite must equally belong to the individual. It is absurd, then, to class these actions among the involuntary.

--- p30 ---
SRC: Having thus drawn out the distinction between voluntary and involuntary action our next step is to examine into the nature of Moral Choice, because this seems most intimately connected with Virtue and to be a more decisive test of moral character than a man’s acts are.
CAND: Having drawn the distinction between voluntary and involuntary action, our next step is to examine the nature of moral choice. This seems more intimately connected with virtue, and a better test of character, than actions are.

--- p31 ---
SRC: Now Moral Choice is plainly voluntary, but the two are not co-extensive, voluntary being the more comprehensive term; for first, children and all other animals share in voluntary action but not in Moral Choice; and next, sudden actions we call voluntary but do not ascribe them to Moral Choice.
CAND: Moral choice is clearly voluntary, but the two terms are not co-extensive; voluntary is the broader term. Children and other animals share in voluntary action but not in moral choice; and sudden actions we call voluntary but do not ascribe to moral choice.

--- p32 ---
SRC: Nor do they appear to be right who say it is lust or anger, or wish, or opinion of a certain kind; because, in the first place, Moral Choice is not shared by the irrational animals while Lust and Anger are. Next; the man who fails of self-control acts from Lust but not from Moral Choice; the man of self-control, on the contrary, from Moral Choice, not from Lust. Again: whereas Lust is frequently opposed to Moral Choice, Lust is not to Lust.
CAND: Nor is moral choice the same as appetite, anger, wish, or any sort of opinion, as some have said. For in the first place, irrational animals have appetite and anger but not moral choice. Next, the man who fails in self-control acts from appetite, not moral choice; the self-controlled man from moral choice, not appetite. Again, appetite is often opposed to moral choice, but not to appetite itself.

--- p33 ---
SRC: Lastly: the object-matter of Lust is the pleasant and the painful, but of Moral Choice neither the one nor the other. Still less can it be Anger, because actions done from Anger are thought generally to be least of all consequent on Moral Choice.
CAND: Lastly, the object of appetite is the pleasant and the painful, but of moral choice neither. Still less can moral choice be anger, since actions done from anger are generally thought to be the least results of deliberate choice.

--- p34 ---
SRC: Nor is it Wish either, though appearing closely connected with it; because, in the first place, Moral Choice has not for its objects impossibilities, and if a man were to say he chose them he would be thought to be a fool; but Wish may have impossible things for its objects, immortality for instance.
CAND: Nor is it wish, though wish seems closely related. For moral choice cannot have impossibilities as its objects; if a man said he chose them, he would be thought a fool. But wish may have impossibilities as objects — immortality, for instance.

--- p35 ---
SRC: Wish again may be exercised on things in the accomplishment of which one’s self could have nothing to do, as the success of any particular actor or athlete; but no man chooses things of this nature, only such as he believes he may himself be instrumental in procuring.
CAND: Wish may also be directed at things one could do nothing to achieve oneself, such as the victory of a particular actor or athlete. But no one chooses such things, only those he believes he can himself help bring about.

--- p36 ---
SRC: Further: Wish has for its object the End rather, but Moral Choice the means to the End; for instance, we wish to be healthy but we choose the means which will make us so; or happiness again we wish for, and commonly say so, but to say we choose is not an appropriate term, because, in short, the province of Moral Choice seems to be those things which are in our own power.
CAND: Further, wish has the end rather as its object, while moral choice has the means to the end. We wish to be healthy, but we choose the means that will make us so. We wish to be happy — we commonly say so — but to say we choose happiness is not quite right; for moral choice seems to be concerned with what is in our own power.

--- p37 ---
SRC: Neither can it be Opinion; for Opinion is thought to be unlimited in its range of objects, and to be exercised as well upon things eternal and impossible as on those which are in our own power: again, Opinion is logically divided into true and false, not into good and bad as Moral Choice is.
CAND: Nor can it be opinion. Opinion is thought to range without limit, over eternal and impossible things as well as over what is in our own power. Opinion is divided logically into true and false, not into good and bad like moral choice.

--- p38 ---
SRC: However, nobody perhaps maintains its identity with Opinion simply; but it is not the same with opinion of any kind, because by choosing good and bad things we are constituted of a certain character, but by having opinions on them we are not.
CAND: No one perhaps maintains that moral choice is identical with opinion as such; but it is not even identical with opinion of any particular kind. For by choosing good or bad things we come to be of a certain character, but by having opinions about them we do not.

--- p39 ---
SRC: Again, we choose to take or avoid, and so on, but we opine what a thing is, or for what it is serviceable, or how; but we do not opine to take or avoid.
CAND: Again, we choose to take or avoid and the like; but we opine what a thing is, or what it is good for, or how — we do not opine to take or avoid.

--- p40 ---
SRC: Further, Moral Choice is commended rather for having a right object than for being judicious, but Opinion for being formed in accordance with truth.
CAND: Further, moral choice is praised more for having a right object than for being judicious, while opinion is praised for truth.

--- p41 ---
SRC: Again, we choose such things as we pretty well know to be good, but we form opinions respecting such as we do not know at all.
CAND: We choose things we know to be good, but we form opinions about what we do not know at all.

--- p42 ---
SRC: And it is not thought that choosing and opining best always go together, but that some opine the better course and yet by reason of viciousness choose not the things which they should.
CAND: Choosing and opining well do not always go together. Some opine the better course, but through vice choose not what they should.

--- p43 ---
SRC: It may be urged, that Opinion always precedes or accompanies Moral Choice; be it so, this makes no difference, for this is not the point in question, but whether Moral Choice is the same as Opinion of a certain kind.
CAND: It may be urged that opinion always precedes or accompanies moral choice. Be that as it may; this is not the question. The question is whether moral choice is the same as any kind of opinion.

--- p44 ---
SRC: Since then it is none of the aforementioned things, what is it, or how is it characterised? Voluntary it plainly is, but not all voluntary action is an object of Moral Choice. May we not say then, it is “that voluntary which has passed through a stage of previous deliberation?” because Moral Choice is attended with reasoning and intellectual process. The etymology of its Greek name seems to give a hint of it, being when analysed “chosen in preference to somewhat else.”
CAND: Since, then, it is none of these, what is it, or how is it characterized? It is plainly voluntary, but not everything voluntary is an object of moral choice. Can we not say it is the voluntary that has passed through previous deliberation? For moral choice is attended by reasoning and intellectual process. The etymology of the Greek word for it gives a hint: literally, "chosen in preference to something else."

--- p45 ---
SRC: Well then; do men deliberate about everything, and is anything soever the object of Deliberation, or are there some matters with respect to which there is none? (It may be as well perhaps to say, that by “object of Deliberation” is meant such matter as a sensible man would deliberate upon, not what any fool or madman might.)
CAND: Now, do men deliberate about everything, and is anything whatever an object of deliberation? Or are there matters about which there is none? (It may be better to say that by "object of deliberation" we mean what a sensible man would deliberate about, not what any fool or madman might.)

--- p46 ---
SRC: Well: about eternal things no one deliberates; as, for instance, the universe, or the incommensurability of the diameter and side of a square.
CAND: No one deliberates about eternal things — the universe, for example, or the incommensurability of the diagonal and side of a square.

--- p47 ---
SRC: Nor again about things which are in motion but which always happen in the same way either necessarily, or naturally, or from some other cause, as the solstices or the sunrise.
CAND: Nor about things in motion that always happen in the same way, whether by necessity, by nature, or from some other cause — the solstices or sunrises, for instance.

--- p48 ---
SRC: Nor about those which are variable, as drought and rains; nor fortuitous matters, as finding of treasure.
CAND: Nor about things that vary, such as droughts and rains; nor about matters of chance, such as finding buried treasure.

--- p49 ---
SRC: Nor in fact even about all human affairs; no Lacedæmonian, for instance, deliberates as to the best course for the Scythian government to adopt; because in such cases we have no power over the result.
CAND: Nor about all human affairs. No Spartan, for instance, deliberates about the best course for the Scythian government to adopt; we have no power over such results.

--- p50 ---
SRC: But we do deliberate respecting such practical matters as are in our own power (which are what are left after all our exclusions).
CAND: We deliberate about practical matters that are in our own power — what is left after all these exclusions.

--- p51 ---
SRC: I have adopted this division because causes seem to be divisible into nature, necessity, chance, and moreover intellect, and all human powers.
CAND: I make this division because causes seem to be of four kinds: nature, necessity, chance, and also intellect and all human powers.

--- p52 ---
SRC: And as man in general deliberates about what man in general can effect, so individuals do about such practical things as can be effected through their own instrumentality.
CAND: As humanity in general deliberates about what humanity in general can effect, so individuals deliberate about practical matters they can effect themselves.

--- p53 ---
SRC: Again, we do not deliberate respecting such arts or sciences as are exact and independent: as, for instance, about written characters, because we have no doubt how they should be formed; but we do deliberate on all buch things as are usually done through our own instrumentality, but not invariably in the same way; as, for instance, about matters connected with the healing art, or with money-making; and, again, more about piloting ships than gymnastic exercises, because the former has been less exactly determined, and so forth; and more about arts than sciences, because we more frequently doubt respecting the former.
CAND: We do not deliberate about arts or sciences that are exact and self-contained — for example, about written characters, since we have no doubt how they should be formed. But we do deliberate about things usually done through our own agency that do not invariably turn out in the same way: matters of medicine, say, or money-making. We deliberate more about navigation than about gymnastics, because it is less exactly determined; and more about arts than sciences, because we have more doubt about the former.

--- p54 ---
SRC: So then Deliberation takes place in such matters as are under general laws, but still uncertain how in any given case they will issue, _i.e._ in which there is some indefiniteness; and for great matters we associate coadjutors in counsel, distrusting our ability to settle them alone.
CAND: Deliberation takes place, then, in matters that fall under general rules yet are uncertain how they will turn out in a given case — that is, in matters with some indefiniteness. For important matters we call in associates in counsel, distrusting our ability to settle them alone.

--- p55 ---
SRC: Further, we deliberate not about Ends, but Means to Ends. No physician, for instance, deliberates whether he will cure, nor orator whether he will persuade, nor statesman whether he will produce a good constitution, nor in fact any man in any other function about his particular End; but having set before them a certain End they look how and through what means it may be accomplished: if there is a choice of means, they examine further which are easiest and most creditable; or, if there is but one means of accomplishing the object, then how it may be through this, this again through what, till they come to the first cause; and this will be the last found; for a man engaged in a process of deliberation seems to seek and analyse, as a man, to solve a problem, analyses the figure given him. And plainly not every search is Deliberation, those in mathematics to wit, but every Deliberation is a search, and the last step in the analysis is the first in the constructive process. And if in the course of their search men come upon an impossibility, they give it up; if money, for instance, be necessary, but cannot be got: but if the thing appears possible they then attempt to do it.
CAND: Further, we deliberate not about ends, but about means to ends. No physician deliberates about whether to cure; no orator about whether to persuade; no statesman about whether to produce a good constitution; and no one in any function about his particular end. With a certain end set before them, they consider how and by what means it may be achieved. If there are several possible means, they go on to examine which is easiest and most creditable. If there is only one, they ask how it may be achieved through this one, and then by what means, until they arrive at the first cause — which will be the last found. A man deliberating seems to search and analyze, as if solving a geometrical problem. Not every search is deliberation (those in mathematics are not), but every deliberation is a search. The last step in the analysis is the first in the process of execution. If in the course of searching men come upon an impossibility, they give up; if money is needed but cannot be got, for instance. But if the thing appears possible, they attempt it.

--- p56 ---
SRC: And by possible I mean what may be done through our own instrumentality (of course what may be done through our friends is through our own instrumentality in a certain sense, because the origination in such cases rests with us). And the object of search is sometimes the necessary instruments, sometimes the method of using them; and similarly in the rest sometimes through what, and sometimes how or through what.
CAND: By possible I mean what can be done through our own agency. What can be done through our friends is through our own agency in a certain sense, because the origin of action in such cases rests with us. The object of search is sometimes the necessary instruments, sometimes the method of using them; and in other cases sometimes the means, sometimes the procedure.

--- p57 ---
SRC: So it seems, as has been said, that Man is the originator of his actions; and Deliberation has for its object whatever may be done through one’s own instrumentality, and the actions are with a view to other things; and so it is, not the End, but the Means to Ends on which Deliberation is employed.
CAND: It seems, as has been said, that man is the originator of his actions; deliberation is concerned with whatever can be done through one's own agency, and the actions are for the sake of other things. So deliberation is not about the end but about the means to ends.

--- p58 ---
SRC: Nor, again, is it employed on matters of detail, as whether the substance before me is bread, or has been properly cooked; for these come under the province of sense, and if a man is to be always deliberating, he may go on _ad infinitum_.
CAND: Nor is deliberation about matters of detail — whether this substance is bread, say, or whether it has been properly cooked; such things fall under sense perception. If a man is always to be deliberating, the process may go on endlessly.

--- p59 ---
SRC: Further, exactly the same matter is the object both of Deliberation and Moral Choice; but that which is the object of Moral Choice is thenceforward separated off and definite, because by object of Moral Choice is denoted that which after Deliberation has been preferred to something else: for each man leaves off searching how he shall do a thing when he has brought the origination up to himself, _i.e_. to the governing principle in himself, because it is this which makes the choice. A good illustration of this is furnished by the old regal constitutions which Homer drew from, in which the Kings would announce to the commonalty what they had determined before.
CAND: The object of deliberation and of moral choice is exactly the same matter; but the object of moral choice is, from that moment on, separated off and definite. For the object of moral choice is what has been preferred to something else after deliberation. A man stops searching how he shall do something when he has traced the origin of action back to himself — to the governing principle in himself; for this is what makes the choice. A good illustration is found in the old kingly constitutions Homer drew on, in which kings would announce to the people what they had already decided.

--- p60 ---
SRC: Now since that which is the object of Moral Choice is something in our own power, which is the object of deliberation and the grasping of the Will, Moral Choice must be “a grasping after something in our own power consequent upon Deliberation:” because after having deliberated we decide, and then grasp by our Will in accordance with the result of our deliberation.
CAND: Since the object of moral choice is something in our power, which is the object of deliberation and of reaching by the will, moral choice must be a reaching after something in our power, consequent on deliberation. After deliberating we decide, and then reach by our will in accordance with the result of deliberation.

--- p61 ---
SRC: Let this be accepted as a sketch of the nature and object of Moral Choice, that object being “Means to Ends.”
CAND: Let this stand as a sketch of moral choice — its nature and the object it has, namely "means to ends."

--- p62 ---
SRC: That Wish has for its object-matter the End, has been already stated; but there are two opinions respecting it; some thinking that its object is real good, others whatever impresses the mind with a notion of good.
CAND: That wish has the end as its object has already been said. There are two views on this: some think its object is the real good, others whatever appears good to the mind.

--- p63 ---
SRC: Now those who maintain that the object of Wish is real good are beset by this difficulty, that what is wished for by him who chooses wrongly is not really an object of Wish (because, on their theory, if it is an object of wish, it must be good, but it is, in the case supposed, evil). Those who maintain, on the contrary, that that which impresses the mind with a notion of good is properly the object of Wish, have to meet this difficulty, that there is nothing naturally an object of Wish but to each individual whatever seems good to him; now different people have different notions, and it may chance contrary ones.
CAND: Those who hold that the object of wish is the real good face the difficulty that what is wished for by a man who chooses wrongly is not really an object of wish: on their theory, if it is wished, it must be good, but in the case supposed it is evil. Those who hold that whatever appears good to the mind is the proper object of wish face the objection that there is no object of wish by nature; to each individual, what seems good to him. But different people have different notions, possibly opposite ones.

--- p64 ---
SRC: But, if these opinions do not satisfy us, may we not say that, abstractedly and as a matter of objective truth, the really good is the object of Wish, but to each individual whatever impresses his mind with the notion of good. And so to the good man that is an object of Wish which is really and truly so, but to the bad man anything may be; just as physically those things are wholesome to the healthy which are really so, but other things to the sick. And so too of bitter and sweet, and hot and heavy, and so on. For the good man judges in every instance correctly, and in every instance the notion conveyed to his mind is the true one.
CAND: If these views do not satisfy us, may we not say that, absolutely and as a matter of objective truth, the really good is the object of wish; but to each individual, whatever appears good. To the good man, what is truly good is the object of wish; to the bad man, anything at all may be. Just as in the body, what is wholesome to the healthy really is so, while other things are wholesome to the sick; and so with the bitter, sweet, hot, heavy, and the rest. The good man judges rightly in every case; in every case the impression conveyed to his mind is the true one.

--- p65 ---
SRC: For there are fair and pleasant things peculiar to, and so varying with, each state; and perhaps the most distinguishing characteristic of the good man is his seeing the truth in every instance, he being, in fact, the rule and measure of these matters.
CAND: For each state of character has its own proper noble and pleasant things, and these vary with the state. Perhaps the most distinguishing characteristic of the good man is that he sees the truth in every case; he is, as it were, the rule and measure in these matters.

--- p66 ---
SRC: The multitude of men seem to be deceived by reason of pleasure, because though it is not really a good it impresses their minds with the notion of goodness, so they choose what is pleasant as good and avoid pain as an evil.
CAND: Most people seem to be deceived because of pleasure. Though pleasure is not truly a good, it appears so to them; so they choose what is pleasant as good and avoid pain as evil.

--- p67 ---
SRC: Now since the End is the object of Wish, and the means to the End of Deliberation and Moral Choice, the actions regarding these matters must be in the way of Moral Choice, _i.e._ voluntary: but the acts of working out the virtues are such actions, and therefore Virtue is in our power.
CAND: Since the end is the object of wish, and the means to the end the object of deliberation and moral choice, actions concerning such means must be objects of moral choice, and so voluntary. The activities by which the virtues are built up are such actions. Therefore virtue is in our power.

--- p68 ---
SRC: And so too is Vice: because wherever it is in our power to do it is also in our power to forbear doing, and _vice versâ_: therefore if the doing (being in a given case creditable) is in our power, so too is the forbearing (which is in the same case discreditable), and _vice versâ_.
CAND: So is vice. Wherever it is in our power to act, it is also in our power to refrain, and vice versa. So if acting (when noble) is in our power, so is refraining (which is in that case base); and conversely.

--- p69 ---
SRC: But if it is in our power to do and to forbear doing what is creditable or the contrary, and these respectively constitute the being good or bad, then the being good or vicious characters is in our power.
CAND: If it is in our power to do and to refrain from what is noble or base, and if doing or refraining in these cases is what constitutes being good or bad, then being good or vicious is in our power.

--- p70 ---
SRC: As for the well-known saying, “No man voluntarily is wicked or involuntarily happy,” it is partly true, partly false; for no man is happy against his will, of course, but wickedness is voluntary. Or must we dispute the statements lately made, and not say that Man is the originator or generator of his actions as much as of his children?
CAND: The familiar saying, "no man is voluntarily wicked nor involuntarily happy," is partly true and partly false. No one is happy against his will, of course, but wickedness is voluntary. Or must we dispute what has just been said and deny that a man is the originator and begetter of his actions as he is of his children?

--- p71 ---
SRC: But if this is matter of plain manifest fact, and we cannot refer our actions to any other originations beside those in our own power, those things must be in our own power, and so voluntary, the originations of which are in ourselves.
CAND: If the facts are as described and we cannot trace our actions back to any origins other than those in our own power, then whatever has its origin in us is in our power, and is voluntary.

--- p72 ---
SRC: Moreover, testimony seems to be borne to these positions both privately by individuals, and by law-givers too, in that they chastise and punish those who do wrong (unless they do so on compulsion, or by reason of ignorance which is not self-caused), while they honour those who act rightly, under the notion of being likely to encourage the latter and restrain the former. But such things as are not in our own power, _i.e._ not voluntary, no one thinks of encouraging us to do, knowing it to be of no avail for one to have been persuaded not to be hot (for instance), or feel pain, or be hungry, and so forth, because we shall have those sensations all the same.
CAND: Testimony supports these positions, both from private individuals and from lawgivers. Lawgivers punish those who do wrong (unless they act on compulsion, or from an ignorance not self-caused), and honor those who act rightly, with a view to encouraging the latter and restraining the former. But what is not in our own power — not voluntary — no one thinks of encouraging. No one bothers to persuade us not to be hot, or to feel pain, or to be hungry, since we shall feel these things all the same.

--- p73 ---
SRC: And what makes the case stronger is this: that they chastise for the very fact of ignorance, when it is thought to be self-caused; to the drunken, for instance, penalties are double, because the origination in such case lies in a man’s own self: for he might have helped getting drunk, and this is the cause of his ignorance.
CAND: What makes the case stronger is that lawgivers punish for the very fact of ignorance, when it is thought to be self-caused. For the drunken, penalties are doubled, because the origin lies in the man himself: he might have helped getting drunk, and this is the cause of his ignorance.

--- p74 ---
SRC: Again, those also who are ignorant of legal regulations which they are bound to know, and which are not hard to know, they chastise; and similarly in all other cases where neglect is thought to be the cause of the ignorance, under the notion that it was in their power to prevent their ignorance, because they might have paid attention.
CAND: Again, those ignorant of legal regulations they are bound to know, and which are not hard to know, are punished. So too with other cases where neglect is thought to be the cause of ignorance, on the ground that it was in the agent's power to prevent his ignorance by paying attention.

--- p75 ---
SRC: But perhaps a man is of such a character that he cannot attend to such things: still men are themselves the causes of having become such characters by living carelessly, and also of being unjust or destitute of self-control, the former by doing evil actions, the latter by spending their time in drinking and such-like; because the particular acts of working form corresponding characters, as is shown by those who are practising for any contest or particular course of action, for such men persevere in the acts of working.
CAND: Perhaps it will be said that a man is of such a character that he cannot attend to such things. But men are themselves the causes of becoming such characters, by living carelessly; and of being unjust or self-indulgent, by doing evil actions or spending their time in drinking and the like. In each case the particular activities form the corresponding character. This is clear from those who train for a contest or any course of action: they persevere in the activities.

--- p76 ---
SRC: As for the plea, that a man did not know that habits are produced from separate acts of working, we reply, such ignorance is a mark of excessive stupidity.
CAND: As for the plea that a man did not know that habits are produced from particular actions — such ignorance is a mark of excessive stupidity.

--- p77 ---
SRC: Furthermore, it is wholly irrelevant to say that the man who acts unjustly or dissolutely does not _wish_ to attain the habits of these vices: for if a man wittingly does those things whereby he must become unjust he is to all intents and purposes unjust voluntarily; but he cannot with a wish cease to be unjust and become just. For, to take the analogous case, the sick man cannot with a wish be well again, yet in a supposable case he is voluntarily ill because he has produced his sickness by living intemperately and disregarding his physicians. There was a time then when he might have helped being ill, but now he has let himself go he cannot any longer; just as he who has let a stone out of his hand cannot recall it, and yet it rested with him to aim and throw it, because the origination was in his power. Just so the unjust man, and he who has lost all self-control, might originally have helped being what they are, and so they are voluntarily what they are; but now that they are become so they no longer have the power of being otherwise.
CAND: And it is wholly beside the point to say that a man who acts unjustly or licentiously does not wish to form the habits of these vices. For if a man wittingly does the things by which he must become unjust, he is unjust voluntarily; he cannot by a wish cease to be unjust and become just. The analogous case: the sick man cannot by a wish be well again, yet in a supposable case he is voluntarily ill, because he produced his sickness by intemperate living and disregarding his doctor. There was a time when he could have helped being ill; but now, having let himself go, he cannot. Just as he who has let a stone out of his hand cannot recall it, yet it rested with him to aim and throw it — the origin was in his power. So the unjust man, or the man who has lost all self-control, might originally have helped becoming what he is; he is voluntarily what he is. But now that he is such, he cannot be otherwise.

--- p78 ---
SRC: And not only are mental diseases voluntary, but the bodily are so in some men, whom we accordingly blame: for such as are naturally deformed no one blames, only such as are so by reason of want of exercise, and neglect: and so too of weakness and maiming: no one would think of upbraiding, but would rather compassionate, a man who is blind by nature, or from disease, or from an accident; but every one would blame him who was so from excess of wine, or any other kind of intemperance. It seems, then, that in respect of bodily diseases, those which depend on ourselves are censured, those which do not are not censured; and if so, then in the case of the mental disorders, those which are censured must depend upon ourselves.
CAND: Not only mental but bodily diseases can be voluntary in some men, whom we accordingly blame. No one blames those who are deformed by nature, only those who are so from lack of exercise and neglect; and likewise for weakness and maiming. No one would reproach a man who is blind from nature, disease, or accident — rather, he would be pitied. But anyone would blame him who was so from excess of wine or any other intemperance. With bodily ailments, then, those that depend on ourselves are censured, those that do not are not. If so, then in the case of mental disorders, the ones that are censured must depend on ourselves.

--- p79 ---
SRC: But suppose a man to say, “that (by our own admission) all men aim at that which conveys to their minds an impression of good, and that men have no control over this impression, but that the End impresses each with a notion correspondent to his own individual character; that to be sure if each man is in a way the cause of his own moral state, so he will be also of the kind of impression he receives: whereas, if this is not so, no one is the cause to himself of doing evil actions, but he does them by reason of ignorance of the true End, supposing that through their means he will secure the chief good. Further, that this aiming at the End is no matter of one’s own choice, but one must be born with a power of mental vision, so to speak, whereby to judge fairly and choose that which is really good; and he is blessed by nature who has this naturally well: because it is the most important thing and the fairest, and what a man cannot get or learn from another but will have such as nature has given it; and for this to be so given well and fairly would be excellence of nature in the highest and truest sense.”
CAND: But suppose someone says: by our own admission, all men aim at what appears good to their minds; they have no control over this appearance; the end presents itself to each according to his individual character. If each is in some way cause of his own moral state, then he will also be cause of the impression he receives; but if not, no one is cause to himself of doing evil actions — he does them through ignorance of the true end, thinking he will secure the chief good through them. This aiming at the end, the objector says, is not a matter of choice; one must be born with a power of mental vision, so to speak, to judge well and choose what is truly good. He is blessed by nature who has this faculty well developed, since it is the most important and most noble thing, and one he cannot get or learn from another but has as nature has given it. For it to be so given well and finely would be excellence of nature in the highest and truest sense.

--- p80 ---
SRC: If all this be true, how will Virtue be a whit more voluntary than Vice? Alike to the good man and the bad, the End gives its impression and is fixed by nature or howsoever you like to say, and they act so and so, referring everything else to this End.
CAND: If all this is true, how will virtue be any more voluntary than vice? To both the good man and the bad, the end gives its impression and is fixed by nature (or however you wish to say). They act in light of it, referring everything else to that end.

--- p81 ---
SRC: Whether then we suppose that the End impresses each man’s mind with certain notions not merely by nature, but that there is somewhat also dependent on himself; or that the End is given by nature, and yet Virtue is voluntary because the good man does all the rest voluntarily, Vice must be equally so; because his own agency equally attaches to the bad man in the actions, even if not in the selection of the End.
CAND: Whether we suppose that the end presents itself to each man's mind not only by nature, but with some contribution from the man himself; or that the end is given by nature while virtue is voluntary because the good man does all the rest voluntarily — vice too must be equally voluntary. The bad man's own agency equally attaches to him in the actions, even if not in the selection of the end.

--- p82 ---
SRC: If then, as is commonly said, the Virtues are voluntary (because we at least cooperate in producing our moral states, and we assume the End to be of a certain kind according as we are ourselves of certain characters), the Vices must be voluntary also, because the cases are exactly similar.
CAND: If then, as is commonly said, the virtues are voluntary (since we cooperate in producing our moral states, and we assume the end to be of a certain kind according as we are of certain characters), the vices must be voluntary too, since the cases are exactly parallel.

--- p83 ---
SRC: Well now, we have stated generally respecting the Moral Virtues, the genus (in outline), that they are mean states, and that they are habits, and how they are formed, and that they are of themselves calculated to act upon the circumstances out of which they were formed, and that they are in our own power and voluntary, and are to be done so as right Reason may direct.
CAND: We have now stated in a general way about the moral virtues: their genus in outline, that they are mean states; that they are habits; how they are produced; that they act by their nature on the very circumstances out of which they were produced; that they are in our own power and voluntary; and that they are to be exercised as right reason directs.

--- p84 ---
SRC: But the particular actions and the habits are not voluntary in the same sense; for of the actions we are masters from beginning to end (supposing of course a knowledge of the particular details), but only of the origination of the habits, the addition by small particular accessions not being cognisiable (as is the case with sicknesses): still they are voluntary because it rested with us to use our circumstances this way or that.
CAND: But the particular actions and the habits are not voluntary in the same sense. Of the actions we are masters from beginning to end, supposing knowledge of the particulars; but of the habits we are masters only at the starting point. The gradual additions are imperceptible — as with diseases — yet the habits are still voluntary, because it rested with us to use our circumstances one way rather than another.

--- p85 ---
SRC: Here we will resume the particular discussion of the Moral Virtues, and say what they are, what is their object-matter, and how they stand respectively related to it: of course their number will be thereby shown.
CAND: Let us now resume the particular discussion of the moral virtues: what each is, what its object-matter is, and how it is related to that object-matter. The number of them will become clear in the process.

--- p86 ---
SRC: First, then, of Courage. Now that it is a mean state, in respect of fear and boldness, has been already said: further, the objects of our fears are obviously things fearful or, in a general way of statement, evils; which accounts for the common definition of fear, viz. “expectation of evil.”
CAND: First, then, courage. That it is a mean state with respect to fear and confidence has been said already. The objects of our fears are plainly fearful things, or in general evils; hence the common definition of fear as "expectation of evil."

--- p87 ---
SRC: Of course we fear evils of all kinds: disgrace, for instance, poverty, disease, desolateness, death; but not all these seem to be the object-matter of the Brave man, because there are things which to fear is right and noble, and not to fear is base; disgrace, for example, since he who fears this is a good man and has a sense of honour, and he who does not fear it is shameless (though there are those who call him Brave by analogy, because he somewhat resembles the Brave man who agrees with him in being free from fear); but poverty, perhaps, or disease, and in fact whatever does not proceed from viciousness, nor is attributable to his own fault, a man ought not to fear: still, being fearless in respect of these would not constitute a man Brave in the proper sense of the term.
CAND: We fear evils of all kinds: disgrace, poverty, disease, friendlessness, death. But not all of these are the brave man's object-matter, since there are things it is right and noble to fear, and not to fear them is base — disgrace, for example. The man who fears disgrace is good and has a sense of honor; the one who does not is shameless (though some call him brave by analogy, because he resembles the brave man in being free from fear). But poverty, or disease, or anything not proceeding from vice nor attributable to one's own fault, one ought not to fear. Still, being fearless in respect of these would not make a man brave in the proper sense.

--- p88 ---
SRC: Yet we do apply the term in right of the similarity of the cases; for there are men who, though timid in the dangers of war, are liberal men and are stout enough to face loss of wealth.
CAND: Yet we do apply the term "brave" because of the similarity of cases. Some men, though timid in the dangers of war, are liberal and strong enough to face the loss of wealth.

--- p89 ---
SRC: And, again, a man is not a coward for fearing insult to his wife or children, or envy, or any such thing; nor is he a Brave man for being bold when going to be scourged.
CAND: A man is not a coward for fearing insult to his wife and children, or envy, or anything of that kind; nor is he a brave man for being bold when about to be flogged.

--- p90 ---
SRC: What kind of fearful things then do constitute the object-matter of the Brave man? first of all, must they not be the greatest, since no man is more apt to withstand what is dreadful. Now the object of the greatest dread is death, because it is the end of all things, and the dead man is thought to be capable neither of good nor evil. Still it would seem that the Brave man has not for his object-matter even death in every circumstance; on the sea, for example, or in sickness: in what circumstances then? must it not be in the most honourable? now such is death in war, because it is death in the greatest and most honourable danger; and this is confirmed by the honours awarded in communities, and by monarchs.
CAND: What kinds of fearful things, then, make up the brave man's object-matter? First of all, the greatest, since no man is more resolute in the face of what is dreadful. The greatest object of dread is death, since it is the end of all things, and the dead are thought no longer capable of good or evil. Even so, the brave man's object-matter is not death in every circumstance — on the sea, for instance, or in illness. In what circumstances, then? Surely in the most noble. And such is death in war, because it is death amid the greatest and most noble danger. This is confirmed by the honors awarded in communities, and by monarchs.

--- p91 ---
SRC: He then may be most properly denominated Brave who is fearless in respect of honourable death and such sudden emergencies as threaten death; now such specially are those which arise in the course of war.
CAND: A man may most properly be called brave who is fearless in the face of a noble death, and of those sudden emergencies that threaten death — especially those arising in war.

--- p92 ---
SRC: It is not meant but that the Brave man will be fearless also on the sea (and in sickness), but not in the same way as sea-faring men; for these are light-hearted and hopeful by reason of their experience, while landsmen though Brave are apt to give themselves up for lost and shudder at the notion of such a death: to which it should be added that Courage is exerted in circumstances which admit of doing something to help one’s self, or in which death would be honourable; now neither of these requisites attach to destruction by drowning or sickness.
CAND: Not that the brave man is not fearless on the sea (and in sickness) too, but not in the same way as sailors. They are hopeful from experience, while landsmen, though brave, give themselves up for lost and shudder at such a death. Courage is exercised in circumstances that admit of doing something to help oneself, or where death would be noble; neither of these conditions holds in death by drowning or by disease.

--- p93 ---
SRC: Again, fearful is a term of relation, the same thing not being so to all, and there is according to common parlance somewhat so fearful as to be beyond human endurance: this of course would be fearful to every man of sense, but those objects which are level to the capacity of man differ in magnitude and admit of degrees, so too the objects of confidence or boldness.
CAND: Fearful is a relative term; the same thing is not fearful to everyone. Some things are said in common speech to be so fearful as to be beyond human endurance; these, of course, would be fearful to every sensible man. But things within human capacity differ in magnitude and admit of degrees; so do the objects of confidence or boldness.

--- p94 ---
SRC: Now the Brave man cannot be frighted from his propriety (but of course only so far as he is man); fear such things indeed he will, but he will stand up against them as he ought and as right reason may direct, with a view to what is honourable, because this is the end of the virtue.
CAND: The brave man is not to be frightened out of his composure — so far as he is a man. He will fear such things, yes, but stand up against them as he ought and as reason directs, with a view to what is noble, since this is the end of the virtue.

--- p95 ---
SRC: Now it is possible to fear these things too much, or too little, or again to fear what is not really fearful as if it were such. So the errors come to be either that a man fears when he ought not to fear at all, or that he fears in an improper way, or at a wrong time, and so forth; and so too in respect of things inspiring confidence. He is Brave then who withstands, and fears, and is bold, in respect of right objects, from a right motive, in right manner, and at right times: since the Brave man suffers or acts as he ought and as right reason may direct.
CAND: One may fear such things too much, or too little, or fear what is not really fearful as if it were. So the errors are: a man fears when he ought not to fear at all; or fears in the wrong way; or at the wrong time; and so on. And similarly with things inspiring confidence. The brave man, then, withstands, fears, and is bold with respect to the right objects, from a right motive, in the right manner, and at the right times. He feels or acts as he ought, and as right reason directs.

--- p96 ---
SRC: Now the end of every separate act of working is that which accords with the habit, and so to the Brave man Courage; which is honourable; therefore such is also the End, since the character of each is determined by the End.
CAND: The end of every activity is what accords with the corresponding habit. To the brave man, this is courage, which is noble. So this is also the end, since the character of each activity is determined by its end.

--- p97 ---
SRC: So honour is the motive from which the Brave man withstands things fearful and performs the acts which accord with Courage.
CAND: Thus honor is the motive from which the brave man withstands fearful things and performs acts of courage.

--- p98 ---
SRC: Of the characters on the side of Excess, he who exceeds in utter absence of fear has no appropriate name (I observed before that many states have none), but he would be a madman or inaccessible to pain if he feared nothing, neither earthquake, nor the billows, as they tell of the Celts.
CAND: Of the characters on the side of excess, the man who exceeds in utter fearlessness has no proper name (as I noted before, many states do not); he would be a madman or insensitive to pain if he feared nothing — neither earthquake, nor the sea, as the story is told of the Celts.

--- p99 ---
SRC: He again who exceeds in confidence in respect of things fearful is rash. He is thought moreover to be a braggart, and to advance unfounded claims to the character of Brave: the relation which the Brave man really bears to objects of fear this man wishes to appear to bear, and so imitates him in whatever points he can; for this reason most of them exhibit a curious mixture of rashness and cowardice; because, affecting rashness in these circumstances, they do not withstand what is truly fearful.
CAND: The man who exceeds in confidence in the face of fearful things is rash. He is also a braggart who falsely claims the character of brave. He wishes to appear toward fearful things as the brave man really is, and imitates him where he can. For this reason most of them display a curious mixture of rashness and cowardice: pretending rashness in these circumstances, they do not hold up against what is really fearful.

--- p100 ---
SRC: The man moreover who exceeds in feeling fear is a coward, since there attach to him the circumstances of fearing wrong objects, in wrong ways, and so forth. He is deficient also in feeling confidence, but he is most clearly seen as exceeding in the case of pains; he is a fainthearted kind of man, for he fears all things: the Brave man is just the contrary, for boldness is the property of the light-hearted and hopeful.
CAND: The man who exceeds in feeling fear is a coward: he fears the wrong objects, in the wrong ways, and so on. He is also deficient in confidence, but he is most clearly seen to exceed in respect of pains. He is a faint-hearted kind of man, fearing everything. The brave man is his opposite: boldness belongs to the lighthearted and hopeful.

--- p101 ---
SRC: So the coward, the rash, and the Brave man have exactly the same object-matter, but stand differently related to it: the two first-mentioned respectively exceed and are deficient, the last is in a mean state and as he ought to be. The rash again are precipitate, and, being eager before danger, when actually in it fall away, while the Brave are quick and sharp in action, but before are quiet and composed.
CAND: So the coward, the rash man, and the brave man have the same object-matter, but stand differently related to it. The first two exceed or fall short; the last is in a mean state and as he ought to be. The rash are also precipitate, eager before danger but falling away when it is upon them; while the brave are quick and sharp in action, but quiet and composed beforehand.

--- p102 ---
SRC: Well then, as has been said, Courage is a mean state in respect of objects inspiring boldness or fear, in the circumstances which have been stated, and the Brave man chooses his line and withstands danger either because to do so is honourable, or because not to do so is base. SRC: But the man of Perfected Self-Mastery is in the mean with respect to these objects: that is to say, he neither takes pleasure in the things which delight the vicious man, and in fact rather dislikes them, nor at all in improper objects; nor to any great degree in any object of the class; nor is he pained at their absence; nor does he desire them; or, if he does, only in moderation, and neither more than he ought, nor at improper times, and so forth; but such things as are conducive to health and good condition of body, being also pleasant, these he will grasp at in moderation and as he ought to do, and also such other pleasant things as do not hinder these objects, and are not unseemly or disproportionate to his means; because he that should grasp at such would be liking such pleasures more than is proper; but the man of Perfected Self-Mastery is not of this character, but regulates his desires by the dictates of right reason.
CAND: The self-masterer is in the mean with respect to these things. He takes no pleasure in what delights the vicious man — indeed rather dislikes it — and none in improper objects. He takes no great pleasure in any object of this class; he is not pained at their absence; he does not desire them, or desires them only in moderation, neither more than he ought nor at improper times, and so on. Such pleasant things as are conducive to health and to good bodily condition he will reach for in moderation and as he ought, along with other pleasant things that do not hinder these objects and are not unseemly or beyond his means. Reaching for things outside these limits would be liking such pleasures more than is proper; but the self-masterer is not of that kind. He regulates his desires by the dictates of right reason.

--- p148 ---
SRC: Now the vice of being destitute of all Self-Control seems to be more truly voluntary than Cowardice, because pleasure is the cause of the former and pain of the latter, and pleasure is an object of choice, pain of avoidance. And again, pain deranges and spoils the natural disposition of its victim, whereas pleasure has no such effect and is more voluntary and therefore more justly open to reproach.
CAND: Self-indulgence seems to be more truly voluntary than cowardice. Pleasure is the cause of the former, pain of the latter; pleasure is an object of choice, pain of avoidance. Pain deranges and spoils the disposition of its victim; pleasure has no such effect, and is more voluntary and therefore more justly open to reproach.

--- p149 ---
SRC: It is so also for the following reason; that it is easier to be inured by habit to resist the objects of pleasure, there being many things of this kind in life and the process of habituation being unaccompanied by danger; whereas the case is the reverse as regards the objects of fear.
CAND: It is also easier to be trained by habit to resist objects of pleasure. There are many such things in life, and the process of habituation is unaccompanied by danger; whereas the case is the reverse with objects of fear.

--- p150 ---
SRC: Again, Cowardice as a confirmed habit would seem to be voluntary in a different way from the particular instances which form the habit; because it is painless, but these derange the man by reason of pain so that he throws away his arms and otherwise behaves himself unseemly, for which reason they are even thought by some to exercise a power of compulsion.
CAND: Cowardice, as a settled habit, would seem to be voluntary in a different way from the particular instances that form it. It is itself painless; but its instances derange the man through pain, so that he throws away his arms and otherwise behaves disgracefully. For this reason some even think they exercise a compelling force.

--- p151 ---
SRC: But to the man destitute of Self-Control the particular instances are on the contrary quite voluntary, being done with desire and direct exertion of the will, but the general result is less voluntary: since no man desires to form the habit.
CAND: With self-indulgence, by contrast, the particular instances are themselves fully voluntary, done with desire and direct willing; but the general result — the habit — is less voluntary, since no one desires to form the habit.

--- p152 ---
SRC: The name of this vice (which signifies etymologically unchastened-ness) we apply also to the faults of children, there being a certain resemblance between the cases: to which the name is primarily applied, and to which secondarily or derivatively, is not relevant to the present subject, but it is evident that the later in point of time must get the name from the earlier. And the metaphor seems to be a very good one; for whatever grasps after base things, and is liable to great increase, ought to be chastened; and to this description desire and the child answer most truly, in that children also live under the direction of desire and the grasping after what is pleasant is most prominently seen in these.
CAND: The Greek name for this vice (meaning literally "unchastened-ness") we apply also to the faults of children, there being a resemblance. Which is the primary and which the derivative application is not relevant here, but the later application clearly takes its name from the earlier. The metaphor is good. What grasps after base things and is liable to great increase ought to be chastened; and to this description desire and the child answer most truly, since children too live at the direction of desire, and grasping after what is pleasant is most prominent in them.

--- p153 ---
SRC: Unless then the appetite be obedient and subjected to the governing principle it will become very great: for in the fool the grasping after what is pleasant is insatiable and undiscriminating; and every acting out of the desire increases the kindred habit, and if the desires are great and violent in degree they even expel Reason entirely; therefore they ought to be moderate and few, and in no respect to be opposed to Reason. Now when the appetite is in such a state we denominate it obedient and chastened.
CAND: Unless the appetite is obedient and subject to the governing principle, it will grow very great. In the fool, the grasping after what is pleasant is insatiable and indiscriminate. Every activation of desire increases the corresponding habit; and if desires are great and violent in degree, they even drive out reason entirely. So they ought to be moderate and few, and in no respect opposed to reason. When the appetite is in such a state, we call it obedient and chastened.

--- p154 ---
SRC: In short, as the child ought to live with constant regard to the orders of its educator, so should the appetitive principle with regard to those of Reason.
CAND: In short, as the child ought to live in constant regard for the instructions of his educator, so the appetitive part ought to live in constant regard for those of reason.

--- p155 ---
SRC: So then in the man of Perfected Self-Mastery, the appetitive principle must be accordant with Reason: for what is right is the mark at which both principles aim: that is to say, the man of perfected self-mastery desires what he ought in right manner and at right times, which is exactly what Reason directs. Let this be taken for our account of Perfected Self-Mastery.
CAND: So in the self-masterer the appetitive part must be in accord with reason, for what is right is the mark at which both aim. The self-masterer desires what he ought, in the right manner, and at the right times — which is exactly what reason directs. Let this be our account of self-mastery.
