=== BOOK 5: Book 5 — Justice ===

--- p0 ---
SRC: Now the points for our enquiry in respect of Justice and Injustice are, what kind of actions are their object-matter, and what kind of a mean state Justice is, and between what points the abstract principle of it, i.e. the Just, is a mean. And our enquiry shall be, if you please, conducted in the same method as we have observed in the foregoing parts of this Treatise.
CAND: The questions for our inquiry on justice and injustice are these: what kinds of actions are their object-matter, what kind of mean state justice is, and between what points the abstract principle — the just — is a mean. Let us conduct the inquiry by the method we have used in the earlier parts of this treatise.

--- p1 ---
SRC: We see then that all men mean by the term Justice a moral state such that in consequence of it men have the capacity of doing what is just, and actually do it, and wish it: similarly also with respect to Injustice, a moral state such that in consequence of it men do unjustly and wish what is unjust: let us also be content then with these as a ground-work sketched out.
CAND: By justice, all men mean a moral state by which men are capable of doing what is just, actually do it, and wish it. Similarly, by injustice a state by which men act unjustly and wish what is unjust. Let us take this as a rough starting sketch.

--- p2 ---
SRC: I mention the two, because the same does not hold with regard to States whether of mind or body as with regard to Sciences or Faculties: I mean that whereas it is thought that the same Faculty or Science embraces contraries, a State will not: from health, for instance, not the contrary acts are done but the healthy ones only; we say a man walks healthily when he walks as the healthy man would.
CAND: I mention wishing because states of mind and body are not like sciences or capacities. A capacity or science is thought to embrace contraries; a state does not. From health one does not do the contrary acts, only the healthy ones; we say a man walks healthily when he walks as the healthy man would.

--- p3 ---
SRC: However, of the two contrary states the one may be frequently known from the other, and oftentimes the states from their subject-matter: if it be seen clearly what a good state of body is, then is it also seen what a bad state is, and from the things which belong to a good state of body the good state itself is seen, and _vice versâ_. If, for instance, the good state is firmness of flesh it follows that the bad state is flabbiness of flesh; and whatever causes firmness of flesh is connected with the good state.
CAND: Still, of two contrary states the one may often be known from the other, and often the states from their object-matter. If it is clear what a good bodily condition is, then it is also clear what a bad one is; and from what belongs to the good, the good itself is seen, and conversely. If the good state is firmness of flesh, then the bad state is flabbiness, and whatever causes firmness is connected with the good state.

--- p4 ---
SRC: It follows moreover in general, that if of two contrary terms the one is used in many senses so also will the other be; as, for instance, if “the Just,” then also “the Unjust.” Now Justice and Injustice do seem to be used respectively in many senses, but, because the line of demarcation between these is very fine and minute, it commonly escapes notice that they are thus used, and it is not plain and manifest as where the various significations of terms are widely different for in these last the visible difference is great, for instance, the word κλεὶς is used equivocally to denote the bone which is under the neck of animals and the instrument with which people close doors.
CAND: In general, if of two contrary terms the one is used in many senses, the other will be too. So if "the just" is, then "the unjust" is. Now justice and injustice do seem to be used in many senses, but the line between these senses is so fine that it commonly escapes notice. It is not as plain as where the various senses of a word differ widely — as with the Greek kleis, which ambiguously denotes both the collarbone and the key used to lock a door.

SRC: Let it be ascertained then in how many senses the term “Unjust man” is used. Well, he who violates the law, and he who is a grasping man, and the unequal man, are all thought to be Unjust and so manifestly the Just man will be, the man who acts according to law, and the equal man “The Just” then will be the lawful and the equal, and “the Unjust” the unlawful and the unequal.
CAND: Let us ascertain, then, in how many senses "unjust man" is used. The lawbreaker, the grasping man, and the unequal man are all thought unjust. So the just man must be the lawful man and the equal man. The just, then, is the lawful and the equal; the unjust is the unlawful and the unequal.

--- p6 ---
SRC: Well, since the Unjust man is also a grasping man, he will be so, of course, with respect to good things, but not of every kind, only those which are the subject-matter of good and bad fortune and which are in themselves always good but not always to the individual. Yet men pray for and pursue these things: this they should not do but pray that things which are in the abstract good may be so also to them, and choose what is good for themselves.
CAND: Since the unjust is also a grasping man, he will be so with respect to good things — but not all of them, only those that are the subject-matter of good and bad fortune, and are in themselves always good though not always so to the individual. Men pray for and pursue these things, though they should not; they should pray that what is good in the abstract may also be good for them, and choose what is good for themselves.

--- p7 ---
SRC: But the Unjust man does not always choose actually the greater part, but even sometimes the less; as in the case of things which are simply evil: still, since the less evil is thought to be in a manner a good and the grasping is after good, therefore even in this case he is thought to be a grasping man, _i.e._ one who strives for more good than fairly falls to his share: of course he is also an unequal man, this being an inclusive and common term.
CAND: But the unjust man does not always choose the greater share; sometimes even the lesser, in the case of things that are simply evil. Still, since the lesser evil is thought in a way to be a good, and grasping is after good, he is in this case too thought to be a grasping man — that is, one who strives for more good than falls to his share. He is also an unequal man, this being an inclusive general term.

--- p8 ---
SRC: We said that the violator of Law is Unjust, and the keeper of the Law Just: further, it is plain that all Lawful things are in a manner Just, because by Lawful we understand what have been defined by the legislative power and each of these we say is Just. The Laws too give directions on all points, aiming either at the common good of all, or that of the best, or that of those in power (taking for the standard real goodness or adopting some other estimate); in one way we mean by Just, those things which are apt to produce and preserve happiness and its ingredients for the social community.
CAND: We said the lawbreaker is unjust and the law-keeper just. All lawful things are in a manner just, because what has been defined by the legislative power we call lawful, and each of these we say is just. The laws give directions on all matters, aiming either at the common good of all, or that of the best, or that of those in power (taking their standard from real goodness, or from some other criterion). SRC: Further, the Law commands the doing the deeds not only of the brave man (as not leaving the ranks, nor flying, nor throwing away one’s arms), but those also of the perfectly self-mastering man, as abstinence from adultery and wantonness; and those of the meek man, as refraining from striking others or using abusive language: and in like manner in respect of the other virtues and vices commanding some things and forbidding others, rightly if it is a good law, in a way somewhat inferior if it is one extemporised.
CAND: The law commands the actions of the brave man (not leaving the ranks, not fleeing, not throwing away one's arms), and those of the self-masterer (abstaining from adultery and wantonness), and those of the meek man (refraining from striking others or using abusive language). --- p17 ---
SRC: However, Justice in this sense is not a part of Virtue but is co-extensive with Virtue; nor is the Injustice which answers to it a part of Vice but co-extensive with Vice. Now wherein Justice in this sense differs from Virtue appears from what has been said: it is the same really, but the point of view is not the same: in so far as it has respect to one’s neighbour it is Justice, in so far as it is such and such a moral state it is simply Virtue.
CAND: Justice in this sense is not a part of virtue but co-extensive with virtue; and the corresponding injustice is not a part of vice but co-extensive with vice. Wherein justice in this sense differs from virtue appears from what has been said: it is really the same, but the point of view differs. As it is related to one's neighbor, it is justice; as it is a certain moral state, it is simply virtue.

--- p18 ---
SRC: But the object of our enquiry is Justice, in the sense in which it is a part of Virtue (for there is such a thing, as we commonly say), and likewise with respect to particular Injustice. And of the existence of this last the following consideration is a proof: there are many vices by practising which a man acts unjustly, of course, but does not grasp at more than his share of good; if, for instance, by reason of cowardice he throws away his shield, or by reason of ill-temper he uses abusive language, or by reason of stinginess does not give a friend pecuniary assistance; but whenever he does a grasping action, it is often in the way of none of these vices, certainly not in all of them, still in the way of some vice or other (for we blame him), and in the way of Injustice. There is then some kind of Injustice distinct from that co-extensive with Vice and related to it as a part to a whole, and some “Unjust” related to that which is co-extensive with violation of the law as a part to a whole.
CAND: But the object of our inquiry is justice as a part of virtue (for there is such a thing, as we commonly say), and likewise particular injustice. That this exists is shown by the following. There are many vices by practicing which a man acts unjustly but does not grasp at more than his share of good. If, from cowardice, he throws away his shield; or from temper, uses abusive language; or from stinginess, refuses a friend monetary help. But whenever a man does a grasping action, it is often in the way of none of these vices, certainly not of all of them, but still in the way of some vice, and in the way of injustice. There is, then, some kind of injustice distinct from that co-extensive with vice and related to it as a part to a whole; and some "unjust" related as a part to a whole to the unjust co-extensive with violation of law.

--- p19 ---
SRC: Again, suppose one man seduces a man’s wife with a view to gain and actually gets some advantage by it, and another does the same from impulse of lust, at an expense of money and damage; this latter will be thought to be rather destitute of self-mastery than a grasping man, and the former Unjust but not destitute of self-mastery: now why? plainly because of his gaining.
CAND: Suppose one man seduces a married woman with a view to gain and actually profits, and another does the same from lust, at cost of money and damage to himself. The latter will be thought lacking in self-mastery rather than grasping, while the former is unjust and not lacking in self-mastery. Why? Clearly because of his gain.

--- p20 ---
SRC: Again, all other acts of Injustice we refer to some particular depravity, as, if a man commits adultery, to abandonment to his passions; if he deserts his comrade, to cowardice; if he strikes another, to anger: but if he gains by the act to no other vice than to Injustice.
CAND: All other acts of injustice we refer to some particular depravity: adultery to abandonment to passion, desertion of a comrade to cowardice, striking another to anger. But gaining by the act, we refer to no other vice than injustice.

--- p21 ---

CAND: Thus it is clear that there is a kind of injustice distinct from and besides the total injustice that includes all vice, but having the same name because its definition is in the same genus. Both have their force in dealings with others, but the one acts upon honor, wealth, safety — or whatever single name we can use to include all these — and is moved by the pleasure attendant on gain, while the other acts upon the whole sphere of the good man's action.

CAND: The unjust has been divided into the unlawful and the unequal, and the just into the lawful and the equal. The injustice previously described is the unlawful kind. If self-injustice were possible, it would imply that the same thing could be taken away from and added to the same person — but this is impossible, the just and the unjust always implying more than one person.
