=== BOOK 4: Book 4 — Further Moral Virtues ===

--- p0 ---
SRC: We will next speak of Liberality. Now this is thought to be the mean state, having for its object-matter Wealth: I mean, the Liberal man is praised not in the circumstances of war, nor in those which constitute the character of perfected self-mastery, nor again in judicial decisions, but in respect of giving and receiving Wealth, chiefly the former. By the term Wealth I mean “all those things whose worth is measured by money.”
CAND: Next, liberality. This is thought to be a mean state with wealth as its object-matter. The liberal man is praised not in the context of war, nor in matters of self-mastery, nor in judicial decisions, but in respect of the giving and receiving of wealth — chiefly the giving. By wealth I mean all things whose worth is measured in money.

--- p1 ---
SRC: Now the states of excess and defect in regard of Wealth are respectively Prodigality and Stinginess: the latter of these terms we attach invariably to those who are over careful about Wealth, but the former we apply sometimes with a complex notion; that is to say, we give the name to those who fail of self-control and spend money on the unrestrained gratification of their passions; and this is why they are thought to be most base, because they have many vices at once.
CAND: The states of excess and deficiency regarding wealth are prodigality and stinginess. Stinginess we always apply to those who care too much about wealth. Prodigality we sometimes apply with a more complex sense: we give the name to those who lack self-control and spend money on the unrestrained gratification of their passions. This is why they are thought the most base, because they combine many vices at once.

--- p2 ---
SRC: It must be noted, however, that this is not a strict and proper use of the term, since its natural etymological meaning is to denote him who has one particular evil, viz. the wasting his substance: he is unsaved (as the term literally denotes) who is wasting away by his own fault; and this he really may be said to be; the destruction of his substance is thought to be a kind of wasting of himself, since these things are the means of living. Well, this is our acceptation of the term Prodigality.
CAND: Still, this is not a strict and proper use of the term. Its natural etymological meaning refers to one particular evil — wasting one's substance. The prodigal (literally "unsaved") is wasting away by his own fault; and he may be said to be so, since the destruction of his substance is a kind of wasting of himself, these being the means of life. This, then, is our meaning of prodigality.

--- p3 ---
SRC: Again. Whatever things are for use may be used well or ill, and Wealth belongs to this class. He uses each particular thing best who has the virtue to whose province it belongs: so that he will use Wealth best who has the virtue respecting Wealth, that is to say, the Liberal man.
CAND: Whatever is for use may be used well or badly; and wealth is such a thing. He uses each thing best who has the virtue whose province it is. So he will use wealth best who has the virtue concerned with wealth — that is, the liberal man.

--- p4 ---
SRC: Expenditure and giving are thought to be the using of money, but receiving and keeping one would rather call the possessing of it. And so the giving to proper persons is more characteristic of the Liberal man, than the receiving from proper quarters and forbearing to receive from the contrary. In fact generally, doing well by others is more characteristic of virtue than being done well by, and doing things positively honourable than forbearing to do things dishonourable; and any one may see that the doing well by others and doing things positively honourable attaches to the act of giving, but to that of receiving only the being done well by or forbearing to do what is dishonourable.
CAND: Expenditure and giving are thought to be the using of money; receiving and keeping are rather its possessing. So giving to the right people is more characteristic of the liberal man than receiving from the right quarters and abstaining from the wrong. Generally, doing well by others is more characteristic of virtue than being done well by, and doing positively noble things more than refraining from disgraceful ones. Anyone can see that doing well by others and doing things positively noble belong to the act of giving; to receiving belongs only being done well by or refraining from what is disgraceful.

--- p5 ---
SRC: Besides, thanks are given to him who gives, not to him who merely forbears to receive, and praise even more. Again, forbearing to receive is easier than giving, the case of being too little freehanded with one’s own being commoner than taking that which is not one’s own.
CAND: Besides, thanks go to the giver, not to one who merely refrains from receiving, and praise even more. Also, refraining from receiving is easier than giving, since people are more often too little freehanded with their own than inclined to take what is not theirs.

--- p6 ---
SRC: And again, it is they who give that are denominated Liberal, while they who forbear to receive are commended, not on the score of Liberality but of just dealing, while for receiving men are not, in fact, praised at all.
CAND: And it is the givers who are called liberal; those who refrain from receiving are commended not for liberality but for just dealing, while for receiving men are not praised at all.

--- p7 ---
SRC: And the Liberal are liked almost best of all virtuous characters, because they are profitable to others, and this their profitableness consists in their giving.
CAND: The liberal are liked almost best among virtuous characters, because they are useful to others; and their usefulness lies in their giving.

--- p8 ---
SRC: Furthermore: all the actions done in accordance with virtue are honourable, and done from the motive of honour: and the Liberal man, therefore, will give from a motive of honour, and will give rightly; I mean, to proper persons, in right proportion, at right times, and whatever is included in the term “right giving:” and this too with positive pleasure, or at least without pain, since whatever is done in accordance with virtue is pleasant or at least not unpleasant, most certainly not attended with positive pain.
CAND: All actions in accordance with virtue are noble and done from the motive of honor. The liberal man will therefore give from this motive, and give rightly: to the right people, in right proportion, at the right times, and in whatever ways "right giving" requires. He will do this with positive pleasure, or at least without pain, since what is done in accordance with virtue is pleasant or at least not unpleasant, and certainly not painful.

--- p9 ---
SRC: But the man who gives to improper people, or not from a motive of honour but from some other cause, shall be called not Liberal but something else. Neither shall he be so denominated who does it with pain: this being a sign that he would prefer his wealth to the honourable action, and this is no part of the Liberal man’s character; neither will such an one receive from improper sources, because the so receiving is not characteristic of one who values not wealth: nor again will he be apt to ask, because one who does kindnesses to others does not usually receive them willingly; but from proper sources (his own property, for instance) he will receive, doing this not as honourable but as necessary, that he may have somewhat to give: neither will he be careless of his own, since it is his wish through these to help others in need: nor will he give to chance people, that he may have wherewith to give to those to whom he ought, at right times, and on occasions when it is honourable so to do.
CAND: But the man who gives to improper people, or not from a motive of honor but from some other cause, is to be called something other than liberal. Nor is he to be called liberal who gives with pain; that would show he would prefer his wealth to the noble action, which is no part of the liberal man's character. Nor will such a man receive from improper sources; receiving thus is not characteristic of one who does not value wealth. Nor will he be apt to ask favors, since one who does kindnesses does not usually receive them willingly. But he will receive from proper sources (his own property, for instance), doing so not as something noble but as necessary, so that he may have something to give. Nor will he be careless of his own, since his wish is to use it to help those in need. Nor will he give to chance persons, since he wants to have something to give to those he should, at the right times, and on occasions when it is noble to give.

--- p10 ---
SRC: Again, it is a trait in the Liberal man’s character even to exceed very much in giving so as to leave too little for himself, it being characteristic of such an one not to have a thought of self.
CAND: It is a trait of the liberal man to go too far in giving, so that he leaves too little for himself; for not to think of oneself is characteristic of such a man.

--- p11 ---
SRC: Now Liberality is a term of relation to a man’s means, for the Liberal-ness depends not on the amount of what is given but on the moral state of the giver which gives in proportion to his means. There is then no reason why he should not be the more Liberal man who gives the less amount, if he has less to give out of.
CAND: Liberality is a term relative to a man's means; liberality depends not on the amount given but on the moral state of the giver, who gives in proportion to his means. There is no reason why the more liberal man should not be the one who gives a smaller amount, if he has less to give out of.

--- p12 ---
SRC: Again, they are thought to be more Liberal who have inherited, not acquired for themselves, their means; because, in the first place, they have never experienced want, and next, all people love most their own works, just as parents do and poets.
CAND: Those are thought more liberal who have inherited rather than acquired their means; for they have never experienced want, and besides, everyone loves best his own works, as parents and poets do.

--- p13 ---
SRC: It is not easy for the Liberal man to be rich, since he is neither apt to receive nor to keep but to lavish, and values not wealth for its own sake but with a view to giving it away. Hence it is commonly charged upon fortune that they who most deserve to be rich are least so. Yet this happens reasonably enough; it is impossible he should have wealth who does not take any care to have it, just as in any similar case.
CAND: It is not easy for the liberal man to be rich, since he is neither apt to receive nor to hold on to wealth, but to lavish it. He values wealth not for its own sake but for the sake of giving it away. Hence the common complaint to fortune that those who most deserve to be rich are least so. Yet this happens reasonably enough: it is impossible to be wealthy if one takes no trouble to be so, just as in any similar case.

--- p14 ---
SRC: Yet he will not give to improper people, nor at wrong times, and so on: because he would not then be acting in accordance with Liberality, and if he spent upon such objects, would have nothing to spend on those on which he ought: for, as I have said before, he is Liberal who spends in proportion to his means, and on proper objects, while he who does so in excess is prodigal (this is the reason why we never call despots prodigal, because it does not seem to be easy for them by their gifts and expenditure to go beyond their immense possessions).
CAND: Still the liberal man will not give to the wrong people, at the wrong times, or the like; he would then not be acting in accordance with liberality, and if he spent on such objects he would have nothing to spend where he ought. As I said, the liberal man spends in proportion to his means and on proper objects, while the one who does so in excess is prodigal. (We never call despots prodigal, because it does not seem easy for them by their gifts and expenditure to go beyond their immense possessions.)

--- p15 ---
SRC: To sum up then. Since Liberality is a mean state in respect of the giving and receiving of wealth, the Liberal man will give and spend on proper objects, and in proper proportion, in great things and in small alike, and all this with pleasure to himself; also he will receive from right sources, and in right proportion: because, as the virtue is a mean state in respect of both, he will do both as he ought, and, in fact, upon proper giving follows the correspondent receiving, while that which is not such is contrary to it. (Now those which follow one another come to co-exist in the same person, those which are contraries plainly do not.)
CAND: To sum up: since liberality is a mean state with respect to giving and receiving wealth, the liberal man will give and spend on proper objects and in proper proportion, in great and small matters alike, and all this with pleasure to himself. He will also receive from right sources and in right proportion. Since the virtue is a mean state with respect to both, he will do both as he ought; proper giving is followed by a corresponding receiving, and improper receiving is contrary to it. (What follow one another co-exist in the same person; contraries clearly do not.)

--- p16 ---
SRC: Again, should it happen to him to spend money beyond what is needful, or otherwise than is well, he will be vexed, but only moderately and as he ought; for feeling pleasure and pain at right objects, and in right manner, is a property of Virtue.
CAND: Should it happen that he spends beyond what is needful or not well, he will be vexed — but only moderately and as he ought. Feeling pleasure and pain at the right objects and in the right way is a property of virtue.

--- p17 ---
SRC: The Liberal man is also a good man to have for a partner in respect of wealth: for he can easily be wronged, since he values not wealth, and is more vexed at not spending where he ought to have done so than at spending where he ought not, and he relishes not the maxim of Simonides.
CAND: The liberal man is a good partner in matters of wealth. He is easily wronged, since he does not value wealth, and he is more vexed by failing to spend where he should than by spending where he should not. He has no taste for the maxim of Simonides.

--- p18 ---
SRC: But the Prodigal man goes wrong also in these points, for he is neither pleased nor pained at proper objects or in proper manner, which will become more plain as we proceed.
CAND: The prodigal goes wrong here too: he is neither pleased nor pained at the proper objects or in the proper way, as will become clearer.

--- p19 ---
SRC: We have said already that Prodigality and Stinginess are respectively states of excess and defect, and this in two things, giving and receiving (expenditure of course we class under giving). Well now, Prodigality exceeds in giving and forbearing to receive and is deficient in receiving, while Stinginess is deficient in giving and exceeds in receiving, but it is in small things.
CAND: We have said that prodigality and stinginess are states of excess and deficiency, and this in two things: giving and receiving. (Expenditure we class under giving.) Prodigality exceeds in giving and in not receiving, and is deficient in receiving; stinginess is deficient in giving and exceeds in receiving, though in small things.

--- p20 ---
SRC: The two parts of Prodigality, to be sure, do not commonly go together; it is not easy, I mean, to give to all if you receive from none, because private individuals thus giving will soon find their means run short, and such are in fact thought to be prodigal. He that should combine both would seem to be no little superior to the Stingy man: for he may be easily cured, both by advancing in years, and also by the want of means, and he may come thus to the mean: he has, you see, already the _facts_ of the Liberal man, he gives and forbears to receive, only he does neither in right manner or well. So if he could be wrought upon by habituation in this respect, or change in any other way, he would be a real Liberal man, for he will give to those to whom he should, and will forbear to receive whence he ought not. This is the reason too why he is thought not to be low in moral character, because to exceed in giving and in forbearing to receive is no sign of badness or meanness, but only of folly.
CAND: The two sides of prodigality do not commonly go together; it is not easy to give to all while receiving from none, since private individuals who do this soon find their means run short. Such are in fact thought prodigal. A man who combines both these faults would seem considerably superior to the stingy man; he can easily be cured, both by advancing years and by lack of means, and so may come to the mean. He already has the facts of the liberal man about him: he gives and refrains from receiving, only not in a right manner or well. If he could be worked on by habituation in this respect, or change in any other way, he would be a true liberal man, since he will give to those he should and refrain from receiving where he should not. This is also why he is thought not of low moral character; exceeding in giving and in not receiving is no sign of badness or meanness, but only of folly.

--- p21 ---
SRC: Well then, he who is Prodigal in this fashion is thought far superior to the Stingy man for the aforementioned reasons, and also because he does good to many, but the Stingy man to no one, not even to himself. But most Prodigals, as has been said, combine with their other faults that of receiving from improper sources, and on this point are Stingy: and they become grasping, because they wish to spend and cannot do this easily, since their means soon run short and they are necessitated to get from some other quarter; and then again, because they care not for what is honourable, they receive recklessly, and from all sources indifferently, because they desire to give but care not how or whence.
CAND: The prodigal who acts in this fashion is thought far superior to the stingy man, for these reasons and also because he does good to many, while the stingy man does good to no one, not even himself. But most prodigals, as has been said, also receive from improper sources and on this point are stingy. They become grasping because they want to spend but cannot do so easily; their means run short, and they must get more from some other quarter. Then, since they care nothing for the noble, they receive recklessly from all sources indifferently; for they desire to give but care not how or whence.

--- p22 ---
SRC: And for this reason their givings are not Liberal, inasmuch as they are not honourable, nor purely disinterested, nor done in right fashion; but they oftentimes make those rich who should be poor, and to those who are quiet respectable kind of people they will give nothing, but to flatterers, or those who subserve their pleasures in any way, they will give much. And therefore most of them are utterly devoid of self-restraint; for as they are open-handed they are liberal in expenditure upon the unrestrained gratification of their passions, and turn off to their pleasures because they do not live with reference to what is honourable.
CAND: For this reason their giving is not liberal, since it is neither noble, nor purely disinterested, nor done in a right way. They often make rich those who should be poor, and give nothing to respectable quiet people, but much to flatterers and those who serve their pleasures. Most prodigals, therefore, are also without self-restraint; being open-handed, they spend lavishly on the unrestrained gratification of their passions, and they turn aside to their pleasures because they do not live with reference to what is noble.

--- p23 ---
SRC: Thus then the Prodigal, if unguided, slides into these faults; but if he could get care bestowed on him he might come to the mean and to what is right.
CAND: The prodigal, if unguided, slides into these faults; but with care he might come to the mean, and to what is right.

--- p24 ---
SRC: Stinginess, on the contrary, is incurable: old age, for instance, and incapacity of any kind, is thought to make people Stingy; and it is more congenial to human nature than Prodigality, the mass of men being fond of money rather than apt to give: moreover it extends far and has many phases, the modes of stinginess being thought to be many. For as it consists of two things, defect of giving and excess of receiving, everybody does not have it entire, but it is sometimes divided, and one class of persons exceed in receiving, the other are deficient in giving. I mean those who are designated by such appellations as sparing, close-fisted, niggards, are all deficient in giving; but other men’s property they neither desire nor are willing to receive, in some instances from a real moderation and shrinking from what is base.
CAND: Stinginess, by contrast, is incurable. Old age and any kind of incapacity are thought to make people stingy, and it is more congenial to human nature than prodigality — most men are fond of money rather than apt to give. It also extends far and has many forms; the modes of stinginess are thought to be many. Since it consists of two things — deficiency in giving and excess in receiving — not everyone has it entire; sometimes it is divided. Some exceed in receiving, others are deficient in giving. Those called sparing, close-fisted, niggards, are all deficient in giving; but they neither desire nor want to receive other men's property, in some cases from genuine moderation and shrinking from what is base.

--- p25 ---
SRC: There are some people whose motive, either supposed or alleged, for keeping their property is this, that they may never be driven to do anything dishonourable: to this class belongs the skinflint, and every one of similar character, so named from the excess of not-giving. Others again decline to receive their neighbour’s goods from a motive of fear; their notion being that it is not easy to take other people’s things yourself without their taking yours: so they are content neither to receive nor give.
CAND: Some keep their property, as they either say or suppose, so as never to be driven to do anything disgraceful. To this class belongs the skinflint and every similar character, so named from the excess of not-giving. Others decline to receive what is their neighbor's from fear: their notion is that it is not easy to take other people's things without their taking yours. So they are content neither to give nor to receive.

--- p26 ---
SRC: The other class again who are Stingy in respect of receiving exceed in that they receive anything from any source; such as they who work at illiberal employments, brothel keepers, and such-like, and usurers who lend small sums at large interest: for all these receive from improper sources, and improper amounts. Their common characteristic is base-gaining, since they all submit to disgrace for the sake of gain and that small; because those who receive great things neither whence they ought, nor what they ought (as for instance despots who sack cities and plunder temples), we denominate wicked, impious, and unjust, but not Stingy.
CAND: The other class, stingy in receiving, exceeds in receiving anything from any source: such as those who work at disgraceful employments, brothel-keepers and the like, and usurers who lend small sums at high interest. All these receive from improper sources and in improper amounts. Their common feature is base gain; they all submit to disgrace for the sake of gain, and a small one at that. Those who receive great things from wrong sources and of the wrong kind, such as despots who sack cities and plunder temples, we call wicked, impious, and unjust — but not stingy.

--- p27 ---
SRC: Now the dicer and bath-plunderer and the robber belong to the class of the Stingy, for they are given to base gain: both busy themselves and submit to disgrace for the sake of gain, and the one class incur the greatest dangers for the sake of their booty, while the others make gain of their friends to whom they ought to be giving.
CAND: The dicer, the bath-plunderer, and the robber belong to the class of the stingy; they too are given to base gain. Both the one sort and the other take trouble and submit to disgrace for the sake of gain — the former incurring the greatest dangers for their booty, the latter making gain of their friends to whom they ought to be giving.

--- p28 ---
SRC: So both classes, as wishing to make gain from improper sources, are given to base gain, and all such receivings are Stingy. And with good reason is Stinginess called the contrary of Liberality: both because it is a greater evil than Prodigality, and because men err rather in this direction than in that of the Prodigality which we have spoken of as properly and completely such.
CAND: So both classes, wishing to gain from improper sources, are given to base gain; and all such receivings are stingy. With good reason stinginess is called the contrary of liberality; it is a greater evil than prodigality, and men more often err in this direction than in that of prodigality proper.

--- p29 ---
SRC: Let this be considered as what we have to say respecting Liberality and the contrary vices.
CAND: Let this stand as our discussion of liberality and the contrary vices.

SRC: but for doing so in great matters: that is to say, the Magnificent man is liberal, but the liberal is not thereby Magnificent. The falling short of such a state is called Meanness, the exceeding it Vulgar Profusion, Want of Taste, and so on; which are faulty, not because they are on an excessive scale in respect of right objects but, because they show off in improper objects, and in improper manner: of these we will speak presently. The Magnificent man is like a man of skill, because he can see what is fitting, and can spend largely in good taste; for, as we said at the commencement, the confirmed habit is determined by the separate acts of working, and by its object-matter.

--- p33 ---
SRC: Well, the expenses of the Magnificent man are great and fitting: such also are his works (because this secures the expenditure being not great merely, but befitting the work). So then the work is to be proportionate to the expense, and this again to the work, or even above it: and the Magnificent man will incur such expenses from the motive of honour, this being common to all the virtues, and besides he will do it with pleasure and lavishly; excessive accuracy in calculation being Mean. He will consider also how a thing may be done most beautifully and fittingly, rather, than for how much it may be done, and how at the least expense.
CAND: The magnificent man's expenses are great and fitting. So are his works; this ensures the expenditure is not merely great but befitting the work. The work must be proportionate to the expense, and the expense to the work, or even above it. The magnificent man will incur such expenses from the motive of honor (which is common to all virtues), and besides will do it with pleasure and lavishly; excessive accuracy of calculation is mean. He will consider rather how a thing may be done most beautifully and fittingly than how much it costs, or how it might be done most cheaply.

--- p34 ---
SRC: So the Magnificent man must be also a liberal man, because the liberal man will also spend what he ought, and in right manner: but it is the Great, that is to say tke large scale, which is distinctive of the Magnificent man, the object-matter of liberality being the same, and without spending more money than another man he will make the work more magnificent. I mean, the excellence of a possession and of a work is not the same: as a piece of property that thing is most valuable which is worth most, gold for instance; but as a work that which is great and beautiful, because the contemplation of such an object is admirable, and so is that which is Magnificent. So the excellence of a work is Magnificence on a large scale. There are cases of expenditure which we call honourable, such as are dedicatory offerings to the gods, and the furnishing their temples, and sacrifices, and in like manner everything that has reference to the Deity, and all such public matters as are objects of honourable ambition, as when men think in any case that it is their duty to furnish a chorus for the stage splendidly, or fit out and maintain a trireme, or give a general public feast.
CAND: The magnificent man must therefore also be liberal, for the liberal man will also spend what he ought and in the right way. But it is the great — the large scale — that is distinctive of the magnificent man; the object-matter of liberality is the same, but without spending more money than another he will make the work more magnificent. The excellence of a possession and of a work are different: as a piece of property the most valuable thing is what is worth most, like gold; as a work it is what is great and beautiful, because the contemplation of such an object is admirable, and so is what is magnificent. The excellence of a work lies in magnificence on a large scale. Some expenditures we call honorable: dedicatory offerings to the gods, the furnishing of temples, sacrifices, and in general whatever has reference to the gods; and all such public matters as are objects of honorable ambition — as when one thinks it his duty to furnish a chorus splendidly for the stage, or fit out and maintain a trireme, or give a general public feast.

--- p35 ---
SRC: Now in all these, as has been already stated, respect is had also to the rank and the means of the man who is doing them: because they should be proportionate to these, and befit not the work only but also the doer of the work. For this reason a poor man cannot be a Magnificent man, since he has not means wherewith to spend largely and yet becomingly; and if he attempts it he is a fool, inasmuch as it is out of proportion and contrary to propriety, whereas to be in accordance with virtue a thing must be done rightly.
CAND: In all such cases, as I said, regard is to be paid to the rank and means of the person involved; they should be proportionate to these and befit not only the work but also the doer. For this reason a poor man cannot be magnificent; he has no means by which to spend largely and yet becomingly. If he attempts it, he is a fool, since he acts out of proportion and against propriety, whereas anything done in accordance with virtue must be done rightly.

--- p36 ---
SRC: Such expenditure is fitting moreover for those to whom such things previously belong, either through themselves or through their ancestors or people with whom they are connected, and to the high-born or people of high repute, and so on: because all these things imply greatness and reputation.
CAND: Such expenditure is fitting also for those to whom such things already belong — whether in themselves, or through their ancestors, or through people connected with them — and for the highborn or people of high repute. For all these things imply greatness and reputation.

--- p37 ---
SRC: So then the Magnificent man is pretty much as I have described him, and Magnificence consists in such expenditures: because they are the greatest and most honourable: and of private ones such as come but once for all, marriage to wit, and things of that kind; and any occasion which engages the interest of the community in general, or of those who are in power; and what concerns receiving and despatching strangers; and gifts, and repaying gifts: because the Magnificent man is not apt to spend upon himself but on the public good, and gifts are pretty much in the same case as dedicatory offerings.
CAND: The magnificent man is pretty much as I have described him. Magnificence consists in such expenditures, because they are the greatest and most honorable. It also includes private expenditures that come once for all — such as marriage and things of that kind — and any occasion that engages the interest of the community or those in power, and the reception and sending off of strangers, and gifts and the repaying of gifts. The magnificent man is not apt to spend on himself but on the public good, and gifts are in much the same case as dedicatory offerings.

--- p38 ---
SRC: It is characteristic also of the Magnificent man to furnish his house suitably to his wealth, for this also in a way reflects credit; and again, to spend rather upon such works as are of long duration, these being most honourable. --- p41 ---
SRC: The man who is in the state of excess, called one of Vulgar Profusion, is in excess because he spends improperly, as has been said. I mean in cases requiring small expenditure he lavishes much and shows off out of taste; giving his club a feast fit for a wedding-party, or if he has to furnish a chorus for a comedy, giving the actors purple to wear in the first scene, as did the Megarians. And all such things he will do, not with a view to that which is really honourable, but to display his wealth, and because he thinks he shall be admired for these things; and he will spend little where he ought to spend much, and much where he should spend little.

--- p42 ---
SRC: The Mean man will be deficient in every case, and even where he has spent the most he will spoil the whole effect for want of some trifle; he is procrastinating in all he does, and contrives how he may spend the least, and does even that with lamentations about the expense, and thinking that he does all things on a greater scale than he ought.

--- p43 ---
SRC: Of course, both these states are faulty, but they do not involve disgrace because they are neither hurtful to others nor very unseemly.

--- p44 ---
SRC: The very name of Great-mindedness implies, that great things are its object-matter; and we will first settle what kind of things. It makes no difference, of course, whether we regard the moral state in the abstract or as exemplified in an individual.

--- p45 ---
SRC: Well then, he is thought to be Great-minded who values himself highly and at the same time justly, because he that does so without grounds is foolish, and no virtuous character is foolish or senseless. Well, the character I have described is Great-minded. The man who estimates himself lowly, and at the same time justly, is modest; but not Great-minded, since this latter quality implies greatness, just as beauty implies a large bodily conformation while small people are neat and well made but not beautiful.
CAND: He is thought to be great-souled who values himself highly, and does so justly; for a man who does this without grounds is a fool, and no virtuous character is foolish or senseless. The man I have described is great-souled. The man who estimates himself low, and does so justly, is modest; but not great-souled, since greatness of soul implies greatness, just as beauty implies large bodily conformation, while small people may be neat and well-made but not beautiful.

--- p46 ---
SRC: Again, he who values himself highly without just grounds is a Vain man: though the name must not be applied to every case of unduly high self-estimation. He that values himself below his real worth is Small-minded, and whether that worth is great, moderate, or small, his own estimate falls below it. --- p47 ---
SRC: The Great-minded man is then, as far as greatness is concerned, at the summit, but in respect of propriety he is in the mean, because he estimates himself at his real value (the other characters respectively are in excess and defect). So the Great-minded man bears himself as he ought in respect of honour and dishonour. In fact, without need of words, the Great-minded plainly have honour for their object-matter: since honour is what the great consider themselves specially worthy of, and according to a certain rate.

Now the Great-minded man, being by the hypothesis worthy of the greatest things, must be of the highest excellence, since the better a man is the more is he worth, and he who is best is worth the most: it follows then, that to be truly Great-minded a man must be good, and whatever is great in each virtue would seem to belong to the Great-minded. It would no way correspond with the character of the Great-minded to flee spreading his hands all abroad; nor to injure any one; for with what object in view will he do what is base, in whose eyes nothing is great? in short, if one were to go into particulars, the Great-minded man would show quite ludicrously unless he were a good man: he would not be in fact deserving of honour if he were a bad man, honour being the prize of virtue and given to the good.
CAND: The small-souled man falls short both in self-estimate and in comparison with the great-souled; the vain man exceeds in self-estimate, but does not exceed the great-souled. Now the great-souled man, being by hypothesis worthy of the greatest things, must be of the highest excellence. The better a man is, the more he is worth; the best is worth the most. SRC: This virtue, then, of Great-mindedness seems to be a kind of ornament of all the other virtues, in that it makes them better and cannot be without them; and for this reason it is a hard matter to be really and truly Great-minded; for it cannot be without thorough goodness and nobleness of character.
CAND: This virtue, then, seems to be a kind of ornament of the other virtues; it makes them better and cannot exist without them. For this reason it is hard to be truly great-souled: the thing cannot exist without thorough goodness and nobility of character.

--- p50 ---

But such as is given by ordinary people and on trifling grounds he will entirely despise, because these do not come up to his deserts: and dishonour likewise, because in his case there cannot be just ground for it.
CAND: Honor and dishonor are especially the great-souled man's object-matter. --- p51 ---
SRC: Now though, as I have said, honour is specially the object-matter of the Great-minded man, I do not mean but that likewise in respect of wealth and power, and good or bad fortune of every kind, he will bear himself with moderation, fall out how they may, and neither in prosperity will he be overjoyed nor in adversity will he be unduly pained. --- p52 ---
SRC: It seems too that pieces of good fortune contribute to form this character of Great-mindedness: I mean, the nobly born, or men of influence, or the wealthy, are considered to be entitled to honour, for they are in a position of eminence and whatever is eminent by good is more entitled to honour: and this is why such circumstances dispose men rather to Great-mindedness, because they receive honour at the hands of some men.
CAND: Pieces of good fortune also seem to contribute to this character. The well-born, the powerful, and the wealthy are considered entitled to honor, being in a position of eminence; what is eminent by good is more entitled to honor. So such circumstances dispose men toward greatness of soul, because they receive honor from some.

--- p53 ---
SRC: Now really and truly the good man alone is entitled to honour; only if a man unites in himself goodness with these external advantages he is thought to be more entitled to honour: but they who have them without also having virtue are not justified in their high estimate of themselves, nor are they rightly denominated Great-minded; since perfect virtue is one of the indispensable conditions to such & character.
CAND: Truly and rightly, only the good man is entitled to honor. If he unites goodness with these external advantages, he is thought more entitled. --- p54 ---
SRC: Further, such men become supercilious and insolent, it not being easy to bear prosperity well without goodness; and not being able to bear it, and possessed with an idea of their own superiority to others, they despise them, and do just whatever their fancy prompts; for they mimic the Great-minded man, though they are not like him, and they do this in such points as they can, so without doing the actions which can only flow from real goodness they despise others. Whereas the Great-minded man despises on good grounds (for he forms his opinions truly), but the mass of men do it at random.
CAND: Such men become supercilious and insolent; it is not easy to bear prosperity well without goodness. --- p55 ---
SRC: Moreover, he is not a man to incur little risks, nor does he court danger, because there are but few things he has a value for; but he will incur great dangers, and when he does venture he is prodigal of his life as knowing that there are terms on which it is not worth his while to live. He is the sort of man to do kindnesses, but he is ashamed to receive them; the former putting a man in the position of superiority, the latter in that of inferiority; accordingly he will greatly overpay any kindness done to him, because the original actor will thus be laid under obligation and be in the position of the party benefited. SRC: And he is by no means apt to make laments about things which cannot be helped, or requests about those which are trivial; because to be thus disposed with respect to these things is consequent only upon real anxiety about them. --- p60 ---
SRC: Also slow motion, deep-toned voice, and deliberate style of speech, are thought to be characteristic of the Great-minded man: for he who is earnest about few things is not likely to be in a hurry, nor he who esteems nothing great to be very intent: and sharp tones and quickness are the result of these.
CAND: Slow motion, a deep-toned voice, and a deliberate style of speech are thought characteristic of the great-souled man; for one who is earnest about few things is not likely to be in a hurry, and one who esteems nothing great is not likely to be intent. CAND: This is my idea of the great-souled man. The man in the deficiency is small-souled; the one in excess is vain. As with the last character discussed, these extremes are not considered exactly vicious, only mistaken, since they do no harm.

Thus much, however, is plain, that the mean state is praiseworthy, in virtue of which we are angry with those with whom, and at those things with which, we ought to be angry, and in right manner, and so on; while the excesses and defects are blameable, slightly so if only slight, more so if greater, and when considerable very blameable.

SRC: Next, as regards social intercourse and interchange of words and acts, some men are thought to be Over-Complaisant who, with a view solely to giving pleasure, agree to everything and never oppose, but think their line is to give no pain to those they are thrown amongst: they, on the other hand, are called Cross and Contentious who take exactly the contrary line to these, and oppose in everything, and have no care at all whether they give pain or not.
CAND: Next, in social intercourse and exchange of words and deeds: some men are thought overly obliging, agreeing to everything and never opposing, with a view solely to giving pleasure and giving no pain to those they are thrown among. --- p80 ---
SRC: Now it is quite clear of course, that the states I have named are blameable, and that the mean between them is praiseworthy, in virtue of which a man will let pass what he ought as he ought, and also will object in like manner. --- p89 ---
SRC: Now the Exaggerator is thought to have a tendency to lay claim to things reflecting credit on him, both when they do not belong to him at all and also in greater degree than that in which they really do: whereas the Reserved man, on the contrary, denies those which really belong to him or else depreciates them, while the mean character being a Plain-matter-of-fact person is Truthful in life and word, admitting the existence of what does really belong to him and making it neither greater nor less than the truth.
CAND: The exaggerator is thought to be apt to lay claim to things that reflect credit on him, both when they do not belong to him at all, and in greater degree than they really do. CAND: Each of these lines may be taken with or without some further motive; but in general men speak, act, and live each according to his particular character and disposition, unless he is acting from a special motive.

CAND: Since falsehood is in itself base and blameable, while truth is noble and praiseworthy, it follows that the truthful man (who is also in the mean) is praiseworthy, and the two who depart from strict truth are both blameable, but especially the exaggerator.
