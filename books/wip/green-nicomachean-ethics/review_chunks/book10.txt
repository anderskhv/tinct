=== BOOK 10: Book 10 — Pleasure and Happiness ===

--- p0 ---
SRC: Next, it would seem, follows a discussion respecting Pleasure, for it is thought to be most closely bound up with our kind: and so men train the young, guiding them on their course by the rudders of Pleasure and Pain. And to like and dislike what one ought is judged to be most important for the formation of good moral character: because these feelings extend all one’s life through, giving a bias towards and exerting an influence on the side of Virtue and Happiness, since men choose what is pleasant and avoid what is painful.
CAND: Next comes a discussion of pleasure, which is thought to be most closely bound up with our kind. Men train the young by steering them with the rudders of pleasure and pain. To like and dislike what one ought is judged most important for forming good moral character, since these feelings extend throughout life, exerting an influence and giving a bias toward virtue and happiness; men choose what is pleasant and avoid what is painful.

--- p1 ---
SRC: Subjects such as these then, it would seem, we ought by no means to pass by, and specially since they involve much difference of opinion. There are those who call Pleasure the Chief Good; there are others who on the contrary maintain that it is exceedingly bad; some perhaps from a real conviction that such is the case, others from a notion that it is better, in reference to our life and conduct, to show up Pleasure as bad, even if it is not so really; arguing that, as the mass of men have a bias towards it and are the slaves of their pleasures, it is right to draw them to the contrary, for that so they may possibly arrive at the mean.
CAND: We should by no means pass over such subjects, especially since they involve much difference of opinion. Some call pleasure the chief good; others, on the contrary, maintain that it is exceedingly bad — some from a real conviction, others from the notion that it is better, with a view to life and conduct, to show pleasure up as bad even if it is not so really, arguing that the mass of men are biased toward it and are slaves to their pleasures, and so should be drawn the other way to possibly arrive at the mean.

--- p2 ---
SRC: I confess I suspect the soundness of this policy; in matters respecting men’s feelings and actions theories are less convincing than facts: whenever, therefore, they are found conflicting with actual experience, they not only are despised but involve the truth in their fall: he, for instance, who deprecates Pleasure, if once seen to aim at it, gets the credit of backsliding to it as being universally such as he said it was, the mass of men being incapable of nice distinctions.
CAND: I suspect this policy is unsound. In matters of human feelings and actions, theories are less convincing than facts; when found conflicting with actual experience, they are not only despised but drag the truth down with them. He who deprecates pleasure, if once seen to aim at it, gets the credit of backsliding to it as being universally such as he said it was — the mass of men being incapable of nice distinctions.

--- p3 ---
SRC: Real accounts, therefore, of such matters seem to be most expedient, not with a view to knowledge merely but to life and conduct: for they are believed as being in harm with facts, and so they prevail with the wise to live in accordance with them.
CAND: Real accounts of such matters, then, seem most expedient, not for knowledge merely but for life and conduct. They are believed as being in harmony with facts, and so they prevail with the wise to live in accordance with them.

--- p5 ---
SRC: Now Eudoxus thought Pleasure to be the Chief Good because he saw all, rational and irrational alike, aiming at it: and he argued that, since in all what was the object of choice must be good and what most so the best, the fact of all being drawn to the same thing proved this thing to be the best for all: “For each,” he said, “finds what is good for itself just as it does its proper nourishment, and so that which is good for all, and the object of the aim of all, is their Chief Good.”
CAND: Eudoxus thought pleasure was the chief good, since he saw all, rational and irrational alike, aiming at it. He argued that, since what is the object of choice must be good and what most so the best, the fact of all being drawn to the same thing proved this thing to be the best for all. "Each," he said, "finds what is good for itself just as it does its proper nourishment, and so what is good for all and the object of aim for all is their chief good."

--- p6 ---
SRC: (And his theories were received, not so much for their own sake, as because of his excellent moral character; for he was thought to be eminently possessed of perfect self-mastery, and therefore it was not thought that he said these things because he was a lover of Pleasure but that he really was so convinced.)
CAND: (His theories were received not so much for their own sake as because of his excellent moral character; he was thought eminently to possess perfect self-mastery, and so it was not thought that he said these things because he was a lover of pleasure, but that he really was convinced.)

--- p7 ---
SRC: And he thought his position was not less proved by the argument from the contrary: that is, since Pain was in itself an object of avoidance to all the contrary must be in like manner an object of choice.
CAND: He thought his position no less proved by the argument from the contrary: since pain is in itself an object of avoidance to all, its contrary must likewise be an object of choice.

--- p8 ---
SRC: Again he urged that that is most choice-worthy which we choose, not by reason of, or with a view to, anything further; and that Pleasure is confessedly of this kind because no one ever goes on to ask to what purpose he is pleased, feeling that Pleasure is in itself choice-worthy.
CAND: Again he urged that what is most choice-worthy is what we choose not for the sake of, or with a view to, anything further; pleasure is confessedly of this kind, since no one ever asks to what purpose he is pleased, feeling that pleasure is in itself choice-worthy.

--- p9 ---
SRC: Again, that when added to any other good it makes it more choice-worthy; as, for instance, to actions of justice, or perfected self-mastery; and good can only be increased by itself.
CAND: Again, when added to any other good, it makes that good more choice-worthy — as, for instance, just actions or acts of self-mastery; and good can only be increased by itself.

--- p10 ---
SRC: However, this argument at least seems to prove only that it belongs to the class of goods, and not that it does so more than anything else: for every good is more choicewortby in combination with some other than when taken quite alone. In fact, it is by just such an argument that Plato proves that Pleasure is not the Chief Good: “For,” says he, “the life of Pleasure is more choice-worthy in combination with Practical Wisdom than apart from it; but, if the compound better then simple Pleasure cannot be the Chief Good; because the very Chief Good cannot by any addition become choice-worthy than it is already:” and it is obvious that nothing else can be the Chief Good, which by combination with any of the things in themselves good comes to be more choice-worthy.
CAND: But this argument at least seems to prove only that pleasure belongs to the class of goods, not that it does so more than anything else. Every good is more choice-worthy in combination with another than taken alone. In fact, it is by just such an argument that Plato proves pleasure is not the chief good: "the life of pleasure is more choice-worthy in combination with practical wisdom than apart from it; if the compound is better, then simple pleasure cannot be the chief good, since the chief good cannot by any addition become more choice-worthy than it is already." Obviously nothing else can be the chief good which by combination with things in themselves good becomes more choice-worthy.

SRC: What is there then of such a nature? (meaning, of course, whereof we can partake; because that which we are in search of must be such).
CAND: What is there, then, of such a nature? (Meaning, of course, what we can partake of, since what we are looking for must be such.)

--- p12 ---
SRC: As for those who object that “what all aim at is not necessarily good,” I confess I cannot see much in what they say, because what all _think_ we say _is_. And he who would cut away this ground from under us will not bring forward things more dependable: because if the argument had rested on the desires of irrational creatures there might have been something in what he says, but, since the rational also desire Pleasure, how can his objection be allowed any weight? and it may be that, even in the lower animals, there is some natural good principle above themselves which aims at the good peculiar to them.
CAND: As for those who object that "what all aim at is not necessarily good," I cannot see much in what they say. What all think, we say, is. --- p13 ---
SRC: Nor does that seem to be sound which is urged respecting the argument from the contrary: I mean, some people say “it does not follow that Pleasure must be good because Pain is evil, since evil may be opposed to evil, and both evil and good to what is indifferent:” now what they say is right enough in itself but does not hold in the present instance. If both Pleasure and Pain were bad both would have been objects of avoidance; or if neither then neither would have been, at all events they must have fared alike: but now men do plainly avoid the one as bad and choose the other as good, and so there is a complete opposition.
CAND: Nor does that seem sound which is urged about the argument from the contrary. Some say, "it does not follow that pleasure must be good because pain is evil, since evil may be opposed to evil, and both evil and good to what is indifferent." What they say is right enough in itself, but does not hold in the present instance. If both pleasure and pain were bad, both would be objects of avoidance; if neither, neither would be — at all events both would fare alike. But now men plainly avoid one as bad and choose the other as good, so there is a complete opposition.

--- p14 ---
SRC: Nor again is Pleasure therefore excluded from being good because it does not belong to the class of qualities: the acts of Virtue are not qualities, neither is Happiness [yet surely both are goods].
CAND: Nor is pleasure excluded from being good because it does not belong to the class of qualities: the acts of virtue are not qualities either, nor is happiness — yet both are goods.

--- p15 ---
SRC: Again, they say the Chief Good is limited but Pleasure unlimited, in that it admits of degrees.
CAND: They say the chief good is limited but pleasure unlimited, since it admits of degrees.

--- p16 ---
SRC: Now if they judge this from the act of feeling Pleasure then the same thing will apply to justice and all the other virtues, in respect of which clearly it is said that men are more or less of such and such characters (according to the different virtues), they are more just or more brave, or one may practise justice and self-mastery more or less.
CAND: If they judge this from the act of feeling pleasure, then the same applies to justice and the other virtues; men are said to be more or less of such characters (according to the different virtues), more just or more brave; and one may practice justice and self-mastery more or less.

--- p17 ---
SRC: If, on the other hand, they judge in respect of the Pleasures themselves then it may be they miss the true cause, namely that some are unmixed and others mixed: for just as health being in itself limited, admits of degrees, why should not Pleasure do so and yet be limited? in the former case we account for it by the fact that there is not the same adjustment of parts in all men, nor one and the same always in the same individual: but health, though relaxed, remains up to a certain point, and differs in degrees; and of course the same may be the case with Pleasure.
CAND: If, on the other hand, they judge from the pleasures themselves, they may miss the true cause, namely, that some are unmixed and others mixed. Just as health, being in itself limited, still admits of degrees, why should not pleasure do so and yet be limited? In the former case we account for it by the fact that there is not the same adjustment of parts in all men, nor one and the same in the same individual; but health, though relaxed, remains up to a certain point, and differs in degrees. The same may be the case with pleasure.

--- p18 ---
SRC: Again, assuming the Chief Good to be perfect and all Movements and Generations imperfect, they try to shew that Pleasure is a Movement and a Generation.
CAND: Again, assuming the chief good to be perfect and all movements and generations imperfect, they try to show pleasure is a movement and a generation.

--- p19 ---
SRC: Yet they do not seem warranted in saying even that it is a Movement: for to every Movement are thought to belong swiftness and slowness, and if not in itself, as to that of the universe, yet relatively: but to Pleasure neither of these belongs: for though one may have got quickly into the state Pleasure, as into that of anger, one cannot be in the state quickly, nor relatively to the state of any other person; but we can walk or grow, and so on, quickly or slowly.
CAND: Yet they do not seem warranted even in calling it a movement. To every movement swiftness and slowness are thought to belong — if not in itself (as the motion of the universe), then relatively. But neither belongs to pleasure. Though one may have gotten quickly into the state of pleasure (as into that of anger), one cannot be in the state quickly, nor relatively to the state of any other person. But we can walk or grow, and so on, quickly or slowly.

--- p20 ---
SRC: Of course it is possible to change into the state of Pleasure quickly or slowly, but to act in the state (by which, I mean, have the perception of Pleasure) quickly, is not possible.
CAND: Of course one can change into the state of pleasure quickly or slowly, but to act in the state (that is, have the perception of pleasure) quickly is not possible.

--- p21 ---
SRC: And how can it be a Generation? because, according to notions generally held, not _any_thing is generated from _any_thing, but a thing resolves itself into that out of which it was generated: whereas of that of which Pleasure is a Generation Pain is a Destruction.
CAND: How can it be a generation? According to common views, not anything is generated from anything, but a thing resolves itself into that out of which it was generated; whereas, of that of which pleasure is supposed to be a generation, pain is a destruction.

--- p22 ---
SRC: Again, they say that Pain is a lack of something suitable to nature and Pleasure a supply of it.
CAND: Again, they say pain is a lack of something suitable to nature, and pleasure a supply of it.

--- p23 ---
SRC: But these are affections of the body: now if Pleasure really is a supplying of somewhat suitable to nature, that must feel the Pleasure in which the supply takes place, therefore the body of course: yet this is not thought to be so: neither then is Pleasure a supplying, only a person of course will be pleased when a supply takes place just as he will be pained when he is cut short.
CAND: But these are affections of the body. If pleasure really is a supplying of what is suitable to nature, that which the supply occurs in must feel the pleasure — that is, the body; yet this is not thought to be so. So pleasure is not a supplying; only a person, of course, will be pleased when a supply takes place, just as he will be pained when cut short.

--- p24 ---
SRC: This notion would seem to have arisen out of the Pains and Pleasures connected with natural nourishment; because, when people have felt a lack and so have had Pain first, they, of course, are pleased with the supply of their lack.
CAND: This notion seems to have arisen from the pains and pleasures connected with natural nourishment; when people feel a lack and so have pain first, they are, of course, pleased with the supply of their lack.

--- p25 ---
SRC: But this is not the case with all Pleasures: those attendant on mathematical studies, for instance, are unconnected with any Pain; and of such as attend on the senses those which arise through the sense of Smell; and again, many sounds, and sights, and memories, and hopes: now of what can these be Generations? because there has been here no lack of anything to be afterwards supplied.
CAND: But this is not the case with all pleasures. Those attending mathematical studies are unconnected with any pain; among the senses, those from the sense of smell; and many sounds, sights, memories, and hopes. Of what can these be generations? SRC: And to those who bring forward disgraceful Pleasures we may reply that these are not really pleasant things; for it does not follow because they are pleasant to the ill-disposed that we are to admit that they are pleasant except to them; just as we should not say that those things are really wholesome, or sweet, or bitter, which are so to the sick, or those objects really white which give that impression to people labouring under ophthalmia.
CAND: To those who bring forward disgraceful pleasures, we may reply that these are not really pleasant. --- p27 ---
SRC: Or we might say thus, that the Pleasures are choice-worthy but not as derived from these sources: just as wealth is, but not as the price of treason; or health, but not on the terms of eating anything however loathsome.
CAND: Or we might say pleasures are choice-worthy but not as derived from these sources — just as wealth is, but not as the price of treason; or health, but not on the terms of eating anything however loathsome.

--- p28 ---
SRC: Or again, may we not say that Pleasures differ in kind? those derived from honourable objects, for instance are different from those arising from disgraceful ones; and it is not possible to experience the Pleasure of the just man without being just, or of the musical man without being musical; and so on of others.
CAND: Or we may say pleasures differ in kind. Those derived from honorable objects, for instance, differ from those arising from disgraceful ones; it is not possible to experience the pleasure of the just man without being just, or of the musical man without being musical; and so on.

SRC: The distinction commonly drawn between the friend and the flatterer would seem to show clearly either that Pleasure is not a good, or that there are different kinds of Pleasure: for the former is thought to have good as the object of his intercourse, the latter Pleasure only; and this last is reproached, but the former men praise as having different objects in his intercourse.
CAND: The commonly drawn distinction between the friend and the flatterer would seem to show clearly either that pleasure is not a good, or that there are different kinds of pleasure. CAND: Again, no one would choose to live his whole life through with a child's intellect, though receiving the highest possible pleasure from such objects as children receive it from; or to take pleasure in doing any of the most disgraceful things, though sure never to be pained.

CAND: There are many things about which we should be diligent even though they brought no pleasure — seeing, remembering, knowing, possessing the various excellences. That pleasures do follow on these naturally makes no difference; we should certainly choose them even if no pleasure resulted.

SRC: An act of Sight is thought to be complete at any moment; that is to say, it lacks nothing the accession of which subsequently will complete its whole nature.
CAND: An act of sight is thought to be complete at any moment; it lacks nothing the later accession of which would complete its whole nature.

--- p35 ---
SRC: Well, Pleasure resembles this: because it is a whole, as one may say; and one could not at any moment of time take a Pleasure whose whole nature would be completed by its lasting for a longer time. And for this reason it is not a Movement: for all Movement takes place in time of certain duration and has a certain End to accomplish; for instance, the Movement of house-building is then only complete when the builder has produced what he intended, that is, either in the whole time [necessary to complete the whole design], or in a given portion. But all the subordinate Movements are incomplete in the parts of the time, and are different in kind from the whole movement and from one another (I mean, for instance, that the fitting the stones together is a Movement different from that of fluting the column, and both again from the construction of the Temple as a whole: but this last is complete as lacking nothing to the result proposed; whereas that of the basement, or of the triglyph, is incomplete, because each is a Movement of a part merely).
CAND: Pleasure resembles this; it is a whole, so to speak, and one could not at any moment take a pleasure whose whole nature would be completed by lasting longer. For this reason it is not a movement; all movement takes place in time of a certain duration and has a certain end to accomplish. The movement of house-building, for instance, is complete only when the builder has produced what he intended — either in the whole time required for the whole design, or in a given portion. All subordinate movements are incomplete in the parts of the time, and differ in kind from the whole movement and from one another (the fitting of stones together is a movement different from the fluting of a column, and both from the construction of the temple as a whole; this last is complete as lacking nothing in the proposed result, while those of the basement or the triglyph are incomplete, each being a movement of a part).

--- p36 ---
SRC: As I said then, they differ in kind, and you cannot at any time you choose find a Movement complete in its whole nature, but, if at all, in the whole time requisite.
CAND: As I said, they differ in kind; you cannot at any moment you choose find a movement complete in its whole nature — only, if at all, in the whole time required.

CAND: So too with the movement of walking and all others. If motion is a movement from one place to another, it has different kinds: flying, walking, leaping, and such. --- p39 ---
SRC: But of Pleasure the whole nature is complete at any given moment: it is plain then that Pleasure and Movement must be different from one another, and that Pleasure belongs to the class of things whole and complete. CAND: From these considerations, then, it is plain that people are not warranted in saying pleasure is a movement or a generation. --- p41 ---
SRC: Now since every Percipient Faculty works upon the Object answering to it, and perfectly the Faculty in a good state upon the most excellent of the Objects within its range (for Perfect Working is thought to be much what I have described; and we will not raise any question about saying “the Faculty” works, instead of, “that subject wherein the Faculty resides”), in each case the best Working is that of the Faculty in its best state upon the best of the Objects answering to it. And this will be, further, most perfect and most pleasant: for Pleasure is attendant upon every Percipient Faculty, and in like manner on every intellectual operation and speculation; and that is most pleasant which is most perfect, and that most perfect which is the Working of the best Faculty upon the most excellent of the Objects within its range.
CAND: Every perceptive faculty works upon the object corresponding to it, and perfectly when the faculty is in a good state and works on the most excellent object within its range (perfect working being much what I have described; let us not quibble about saying "the faculty" works instead of "the subject in which the faculty resides"). In each case the best working is that of the faculty in its best state upon the best of its objects. This will further be the most perfect and most pleasant; pleasure attends every perceptive faculty, every intellectual operation and speculation; and what is most pleasant is what is most perfect, which is the working of the best faculty upon the most excellent of its objects.

--- p42 ---
SRC: And Pleasure perfects the Working. But Pleasure does not perfect it in the same way as the Faculty and Object of Perception do, being good; just as health and the physician are not in similar senses causes of a healthy state.
CAND: Pleasure perfects the working. But pleasure does not perfect it in the same way as the faculty and the object of perception do, being good; just as health and the physician are not in similar senses causes of a healthy state.

--- p43 ---

CAND: That pleasure does arise upon the exercise of every perceptive faculty is evident; we commonly say sights and sounds are pleasant. --- p44 ---
SRC: Furthermore, Pleasure perfects the act of Working not in the way of an inherent state but as a supervening finish, such as is bloom in people at their prime. Therefore so long as the Object of intellectual or sensitive Perception is such as it should be and also the Faculty which discerns or realises the Object, there will be Pleasure in the Working: because when that which has the capacity of being acted on and that which is apt to act are alike and similarly related, the same result follows naturally.
CAND: Pleasure perfects the act of working not as an inherent state but as a supervening finish — such as bloom in people at their prime. --- p45 ---

But there are some things which please when new, but afterwards not in the like way, for exactly the same reason: that at first the mind is roused and works on these Objects with its powers at full tension; just as they who are gazing stedfastly at anything; but afterwards the act of Working is not of the kind it was at first, but careless, and so the Pleasure too is dulled.
CAND: How is it, then, that no one feels pleasure continuously? Is it not that he grows weary, since all human faculties are incapable of unintermitting exertion? --- p46 ---

And Pleasure perfects the acts of Working, and so Life after which men grasp. No wonder then that they aim also at Pleasure, because to each it perfects Life, which is itself choice-worthy. (We will take leave to omit the question whether we choose Life for Pleasure’s sake of Pleasure for Life’s sake; because these two plainly are closely connected and admit not of separation; since Pleasure comes not into being without Working, and again, every Working Pleasure perfects.)
