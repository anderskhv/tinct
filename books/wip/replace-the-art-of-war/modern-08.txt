Master Sun gave this teaching.
Warfare generally begins in this fashion: the commander takes his ruler's instructions and then brings the armies together.
Avoid making camp in low or marshy places. Establish friendship with nearby states. Do not remain long in a distant land. Use cunning in wooded, mountainous terrain. When on death ground, give battle.
There are always some routes one must refuse to take, some troops one must refuse to assault, some castles one must refuse to besiege, some places one must refuse for battle, and some instructions from one's ruler one must refuse to carry out.
A commander familiar with the Nine Changes understands how soldiers should be used. Without that understanding, even his knowledge of the landscape cannot be put to work.
When directing armies, if you understand the practice of the Nine Changes, knowing the Five Advantages is of no use.
The discerning person weighs both benefits and drawbacks. He finds an escape from hardship, yet remains alert to danger even on the day he wins.
To bring an opponent into submission, do him every possible injury and draw him into pointless ventures. Tempt neighbouring rulers with profit as well, so that their actions follow your wishes.
In managing a campaign, therefore, trust your own readiness instead of assuming the opponent will stay away. Rather than assuming your stronghold will escape attack, ensure that every part is defended.
A commander must watch for five failings that place him in danger.
Thoughtless rashness carries him towards his death.
Excessive caution leaves him liable to be taken prisoner.
An easily roused temper exposes him to insult.
Excessive rigidity about proper behaviour opens him to dishonour.
Too much concern for the soldiers creates difficulties.
These five defects in a commander bring disaster in warfare. They cause armies to be overthrown and their leaders to be killed. They therefore demand close attention.
