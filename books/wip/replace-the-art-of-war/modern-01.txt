These are the teachings of Master Sun.
For every nation, war is a matter of immense importance. The army determines whether people live or die: through it the State survives or is destroyed.
It therefore demands careful and sustained study.
In warfare, alongside strategy and circumstances, five things are essential. They are named, in order, The Way, Heaven, Earth, the Leader, and Law. None of these five can be left out.
The Way means right human conduct. When those who govern act honourably, the people stand together: they face danger without fear and place their lives at their Lord's disposal.
Heaven embraces Yin and Yang, cold and heat, the season and the time.
Earth concerns distance, character, size, and strategic location.
The Leader needs understanding, honesty, kindness, bravery, and firmness.
Law means dividing troops and arranging their organisation.
A commander must understand all these matters. Understanding them brings conquest; failing to understand them brings defeat.
We must also compare our situation with the enemy's, both in these respects and in the seven further matters that follow.
There are seven points to consider.
The ruler's moral character; the commander's competence; advantages supplied by nature; how disciplined the armies are; how strong the troops are; how well they are trained; and fairness in giving rewards and imposing penalties.
With this knowledge, I can predict who will win.
A commander serving me who follows my plans in battle wins consistently, and I keep him in my service. One who departs from those plans will lose and be removed from his command.
Thus, after considering these matters, finding the advantage on our side, and securing the generals' agreement, we establish circumstances that offer the prospect of victory. But neither the precise moment nor the method can be settled in advance: circumstances must shape changes to the plan.
Warfare involves pretence. When we are able to act, we present ourselves as unable; when we are close to our opponent, we suggest we are distant; when distant, we suggest we are close.
Tempt your opponent with a modest gain. Bewilder him and take him captive. Where you have shortcomings, present a faultless appearance and overawe him. Make a show of strength so that he stays away. Stir up his anger and disrupt his plans. Appear beneath him so that he looks down on you. If he has strength to spare, exhaust it; if his camp is united, divide it. Strike where he is vulnerable and turn up where you are least expected.
These are the successful planner's hidden methods. They must therefore remain undisclosed until the time for using them.
In the assessment made in the Sanctuary before battle, success belongs to whichever side surpasses the other in these matters. Those possessing many such advantages will win; those possessing few will fail to win; those possessing none are truly without hope.
Once I know how each side stands in these respects, I can predict which of them will win.
