Master Sun gave this teaching.
Dividing a large body of men into smaller units makes it possible to command them just as one commands a small body.
Drums, bells, and flags make it possible to direct great numbers in combat in the same way that one directs a few.
An army is reliably protected from defeat by expertly alternating ordinary manoeuvres with extraordinary ones.
Understanding an opponent's strong and weak points, and using both reality and deception, allows us to crush him as a millstone crushes an egg beneath it.
In combat, moreover, the ordinary force engages the opponent, while the extraordinary force brings about his defeat.
Expertly directed, the extraordinary force endures like earth and heaven. It never stops, like rivers flowing and tides returning. It alternates endlessly like moon and sun, arriving and departing as the seasons do.
Five notes can be arranged into countless harmonies. Only five colours exist, yet blending them creates endless shades. Five tastes exist, yet combining them yields more flavours than our tongues can tell apart.
Warfare has only two forces, ordinary and extraordinary, yet their variations have no limit. They change into each other like a wheel revolving without a starting or finishing point. No one can fully unravel their mystery.
The troops' spirit resembles a rushing torrent carrying rocks along upon its shoulders.
Time the blow as precisely as a falcon judges its flight, striking and crushing its prey in an instant.
The effective fighter therefore has a terrifying spirit and strikes at sudden moments. He resembles a cocked crossbow: one touch of the trigger lets its taut string go.
Amid battle's bewildering noise and movement, confusion is absent; at the very heart of the fighting, the formation cannot be penetrated.
Complete discipline allows an appearance of disorder; real bravery allows an appearance of fear; genuine power allows an appearance of weakness.
Subdivision lets us imitate disorder; spirit lets us imitate fear; our fighting formation lets us imitate weakness.
By changing our formations, we compel our opponent to adjust to them, and so make him move.
Give the opponent an advantageous point and he is sure to seize it. By granting him that benefit, we draw him into motion and then descend upon him.
Thus the effective fighter looks to spirit for success, rather than relying wholly on his men's individual skill. He selects them carefully and entrusts the rest to the force of battle. Still, whenever an opening or advantage appears, he exploits it as far as it will go.
A log or stone rests motionless on level earth, but on a slope gathers force as it travels. Wait for your chance in that same way, and act with that force when the chance comes.
Under an accomplished general, the men's spirit has the momentum of a rounded stone sent rolling down from a lofty mountain peak.
