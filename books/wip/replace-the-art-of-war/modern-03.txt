Master Sun gave this teaching.
The principles of warfare favour taking a country without a struggle over defeating it through burning and killing.
Taking the opposing army whole is preferable to beating it down after it has resisted fiercely.
It is preferable to take the "Lu," the "Tsu," or the "Wu" entire, rather than destroy these units on the battlefield.
Winning a hundred battles does not represent the highest achievement. The greatest skill lies in bringing an opponent into submission without resorting to combat.
The finest soldier therefore defeats his opponent's thinking with a better plan. Next comes the one who stops hostile forces from joining together; after him, the one who fights their army. Laying siege to their stronghold is the least desirable course.
Avoid a siege whenever there is any possible alternative. Before one can begin, three months must be spent building platforms, rams, and engines of siege. Another three months before the stronghold are then needed to construct the "Chuyin." The commander becomes angry and runs out of patience; his men swarm against the walls like ants before the proper moment, and one third die without achieving anything. These are the disasters that accompany sieges.
An expert in warfare therefore makes hostile troops submit without battle, takes their stronghold without a siege, and acquires their kingdom without a prolonged struggle. He secures the full benefit while his weapons remain untarnished.
This is what attacking through strategy means.
The rules of warfare prescribe encirclement when your strength is tenfold the enemy's, an attack when it is fivefold, and division when your numbers are double. With equal strength, fight with every effort. With fewer men, manoeuvre until an opening comes. If overwhelmingly inferior, give no opportunity for battle. Stubborn resistance by a smaller force only results in its being taken prisoner.
The soldier sustains the country. When his support is complete, the country must be strong; any shortcoming in that support leaves the country weak.
A ruler can hamper his own army in three different ways.
He may command the army on campaign to advance without realising that it should stay back, or order it to withdraw without realising that it should hold its ground.
This binds the troops as though they were tied with cord.
He may govern the troops just as he governs the state, despite knowing nothing of military business.
This leaves the men bewildered and uncertain.
He may determine where troops are placed without understanding the army's circumstances.
This plants suspicion in the minds of the troops.
When bewilderment and suspicion possess an army, neighbouring rulers become a threat. The troops are thrown into confusion and delivered into their opponent's hands.
Five circumstances allow us to predict a successful outcome.
The commander recognises when battle is appropriate and when it is not; he understands when many troops or only a few should be used; the people and their government agree; the state stands ready and attacks during an unguarded moment of its opponent; the commander is capable and his ruler leaves him free to act.
These five signs announce that success is coming.
An old teaching says that understanding both sides removes fear even through a hundred encounters. Someone who ignores his opponent and attends only to himself wins once and loses the next time. Someone ignorant both of his opponent and of the resources he himself possesses loses every time.
