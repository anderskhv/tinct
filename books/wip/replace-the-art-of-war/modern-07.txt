Master Sun gave this teaching.
Military business generally proceeds in the following manner.
The commander takes his ruler's instructions, gathers the troops, establishes harmony among them, and goes out on campaign.
Nothing is harder than battlefield tactics. The difficulty consists in reckoning distance and timing correctly and turning an unfavourable turn of events around.
A master of manoeuvre tempts his opponent with a gain that draws him onto a roundabout road, then sets out later yet reaches the destination ahead of him.
An army's actions can bring benefits; the quarrelling of an unruly crowd carries grave dangers.
If we use the entire army at once to gain an edge over our opponent, we may run out of time before achieving our aim. If only part presses ahead, the transport is lost. Suppose men throw aside armour and helmets, halt neither by night nor by day, cover twice the distance and perform twice the labour, then finally face the opponent one hundred leagues away. The result is the loss of their commander. The strongest arrive first while exhausted men fall behind, leaving only one tenth of the force ready for use.
A forced march of fifty leagues in pursuit of an advantage can bring failure to the advance guard's commander: only half of his troops will reach the destination.
Force an army to march thirty leagues for an advantage, and only two thirds of its men will be ready for use.
A shortage of ammunition, provisions, or other stores can also bring about catastrophe.
A ruler cannot negotiate with neighbouring rulers while remaining ignorant of what they intend.
A person unfamiliar with mountains, woods, narrow passes, and marshland cannot command an army on the march.
Without employing someone to guide him, a commander cannot turn the terrain to his benefit.
Conceal what you are doing; wait for a promising moment; separate or concentrate your forces as the situation requires.
Attack with the wind's speed; march with the forest's calm; lay waste like fire as you occupy. When defending, stand as solidly as a mountain, beyond the opponent's understanding like darkness. Move with the rapidity of a lightning flash.
Allow the greatest possible number to share in plundering, and divide among them the gains from the territory you seize.
Thus success goes to whoever understands both the winding route and the direct one.
These are the practices that make up battlefield tactics.
The old military writings explain that drums and bells serve where spoken commands cannot be heard, and flags help men to see. Bells, drums, banners, and flags bring all ears and eyes together in shared attention.
Once everyone acts together, brave men cannot press ahead on their own, nor can frightened men fall back without restraint. This is how a large body of men can be employed.
Accordingly, fighting at night makes extensive use of drums and signal fires, while fighting by day uses many flags and banners. These bewilder the opponent's hearing and sight.
In this way we intimidate his troops and frustrate the aims of their commander.
Men's spirits are eager in the morning, sluggish around noon, and inclined towards returning by evening. A commander who handles troops well therefore avoids the hour of their eagerness and strikes his opponent when his men are listless or turning towards camp.
This is how to put the changing character of energy to use.
Keeping your own heart governed means meeting disorder with discipline and noisy uproar with stillness.
Preserve strength by waiting for an opponent travelling from far away, meeting his hunger with your own fullness, and bringing fatigue against rest.
Where rows of flags flutter and tightly packed fighting ranks extend, withhold your attack and wait patiently for the right moment.
Avoid attacking anyone who occupies the heights or has heights behind him. Refuse to chase a pretended retreat, and do not assault troops whose spirit is high.
Refuse whatever bait the opponent puts before you.
Leave alone an opponent who has broken camp and is preparing to withdraw. When you encircle him, leave a way out. Do not bear down upon him when he is desperate.
These are the practices for putting soldiers to use.
