Master Sun gave this teaching.
The needs of a campaign are these: one thousand light chariots, each drawn by four horses; one thousand chariots covered with leather; and one hundred thousand men in armour. Supplies must also travel to distant battlefields. Spending at home and on campaign, hospitality for guests, repair materials such as glue and lacquer, and everything needed to maintain armour and wagons together consume one thousand gold pieces every day. With that expenditure, an army of one hundred thousand can be assembled: the means of winning are then in your hands.
Yet even a successful campaign, if kept going too long, cools the troops' enthusiasm and wears down their weapons. If they undertake a siege, their strength drains away.
Moreover, a country cannot meet the demands of a war that continues too long. Once the troops are exhausted, their weapons dull, their power spent, and their money gone, neighbouring rulers rise against the weakened state. Even the wisest person cannot put matters right then.
Speedy completion has sometimes brought victory to an unskilled commander; but no skilled commander has ever profited by dragging out a campaign.
Indeed, no country has ever gained by keeping a war going for a long time.
Anyone who fails to understand the harm war causes will gain nothing from it. A capable campaigner needs no second recruitment of troops, nor a third loading of his supply wagons.
We bring weapons and military equipment from our own country, but can obtain enough food for the troops by taking it from our opponent.
The greatest burden on a state's resources is supplying troops on faraway battlefields. When the fighting is distant, its citizens are reduced to poverty.
Prices rise wherever an army is nearby, consuming the money of both troops and followers. The state's treasury also runs dry, requiring repeated levies. Military strength ebbs, money disappears, and the citizen's household is stripped bare: altogether, he loses seven tenths of his income. Public property suffers too: chariots break, horses wear out, and armour, helmets, arrows, bows, spears, shields, pikes, fighting towers, wagons, and oxen are consumed or lost. In this way, six tenths of the government's revenue is used up.
A discerning commander therefore tries to feed his men from enemy supplies. A single bale of their rice is worth twenty brought in our own wagons; one bundle of their animal fodder is worth more than twenty of ours.
Men must be given encouragement to defeat their opponent.
Reward those who secure an advantage over the opposing side.
Reward the first men to seize more than ten enemy chariots. Replace the enemy flags on those vehicles with ours, and put the captured vehicles into service alongside our own.
Treat the warriors who come with them kindly. In this way, as the opposing side suffers defeat, our own forces become stronger.
The purpose of warfare is to win, rather than to conduct protracted campaigns, however expertly they are managed.
A capable general holds the people's lives in his hands and watches over the well-being of the country.
