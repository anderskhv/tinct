Master Sun gave this teaching.
The great commanders of old first put their own armies beyond defeat. Then they waited for their opponent to become vulnerable to certain defeat.
What brings defeat originates on our own side; what brings victory originates among our opponents.
Accomplished soldiers put defeat beyond possibility and also deprive their opponent of the ability to win.
Yet the written teaching warns that the circumstances needed to win may exist without always being available to us.
When winning is beyond reach, we defend our position; when success is assured, we go on the attack.
Shortage forces us to defend; abundance enables us to attack.
Expert defenders crouch concealed in the deepest darkness; expert attackers press onward to the very heights of heaven.
Follow these instructions, and the outcome will certainly be victory.
What ordinary people celebrate as a victory may not amount to real success. Winning a battle and hearing the kingdom declare, "You have done well," is not the highest achievement. Lifting an autumn fleece does not demonstrate strength. Eyes that can make out only the moon and sun have none of an eagle's keenness; hearing a thunderclap is no remarkable feat.
An old saying teaches that the accomplished soldier wins without desperate, blood-soaked encounters, and consequently gains no fame for cleverness or heroism. For him, entering battle means winning it: he attacks only after his opponent has already created the causes of his own defeat.
Furthermore, the capable soldier, secure in his own position, never allows the proper moment for attacking his opponent to slip away.
A successful army secures the conditions for winning before it goes looking for a fight.
An army headed for failure fights first, hoping that good fortune will make its weapons prevail.
The capable commander holds firmly to the "Way" and maintains the Law, thus directing the outcome himself.
The principles of warfare are said to proceed through five stages: the rule, the measure, the tables, the scales, and finally the prediction of success.
The rule surveys the territory; the measure establishes how much it produces; the tables record its inhabitants; the scales reveal their weight or quality. From this we can then work out whether the result will be success or failure.
On the scales, the army that will win outweighs the army that will lose as a beam outweighs a feather. When victorious troops attack, they resemble water long held back suddenly bursting down into deep valleys.
These are the arrangements that govern battle.
