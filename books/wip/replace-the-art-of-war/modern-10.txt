Master Sun gave this teaching.
Terrain can be divided according to the following different characteristics.
There is open terrain, broken terrain, suspended terrain, narrow passes, steep cliffs, and distant countries.
Open terrain permits both forces to move freely. Hurry to secure nearby heights, and give careful thought to the route by which supplies will come.
On broken terrain, moving forward is simple but withdrawing is hard. We may succeed here against an unprepared opponent. But if he is ready and beats us, leaving withdrawal impossible, catastrophe follows.
On suspended terrain, the first side to act puts itself at a disadvantage. Here we should refuse to advance even when the opponent holds out a temptation. Instead, pretend to withdraw and wait for him to commit half his troops; we can then strike to our own benefit.
Be prompt in securing narrow passes. Hold them with a strong garrison and wait for the opponent. If he has arrived first with a powerful force, avoid battle; if some points remain unoccupied, attack him.
In steep terrain, hurry to secure a sunlit height and wait for the opponent there. If he has arrived ahead of you, pull back rather than attack.
When your opponent is far away and your forces are equally matched, acting first puts you at a disadvantage.
These, then, are the six categories of terrain. Commanders have a duty to examine and understand them.
There are also six disasters that can afflict troops, caused neither by bad terrain nor by an absence of opportunity, but by their commander's incompetence.
They are being driven back, slackness, distress, breakdown of organisation, confusion, and complete flight.
Send troops against an opponent of equal fighting quality but with tenfold their numbers, and they come back beaten.
Powerful troops commanded by ineffectual officers bring slackness.
Competent officers commanding feeble troops bring distress.
Furious senior officers who attack without permission, refusing their general's authority because he fails to acknowledge their abilities, cause the organisation to break down.
A mild but ineffectual general creates confusion when his guidance and commands lack clarity, the duties of officers and men remain unsettled, and his arrangements contradict one another.
A commander causes his army to flee when he misjudges his opponent, pits a few against many or weakness against power, and fails to station selected troops at the head of his force.
These six conditions bring failure. A commander has a duty to examine them with care.
Terrain serves as an attendant to success.
A sound commander can judge the opposing force and plan how to win. He has an eye for slopes, positions that command the ground, and distances.
Understanding these matters brings success; failing to grasp them brings defeat.
If military judgment makes success certain, give battle even when your ruler has prohibited it.
If military judgment makes failure certain, withhold battle even when your ruler has ordered it.
A commander is a treasure of the state when he advances without seeking personal fame, or withdraws without regard for punishment, striving solely for the well-being of the people and the benefit of his ruler.
A worthy commander looks after his men, treating them with the affection he would give his children. They consequently follow him into deep valleys and remain at his side even in death.
Yet excessive concern can make men disobedient, excessive attention can leave them unfit for service, and excessive indulgence can breed disorder. Like children spoiled by their parents, they become impossible to employ.
A commander may trust his own troops, yet fail to realise that the opponent ought not to be attacked. Such a man cannot be sure of winning.
A commander may recognise a profitable chance to strike the opponent, yet fail to understand his own troops. He too cannot be sure of winning.
Trust in the troops and sound judgment about the moment to strike still leave success uncertain if knowledge of the terrain is lacking.
Once moving, a discerning soldier neither hesitates nor finds himself unable to decide what to do.
As the saying puts it: "Understand your own side; understand your opponent; then have no anxiety about winning."
Furthermore, full success is assured when the season and opportunity are recognised and the terrain is understood.
