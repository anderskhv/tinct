Master Sun gave this teaching.
Arrive on the battlefield before your opponent and wait there for him: this preserves your strength.
Arriving behind time and rushing forward to encounter the opponent wears you out.
An effective fighter arranges for his opponent to come towards him. He refuses to let the opponent lure him in turn.
He offers something that looks profitable, drawing the opponent into a position that will defeat him. He sets obstacles in the way of any action that could endanger his own arrangements.
Disturb an opponent who rests in pleasant quarters. Deprive him of supplies if he lives in abundance. If he calmly sits waiting to be attacked, make him leave his position.
You can accomplish this by appearing where he is absent and striking at points he did not expect.
By travelling through places our opponent does not occupy, we can cover a thousand leagues without wearing ourselves out.
Positions left undefended by the opponent always fall when we assault them. In defence, however, we must be powerful even at points where an assault seems unlikely.
An expert attacker leaves the opponent uncertain about what needs defending; an expert defender leaves him uncertain about what can be attacked.
The hidden methods of attacking cannot be grasped as readily as a definite shape or a sound perceived by the senses. Once these methods have been learned, however, the opponent comes under our control.
Our attacks meet no effective resistance because they strike his shortcomings. Our withdrawals leave him unable to follow because we move away too swiftly.
Suppose we wish for battle, but the opponent rests content behind tall walls and broad, deep moats. We then strike at another place whose relief will force him to emerge.
When we wish to avoid a fight, we hold a line without fortifications and keep the opponent uncertain, thereby stopping him from attacking.
Feigned movements keep the opponent guessing about what we are doing. They allow us to concentrate while compelling him to spread out.
Our troops form a single body, while his are scattered into ten portions. Our united body strikes those divided ten. We are numerous where he is few, and that numerical advantage saves our strength.
Keep the intended point of assault concealed. Without knowing where the blow will land, the opponent has to prepare on all sides, leaving him weak at every point.
Reinforcing the front requires him to weaken the back. Reinforcing the right reduces strength on the left; reinforcing the left reduces strength on the right.
Preparing at every point means weakness at every point. The breadth of the opponent's preparations makes him weaker and makes us stronger.
Once we settle where and when to attack, we can defeat the opponent even though he is one hundred leagues distant.
Without knowing the terrain and the moment, the troops in front cannot aid those behind, nor the left aid the right, the right aid the left, or the rear aid the front. Parts of an army may be forty leagues apart; by comparison, four or five leagues is near at hand.
Wu has fewer troops in its army than Yueh. But greater numbers do not necessarily ensure success, and so I maintain that victory can still be ours.
If our opponent has many men, stop him exploiting that advantage and find out his campaign plan. Stir him into action to learn the condition of his troops; make a feint to test his position's strength. Spread and beat your wings to reveal whether his resources are adequate. Repeated feints and forays can make us seem impossible to grasp, an impression that neither spying nor cleverness can remove.
The commander shapes his plans around the opponent's arrangements and sets his armies moving. The mass of men cannot understand his purpose: they perceive the indications of success, but cannot work out how it is achieved.
Do not use a plan again merely because it once brought success. Change your methods as circumstances change.
An army can be compared with flowing water.
Water abandons the heights, leaving them dry, and runs into low places. Troops turn away from power and strike at what is empty.
The contours of the ground direct the course that water takes. Acting to suit the opponent's condition is what brings military success.
Water has no predetermined shape; in the same way, the spirit governing warfare has no permanent form.
A commander who adapts his methods to his opponent and thus determines the outcome deserves the name God of war.
None of the five elements holds permanent precedence. The four seasons pass and return; days lengthen and shorten; the moon grows and diminishes. Warfare, too, has nothing permanently fixed.
