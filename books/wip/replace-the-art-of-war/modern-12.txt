Master Sun gave this teaching.
Fire can be used to attack in five ways.
The first burns the barracks; the second burns provisions; the third burns equipment; the fourth burns stores; and the fifth burns the company itself.
Choose a fitting moment for an assault with fire. Also keep the necessary equipment ready at all times.
Both the time and the day must suit the starting and execution of a fire assault. Choose a dry spell, and a day when the moon occupies the quarters of Chi, Pi, I, or Chen, the stars: such days bring wind.
Watch closely for the consequences that fire is sure to produce, and act in response. If flames erupt within the opposing camp, rush at it from outside as fast as you can. But if its troops stay quiet, hold back and refrain from attacking.
At the fiercest point of the blaze, let the opportunity determine whether you launch an assault or hold back.
When conditions favour it, ignite the opposing camp yourself instead of waiting for flames to appear within it.
If flames arise on the side facing the wind, avoid an assault from the sheltered side.
A wind beginning by daylight continues for a long time. One beginning after dark soon dies away.
Understand the distinctive features of all five forms of burning and examine the calendar. For fire to support the assault, it must be impossible to put out.
For water to support an assault, its flood must sweep everything before it.
Water can cut an opponent off or separate his forces, and flames can destroy his camp. Yet without securing victory or possession, you allow him to recover swiftly and invite calamity. Fighting continues without an end, consuming money.
Let a discerning ruler think carefully, and a capable commander keep the chief purpose before him. Take no action that offers no benefit. Commit no troops without a prospect of winning, and enter no battle unless the state faces danger.
A ruler's passing fit of passion is no reason to start a war. Anger in a commander's heart is no reason to enter battle.
Only undertake warfare when it can bring success. Act where a prospect of winning exists; where that prospect is absent, stay still.
Passion can give way to happiness, and anger can fade. But an overthrown country cannot be made whole again, nor can those who have died return to life.
The written teaching therefore calls for caution in a discerning ruler and attentiveness in a capable commander. With these, the country remains safe and its troops win their battles.
