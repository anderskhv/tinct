Master Sun gave this teaching.
The conduct of warfare involves the following kinds of ground.
Distracting ground, disturbing ground, contested ground, intersecting ground, ground crossed by roads, deeply involved ground, difficult ground, enclosed ground, and death ground.
Whenever a ruler campaigns within his own lands, the place is termed distracting ground.
A position just a little beyond the opponent's frontier is termed disturbing ground.
A position that benefits whichever army holds it is termed contested ground.
A position accessible to both armies is termed intersecting ground.
A position between three provinces, whose first occupier can control the peoples of the earth, is termed ground crossed by roads.
A position deep inside hostile territory, with many of its fortified towns behind you, is termed deeply involved ground.
Mountains, woodland, cliffs, ravines, marshes, swamps, and every other place hard to traverse are termed difficult ground.
A place with a tight entrance and twisting exit, where a few can resist many, is termed enclosed ground.
A place where lingering brings catastrophe is termed death ground.
Therefore avoid battle on distracting ground, and avoid delay on disturbing ground.
When your opponent holds contested ground, refrain from an assault.
On intersecting ground, keep the main roads open.
Where main roads meet, develop relations with others.
When deeply involved, requisition your opponent's goods and build up stores.
Leave difficult ground without delay.
On enclosed ground, resort to cunning.
On death ground, give battle.
The accomplished fighters of earlier times took care to sever their opponent's front from his rear. They separated his small detachments from his larger forces, stopped officers and men from helping one another, and sowed suspicion between higher and lower ranks. They dispersed his troops and kept them from gathering together; even when assembled, those troops lacked a common purpose.
Act when an opportunity to win exists; when success offers no prospect, hold your position.
If asked how to confront a formidable opposing force acting in unity, I would answer: seize something it holds dear, and its actions will follow our wishes.
More than anything else in war, rapid action keeps the troops' spirit alive. Strike before your opponent has prepared; come upon his unreadiness from a direction he does not anticipate.
Consider warfare abroad. Troops who are strangers in a distant land stand together and withstand defeat. Raid productive plains to feed the army. Look after the men's health, avoid wearing them out needlessly, bring their minds together, accumulate strength, and plan carefully in secret. When they have nowhere to find shelter, the men will not run from death.
When death is the only alternative, men make the greatest effort of which they are capable.
In places of desperation, troops cease to feel afraid.
Where the men have nowhere to find shelter, there is no hesitation.
Deep inside the opponent's territory, men are bound together.
When fighting cannot be avoided, the men fight with all their strength. They stay alert even without reminders, comply without being pressed, remain manageable without conditions, and trust and follow their commander without needing detailed instructions.
Forbid talk about portents and omens, and clear uncertainty from the men's minds. Then nothing will distract them, even up to death itself.
Soldiers are denied wealth, but not because money is wicked. They are forbidden old age, but not because a long life is wrong. Difficulty and peril are the soldier's rightful share.
When the command to attack comes, men sitting upright may have collars soaked in tears, while tears may run down the faces of those lying back. Yet, placed in desperation, these same men will show the bravery of Chu and Kuei in battle.
Use soldiers as the snakes of Mt. Chang act. A blow to a snake's head brings its tail lashing against you; a blow to its tail brings its head against you; a blow to its middle brings both ends against you at once.
If someone asks whether men can be made to act like these snakes, my answer is yes. Wu's men and Yueh's men detest one another. Yet let them share a boat across a river and encounter a storm: they will aid one another as one's two hands do.
You may tether the horses and sink the chariot wheels into mud, yet that alone will not stop men from fleeing.
Sound command produces shared bravery and a common purpose.
Using the terrain correctly brings out the finest performance from the weak as well as the strong.
An accomplished soldier can guide his army as one person guides another by the hand, because he puts it where its situation is desperate.
A commander should remain composed, unreadable, fair, and careful. He should leave officers and men unaware of his intentions and tell nobody of alterations or new directions. By shifting camps and choosing roundabout, surprising routes, he keeps others from guessing his designs.
Once his men have gathered for battle, the commander acts like someone pulling away the ladder from people standing on a roof. He enters the depths of hostile territory before revealing his intentions. He moves the troops back and forth like sheep in a flock, unaware of where they are going.
The commander should therefore gather his forces and put them in a situation of desperation.
Study the distinct qualities of the Nine Grounds, the fitting of methods to circumstances, and what lies in human hearts.
Deep within hostile territory, men hold together; at its edges, their attention is divided. Leaving home and passing beyond the frontier frees them from interference.
On distracting ground, bring the men's thoughts together.
On disturbing ground, remain in a single body.
On contested ground, attempt to get behind your opponent.
On intersecting ground, give the defences careful attention.
On ground crossed by roads, develop relations with others.
On deeply involved ground, take great care over provisions.
On difficult ground, avoid any delay.
On enclosed ground, shut off the way out.
On death ground, make the men see that survival offers no prospect.
Soldiers naturally defend themselves when encircled, fight vigorously when forced to do so, and chase their opponent when he withdraws.
A man ignorant of other rulers' ambitions cannot negotiate with them.
A man unfamiliar with wooded and mountainous country, cliffs, ravines, lakes, and marshland cannot lead an army through it.
A commander who employs no guides cannot turn the terrain to his benefit.
Without a thorough understanding of the Nine Grounds, a commander cannot achieve supremacy in war.
When a great commander attacks a mighty nation, he keeps its ruler from gathering the armies together.
He so intimidates his opponent that other countries cannot unite against him.
He neither competes for other countries' approval nor takes care to respect their rights. Trusting his own powers, he intimidates his opponent.
As a result, he readily captures the stronghold or brings the country under his authority.
When giving rewards or issuing commands, he is not constrained by rules inherited from the past.
He directs his troops as if they formed a single person.
Commands should guide the troops. Reveal to them what offers an advantage, but keep what is harmful hidden from them.
Throw troops into peril and they survive; put them on death ground and they can recover. It is the force facing danger that wins.
Match your opponent's movements to uncover his intentions. Once you know them, a single blow can kill his commander, even at a distance of one hundred leagues.
Upon declaring war, shut the barriers, destroy travel passes, stop hostile spies from passing through, and attend vigilantly to the affairs of government.
Exploit your opponent's vulnerability at once. Find out what he prizes most highly and make a plan to take it.
Let established principles and your opponent's circumstances determine how you plan.
Begin with a young maiden's careful discretion. Then, as soon as your opponent leaves an opening, spring into it with a rabbit's speed.
Your opponent will be unable to protect himself.
