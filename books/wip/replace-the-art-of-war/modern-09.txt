Master Sun gave this teaching.
Consider the placing of troops and watching of opponents during warfare in the mountains.
Pass across mountains, then make camp in valleys, choosing secure positions.
Station your troops on elevated ground, and keep clear of an opponent occupying the heights.
Now consider operations near water.
Once across a body of water, move away from it at once. If an opponent is fording a stream, do not fight him in the water itself: strike after half his troops have crossed. Avoid advancing against an opponent beside water; instead, station your men securely on elevated ground.
Avoid battle if your opponent stands between your own troops and the river's source.
Now consider operations in marshland.
Move rapidly through salt marshes and avoid remaining in their vicinity.
If circumstances force you to fight near a marsh, find a position with grass and water, and plenty of trees at your back.
On open land, station the troops in a suitable position with higher ground behind them to the right. Death will then lie ahead, while safety lies behind.
This is the conduct of warfare on level land.
Following these practices enabled Huangti to defeat four Princes in war.
Troops generally favour the heights over low ground. They favour places reached by sunlight over those left in shade.
Care for the men's health and place their camp on elevated, sunlit ground: this will keep sickness away and ensure a successful outcome.
Where the land rises, make camp before the rise on its sunlit side. This benefits the men and allows us to make profitable use of the terrain.
If rain upstream makes a river surge, wait for its flow to settle before attempting a crossing.
Leave steep, impassable valleys quickly; likewise hollows like wells, cramped places, tangled ground that cannot be penetrated, marshes and bogs, and constricted passages containing traps. Do not go near them. Draw the opponent towards these places while remaining clear yourself. Face towards them, leaving them behind your opponent.
If cliffs, ponds, lakes, beds of reeds and rushes, or dense woodland lie near the troops, examine them completely. Such places are likely to conceal an opposing force waiting in ambush.
An opponent who stays quiet despite being nearby draws confidence from strong natural protection.
A challenge issued from far away is intended to make you come forward.
An opponent who makes his camp out in open country has a particular purpose for doing so.
Stirring in the trees indicates an approaching opponent. Treat broken boughs and flattened grass that suggest a great force has passed as grounds for suspicion.
Birds suddenly taking wing indicate a hidden ambush.
Frightened animals indicate that hostile troops are creeping closer from more than one direction.
Upright jets of dust rising high indicate the approach of chariots.
Broad stretches of dust hanging low indicate that foot soldiers are approaching.
Scattered narrow pillars of dust rising high indicate men gathering fuel for fires and fodder for animals.
Little dust clouds travelling back and forth indicate preparations by the opposing force to make a temporary camp.
Pleasant talk combined with energetic preparations indicates that the opposing force intends to move forward and attack.
Boastful language accompanied by horsemen being urged ahead indicates that the opposing force intends to pull back.
Light chariots moving up towards the sides of the camp indicate that the opposing force is coming out for battle.
A sudden request to suspend hostilities, made without discussion, points to a concealed purpose.
Messengers travelling back and forth, along with troops taking formation, indicate that the opposing force has a move in preparation.
Moving forward and then abruptly pulling back is meant to entice an assault.
When opposing troops lean on their weapons for support, they lack food.
When men sent to fetch water drink straight from the river, their army is thirsty.
Failure to pick up plunder that is within easy reach indicates that the troops are worn out.
Birds gathering around a position indicate that no troops are holding it.
Calls heard after dark indicate fear in the camp.
Confusion among the troops indicates that their commander is not being heeded.
Shifts in the placement of standards and flags indicate unrest among the troops.
Officers become angry when their men are exhausted and respond slowly to commands.
Slaughtering horses to eat indicates that the opposing force is running short of food.
Cooking vessels suspended on the wall, with the men no longer turning in, indicate that the opposing force has exhausted its resources.
Overly warm and familiar behaviour by a commander indicates that his men no longer have confidence in him.
Rewards given again and again indicate the collapse of discipline.
Repeated penalties indicate that the commander finds himself in trouble.
A commander who starts by bullying and then becomes servile lacks understanding of the situation.
Anyone who presents apologies along with hostages is eager to obtain a truce.
If two forces keen to fight remain facing one another for a long while without moving forward or back, the situation calls for the greatest alertness and care.
A large number of men does not guarantee power.
Even troops unable to mount a rushing assault may win, provided they act together and have learned their opponent's condition.
Someone who dismisses his opponent without first thinking carefully is certain to become a prisoner.
When a commander unfamiliar to the troops punishes them, they stop following his orders. Troops who will not obey cannot be put to effective use.
Troops who know their commander but remain unmoved by the penalties he imposes are of no use either.
Kind treatment secures compliance; authority secures consistency. Together these bring us success in war.
People taught to obey from the outset give due respect to the instructions of their commander.
People who have not been taught obedience early fail to give their commander's instructions due respect.
Where commander and men understand one another, instructions are consistently carried out.
