# Review 2: blind read of the restored Symposium opening (ch. 1, ¶1.0–1.8)

- **Role:** blind reader and copy editor, no source access
- **Date:** 2026-09-25
- **Read:** only `scratchpad/blind/modern-extract.txt`. I did not consult any other Symposium translation, any repository file or the web.
- **Scope:** the new paragraphs 1.0–1.8, how they fit the existing 1.9–1.48, and the chapter 7 → chapter 8 boundary.

## Verdict: ACCEPT WITH CHANGES

The new opening reads well. It is idiomatic and easy to follow, and it sounds like the frame paragraphs that follow (1.9–1.15). It also fills a real gap. "Aristodemus" (1.11), "my informant" (1.32) and "people say I'm crazy" (1.9) now point back to something the reader has already met.

I found no contradictions of names, times or facts. The quotation marks open and close correctly in every new paragraph.

Three sentences should be fixed before release:
- the unmarked change of listener in 1.7 (finding 1)
- an ambiguous "his" in 1.7 (finding 2)
- the hard-to-parse nickname sentence in 1.8 (finding 3)

Everything else is optional polish or a note.

### Summary by question

1. **Clarity:** Almost every sentence is clear on first reading. One sentence needs restructuring (finding 3). The rest are small pronoun and idiom fixes (findings 2 and 4–13).
2. **Who speaks to whom, and when:** The three time layers follow each other clearly. First is now: Apollodorus speaking to his companion. Then two days ago: the walk with Glaucon. Then long ago: the party, as Aristodemus told it. One weak point is the silent return to the present inside 1.7 (finding 1). There are two pronoun snags (findings 2 and 5).
3. **Consistency:** The facts agree throughout (finding 18), and the join from 1.8 to 1.9 works (finding 17). One small wobble: 1.7 addresses several listeners, while the rest of the frame has one (finding 12).
4. **Style:** Punctuation, spelling and contractions match the frame paragraphs (finding 16). A few turns of phrase are dated (findings 3, 10 and 13).
5. **Anything missing or unexplained:** Nothing is missing. Small stumbles: "deme" (finding 10) and "your friend" before the reader knows who that friend is (finding 6). The "Phalerian! Halt!" joke works (finding 15).
6. **Chapter 7 → 8:** Both ends are natural break points (finding 20). There are some quotation-mark problems in the existing text just after the boundary (finding 21).

## Findings

### SHOULD-FIX

**1. SHOULD-FIX | ¶1.7, sentence 7 | The listener changes with no signal**
- Quoted: "So we walked along and talked about the speeches on love. That is why, as I said at the start, I'm well prepared to do what you ask, and I'll go through them again if you like."
- Problem: Up to this point in the paragraph, "you" has been Glaucon, and Glaucon has just asked for "the whole story." Here "you" becomes the present-day companion, and no words signal the switch. A reader can take "do what you ask" as Glaucon's request until "you rich men and businessmen" two sentences later. The quotation marks are correct. What is missing is a verbal cue.
- Suggested: "That is why, as I told you at the start, I'm well prepared to do what you ask — and if you like, I'll go through the speeches again, this time for you." If paragraph alignment with the companion edition allows, also start a new paragraph at "That is why," where the story returns to the present. If not, the wording cue alone is enough.

**2. SHOULD-FIX | ¶1.7, sentence 3 | "his" and "he" mean different people in one sentence**
- Quoted: "What's more, I have asked Socrates about the truth of some parts of his account, and he confirmed them."
- Problem: "his account" comes right after "Socrates," so a reader can take it as Socrates' own account, such as his own speech. Two words later, "he" does mean Socrates. The intended "his" is Aristodemus.
- Suggested: "What's more, I've asked Socrates himself about some parts of what Aristodemus told me, and he confirmed them."

**3. SHOULD-FIX | ¶1.8, sentence 3 | The nickname sentence is hard to parse**
- Quoted: "In this you live up to your old nickname, 'Apollodorus the madman' — though I don't know how you came by it, deserved as it is — for you're always raging against yourself and everybody except Socrates."
- Problem: An aside between dashes contains its own inverted clause ("deserved as it is"). It is then followed by a "for" clause whose link back to "live up to" is easy to lose. Most readers will need two passes. "Deserved as it is" is also the most old-fashioned phrase in the new text.
- Suggested: "In this you live up to your old nickname, 'Apollodorus the madman.' I don't know how you came by it, but you certainly deserve it, since you're always raging against yourself and everybody except Socrates."
- Shorter alternative: "No wonder people call you 'Apollodorus the madman.' I don't know how you got the name, but you've earned it — you're always raging at yourself and everyone except Socrates."
- Either version keeps the setup that 1.9 answers: "the reason people say I'm crazy" answers "I don't know how you came by it."
- Optional while editing: "except Socrates" ends both this sentence and the one before it. The repetition works as emphasis, but "all mankind" could become "everyone."

### OPTIONAL

**4. OPTIONAL | ¶1.0, sentence 3**
- Quoted: "Apollodorus, I was looking for you only just now."
- Problem: "Only just now" is a British idiom. It can be misread as "only now, and not before." It also repeats "only" from "only the day before yesterday" in the previous sentence.
- Suggested: "Apollodorus, I was just looking for you."

**5. OPTIONAL | ¶1.0, sentences 5–6**
- Quoted: "Phoenix, the son of Philip, described them to someone, who then told me; but his account was very vague. He did say that you knew, though, so I'd like you to give me an account of them."
- Problem: "his" and "He" could mean Phoenix or the unnamed go-between. The go-between is intended, as 1.1's "your informant" shows. "Account" also appears in both sentences.
- Suggested: "Someone told me what he'd heard from Phoenix, the son of Philip, but his account was very vague. He did say, though, that you knew the whole story, so I'd like to hear it from you." Making the go-between the subject settles both pronouns, and "the whole story" sets up Glaucon's line in 1.7.

**6. OPTIONAL | ¶1.0, sentence 7**
- Quoted: "Who better than you to report your friend's words?"
- Problem: The reader does not learn that Apollodorus is Socrates' friend until 1.3. Here "your friend" could just as well be Socrates, Alcibiades or Agathon.
- Suggested: "Who better than you to report what your friend Socrates said?"

**7. OPTIONAL | ¶1.3, sentence 2**
- Quoted: "since I got to know Socrates and made it my daily business to know everything he says and does"
- Problem: "Know" appears twice, in two different senses.
- Suggested: "since I began keeping company with Socrates and made it my daily business to know everything he says and does"

**8. OPTIONAL | ¶1.3, sentence 3**
- Quoted: "thinking I was well occupied"
- Problem: The phrase is a little stiff. It also misses a chance to set up the line in 1.7, "you think you're doing something when in reality you're doing nothing."
- Suggested: "thinking I was doing something worthwhile." The echo of 1.7 also sharpens "no better than you are now."

**9. OPTIONAL | ¶1.5**
- Quoted: "'When we were still boys,' I replied, 'when Agathon won the prize with his first tragedy — the day after he and his chorus had offered their victory sacrifice.'"
- Problem: The phrase after the dash can be read as dating the win rather than the gathering. That would put the win after the victory sacrifice, which is backwards, so the reader has to re-read.
- Suggested: "'When we were still boys,' I replied, 'at the time Agathon won the prize with his first tragedy. It was the day after he and his chorus offered their victory sacrifice.'" This still agrees with 1.13: "I turned down his invitation yesterday — his victory sacrifice."

**10. OPTIONAL | ¶1.7, sentence 1**
- Quoted: "'No indeed,' I replied, 'but the same man who told Phoenix — a little fellow who never wore shoes, Aristodemus, of the deme of Cydathenaeum."
- Problems:
  - The sentence is elliptical: the reader has to supply "it was" after "but."
  - The name comes third in a chain of descriptions.
  - Many general readers won't know the word "deme."
  - "No indeed" is dated.
- Suggested: "'No, not Socrates,' I replied. 'It was the same man who told Phoenix — Aristodemus of Cydathenaeum, a little fellow who never wore shoes." Keep "never wore shoes": it pays off in 1.12, where Socrates wearing sandals is "unusual."

**11. OPTIONAL | ¶1.7, sentence 4**
- Quoted: "'Then,' said Glaucon, 'let's have the whole story again."
- Problems:
  - "Again" implies Glaucon has already heard the full story, when he has only heard a vague version. It also repeats "I'll go through them again" three sentences later.
  - Glaucon's line starts inside Apollodorus's paragraph, right after "…and he confirmed them.'" In 1.1–1.6 each speaker gets a new paragraph, and the run-together "them.' 'Then," is a small visual stumble.
- Suggested: Cut "again," so the line reads "let's have the whole story." If paragraph alignment allows, give Glaucon's line its own paragraph. If not, the "said Glaucon" tag that follows at once keeps it readable.

**12. OPTIONAL | ¶1.7, sentence 9**
- Quoted: "especially the talk of you rich men and businessmen, it annoys me; and I pity you, my friends,"
- Problems:
  - "The talk of you rich men" can be misread for a moment as "talk *about* you rich men."
  - "My friends" is plural, but the rest of the frame treats the listener as one person: the label is the singular "COMPANION:", and 1.9 opens "Yes, friend."
- Suggested: "But any other kind of talk annoys me, especially the talk of rich men and businessmen like you; and I pity you, my friend, because you think you're doing something when in reality you're doing nothing." If the edition means Apollodorus to have several listeners, keep "my friends." Readers will then take COMPANION as their spokesman. That choice belongs to the edition.

**13. OPTIONAL | ¶1.7, sentence 10**
- Quoted: "I dare say you pity me in return, and think me an unhappy creature"
- Problem: "I dare say" and "think me an unhappy creature" are the most dated wording in this paragraph. "Creature" also repeats 1.3's "a thoroughly wretched creature."
- Suggested: "I suppose you pity me in return and think I'm miserable — and very probably you're right."

### NOTE

**14. NOTE | ¶1.0, sentence 1 | No speaker label**
- Quoted: "As for what you're asking about, I believe I'm well prepared to answer."
- This works without a label. The next sentence names the speaker ("'Apollodorus! …'"), and "COMPANION:" in 1.8 names the listener. For one sentence the reader is dropped mid-conversation, which seems intended. The words "as I said at the start" in 1.7 close the loop.
- Suggested: none. A label would be the way to help first-time readers further, but the text doesn't need one.

**15. NOTE | ¶1.0, sentence 2 | The playful greeting**
- Quoted: "'Apollodorus! You there — you Phalerian! Halt!'"
- A modern reader won't catch any wordplay here. Still, "called out playfully" and the earlier "my home in Phalerum" make it read as a mock-official order to stop. "So I did as I was told" completes the joke. Nothing is left unexplained.
- Suggested: none.

**16. NOTE | ¶1.0–1.8 | House style is consistent**
- Quotation marks: straight single quotes throughout, with no curly quotes and no double quotes. The marks balance in every new paragraph.
- Dashes and spelling: all 10 em-dashes are spaced, and spelling is American.
- Commas and names: the new text uses the serial comma ("Socrates, Alcibiades, and others," as in 1.40). "Phoenix, the son of Philip" follows the same pattern as "Eryximachus, the son of Acumenus."
- Contractions match the frame paragraphs that follow ("I'm" in 1.9, "I've" in 1.13, "let's" in 1.15).
- Register: The new text is a little more modern than 1.25 onward, as in "Isn't the road to Athens just made for conversation?" and "That's the difference." This suits the conversational frame and does not sound like a different voice. The few dated phrases are covered in findings 3, 10 and 13.
- Suggested: none.

**17. NOTE | ¶1.8 → ¶1.9 | The join works**
- Quoted: 1.8 "'Apollodorus the madman' … I don't know how you came by it" → 1.9 "Yes, friend, and the reason people say I'm crazy and out of my mind is precisely because I hold these views about you and about myself."
- "Madman" leads into "crazy and out of my mind." "I don't know how you came by it" is answered by "the reason people say…." "Raging against yourself and everybody" becomes "these views about you and about myself." In 1.10, "renew my request" also reaches back to "what you're asking about" in 1.0.
- Suggested: none.

**18. NOTE | ¶1.0–1.8 against 1.9 onward | The facts agree**
- **Chain of informants:** Aristodemus told Phoenix, Phoenix told a go-between, and the go-between told Glaucon (1.0, 1.7). Aristodemus also told Apollodorus, who checked parts with Socrates (1.7). Three existing references now have something to point back to: "the exact words of Aristodemus" (1.11), "said my informant" (1.32) and "nor do I recollect all that he related to me" (1.48).
- **Timing:** The party took place "the day after … their victory sacrifice" (1.5). That agrees with 1.13 ("I turned down his invitation yesterday — his victory sacrifice"), 1.25 ("I was looking for you yesterday") and 1.36 ("yesterday's potations").
- **Why Apollodorus wasn't there:** He was a boy at the time (1.5), and he has known Socrates for less than three years (1.3). Agathon has been away for years (1.3), which is why the party can't be recent.
- **"The day before yesterday":** This phrase appears in the frame (1.0, the walk with Glaucon) and inside the story (1.34, Agathon's victory). The two uses are far apart and in clearly different time frames, so readers should not be confused.
- **Names for the party:** "supper" (1.0), "gathering" (1.0 and 1.4, and the chapter title), "occasion" (1.1), "feast" (1.7) and, in the existing text, "banquet" (1.13). All clearly mean the same event.
- Suggested: none.

**19. NOTE (existing text that affects the new frame) | ¶1.14, ¶1.26**
- Quoted: 1.14 "'I'll do as you bid me,' I replied."; 1.26 "I turned round, but Socrates was nowhere to be seen."
- The new opening firmly makes the narrator, "I," Apollodorus, who was not at the party (1.1–1.3). The existing 1.12 tells Aristodemus's story in the third person ("He said that he met Socrates…"). Then 1.14 and 1.26 slip into Aristodemus's first person with no speaker tag. 1.11's "the exact words of Aristodemus" allows this, but a reader may briefly think Apollodorus is the one walking with Socrates.
- This is not a problem in 1.0–1.8. It is flagged for any later pass on the existing paragraphs, which could keep Aristodemus in the third person or tag the first "I."
- Suggested: none in this review.

**20. NOTE | Chapter 7 → chapter 8 | The boundary is natural**
- Chapter 7 ends on the formal close of Socrates' speech (7.68: "The words which I have spoken, you, Phaedrus, may call an encomium of love, or anything else which you please.").
- Chapter 8 opens on the change of scene ("When Socrates had done speaking, the company applauded…"). Alcibiades arrives within its first paragraph, which fits the chapter title.
- The applause could arguably close chapter 7, but starting chapter 8 with it gives a clean opening line for the new scene.
- Suggested: none.

**21. NOTE (existing text, outside scope) | Just after the chapter 7 → 8 boundary**
- Alcibiades' speech is split in the middle of a thought:
  - 8.0 ends "if I come in shall we have the understanding of which I spoke?" and does not close the quotation.
  - 8.1 is a one-line paragraph, "Will you drink with me or not?'", with no opening quotation mark.
- 7.68 continues the quotation left open at the end of 7.67 but also lacks an opening quotation mark.
- 8.0 uses the British spelling "revellers" (American: "revelers") and "ribands," an archaic word most readers won't know.
- No change is requested here. The paragraph splits may be fixed by paragraph alignment with the companion edition.

**22. NOTE (existing text, outside scope) | Quotation-mark problems elsewhere in chapter 1**
- 1.33 has doubled marks: "''I may touch you,' he said, '… until you had found what you sought.''"
- In the Homeric exchange, 1.20 ends with "who'," and 1.22 continues the speech ("But I shall say that I was bidden of you…") without any quotation marks.
- None of this is in the new text. It is listed so it can be sent to a separate cleanup.
