But his son hated him. James hated him for coming up to them, for stopping and looking down at them; for interrupting them; for the lofty exaltation of his gestures; for his magnificent head; for his demands and his egotism (for there he stood, insisting that they attend to him). Most of all, though, he hated the twang and twitter of his father’s emotion, which quivered all around them and upset the perfect simplicity and good sense of the way things were between him and his mother. He stared hard at the page, hoping that would make his father move on; he pointed his finger at a word, hoping to win back his mother’s attention, which, he knew with anger, wavered the moment his father stopped. But no. Nothing would make Mr. Ramsay move on. There he stood, demanding sympathy.

Mrs. Ramsay had been sitting loosely, with her son folded in her arm. She braced herself, and, turning halfway round, seemed to raise herself with an effort, and then at once to send pouring straight up into the air a shower of energy, a column of spray. At the same time she looked vivid and alive, as though every energy she had were being fused into power, burning and lighting everything up (though she sat quietly, picking up her stocking again). And into this delicious fruitfulness, this fountain and spray of life, the fatal sterility of the male drove itself like a beak of brass, barren and bare. He wanted sympathy. He was a failure, he said. Mrs. Ramsay’s needles flashed. Never taking his eyes off her face, Mr. Ramsay said again that he was a failure. She blew the words back at him. “Charles Tansley …” she said. But he needed more than that. What he wanted was sympathy: to be reassured, first of all, that he was a genius, and then to be drawn inside the circle of life, warmed and comforted, to have his senses given back to him, his barrenness made fruitful, and every room in the house filled with life—the drawing room; the kitchen behind it; the bedrooms above the kitchen; and past those, the nurseries. They must be furnished; they must be full of life.

Charles Tansley considered him the greatest metaphysician of the day, she said. But he needed more than that. He needed sympathy. He needed to be told that he, too, lived at the heart of life; that he was needed; not just here, but everywhere in the world. Needles flashing, upright and sure of herself, she conjured up drawing room and kitchen and made them all glow; she invited him to be at ease there, to come and go, to enjoy himself. She laughed; she knitted. James stood very stiffly between her knees and felt all her strength blaze up only to be swallowed and put out by the beak of brass, the parched scimitar of the male, which struck without mercy, over and over, demanding sympathy.

@@ 7.3
He was a failure, he said again. Well, look, then; feel, then. Her needles flashing, glancing all around—out of the window, into the room, at James himself—she reassured him past any shadow of doubt, with her laugh, her poise, her capability (the way a nurse carrying a lamp through a dark room calms a fretful child), that it was real: the house was full; the garden was blossoming. If he trusted her completely, nothing would harm him; however deep he buried himself, or however high he climbed, never for a second would he be without her. And as she boasted like this of her power to enfold and protect, there was hardly a shell of her own self left for her to recognize herself by; it had all been poured out and spent. And James, standing stiffly between her knees, felt her rise up like a fruit tree full of rosy flowers, thick with leaves and dancing branches, and into it the beak of brass, the parched scimitar of his father, that egotistical man, plunged and struck, demanding sympathy.

At last, filled with her words like a child who falls asleep contented, he looked at her with humble gratitude, restored and renewed, and said he would go for a stroll; he would go and watch the children play cricket. Off he went.

@@ 7.5
All at once Mrs. Ramsay seemed to fold up on herself, one petal closing inside another, and the whole fabric collapsed on itself, worn out. She had just strength enough to move her finger across the page of Grimm’s fairy tale, in an exquisite giving-way to exhaustion. And through her there throbbed the rapture of successful creation, like the pulse in a spring that has stretched to its widest and now softly stops beating.

@@ 7.6
As he walked away, every beat of this pulse seemed to enclose her and her husband together, and to give each of them the comfort that two different notes, one high and one low, seem to give each other when they are struck at once and blend. But as the resonance faded, and she went back to the Fairy Tale, Mrs. Ramsay felt not just tired in body (she always felt this afterward, never at the time); her physical tiredness was also tinged with some faintly unpleasant feeling that had a different source. Not that she knew exactly what it came from as she read aloud the story of the Fisherman’s Wife. Nor would she let herself put her discontent into words when she saw—at the turn of the page, when she paused and heard, dully, ominously, a wave fall—what its source was. It was this. She disliked feeling, even for a second, that she was finer than her husband; and, beyond that, she could not stand being less than entirely sure, when she spoke to him, that what she said was true. The universities and the people who wanted him, the lectures and the books, and how supremely important they all were—none of that did she doubt for a moment. What unsettled her was the relationship between them, and the way he came to her like that, in the open, where anyone could see. Because then people said he depended on her, when they must surely know that he was by far the more important of the two, and that what she gave the world was nothing next to what he gave it. But then there was the other thing too: that she could not tell him the truth. She was afraid, say, about the greenhouse roof and how much it would cost to repair, fifty pounds perhaps; and about his books, afraid he might guess what she half suspected, that his latest book was not quite his best (she had picked that up from William Bankes); and then there were the small everyday things she hid, and the children noticing, and the weight it put on them. All this took away from the whole joy, the pure joy, of the two notes sounding together, and made the sound die in her ear now with a dreary flatness.

There was a shadow across the page; she looked up. It was Augustus Carmichael shuffling by—just now, of all moments, when it was painful to be reminded that human relationships fall short, that even the most perfect one had flaws and could not survive the scrutiny that she, loving her husband and having an instinct for truth, turned on it; when it was painful to feel herself found guilty of unworthiness, and held back from doing what she was meant to do by these lies, these exaggerations.
