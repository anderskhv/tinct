@@ 34.0
Yes, that one is their boat, Lily Briscoe decided, as she stood at the edge of the lawn. It was the boat with the grayish-brown sails; she watched it flatten itself on the water now and shoot away across the bay. He’s sitting there, she thought, and the children still haven’t said a word. And she couldn’t reach him either. The sympathy she had held back from him lay heavy on her. It made painting hard.

@@ 34.1
She had always found Mr. Ramsay difficult. Never once, she remembered, had she managed to praise him to his face. Because of that, what was between them had been reduced to something neutral, lacking the element of sex that made him behave so gallantly, almost gaily, toward Minta. For Minta he would pick a flower, or hand over his books. But could he really have believed Minta read them? She used to drag them all over the garden, with leaves stuck in them for bookmarks.

@@ 34.2
“Do you remember, Mr. Carmichael?” Lily was tempted to ask, glancing at the old man. But his hat was pulled halfway down over his forehead. He was sleeping, she supposed, or dreaming, or just lying there catching words.

@@ 34.3
“Do you remember?” she was tempted to ask him as she went past, her mind going back again to Mrs. Ramsay on the beach: the cask bobbing up and down, the pages flying. After all these years, why had that scene lasted? There it was, ringed round and lit up, visible down to the smallest detail, while everything before it was blank and everything after it was blank, for miles and miles.

@@ 34.4
“Is it a boat? Is it a cork?” Mrs. Ramsay would say—Lily repeated the words as she turned back to her canvas, reluctant once more. Thank heaven the problem of space was still there, she thought, and took up her brush again. It glared at her. The entire mass of the picture balanced on that weight. The surface ought to be beautiful and bright, feathery and fleeting, each color melting into the next like the colors on a butterfly’s wing; but underneath, the fabric had to be clamped together with iron bolts. It should be something your breath could ruffle, and something a team of horses couldn’t shift. She began laying on a red, then a gray, and began shaping her way into the hollow there. At the same moment she seemed to be sitting next to Mrs. Ramsay on the beach.

@@ 34.5
“Is it a boat? Is it a cask?” Mrs. Ramsay asked, and started hunting about for her glasses. Once she had found them, she sat without a word, gazing out to sea. And as Lily painted steadily on, she had the feeling that a door had opened: you walked through it and stood looking silently about you in a lofty place like a cathedral, very dark and very solemn. Shouts reached them from a far-off world. On the horizon, steamers disappeared into stalks of smoke. Charles was throwing stones and making them skip.

@@ 34.6
Mrs. Ramsay said nothing. Lily thought she was glad to rest in silence, not talking; to rest in the deep obscurity of human relationships. Who knows what we are? What we feel? Even in a moment of intimacy, who can say, This is knowledge? Mrs. Ramsay might have asked (this silence beside her seemed to have happened so often) whether saying things didn’t spoil them. Weren’t we more expressive this way? At least the moment seemed extraordinarily fertile. She jabbed a little hole in the sand and then covered it over, as though burying the moment’s perfection there. It was like a drop of silver: you dipped into it, and it lit up the darkness of the past.

@@ 34.7
Lily took a step back to put her canvas—so—into perspective. This business of painting was a strange road to walk. You went out and out, farther and farther, until at last you seemed to be standing on a narrow plank over the sea, entirely alone. As she dipped her brush into the blue, she dipped into the past there as well. Now Mrs. Ramsay stood up, she remembered. Time to go back to the house—time for lunch. They all walked up from the beach together, with Lily behind beside William Bankes, and Minta ahead of them with a hole in her stocking. How that small round hole showing her pink heel seemed to flaunt itself at them! And how William Bankes disapproved of it, though as far as she could remember he never said a word! To him it stood for the end of womanhood—for dirt and mess, for servants giving notice and beds still unmade at noon—everything he hated most. He had a habit of shuddering and spreading his fingers wide as if to hide something ugly, and he did it now, holding his hand up in front of him. Minta walked on ahead, and Paul must have met her, and the two of them went off into the garden.

@@ 34.8
The Rayleys, Lily Briscoe thought, as she squeezed out her tube of green. She gathered together what she knew of them. She saw their lives as a string of scenes. One took place on the staircase at dawn. Paul had come home early and gone to bed; Minta was out late. And there she was on the stairs at around three in the morning, wreathed, made up, garish. Paul came out in pajamas with a poker, in case it was burglars. Minta stood halfway up by a window eating a sandwich in the deathly early light, and there was a hole in the carpet. But what did they say to each other? Lily wondered, as if looking hard enough would let her hear. Something violent. While he talked, Minta kept on eating her sandwich, which was maddening. His words were indignant and jealous; he abused her in a mutter so the children wouldn’t wake—the two little boys. He looked withered and drawn; she looked flamboyant and careless. After the first year or so, things had come loose; the marriage had turned out rather badly.

@@ 34.9
And this, Lily thought, loading her brush with green—this inventing of scenes about people—is what we call “knowing” them, “thinking” of them, “being fond” of them! None of it was true, not a word; she had invented it. Yet it was how she knew them, all the same. She kept tunneling on into her picture, into the past.

@@ 34.10
Then there was the time Paul said he “played chess in coffeehouses.” On that remark too she had built an entire structure of imagination. She remembered picturing it even as he spoke: he telephoned home, the maid answered, “Mrs. Rayley’s out, sir,” and so he made up his mind not to go home either. She saw him in the corner of some dismal place where smoke clung to the red plush seats and the waitresses came to know your face, playing chess with a small man in the tea trade who lived at Surbiton—and that was all Paul knew about him. Then Minta was out when he got home, and after that came the scene on the stairs, when he fetched the poker in case of burglars (and no doubt to scare her as well) and spoke so bitterly, telling her she had ruined his life. Anyway, when Lily went down to visit them at a cottage near Rickmansworth, the strain was horrible. Paul led her down the garden to see the Belgian hares he bred, and Minta came after them, singing, and laid her bare arm on his shoulder so that he wouldn’t tell Lily anything.

@@ 34.11
Hares bored Minta, Lily thought. But Minta never gave herself away. She would never say a thing like that about chess in coffeehouses; she was much too self-aware, much too careful. Still, to go on with their story—by now the dangerous stage was behind them. Lily had stayed with them for a while last summer, and the car broke down, and Minta had to pass him his tools. He sat in the road fixing it, and the way she passed him the tools—businesslike, direct, friendly—was proof that all was well now. They were not “in love” anymore. No: he had taken up with another woman, a serious woman who wore her hair in a braid and carried a briefcase (Minta described her with gratitude, almost with admiration). She went to meetings and shared Paul’s views, which had grown more and more pronounced, on taxing land values and on a capital levy. That alliance, far from wrecking the marriage, had put it right. Clearly they were excellent friends, he sitting in the road and she handing him his tools.

@@ 34.12
So that was the Rayleys’ story, Lily thought, smiling. She pictured herself telling it to Mrs. Ramsay, who would be eager to hear how the Rayleys had turned out. There would be a little triumph in it for Lily, telling Mrs. Ramsay that the marriage had not succeeded.

@@ 34.13
But the dead, Lily thought—she had hit some snag in her design that made her stop and consider, stepping back a foot or so—oh, the dead! she murmured. You pitied them, you pushed them aside, you even felt a little contempt for them. They are at our mercy. Mrs. Ramsay has faded away and gone, she thought. We can overrule her wishes and improve away her narrow, old-fashioned ideas. She draws further and further away from us. Lily seemed to see her, mocking, at the far end of the corridor of years, saying the most unsuitable thing imaginable: “Marry, marry!” (sitting bolt upright early in the morning, as the birds began to chirp in the garden outside). And you would have to tell her, It has all gone against your wishes. They’re happy that way; I’m happy this way. Life has changed completely. At that, all of her, even her beauty, turned for a moment dusty and out of date. For a moment Lily, standing there with the sun hot on her back and the Rayleys summed up, triumphed over Mrs. Ramsay. Mrs. Ramsay would never know how Paul went to coffeehouses and kept a mistress; how he sat on the ground while Minta handed him his tools; how Lily stood here painting, and had never married—not even William Bankes.

@@ 34.14
It had been Mrs. Ramsay’s plan. Had she lived, she might have forced it to happen. Even that summer he was already “the kindest of men.” He was “the first scientist of his age, my husband says.” And he was “poor William—it makes me so unhappy, when I go to see him, to find nothing nice in his house—no one to arrange the flowers.” So the two of them were sent off on walks together, and Lily was told—with that slight touch of irony that let Mrs. Ramsay slip through your fingers—that she had a scientific mind, that she was fond of flowers, that she was so precise. Why this mania for marrying people off? Lily wondered, moving back and forth in front of her easel.

@@ 34.15
(All at once, as suddenly as a star slides down the sky, a reddish light seemed to blaze up in her mind; it covered Paul Rayley and poured out from him. It went up like a bonfire lit by savages on some faraway beach to mark a celebration. She heard it roar and crackle. For miles around, the whole sea ran red and gold. A smell like wine was mixed into it and went to her head, because she felt once more her own reckless longing to throw herself off the cliff and drown hunting for a pearl brooch on a beach. And the roar and crackle filled her with fear and disgust and drove her back. It was as if, even while she saw how splendid and powerful it was, she also saw how it fed on the treasure of the house, greedily and disgustingly, and she loathed it. Yet as a spectacle, as a glory, it outshone anything she had ever experienced; year after year it burned like a signal fire on a desert island at the sea’s edge, and you had only to say “in love” for Paul’s fire to leap up again at once, as it did now. Then it died down, and she said to herself with a laugh, “The Rayleys”—how Paul went to coffeehouses and played chess.)

@@ 34.16
Still, she thought, it had been a narrow escape. She had been looking at the tablecloth when it came to her in a flash that she would move the tree to the middle, and that she need never marry anyone, and she had felt tremendous exultation. She had felt she could stand up to Mrs. Ramsay now—which shows what astonishing power Mrs. Ramsay had over you. She said, Do this, and you did it. Even her shadow in the window with James carried authority. Lily remembered how it had shocked William Bankes that she disregarded the significance of mother and son. Didn’t she admire their beauty? he asked. But William had listened, she remembered, with his wise child’s eyes, as she explained that she meant no irreverence: a light here called for a shadow there, and so on. She had no wish to belittle a subject that Raphael, they both agreed, had painted divinely. She wasn’t cynical. Far from it. Because he had a scientific mind, he understood—proof of an unselfish intelligence that had pleased and comforted her enormously. So it was possible, then, to talk seriously to a man about painting. In fact, his friendship had been one of the pleasures of her life. She loved William Bankes.

@@ 34.17
They used to go to Hampton Court, and he always gave her plenty of time to wash her hands, like the perfect gentleman he was, while he wandered along the river. Their whole relationship was like that; a great deal went unsaid. Afterward they strolled through the courtyards and, summer after summer, admired the proportions and the flowers. As they walked he would tell her about perspective and architecture, and he would stop to look at a tree, or at the view across the lake, or to admire a child (his great sorrow was that he had no daughter). He did it in the vague, detached way natural to a man who spent so long in laboratories that the world dazzled him when he came out. So he walked slowly, lifted a hand to shade his eyes, and stopped with his head thrown back just to breathe the air. Then he would mention that his housekeeper was away on vacation and he needed to buy a new stair carpet. Would she perhaps come with him to buy a new stair carpet? And once, for some reason, the talk turned to the Ramsays, and he told her that when he first saw Mrs. Ramsay she was wearing a gray hat; she could not have been more than nineteen or twenty. She was astonishingly beautiful. He stood there gazing down the avenue at Hampton Court as though he could see her among the fountains.

@@ 34.18
Lily looked at the drawing-room step now. Through William’s eyes she saw the shape of a woman, quiet and peaceful, her eyes cast down. She sat lost in thought, pondering (she was wearing gray that day, Lily thought). Her gaze was lowered. She would never raise it. Yes, thought Lily, looking hard, I must have seen her look that way—but not in gray, and not so still, or so young, or so peaceful. The figure came easily enough. She was astonishingly beautiful, William said. But beauty wasn’t everything. Beauty had this price: it came too easily and too completely. It stopped life still—froze it. You forgot the small agitations: the flush, the paleness, some strange twist, some light or shadow that made the face unrecognizable for an instant and yet added something you saw in it ever after. It was simpler to smooth all that away beneath beauty. But what did she look like, Lily wondered, when she clapped her deerstalker hat on her head, or ran across the grass, or scolded Kennedy, the gardener? Who could tell her that? Who could help her?

@@ 34.19
Without meaning to, she had surfaced and found herself half out of the picture, gazing a little dazed, as if at things that weren’t real, at Mr. Carmichael. He was lying in his chair, hands clasped on top of his belly—not reading, not asleep, but basking like some creature stuffed full of existence. His book had dropped onto the grass.

@@ 34.20
She wanted to walk straight over and say, “Mr. Carmichael!” He would look up kindly, as he always did, out of his vague, smoky green eyes. But you only woke people when you knew what you wanted to tell them. And what she wanted to say was not one thing but everything. Small words that chopped the thought up into pieces said nothing at all. “About life, about death; about Mrs. Ramsay”—no, she thought, you could say nothing to anybody. The urgency of the moment always missed. Words fluttered off to the side and struck inches below the target. Then you gave up, and the idea sank back down, and you turned into what most middle-aged people become: cautious, secretive, with lines between the eyes and a look of permanent anxiety. Because how could you put these feelings of the body into words? How could you express that emptiness there? (She was looking at the drawing-room steps. They looked extraordinarily empty.) It was the body that felt it, not the mind. The physical sensations that came with the bare look of those steps had all at once turned extremely unpleasant. Wanting and not having sent a hardness, a hollowness, a strain right through her body. And then wanting and not having—wanting and wanting—how it wrung the heart, over and over! Oh, Mrs. Ramsay! she cried out without a sound, to that essence sitting by the boat, that abstraction made out of her, that woman in gray—as if to scold her for having gone, and then, having gone, for coming back. Thinking of her had seemed so safe. Ghost, air, nothing, something you could toy with easily and safely at any hour of the day or night—that was what she had been. And then suddenly she reached out her hand and wrung the heart like this. Suddenly the empty drawing-room steps, the frill of the chair inside, the puppy tumbling about on the terrace, the whole wave and whisper of the garden, turned into curves and arabesques flourishing around a center of total emptiness.

@@ 34.21
“What does it mean? How do you explain it all?” she wanted to ask, turning again to Mr. Carmichael. At this early hour of the morning the whole world seemed to have melted into a pool of thought, a deep basin of reality, and it was almost possible to imagine that if Mr. Carmichael spoke, a small tear would rip across the pool’s surface. And then what? Something would rise up out of it. A hand would push up; a blade would flash. Nonsense, of course.

@@ 34.22
She had an odd idea that he did hear, after all, the things she couldn’t say. He was an unreadable old man, with his yellow-stained beard and his poetry and his puzzles, sailing calmly through a world that met all his needs; it seemed to her that he had only to lower his hand where he lay on the lawn to fish up whatever he wanted. She looked at her picture. That, presumably, would have been his answer: how “you” and “I” and “she” pass and vanish; nothing lasts; everything changes; but not words, not paint. And yet it would be hung in the attics, she thought; it would be rolled up and tossed under a sofa. Even so, even of a picture like that, it was true. You could say of this scrawl—perhaps not of the actual picture, but of what it tried to do—that it “remained forever.” That was what she was going to say; or rather, since the words sounded too boastful even to her once spoken, she would hint it without words. But when she looked at the picture, she was surprised to find she couldn’t see it. A hot liquid filled her eyes (at first she didn’t think of tears); it left her lips firm, but it thickened the air and ran down her cheeks. In every other way she was in complete control of herself—oh yes! Was she crying for Mrs. Ramsay, then, without being aware of any unhappiness? She turned to old Mr. Carmichael again. What was it, then? What did it mean? Could things push their hands up and grab you? Could the blade cut, the fist clench? Was there no safety? No learning the world’s ways by heart? No guide, no refuge—was it all miracle, and leaping off the top of a tower into the air? Could it be that, even for older people, this was life?—startling, unexpected, unknown? For one moment she felt that if the two of them stood up, here and now on the lawn, and demanded an explanation—why was it so short, why so impossible to explain—and demanded it violently, the way two fully equipped human beings might speak who should have nothing hidden from them, then beauty would roll itself up; the space would fill; those empty flourishes would take shape; if they only shouted loudly enough, Mrs. Ramsay would come back. “Mrs. Ramsay!” she said out loud. “Mrs. Ramsay!” Tears were streaming down her face.
