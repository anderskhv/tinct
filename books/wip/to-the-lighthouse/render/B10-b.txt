@@ 33.0
Over their heads the sails flapped. Water chuckled against the boat and slapped its sides; the boat itself lay drowsing in the sun, not moving. Every so often a little breeze got into the sails and they rippled, but the ripple ran across them and died away. There was no motion at all. In the middle of the boat sat Mr. Ramsay. Any moment now he would get impatient, James thought, and so did Cam, both of them watching their father where he sat between them in the middle (James had the tiller; Cam was alone in the bow), his legs curled up tight. Hanging about was something he hated. And sure enough, after a second or two of fidgeting, he spoke sharply to Macalister’s boy, who brought out his oars and started rowing. But they knew their father would never be content until the boat was flying. He would go on watching for a breeze, fidgeting, muttering things that Macalister and Macalister’s boy would overhear, and both of them would be made horribly uncomfortable. He had made them come. He had forced them to. Angry, they hoped no breeze would ever rise, hoped he would be thwarted in every way possible, because he had forced them to come when they did not want to.

@@ 33.1
The whole way down to the beach the two of them had dragged along behind, together and silent, though he kept calling to them, “Walk up, walk up.” Their heads were bowed, pushed down as if by some relentless gale. Speak to him? That they could not do. Come they must; follow they must; walk behind him they must, loaded with brown paper parcels. Yet as they walked they made a silent vow: they would stand by each other and keep the great compact—to resist tyranny to the death. So they would sit there in silence, one at each end of the boat. Not a word would they say. They would only glance at him from time to time, sitting there with his legs twisted, frowning, fidgeting, pishing and pshawing, muttering to himself, impatient for a breeze. And they hoped for calm. They hoped for him to be thwarted. They hoped the whole trip would be a failure, and that they would have to go back to the beach, parcels and all.

@@ 33.2
But now, once Macalister’s boy had rowed them out a little way, the sails swung slowly round; the boat came alive, flattened itself, and shot away. Mr. Ramsay at once uncurled his legs, as though some great tension had been let go. He took out his tobacco pouch and, with a little grunt, passed it to Macalister, and they knew that he felt perfectly content, whatever they themselves might be suffering. For hours now they would sail along like this. Mr. Ramsay would put some question to old Macalister—probably about the great storm last winter—and old Macalister would answer, and the two men would puff away at their pipes, and Macalister would take up a tarry rope and work at some knot, tying or untying it, and the boy would fish without saying a word to anybody. It would be James’s job to watch the sail every moment. If he forgot, the sail puckered and shivered, the boat lost speed, and Mr. Ramsay would say sharply, “Look out! Look out!” and old Macalister would slowly turn round on his seat. Sure enough, they heard Mr. Ramsay ask something about the great storm at Christmas. “She comes driving round the point,” old Macalister said. He was describing last Christmas’s great storm, when eleven ships had been driven into the bay to shelter, and he had seen “one there, one there, one there” (pointing slowly round the bay, while Mr. Ramsay turned his head to follow). Three men he had seen, clinging to the mast. Then she was gone. “And at last we shoved her off,” he went on. (The children, though, caught only the odd word in their anger and silence, sitting at opposite ends of the boat, bound together by their compact to fight tyranny to the death.) At last they had shoved her off; the lifeboat was launched, and they had got her out beyond the point—so ran Macalister’s story. And though only a word here and there reached them, they were conscious of their father the whole time. They saw how he leaned in, how his voice fell into tune with Macalister’s; how he puffed at his pipe and looked this way and that where Macalister pointed, savoring the thought of the storm, the dark night, the fishermen struggling out there. He liked the idea of men laboring and sweating at night on a windy beach, setting muscle and brain against wind and waves; he liked men to work that way, and women to keep house and sit indoors beside sleeping children while out in the storm the men drowned. James could tell it, and so could Cam (they looked at him; they looked at each other). They could tell from the toss of his head, from how alert he was, from the ring in his voice and the faint Scottish accent that crept into it and made him seem a peasant himself, as he questioned Macalister about the ten ships driven into the bay by a storm. Three had gone down.

He gazed proudly where Macalister was pointing; and Cam, proud of him without quite knowing why, thought that if he had been there, he would have launched the lifeboat, he would have got to the wreck. He was so brave, Cam thought, so adventurous. But then she remembered. There was the compact: to resist tyranny to the death. Their grievance lay heavy on them. They had been forced; they had been ordered about. Once again he had flattened them with his gloom and his authority. On this fine morning he had made them do as he said—come, because he wanted it, with these parcels, to the Lighthouse—and join in those rituals he performed, for his own satisfaction, in memory of the dead. They hated them. So they trailed behind him, and the whole day’s pleasure was ruined.

@@ 33.4
Yes, the breeze was getting stronger. The boat heeled over; the water was cut sharply and fell away in green cascades, in bubbles, in waterfalls. Cam gazed down into the foam, into the sea and all the treasure it held, and its speed put her in a trance, and the bond between her and James went slack a little. It loosened slightly. How fast it goes, she began to think. Where are we going? The motion entranced her, while James steered grimly, his eye on the sail and the horizon. Yet as he steered, a thought began to come to him: he might get away; he might be done with all of it. They might land somewhere, and then be free. For a moment the two of them looked at each other and felt a lift of escape and exhilaration, from the speed and the change. But the breeze roused that same excitement in Mr. Ramsay, too. As old Macalister turned to cast his line over the side, he cried out loud, “We perished,” and then again, “each alone.” Then, in his usual spasm of shyness or remorse, he caught himself up and waved a hand at the shore.

@@ 33.5
“See the little house,” he said, pointing; he wanted Cam to look. Unwillingly she sat up and looked. But which one was it? Up there on the hillside, she could no longer tell which house was theirs. Everything seemed far off, peaceful, strange. The shore looked delicate, remote, unreal. The short way they had sailed had already set them at a great distance from it and given it that altered, settled look of something slipping away, something you no longer have any part in. Which house was theirs? She could not find it.

@@ 33.6
“But I beneath a rougher sea,” Mr. Ramsay murmured—a line from Cowper’s “The Castaway,” about a sailor drowned alone. He had found the house, and seeing it, he had seen himself there too, pacing the terrace alone. Up and down between the urns he was walking, and to himself he seemed very old and stooped. There in the boat he stooped, too, and hunched down, instantly playing his role—the role of a lonely man, widowed and bereft. That way he could summon up crowds of people who pitied him. Sitting in the boat, he put on a little play for himself, one that called for frailty and weariness and grief from him (he held up his hands and looked at how thin they were, to confirm the dream). Then, in abundance, women’s sympathy was handed to him, and he imagined how they would comfort him and feel for him. And catching in his dream some glimmer of the exquisite pleasure that women’s sympathy gave him, he sighed, and said softly and sadly,

@@ 33.9
Cam, though, could see nothing. She was thinking how all those paths and the lawn, so thick and tangled with the lives they had lived there, were gone—rubbed out, over, unreal—and that this was what was real now: the boat, and the sail with its patch; Macalister with his earrings; the sound of the waves. All this was real. As she thought it, she was murmuring to herself, “We perished, each alone,” because her father’s words kept breaking in her mind and breaking again. Then her father saw her staring so vaguely and started to tease her. Didn’t she know the points of the compass? he asked. Couldn’t she tell north from south? Did she honestly think they lived way out there? He pointed once more and showed her where their house stood, there, beside those trees. He did wish she would try to be more precise, he said. “Tell me—which is east, which is west?” he said, half laughing at her and half scolding, for he simply could not grasp how anyone who was not a complete idiot could fail to know the points of the compass. But she did not know them. And when he saw her staring, her eyes vague and by now rather frightened, fixed on a spot where there was no house, Mr. Ramsay forgot his dream: the pacing up and down between the urns on the terrace, the arms held out to him. Women are always like that, he thought; their minds are hopelessly vague; he had never been able to understand it, but that was how it was. His wife had been like that too. Nothing would stay fixed clearly in their heads. But it had been wrong of him to get angry with her; and besides, didn’t he rather like this vagueness in women? It was part of their extraordinary charm. I will make her smile at me, he thought. She looks scared. She was so quiet. He gripped his fingers together and made up his mind that his voice and his face and all those quick, expressive gestures that for so many years had been at his command for making people pity and praise him should all be held in check. He would get her to smile at him. He would think of something simple and easy to say to her. But what? Wrapped up as he was in his work, he had forgotten the kind of thing people said. There was a puppy. They had a puppy. Who was taking care of the puppy today? he asked. Yes, thought James without pity, seeing his sister’s head against the sail, now she’ll give in. I’ll be left to fight the tyrant on my own. The compact would be his alone to keep. Cam would never resist tyranny to the death, he thought grimly, watching her face—sad, sulky, giving way. Sometimes a cloud settles on a green hillside and a heaviness comes down, and there, among all the hills around, is gloom and sorrow, and it seems as though the hills themselves must be brooding over the fate of the one that is clouded and darkened, either pitying her or maliciously enjoying her dismay. That was how overcast Cam felt now, sitting among calm, determined people, wondering how to answer her father about the puppy; how to hold out against his plea—forgive me, care for me—while James the lawgiver, the tablets of eternal wisdom open on his knee (to her, his hand on the tiller had become a symbol), said, Resist him. Fight him. And he was right to say it; he was just. For they must fight tyranny to the death, she thought. Of all human virtues, justice was the one she held most sacred. Her brother was the most godlike, her father the most imploring. And which of them was she yielding to, she wondered, sitting between the two, gazing at a shore whose landmarks were all strange to her, and thinking how the lawn and the terrace and the house were smoothed away now, and peace lived there.

And what name would she give him? her father kept on. When he was a small boy he had had a dog called Frisk. She’s going to give in, James thought, watching a certain look come over her face, a look he remembered. They look down at their knitting, or something, he thought. Then all at once they look up. He remembered a flash of blue, and then someone sitting with him laughed and gave in, and he was furious. It must have been his mother, he thought, in a low chair, with his father standing over her. He began to hunt through the endless series of impressions that time had laid down on his brain, softly, ceaselessly, leaf upon leaf, fold upon fold: scents, sounds; voices, harsh, hollow, sweet; lights going by, and brooms tapping; the wash and hush of the sea; how a man had marched up and down and stopped dead, standing upright over them. Meanwhile, he noticed, Cam was dabbling her fingers in the water, staring at the shore, saying nothing. No, she won’t give in, he thought; she’s not like that. Well, then, if Cam would not answer, he would leave her alone, Mr. Ramsay decided, and felt in his pocket for a book. But she did want to answer him. She longed, passionately, to shift whatever lay on her tongue and blocked it, and to say, Oh yes, Frisk. I’ll call him Frisk. She even wanted to ask, Was that the dog that found its own way across the moor? But however hard she tried, she could think of nothing like that to say—something fierce and faithful to the compact, yet which would pass her father, without James suspecting, a secret sign of the love she felt for him. For as she dabbled her hand (and just then Macalister’s boy caught a mackerel, and it lay kicking on the bottom of the boat with blood on its gills)—for as she looked at James, who kept his eyes on the sail without any feeling, or now and then glanced at the horizon for a second, she thought: you aren’t exposed to it—this pressure, this split feeling, this extraordinary temptation. Her father was searching his pockets; another second and he would have his book. For nobody drew her more than he did. His hands were beautiful to her, and his feet, and his voice, his words, his hurry, his temper, his strangeness, his passion, the way he would say straight out in front of everybody, we perish, each alone, and his remoteness. (He had opened his book.) But what was still unbearable, she thought, sitting up straight and watching Macalister’s boy pull the hook from another fish’s gills, was that crude blindness and tyranny of his. It had poisoned her childhood and stirred up bitter storms, so that even now she would wake at night shaking with rage, remembering some command of his, some insolence: “Do this,” “Do that”; his domination; his “Submit to me.”

So she kept silent, and looked at the shore stubbornly and sadly. It lay wrapped in its mantle of peace, as if the people there had fallen asleep, she thought—as if they were free like smoke, free to drift in and out like ghosts. There is no suffering there, she thought.
