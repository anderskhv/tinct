@@ 18.0
As usual, Lily thought. At that exact moment there was always something that had to be done — something Mrs. Ramsay, for private reasons, had decided to do at once, even if, as now, everyone was standing around making jokes, unable to make up their minds whether to go to the smoking-room, the drawing-room or up to the attics. And then, in the middle of all the noise, you would see Mrs. Ramsay standing there with Minta’s arm in hers, suddenly recollect, “Yes, it’s time for that now,” and slip off at once, secretive, to do something by herself. The minute she left, things began to fall apart. People drifted, hesitated, went their separate ways. Mr. Bankes took Charles Tansley by the arm and headed for the terrace to finish the political argument they had started over dinner, and that shifted the whole balance of the evening, tipped its weight another way. Watching them go, and catching a word or two about Labour Party policy, Lily felt as though the two men had climbed up to the ship’s bridge to take their bearings; that was how the switch from poetry to politics struck her. So off they went, Mr. Bankes and Charles Tansley, while the rest stood and watched Mrs. Ramsay climbing the stairs alone in the lamplight. Where, Lily wondered, was she off to in such a hurry?

@@ 18.1
In fact she was not running or hurrying at all; if anything she went rather slowly. After all that talk, she felt like standing still for a moment and picking out one particular thing — the thing that mattered — detaching it, setting it apart, cleaning off all the emotions and odds and ends that clung to it, so that she could hold it up in front of her and bring it before the court where the judges she had appointed to decide such matters sat assembled in council. Is it good, is it bad, is it right or wrong? Where are we all heading? and so on. In this way she recovered her balance after the shock of what had happened, and without knowing it, and rather absurdly, she used the elm branches outside to help steady herself. Her world was changing; the trees were still. The event had given her a feeling of motion. Everything had to be put in order. That must be set right, and that, she thought, silently admiring how dignified the trees were in their stillness, and then again the magnificent upward sweep of the elm branches (like a ship’s prow riding up a wave) when the wind lifted them. For there was a wind (she paused to look out). There was a wind, so that every so often the leaves brushed aside to uncover a star, and the stars themselves seemed to tremble and dart light, straining to flash out between the leaves’ edges. Yes, then, that was done, finished; and like everything finished, it had turned solemn. Thought of now, stripped of chatter and emotion, it seemed to have been there forever and only now revealed; and in being revealed it fixed everything firmly in place. No matter how long they lived, she thought, walking on, they would return to this night, this moon, this wind, this house — and to her as well. It flattered her, in the place where she was most open to flattery, to think that however long they lived she would be woven into their hearts, wound among them; and this, and this, and this, she thought as she climbed, laughing — but lovingly — at the sofa on the landing (her mother’s), at the rocking-chair (her father’s), at the map of the Hebrides. All of it would be brought back to life in Paul’s and Minta’s lives. “The Rayleys” — she tried out the new name. And with her hand on the nursery door she felt that sense of shared feeling with others that emotion brings, as though the dividing walls had worn so thin that practically (it was a feeling of relief and happiness) everything flowed in one stream; the chairs, the tables, the maps were hers, were theirs, whose did not matter; and Paul and Minta would carry it on after she was dead.

@@ 18.2
Firmly, so it wouldn’t squeak, she turned the handle and went in, her lips pressed together a little as though reminding herself not to talk out loud. But the instant she was inside she saw, irritated, that there had been no need for care. The children were awake. It was really too annoying. Mildred ought to be more careful. James was wide awake, Cam was sitting bolt upright, Mildred was out of bed in her bare feet, it was nearly eleven, and every one of them was talking. What was wrong? That horrid skull again. She had asked Mildred to move it, but of course Mildred had forgotten, and now here were Cam and James, both wide awake and squabbling, when they should have been asleep hours ago. Whatever had made Edward send them this horrid skull? It had been silly of her to let them nail it up there. It was nailed on tight, Mildred said, and Cam couldn’t get to sleep with it in the room, and James screamed if anybody touched it.

@@ 18.3
Then Cam must go to sleep (it had great big horns, Cam said) — must go to sleep and dream about lovely palaces, said Mrs. Ramsay, sitting down beside her on the bed. Cam said she could see the horns all over the room. And so she could. Wherever they set the light (and James couldn’t sleep without one), a shadow fell somewhere.

@@ 18.4
“But think, Cam, it’s just an old pig,” Mrs. Ramsay said, “a nice black pig, like the ones at the farm.” Cam, though, thought it was a horrid thing, sending its branches at her from every corner of the room.

@@ 18.5
“All right, then,” said Mrs. Ramsay, “we’ll cover it up.” They all watched as she went to the chest of drawers and pulled open the little drawers one after another, fast; and when nothing in them would do, she whipped off her own shawl and wrapped it around the skull, round and round and round. Then she went back to Cam, laid her head almost flat on the pillow next to Cam’s, and told her how lovely it looked now; how the fairies would love it; it was like a bird’s nest; it was like a beautiful mountain of the kind she had seen abroad, with valleys and flowers and bells ringing and birds singing and little goats and antelopes. … As she said the words, rhythmically, she could see them echoing in Cam’s mind; Cam was saying after her that it was like a mountain, a bird’s nest, a garden, and there were little antelopes, and her eyes kept opening and closing. And Mrs. Ramsay kept on, ever more monotonous, ever more rhythmic and nonsensical, telling her to shut her eyes and sleep and dream of mountains and valleys and stars falling and parrots and antelopes and gardens and everything lovely, she said — lifting her head very slowly and speaking more and more mechanically — until she was sitting upright and could see that Cam had fallen asleep.

@@ 18.6
Now, she whispered, going across to his bed, James had to go to sleep too, because look, she said, the boar’s skull was still there; nobody had touched it; they’d done exactly what he wanted; there it was, not hurt at all. He checked that the skull really was still under the shawl. But there was something else he wanted to ask her. Would they be going to the Lighthouse tomorrow?

@@ 18.7
No, she said, not tomorrow, but soon, she promised — the next fine day. He was very good about it. He lay down, and she pulled the covers over him. But he would never forget, she knew; and she felt angry with Charles Tansley, with her husband and with herself, because she was the one who had raised his hopes. Then, reaching for her shawl and remembering that it was wrapped around the boar’s skull, she stood up and lowered the window another inch or two. She heard the wind and breathed in the chill night air, which did not care at all; she murmured good night to Mildred, left the room, let the latch-tongue slide slowly out into the lock, and was gone.

@@ 18.8
She hoped he wouldn’t go banging his books on the floor above their heads, she thought, still dwelling on how irritating Charles Tansley was. Neither child was a good sleeper; they were excitable, and a man who could say such things about the Lighthouse seemed to her quite likely to knock over a pile of books just as they were dropping off, clumsily sweeping them off the table with his elbow. She assumed, that is, that he had gone up to work. And yet he looked so forlorn; and yet it would be a relief when he left; and yet she would see to it that he was treated better tomorrow; and yet he was wonderful with her husband; and yet his manners really did need improving; and yet she liked the way he laughed — and as she thought this, coming down the stairs, she noticed that the moon itself was now visible through the staircase window, the yellow harvest moon, and she turned; and they looked up and saw her standing above them on the stairs.

@@ 18.9
“That’s my mother,” Prue thought. Yes; Minta ought to look at her; Paul Rayley ought to look at her. There is the thing itself, she felt, as if there were only one such person in the whole world — her mother. A moment ago, chatting with the others, she had been completely grown-up; now she turned into a child again, and what they had been up to was a game, and she wondered whether her mother would allow their game or forbid it. And thinking what a chance this was for Minta and Paul and Lily to see her, and feeling what an amazing piece of luck it was that she herself had her, and that she would never grow up and never go away from home, she said, like a child, “We thought we’d go down to the beach and watch the waves.”

@@ 18.10
All at once, for no reason whatever, Mrs. Ramsay was like a girl of twenty, brimming with fun. A holiday mood seized her out of nowhere. Of course they must go; of course they must go, she cried, laughing; and running quickly down the last three or four stairs, she turned from one of them to the next, laughing, pulling Minta’s wrap around her, saying how she only wished she could come too — and would they be very late, and did any of them have a watch?

@@ 18.11
“Yes, Paul does,” Minta said. Out of a small chamois-leather case Paul slid a beautiful gold watch to show her. And holding it out on his palm in front of her, he felt, “She knows everything. I don’t need to say a word.” What he was saying to her, as he showed her the watch, was, “I’ve done it, Mrs. Ramsay. I owe it all to you.” And looking at the gold watch lying in his hand, Mrs. Ramsay felt: What extraordinary luck Minta has! She’s marrying a man who keeps his gold watch in a chamois-leather bag!

@@ 18.12
“Oh, how I wish I could come with you!” she cried. But something held her back, something so strong that it never even crossed her mind to ask what it was. Naturally, going with them was out of the question. Still, she would have liked to go, if it weren’t for the other thing; and amused by the absurdity of what she had been thinking (what luck to marry a man with a chamois-leather bag for his watch), she went, smiling, into the other room, where her husband was sitting reading.
