She had given and given and given, and died of it—and left all this behind. Honestly, she was angry with Mrs. Ramsay. Her brush trembling a little between her fingers, she looked at the hedge, the step, the wall. It was all Mrs. Ramsay’s doing. She was dead. And here was Lily, forty-four years old, wasting her time, unable to do anything, standing there pretending to paint—playing at the one thing that was not to be played at—and it was all Mrs. Ramsay’s fault. She was dead. The step she used to sit on was empty. She was dead.

But what was the good of repeating it again and again? Why keep straining after a feeling she simply didn’t have? There was something like blasphemy in doing that. It was all dry: all shriveled: all used up. They should never have invited her; she should never have come. At forty-four you can’t afford to waste your time, she thought. She hated playing at painting. A brush was the one reliable thing in a world of conflict, ruin and chaos—you had no business playing with it, even knowingly; the idea disgusted her. But he forced her to. You won’t lay a finger on that canvas, he seemed to say as he bore down on her, until you’ve given me what I want from you. And here he was, right beside her again, greedy and distraught. Well, thought Lily despairingly, letting her right hand sink to her side, then it would be easier just to get it over with. Surely she could copy from memory the glow, the rapture, the self-surrender she had seen on the faces of so many women (on Mrs. Ramsay’s, for one) when, on an occasion like this, they would blaze up—she could recall the look on Mrs. Ramsay’s face—into a transport of sympathy, of delight in their reward. Why they felt it she could not tell, but plainly it gave them the most perfect bliss that human nature was capable of. Here he was, come to a stop beside her. She would give him what she could.
