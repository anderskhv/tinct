@@ 41.0
Mr. Ramsay was nearly at the end of his reading. One hand hovered above the page, poised to turn it the instant he reached the bottom. He sat there with no hat on, the wind tossing his hair about, extraordinarily exposed to everything. He looked very old. To James—who was lining his head up now with the Lighthouse, now with the empty stretch of water running off into the open—he looked like some old stone lying on the sand; as though his body had become the thing that always lay at the back of both their minds: that loneliness which was, for each of them, the truth about things.

@@ 41.1
He read very fast, as though impatient to reach the end. And they really were very near the Lighthouse now. There it rose up, bare and upright, glaring black and white, and you could see the waves smashing into white splinters, like broken glass, against the rocks. You could make out lines and creases in the rock. You could see the windows plainly—one of them with a dab of white on it—and a little tuft of green on the rock. A man had come out, looked at them through a spyglass, and gone back in. So that was what it was like, James thought—the Lighthouse you had watched across the bay all these years: a bare tower standing on a naked rock. That satisfied him. It bore out some obscure feeling he had about his own nature. The old ladies, he thought, remembering the garden at home, went dragging their chairs about the lawn. Old Mrs. Beckwith, say, was forever going on about how nice it was, how sweet it was, how proud and how happy they all ought to be; but in actual fact, James thought, looking at the Lighthouse planted there on its rock, it’s like that. He looked at his father, reading fiercely, his legs tightly curled. The two of them shared that knowledge. “We are driving before a gale—we must sink,” he began murmuring to himself, half aloud, exactly the way his father said it.

@@ 41.2
It seemed an age since anyone had spoken. Cam had grown tired of looking at the sea. Scraps of black cork had drifted by; in the bottom of the boat the fish lay dead. Her father went on reading, and James looked at him, and she looked at him, and they swore they would fight tyranny to the death; and he read on, with no idea at all of what they were thinking. That was how he got away, she thought. Yes, with that great forehead and great nose of his, holding his little speckled book firmly up in front of him, he got away. You might try to catch hold of him, but then he spread his wings like a bird and floated off, to land out of your reach, somewhere far off on some lonely stump. She stared out over the vast stretch of sea. The island had shrunk so much that it hardly looked like a leaf now. It looked like the tip of a rock that one big wave would cover. And yet, frail as it was, it held all those paths, those terraces, those bedrooms—all those countless things. But just as, on the edge of sleep, things grow simpler until only one detail out of thousands has the power to make itself felt, so, she felt, as she gazed drowsily at the island, all those paths and terraces and bedrooms were fading and vanishing, until nothing remained but a pale blue censer swinging rhythmically to and fro across her mind. It was a hanging garden; it was a valley full of birds and flowers and antelopes. … She was drifting off to sleep.

Mr. Ramsay undid the parcel and passed the sandwiches around. He was happy now, eating bread and cheese with these fishermen. What he would really have liked, James thought, watching him pare his cheese into thin yellow slices with his penknife, was to live in a cottage and loaf about the harbor, spitting, with the other old men.

@@ 41.8
This is right, this is it, Cam kept feeling while she peeled her hard-boiled egg. She felt now just as she did in the study when the old men sat reading _The Times_. Now I can go on thinking anything I please, and I won’t tumble off a cliff or drown, she thought, because there he is, keeping an eye on me.

@@ 41.9
All the while they were racing along so close to the rocks that it was thrilling—as if they were doing two things at the same time: sitting here in the sun eating lunch, and also fighting their way to safety through a great storm after a shipwreck. Would the water hold out? Would the food hold out? she asked herself—telling herself a story, yet knowing all along what the truth was.

@@ 41.10
Their own time would soon be over, Mr. Ramsay was telling old Macalister, but their children would live to see some strange things. Macalister said he had turned seventy-five in March; Mr. Ramsay was seventy-one. Macalister said he had never once been to a doctor, and never lost a tooth. And that’s how I’d like my children to live—Cam felt sure her father was thinking that, because when she was about to throw a sandwich into the sea he stopped her and told her, as if he had the fishermen and their way of life in mind, that if she didn’t want it she should put it back in the parcel. She mustn’t waste it. He said it so wisely, as though he understood so well everything that went on in the world, that she put it back straight away. Then from his own parcel he handed her a gingerbread nut, like some grand Spanish gentleman, she thought, offering a flower to a lady at a window (his manner was so courteous). Yet he was shabby and plain, eating his bread and cheese; and all the same he was leading them on a great expedition in which, for all she knew, they would drown.

@@ 41.16
There! Cam thought, speaking to James without a sound. You’ve got it at last. She knew this was what James had been longing for, and she knew that now that he had it he was so pleased he would not look at her, or at their father, or at anybody. There he sat, bolt upright, his hand on the tiller, looking a bit sulky and frowning a little. He was so pleased that he would not let anyone rob him of one grain of his pleasure. His father had praised him. They must all think he didn’t care in the slightest. But you’ve got it now, Cam thought.

@@ 41.17
They had tacked, and now they were running fast and lightly along beside the reef on long rocking waves that passed them from one to the next with a wonderful lilt and thrill. To the left a line of rocks showed brown through the water, which grew thinner and greener; and on one of them, a taller rock, a wave kept breaking without pause, throwing up a little spout of drops that came down in a shower. You could hear the slap of the water, the patter of drops falling, and a sort of hushing, hissing sound from the waves as they rolled and romped and slapped at the rocks, like wild creatures that were perfectly free and would toss and tumble and play like this forever.

@@ 41.18
They could see two men on the Lighthouse now, watching them and getting ready to meet them.

@@ 41.19
Mr. Ramsay buttoned up his coat and rolled up his trouser legs. He took the big, clumsily wrapped brown paper parcel that Nancy had put together and sat holding it on his knee. Sitting like this, all ready to land, he gazed back at the island. With his long sight he could perhaps see quite plainly that shrunken leaf-shape standing on end on a plate of gold. What could he see? Cam wondered. To her it was only a blur. What was he thinking now? she wondered. What was he searching for, so fixedly, so intently, so silently? The two of them watched him as he sat there hatless with his parcel on his knee, staring and staring at the fragile blue shape, which looked like the vapor of something that had burned itself away. What do you want? both of them wanted to ask. Both of them wanted to say, Ask us for anything and we’ll give it to you. But he did not ask them for anything. He sat looking at the island, and he might have been thinking, We perished, each alone, or he might have been thinking, I have reached it. I have found it; but he said nothing.

Then he put on his hat.

@@ 41.21
“Bring those parcels,” he said, nodding toward the things Nancy had wrapped for them to take to the Lighthouse. “The parcels for the Lighthouse men,” he said. He stood up in the bow of the boat, very straight and very tall—exactly, James thought, as though he were saying, “There is no God,” and exactly, Cam thought, as though he were leaping into space—and they both stood up to follow him as he sprang onto the rock, lightly, like a young man, holding his parcel.

@@ 42.0
“He must have reached it,” Lily Briscoe said aloud, and suddenly she felt utterly exhausted. For the Lighthouse had almost disappeared; it had dissolved into a blue haze. And the strain of looking at it, together with the strain of thinking of him landing there—which seemed to be one and the same strain—had pulled her body and her mind as far as they would go. Ah, but what relief she felt. Whatever it was she had wanted to give him when he left her that morning, she had given it to him at last.

@@ 42.1
“He has landed,” she said aloud. “It is finished.” Then old Mr. Carmichael heaved himself up, puffing a little, and stood beside her like some old pagan god, shaggy, weeds in his hair and the trident (it was only a French novel) in his hand. He stood next to her at the edge of the lawn, his great bulk swaying slightly, and, shading his eyes with his hand, said, “They will have landed,” and she felt she had been right. They had had no need to speak. They had been thinking the same things, and he had answered her without her asking him anything. There he stood, spreading his hands over all the frailty and suffering of mankind; she thought he was looking out, tolerantly, compassionately, over their final fate. Now he has crowned the occasion, she thought, as his hand slowly dropped—as though she had watched him let fall from his great height a wreath of violets and asphodels, which drifted slowly down and came to rest at last on the earth.

@@ 42.2
Quickly, as though something over there had called her back, she turned to her canvas. There it was—her picture. Yes, with all its greens and blues, its lines running upward and across, its attempt at something. It would be hung up in the attic, she thought; it would be thrown away. But what did that matter? she asked herself, and took up her brush once more. She looked at the steps: they were empty. She looked at her canvas: it was blurred. With a sudden intensity, as though for one second she saw it clearly, she drew a line there, in the center. It was done; it was finished. Yes, she thought, laying down her brush in extreme fatigue, I have had my vision.
