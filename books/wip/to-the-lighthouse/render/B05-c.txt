“Yes,” Prue said thoughtfully, in answer to her mother’s question, “I think Nancy did go with them.”

@@ 16.0
Well then, Nancy had gone with them, Mrs. Ramsay supposed. Putting down a brush, picking up a comb, and calling “Come in” at a tap on the door (Jasper and Rose came in), she wondered whether Nancy’s being with them made anything happening less likely or more likely. Less likely, somehow, Mrs. Ramsay felt—quite irrationally—though after all, disaster on such a scale wasn’t probable. They couldn’t all be drowned. And once again she felt herself alone, facing her old enemy, life.

@@ 16.1
Mildred wanted to know, said Jasper and Rose, whether she should hold dinner back.

@@ 16.2
“Not for the Queen of England,” Mrs. Ramsay said firmly.

@@ 16.3
“Not for the Empress of Mexico,” she added, laughing at Jasper; for he shared his mother’s vice: he, too, exaggerated.

@@ 16.4
And while Jasper took the message, she told Rose that, if she liked, she could pick out the jewels her mother would wear. With fifteen people sitting down to dinner, you can’t keep things waiting forever. Now she was starting to feel annoyed with them for being so late. It was thoughtless of them; and on top of her worry about them, it annoyed her that they should pick this very night to stay out late, when she particularly wanted the dinner to go well, since William Bankes had at last agreed to eat with them; and they were having Mildred’s masterpiece—Bœuf en Daube, a French beef stew slow-cooked in wine. Everything depended on dishes going to the table at the exact moment they were ready. The beef, the bay leaf, the wine—every bit had to be cooked to perfection. Keeping it waiting was unthinkable. And yet, naturally, tonight of all nights, out they went, and back they came late, and dishes had to be sent out again, dishes had to be kept warm; the Bœuf en Daube would be utterly ruined.

@@ 16.5
Jasper held out an opal necklace; Rose a gold one. Which looked better against her black dress? Yes, which? said Mrs. Ramsay vaguely, studying her neck and shoulders in the mirror (but keeping away from her face). Then, while the children rummaged through her things, she gazed out of the window at a sight that never failed to amuse her—the rooks trying to make up their minds which tree to settle in. Each time, they seemed to change their minds and rise into the air again, because, she thought, the old rook, the father rook—old Joseph, she called him—had a very trying and difficult temperament. He was a disreputable old bird, missing half his wing feathers. He reminded her of some shabby old gentleman in a top hat she had once seen playing the horn outside a pub.

@@ 16.6
“Look!” she said, laughing. They really were fighting. Joseph and Mary were fighting. In any case, up they all went again, and their black wings shoved the air aside and cut it into exquisite scimitar shapes. That movement of wings beating out, out, out—she never could describe it precisely enough to satisfy herself—was among the loveliest things of all to her. Look at that, she said to Rose, hoping Rose would see it more clearly than she could. Because so often your children gave your own perceptions a little push forward.

@@ 16.7
But which one was it to be? They had opened every tray of her jewel box. The gold necklace, which was Italian, or the opal one Uncle James had brought her from India; or should she wear her amethysts?

@@ 16.8
“Choose, dearests, choose,” she said, hoping they would be quick.

@@ 16.9
But she let them take their time over it. Rose especially she let pick up first this and then that, and hold the jewels against the black dress; for this little ceremony of choosing jewels, gone through every night, was what Rose loved best, she knew. Rose had some secret reason of her own for thinking it tremendously important to choose what her mother wore. What could the reason be? Mrs. Ramsay wondered, standing still while Rose did up the clasp of the necklace she had picked. Through her own past she could guess at some deep, buried, wholly wordless feeling that you had for your mother at Rose’s age. Like every feeling felt for yourself, Mrs. Ramsay thought, it made you sad. What you could give back was so inadequate; and what Rose felt was out of all proportion to anything she really was. And Rose would grow up; and Rose would suffer, she supposed, with such deep feelings. Now she said she was ready, and they would go down, and Jasper, being the gentleman, should give her his arm, and Rose, being the lady, should carry her handkerchief (she handed her the handkerchief), and what else? Oh yes, it might be cold: a shawl. Choose me a shawl, she said, because that would please Rose, who was bound to suffer so. “There,” she said, pausing at the window on the landing, “there they are again.” Joseph had landed on a different treetop. “Don’t you think they mind,” she asked Jasper, “having their wings broken?” Why did he want to shoot poor old Joseph and Mary? He shuffled a bit on the stairs and felt told off, though not seriously, for she didn’t understand the fun of shooting birds; that birds didn’t feel; and being his mother, she lived far away in another part of the world. Still, he rather liked her stories about Mary and Joseph. She made him laugh. But how did she know those were Mary and Joseph? Did she think the same birds came to the same trees every night? he asked. But at this point, all of a sudden, she stopped paying him the slightest attention, the way grown-ups always did. She was listening to a clatter in the hall.

@@ 16.10
“They’re back!” she exclaimed, and immediately she felt far more annoyed with them than relieved. Then she wondered: had it happened? She would go down, and they would tell her—but no. With all these people around, they couldn’t tell her anything. So she had to go down, start dinner, and wait. And like some queen who finds her people gathered in the hall, gazes down at them, descends among them, silently acknowledges their tributes, and accepts their devotion and their bowing down before her (Paul kept perfectly still and looked straight ahead as she went by), she came downstairs and crossed the hall, bowing her head ever so slightly, as though accepting what they couldn’t say: their tribute to her beauty.

@@ 16.11
But she stopped. Something was burning. Could they have let the Bœuf en Daube boil over? she wondered. Please heaven, no! And then the great clanging of the gong announced, solemnly and with authority, that everyone scattered through the house—in attics, in bedrooms, on little perches of their own, reading, writing, giving their hair a final smoothing, or doing up their dresses—must leave all that, and the little odds and ends on their washstands and dressing tables, and the novels on their bedside tables, and the diaries that were so private, and gather in the dining room for dinner.
