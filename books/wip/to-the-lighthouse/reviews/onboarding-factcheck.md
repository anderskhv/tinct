## 2. Spoiler check

- No cast description or angle names a death. Mrs. Ramsay, Prue and Andrew all die in Time Passes (22.4, 25.4, 25.7), and none of these deaths is mentioned. The Rayleys' troubled marriage (34.9–34.14) is not mentioned.
- **Optional, mild:** `about` says "the trip is finally made and Lily goes back to her unfinished picture". This gives away the outcome of Part III. Softer version: "The third part, The Lighthouse, returns to the house, where the long-delayed trip is taken up again and Lily goes back to her unfinished picture."
- **Optional, mild:** angle card 4 says "much of what happens to the family is reported in brackets". This tells the reader that major events, which are deaths, happen offstage. Softer version: "and some of the largest events are reported in brackets."
- The corrected James line (correction 6) also removes the hint about the structure.

## 3. Acclaim: Vanessa Bell letter

**Status: VERIFIED. The exact wording was established from the primary printed source.**

Source: Vanessa Bell to Virginia Woolf, headed "On first reading 'To the Lighthouse'", dated "May 11 [1927]" (the year is the editor's bracketed attribution). It is printed as the Appendix in *The Letters of Virginia Woolf*, vol. III (1923–1928), ed. Nigel Nicolson and Joanne Trautmann (London: Hogarth Press, 1977), p. 572. The manuscript is in the Berg Collection, NYPL (cited by Frances Spalding, *Vanessa Bell*, notes: "Vanessa Bell to Virginia Woolf, 11 May [1927]: Berg"). It is also in Regina Marler (ed.), *Selected Letters of Vanessa Bell* (1993).

I checked the wording phrase by phrase against the Internet Archive full-text index of that volume (item `lettersofvirgini0003wool`, https://archive.org/details/lettersofvirgini0003wool, a lending-restricted scan whose full-text snippets are searchable). The same wording appears in several independent secondary books in the same index, for example Raitt, *Virginia Woolf's To the Lighthouse* ("L, III, 11 May 1927, 572"), Hanson, *Virginia Woolf*, and Gordon/Grove notes.

Exact text, with each segment confirmed in the snippets:

> "Anyhow it seemed to me that in the first part of the book you have given a portrait of mother which is more like her to me than anything I could ever have conceived of as possible. It is almost painful to have her so raised from the dead. You have made one feel the extraordinary beauty of her character, which must be the most difficult thing in the world to do. It was like meeting her again with oneself grown up and on equal terms and it seems to me the most astonishing feat of creation to have been able to see her in such a way."

## 4. Style check

- Declarative and book-focused: passes overall.
- Each `whyItMatters` item ends with one contemporary line: passes (items 1, 2 and 3 each have one closing present-tense line). Item 1's closing line ("Readers used to a single narrating voice still find...") is generic. Optional replacement: "Readers raised on a single narrating voice meet here a different way of paying attention."
- First-person plural: none in `whyItMatters`. **Flag:** angle card 1's title "Whose mind are we in?" uses "we". Suggested title: "Whose mind is this?"
- Aphorisms: item 2's closing "who gives and who takes in a household" leans toward one.
- Parallelism rhythms (flag, optional):
  - Item 2: "the father's need for praise, the mother's authority and self-sacrifice, the children's rage and love" (three parallel possessives) and "who gives and who takes". Suggested: "The novel deals with the Victorian family from inside it, with a father who needs constant praise and a mother whose authority rests on self-sacrifice. Questions about what a household asks of each person in it remain open."
  - Item 3: "how to see clearly, and how to go on working when..." Suggested: "Lily's problem of going on with work that no one expects to matter still applies to anyone making something in private."
  - Angle card 2: "what each of them needs from the other and what each refuses to give" is mild and acceptable for an angle card.
- Item 1 opens with an evaluative superlative ("one of the fullest demonstrations"). It is acceptable, but a flatter alternative is "To the Lighthouse shows what the modernist novel set out to do."
