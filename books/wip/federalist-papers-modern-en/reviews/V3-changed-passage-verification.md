### ch51 p5

Source is a correlative "As ... requires ..., the weakness ... may require, on the other hand". "Because" turns the parallel into a causal inference the source does not draw (the executive's weakness is not said to follow from the legislature's division). Restore the parallel with "Just as ... so".

```
But it is impossible to give every department an equal power of self-defense. In republican government the legislative authority necessarily predominates. The remedy for this problem is to divide the legislature into different branches. These should be made as little connected with each other as the nature of their common functions and their common dependence on the society will permit, by giving them different modes of election and different principles of action. It may even be necessary to guard against dangerous encroachments with still further precautions. Just as the weight of the legislative authority requires that it be divided in this way, so the weakness of the executive may require, on the other hand, that it be strengthened. An absolute negative on the legislature appears, at first view, to be the natural defense with which to arm the chief executive. But perhaps it would be neither entirely safe nor sufficient by itself. On ordinary occasions it might not be used with the needed firmness, and on extraordinary occasions it might be treacherously abused. May not this lack of an absolute negative be made up by some qualified connection between this weaker department and the weaker branch of the stronger department, by which that branch may be led to support the constitutional rights of the executive without being too much detached from the rights of its own department?
```

### ch51 p9

Source: "very improbable, if not impracticable". "Impossible" is a stronger claim than "impracticable" (not workable in practice). Restore the source's qualifier; "impracticable" is plain enough in context.

```
Second. In a republic it matters greatly not only to guard the society against the oppression of its rulers but to guard one part of the society against the injustice of the other part. Different classes of citizens necessarily have different interests. If a majority is united by a common interest, the rights of the minority will be insecure. There are only two ways to provide against this evil. One is to create a will in the community that is independent of the majority, that is, independent of the society itself. The other is to include in the society so many separate kinds of citizens that an unjust combination of a majority of the whole becomes very improbable, if not impracticable. The first way is the one found in all governments that hold an hereditary or self-appointed authority. At best this is a precarious security, because a power independent of the society may as readily take up the unjust views of the majority as the rightful interests of the minority, and may even be turned against both parties. The second way will be exemplified in the federal republic of the United States. All authority in it will be derived from the society and dependent on it, while the society itself will be broken into so many parts, interests and classes of citizens that the rights of individuals, or of the minority, will be in little danger from self-interested combinations of the majority. In a free government the security for civil rights must be the same as that for religious rights. In the one case it consists in the multiplicity of interests, and in the other in the multiplicity of sects. In both cases the degree of security will depend on the number of interests and sects, and this may be presumed to depend on the extent of country and the number of people brought under the same government. This view of the subject must particularly recommend a proper federal system to all the sincere and thoughtful friends of republican government. It shows that to the exact degree that the territory of the Union may be formed into smaller Confederacies or States, oppressive combinations of a majority will be made easier. The best security that the republican forms offer for the rights of every class of citizens will then be weakened. And so the stability and independence of some member of the government, the only other security, must be increased in the same proportion. Justice is the end of government. It is the end of civil society. It always has been, and always will be, pursued until it is obtained, or until liberty is lost in the pursuit. Take a society organized in such a way that the stronger faction can readily unite and oppress the weaker. Anarchy may as truly be said to reign there as in a state of nature, where the weaker individual has no protection against the violence of the stronger. And in the state of nature even the stronger individuals are driven by the uncertainty of their condition to accept a government that can protect the weak as well as themselves. In the same way, in the former case the more powerful factions or parties will gradually be led by a like motive to want a government that will protect all parties, the weaker as well as the more powerful. It can hardly be doubted that if the State of Rhode Island were separated from the Confederacy and left to itself, the insecurity of rights under popular government within such narrow limits would be displayed by such repeated oppressions by factional majorities that some power altogether independent of the people would soon be called for by the voice of the very factions whose misrule had proved the need for it. In the extended republic of the United States, with the great variety of interests, parties and sects that it embraces, a coalition of a majority of the whole society could seldom form on any principles other than those of justice and the general good. And since a minority would thus have less to fear from the will of a majority, there would also be less pretext for providing for its security by bringing into the government a will not dependent on the majority, or, in other words, a will independent of the society itself. It is as certain as it is important, whatever contrary opinions have been held, that the larger the society, provided it lies within a practical sphere, the better able it will be to govern itself. And happily for the REPUBLICAN CAUSE, the practicable sphere may be carried to a very great extent by a judicious modification and mixture of the FEDERAL PRINCIPLE.
```

Clarify inside the sentence with "by members" and restore the plural; the quorum context supports it.

```
Related to the objection about the number of representatives, and properly noted here, is another objection, about the number needed to conduct legislative business. It has been said that a quorum should have required more than a majority, and that in particular cases, if not in all, a decision should have required more than a majority of a quorum. It cannot be denied that such a precaution might have brought some advantages. It might have been an additional shield for some particular interests, and a further general obstacle to hasty and biased measures. But the drawbacks on the other side outweigh these considerations. Whenever justice or the general good might require new laws or active measures, the fundamental principle of free government would be reversed. The majority would no longer rule; power would pass to the minority. Suppose this protective privilege were limited to particular cases. A self-interested minority might use it to shield themselves from fair sacrifices for the general welfare, or, in particular emergencies, to extort unreasonable concessions. Lastly, it would make easy and encourage the harmful practice of secessions by members. This practice has appeared even in States where only a majority is required. It undermines every principle of order and regular government, and it leads more directly to public convulsions and the ruin of popular governments than any other practice yet seen among us.
```
