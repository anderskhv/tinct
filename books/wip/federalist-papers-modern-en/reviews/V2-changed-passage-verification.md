### ch42 p16

```
The differences among the States in the rules of naturalization have long been noted as a fault in our system, and as a basis for intricate and delicate questions. The fourth article of the Confederation declares "that the FREE INHABITANTS of each of these States, paupers, vagabonds, and fugitives from justice, excepted, shall be entitled to all privileges and immunities of FREE CITIZENS in the several States; and THE PEOPLE of each State shall, in every other, enjoy all the privileges of trade and commerce," etc. The language here is remarkably confused. It is hard to tell why the terms FREE INHABITANTS are used in one part of the article, FREE CITIZENS in another and PEOPLE in another, or what was meant by adding "all the privileges of trade and commerce" to "all privileges and immunities of free citizens." It seems nearly unavoidable, though, to conclude that those who count as FREE INHABITANTS of a State, even if not citizens of that State, are entitled in every other State to all the privileges of FREE CITIZENS of that other State. That means greater privileges than they may have in their own State. So a particular State may have the power, or rather every State is placed under a necessity, to confer the rights of citizenship in other States, not only on anyone it admits to such rights within itself, but on anyone it allows to become an inhabitant within its jurisdiction. But suppose we accepted an interpretation of the term "inhabitants" that confined the promised privileges to citizens alone. The difficulty would only be reduced, not removed. Each State would still keep the very improper power of naturalizing aliens in every other State. In one State a short residence confers all the rights of citizenship, and in another more important qualifications are required. So an alien who is legally barred from certain rights in the latter State may get around the bar merely by having lived earlier in the former. In this way the law of one State is absurdly made to override the law of another within that other State's own jurisdiction. We owe it to mere chance that we have so far escaped very serious embarrassments over this. By the laws of several States, certain kinds of aliens who had made themselves obnoxious were put under prohibitions that were inconsistent not only with the rights of citizenship but with the privilege of residence. What would have followed if such persons, by residence or otherwise, had acquired the character of citizens under the laws of another State, and had then claimed their rights as citizens, both to residence and to citizenship, in the State that had banned them? Whatever the legal consequences might have been, other consequences would probably have followed that were too serious not to be guarded against. The new Constitution has accordingly, and very properly, made provision against them, and against all other consequences of the Confederation's defect on this point, by authorizing the general government to establish a uniform rule of naturalization throughout the United States.
```

### ch44 p4

```
Every citizen must be pleased that the prohibition now reaches bills of credit, in proportion to his love of justice and his knowledge of the true sources of public prosperity. Since the peace, America has suffered from the pestilent effects of paper money on the confidence needed between man and man, on the confidence needed in the public councils, on the industry and morals of the people, and on the character of republican government. These losses are an enormous debt, chargeable against the States that adopted this unwise measure, and it must long remain unpaid. Or rather, they are an accumulated guilt that can be expiated only by one thing: a voluntary sacrifice, on the altar of justice, of the very power that has been the instrument of it. Beyond these persuasive considerations, we may observe that the same reasons that show the necessity of denying the States the power of regulating coin prove equally that they should not be free to put a paper medium in the place of coin. If every State had a right to regulate the value of its coin, there might be as many currencies as there are States, and dealings among them would be obstructed. Its value could be altered retroactively, which would injure the citizens of other States and kindle animosities among the States themselves. Subjects of foreign powers might suffer in the same way, and so the Union would be discredited and drawn into quarrels by the indiscretion of a single member. A power in the States to emit paper money is no less open to every one of these harms than a power to coin gold or silver. The power to make anything but gold and silver a tender in payment of debts is withdrawn from the States on the same principle as the power to issue a paper currency.
```

### ch44 p12

```
Suppose the Convention had taken the first course and adopted the second article of the Confederation. It is evident that the new Congress, like its predecessors, would constantly face a choice between two alternatives. It could read the word "EXPRESSLY" so strictly that the government was left with no real authority at all, or so loosely that the restriction lost all its force. It would be easy to show, if it were necessary, that Congress has never been able, and never will be able, to carry out any important power delegated by the Articles of Confederation without turning, more or less, to the doctrine of CONSTRUCTION or IMPLICATION. The powers delegated under the new system are more extensive, so the government administering it would be still more hard pressed by the alternative of betraying the public interests by doing nothing, or violating the Constitution by exercising powers that are indispensably necessary and proper but not EXPRESSLY granted.
```

### ch46 p1

```
Many considerations, in addition to those suggested on a former occasion, seem to put it beyond doubt that the people's first and most natural attachment will be to the governments of their own States. More individuals will expect to rise into the administration of these governments. More offices, with the pay attached to them, will flow from their gift. Their watchful care will regulate and provide for all the more domestic and personal interests of the people. The people will know their affairs more familiarly and in more detail. And a greater share of the people will have ties of personal acquaintance and friendship, and of family and party attachment, with their members. The popular bias may well be expected to lean most strongly toward these governments.
```
