By now my wicked and shameful youth was dead, and I was moving on into early manhood. The older I grew, the more I was soiled by empty things, for I could not picture any substance except the kind these eyes normally see. I did not think of You, O God, as shaped like a human body. From the time I first began to hear something of wisdom I had always steered clear of that idea, and I was glad to find the same view in the faith of our spiritual mother, Your Catholic Church. But what else I was to think You were, I had no idea. And I, a man, and a man like me, was trying to think of You, the supreme, the only, the true God. In the very marrow of my soul I believed that You were incorruptible, beyond injury, and unchangeable. I did not know where the certainty came from or how, yet I saw clearly and was sure that what can be corrupted is worse than what cannot; without hesitating I ranked what cannot be injured above what can, and the unchangeable above whatever is open to change. My heart shouted fiercely against all my phantoms, and with this single blow I tried to drive off the whole filthy swarm that circled before the eye of my mind. But no sooner were they brushed aside than, in the twinkling of an eye, they massed together again, rushed at my face and clouded my sight. So although I no longer pictured You in the form of a human body, I was still forced to picture You as something bodily, spread through space — that incorruptible, uninjurable and unchangeable being I ranked above the corruptible, injurable and changeable — whether poured into the world or stretched out endlessly beyond it. For anything I tried to think of without such space seemed to me to be nothing, absolutely nothing, not even an empty void. It was as if a body were removed from its place and the place stayed there emptied of every body, whether of earth or water, air or heaven, and still remained an empty place, a kind of roomy nothing.

So, gross of heart as I was, and not even clearly visible to myself, I judged that whatever was not stretched through some amount of space — spread out, packed together or swelling — or did not take on, or could not take on, some such dimension, was nothing whatsoever. My heart moved among images of exactly the kind of shapes my eyes are used to moving among. I did not yet see that the very act of mind by which I formed those images was not itself anything of that kind, and yet it could never have formed them unless it were itself something great. In the same way I pictured You, Life of my life, as immense, pervading the whole bulk of the universe on every side through infinite spaces, and reaching beyond it in every direction through measureless, endless spaces, so that the earth held You, the heaven held You, all things held You, and they found their limits in You while You were limited nowhere. The body of the air above the earth does not stop the light of the sun from passing through it; the light goes right through, not by tearing it or cutting it but by filling it entirely. In the same way I supposed that the body not only of heaven, air and sea but of the earth as well lay open to You, penetrable in all its parts, largest and smallest, so as to take in Your presence, which governs from inside and outside, by a hidden breathing-in, all the things You have created. That was my guess, only because I could think of nothing else; and it was false. For on that view a larger part of the earth would hold a larger part of You, and a smaller part a smaller; and all things would be full of You in such a way that an elephant's body would contain more of You than a sparrow's, by as much as it is bigger and fills more room. You would then be making the parts of Yourself present to the parts of the world piece by piece, big pieces to big parts and small ones to small. But You are not like that. But You had not yet lit up my darkness.

Against those deceived deceivers and talkative mutes — mute because Your word did not sound from them — I had enough, Lord. I had the argument Nebridius used to put forward long ago, back in Carthage, which shook every one of us who heard it: "What could that so-called race of darkness, which the Manichees like to set up as a hostile mass over against You, have done to You if You had refused to fight it? If the answer were that it would have done You some harm, then You would be open to injury and corruption. But if they said it could have done You no harm, then there was no reason given for You to fight it at all — let alone to fight in such a way that some portion or limb of You, some offspring of Your very Substance, would be mixed up with hostile powers and with natures You never created, and would be so far corrupted by them and changed for the worse that it fell from happiness into misery and needed help to be rescued and cleansed. And this offspring of Your Substance, they say, is the soul: enslaved, stained and corrupted, it is relieved by Your Word, which is free, pure and whole — yet that Word too must be corruptible, since it is of one and the same Substance. So if they called You, whatever You are — that is, the Substance by which You are — incorruptible, then all these claims of theirs were false and detestable; and if they called You corruptible, that statement was itself false and repulsive the moment it was spoken." This argument of Nebridius, then, was enough against people who deserved in every way to be vomited up out of an overloaded stomach. They had no way out, short of horrible blasphemy of heart and tongue, in thinking and saying such things about You.

Yet even I, at that point — though I said, and firmly held, that You, our Lord, the true God, who made not only our souls but our bodies, and not only our souls and bodies but all beings and all things, could not be defiled or altered and were in no respect changeable — still had no clear and untangled grasp of the cause of evil. But whatever that cause might be, I could see that it had to be searched for in a way that would not drive me to believe that the unchangeable God was changeable, or I would myself become the very evil I was looking for. So I looked for it with this much peace of mind: I was certain that what those people taught, from whom I now fled with my whole heart, was not true. For I saw that in asking where evil came from they had become full of evil themselves, since they would rather believe that Your substance suffers evil than that their own does evil.

So I strained to see clearly what I was now being told: that the free choice of the will is the reason we do wrong, and Your just judgment the reason we suffer it. But I could not see it clearly. I tried to draw the eye of my mind up out of that deep pit, and I sank back into it; I tried again and again, and again and again I sank. What lifted me a little toward Your light was this: I knew that I had a will just as surely as I knew that I was alive. So whenever I wanted something or refused it, I was completely sure that it was I and no one else who was wanting and refusing, and I was on the very point of seeing that here lay the cause of my sin. As for what I did unwillingly, I saw that I underwent it rather than did it, and I judged it to be not my fault but my punishment; and because I thought of You as just, I was quick to admit that I was not being punished unjustly. But then I would ask again: Who made me? Wasn't it my God, who is not only good but goodness itself? Then where did I get this power to will evil and refuse good, which gives me a reason to be justly punished? Who put this in me and planted in me this seedling of bitterness, when the whole of me was made by my most sweet God? If the devil is the one responsible, where did the devil come from? And if he too was a good angel who became a devil through his own perverse will, where did that evil will in him come from, by which he became a devil, when the whole angel had been made by a Creator who is supremely good? These thoughts pushed me down again and choked me. Yet I was not dragged down as far as that hell of error where no one confesses to You, the error that thinks You suffer evil rather than that man does it.

For I was trying to discover the rest in the same way that I had already discovered that the incorruptible is better than the corruptible; and that is why I confessed that You, whatever You might be, were incorruptible. No soul ever has been or ever will be able to think of anything better than You, the highest and the best good. And since it is perfectly true and certain that the incorruptible is to be ranked above the corruptible, as I was already ranking it, then if You were not incorruptible I could have reached, in thought, something better than my God. So where I saw that the incorruptible must be preferred to the corruptible, that was where I ought to have been looking for You, and from there I ought to have worked out "where evil is" — that is, where corruption itself comes from, the corruption that cannot damage Your substance in any way at all. Corruption cannot damage our God in any respect: not by any will, not by any necessity, not by any unforeseen accident. For He is God; what He wills for Himself is good, and He Himself is that very good; and being corrupted is not good. Nor are You ever forced into anything against Your will, since Your will is not greater than Your power. It would be greater only if You were greater than Yourself, for the will and the power of God are God Himself. And what can take You by surprise, when You know all things? Nothing has any nature at all except because You know it. Why say so much, then, about "why the substance that God is cannot be corruptible," when, if it were, it would not be God?

And I kept asking "where evil comes from," and I asked in an evil way, and did not see the evil in my very asking. I set out before the gaze of my spirit the whole of creation: everything we can see in it, such as sea, earth, air, stars, trees and mortal creatures; and everything in it we cannot see, such as the firmament of heaven above, and all the angels, and all its spiritual beings. But my imagination arranged even these in their places as if they were bodies. I made Your creation into one enormous mass, divided up by kinds of bodies — some that really were bodies, and some that I had invented to stand for spirits. And I made this mass huge, not as huge as it actually is, which I had no way of knowing, but as huge as I liked, though bounded on every side. You, Lord, I imagined surrounding it and soaking through it from every direction, but infinite in every direction. It was as if there were a sea everywhere, stretching on every side through immeasurable space, a single sea without end, and inside it there were a sponge, as large as you please but still bounded: that sponge would of course be filled in every part from the measureless sea. That is how I thought of Your creation, finite as it is, as full of You, the Infinite. And I said: Look, here is God, and here is what God has created. God is good, and mightily, immeasurably more excellent than all these things. Yet being good He created them good, and see how He surrounds them and fills them. Then where is evil, and where does it come from, and how did it creep in here? What is its root, and what is its seed? Or does it not exist at all? Then why do we fear and guard against something that does not exist? Or if our fear is groundless, then surely the fear itself is evil, since it goads and tortures the heart for nothing; and it is an evil all the worse because there is nothing to fear and still we fear. So either what we fear is evil, or else the evil is that we fear. Where, then, does it come from, since God, who is good, made all these things good? He, the greater and supreme Good, made these lesser goods; but still, Creator and created alike, all are good. Where does evil come from? Or was there some evil matter out of which He made things, and He shaped it and set it in order, but left something in it that He did not turn into good? Why would He do that? Was He unable to turn and change the whole of it, so that no evil remained, when He is almighty? And finally, why would He choose to make anything out of it at all, instead of using that same almighty power to make it not exist at all? Or could it exist against His will? Or if it was eternal, why did He let it stay that way through infinite stretches of time past, and then decide so much later to make something out of it? Or, if He suddenly wanted to act, surely the Almighty would have acted instead to make that matter cease to be, so that He alone would exist, the whole, true, supreme and infinite Good. Or, if it was not right that He who was good should not also build and establish something good, then He could have taken away that evil matter and reduced it to nothing, and set up good matter of His own from which to create all things. For He would not be almighty if He could not establish anything good without the help of matter He had not made Himself. Such were the thoughts I turned over in my wretched heart, weighed down with the most gnawing worries — the fear of death, and the truth not yet found. Yet the faith of Your Christ, our Lord and Savior, as held in the Catholic Church, stayed firmly fixed in my heart. On many points it was still unformed and drifting away from the rule of doctrine; but my mind did not abandon it, and instead drank it in more and more every day.
