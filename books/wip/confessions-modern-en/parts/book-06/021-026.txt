He was amazed that I, whom he held in no small esteem, should be stuck so fast in the birdlime of that pleasure that I insisted, whenever we discussed it, that I could never live a single life. When I saw his amazement, I defended myself by saying that there was a great difference between his brief and hurried experience, which he barely remembered and so could easily despise, and my own settled habit; and that if the honorable name of marriage were added to it, he ought not to wonder why I could not spurn that way of life. Then he too began to want to marry, not because he was overcome by desire for that pleasure, but out of curiosity. For he said he wanted to know what this thing could be, without which my life, which he found so agreeable, seemed to me not a life but a punishment. His mind, free of that chain, was astonished at my slavery, and through that astonishment it was moving toward a desire to try it, and from there to the trial itself, and from there perhaps to sinking into the very bondage that astonished him, since he was willing to make a covenant with death; and whoever loves danger will fall into it. For whatever honor there is in the duty of rightly ordering a married life and raising children, it attracted neither of us except slightly. For my part, the habit of satisfying an insatiable appetite held me captive and tormented me for the most part and with great force; he, meanwhile, was being drawn toward captivity by wonder. This is how we were, until You, Most High, who do not forsake our dust, took pity on us in our misery and came to our help in wonderful and hidden ways.

@@ 22
I was pressed constantly to marry. I was already making my proposal, and a bride was already being promised to me, chiefly through my mother's efforts. She hoped that once I was married, saving baptism would wash me clean. She rejoiced that I was being made more fit for it day by day, and she saw her prayers and Your promises being fulfilled in my faith. At that time, both at my request and from her own longing, she begged You every day with a strong cry of the heart to show her in a vision something about my future marriage, but You never would. She did see certain empty and fanciful things, such as the force of the human spirit, busy about the matter, conjures up, and she told me about them. But she did not speak with the confidence she usually had when You had shown her something; she made light of them. For she said that by a certain taste, which she could not put into words, she could tell the difference between Your revelations and the dreams of her own soul. Still, the matter was pressed forward, and a girl was asked for in marriage who was about two years short of the marriageable age. Since she was pleasing, we were waiting for her.

Many of us friends, talking together and detesting the turbulent troubles of human life, had considered and had almost decided to live at leisure, away from the crowds. We planned to bring about this leisure as follows: we would put into a common fund whatever each of us could obtain and make a single household out of it all. In this way, through the sincerity of our friendship, nothing would belong to one person or another, but the single whole made up from everyone's goods would belong as a whole to each, and everything to everyone. We thought there might be about ten people in this community. Some of us were very rich, especially Romanianus, a man from our town and a close friend of mine since childhood, whom serious troubles in his affairs had brought to the court at that time. He was the most eager for this project, and his voice carried great weight in urging it, because his large fortune far exceeded the rest. We had also agreed that two of us each year, like magistrates, would see to everything necessary, while the rest remained undisturbed. But when we began to consider whether the wives, whom some of us already had and others hoped to have, would allow this, the whole plan we had been shaping so well fell apart in our hands, and was broken up and thrown aside. After that we turned to sighs and groans, and turned our steps to follow the broad and well-worn roads of the world. For many thoughts were in our heart, but Your counsel stands forever. Out of that counsel You laughed at our plans and prepared Your own for us, intending to give us food in due season, to open Your hand and to fill our souls with blessing.

@@ 24
Meanwhile my sins were multiplying. My concubine was torn from my side as an obstacle to my marriage, and my heart, which clung to her, was cut and wounded and left bleeding. She went back to Africa, vowing to You that she would never know another man, and she left with me the son I had had by her. But I, unhappy man, could not even follow a woman's example. Impatient of delay, since I was not to receive the girl I had asked for until two years had passed, and being not so much a lover of marriage as a slave to lust, I got myself another woman, though certainly not as a wife. In this way the disease of my soul would be kept alive and carried along, either at full strength or even worse, under the escort of an enduring habit, into the realm of marriage. And that wound of mine, made by the cutting away of the first woman, did not heal. After the inflammation and sharpest pain, it festered, and the pain became duller, as it were, but more hopeless.

@@ 25
Praise to You, glory to You, Fountain of mercies! I was becoming more miserable, and You were coming nearer. Your right hand was ready at every moment to snatch me out of the mud and wash me clean, and I did not know it. Nothing called me back from a still deeper whirlpool of fleshly pleasures except the fear of death and of Your judgment to come, which, through all my changes of opinion, never left my heart. In my discussions with my friends Alypius and Nebridius about the ends of good and evil, I held that Epicurus would have won the prize in my mind, if I had not believed that after death the soul lives on and that there are places of reckoning according to people's deserts, which Epicurus refused to believe. And I would ask, "If we were immortal and lived in perpetual bodily pleasure, with no fear of losing it, why would we not be happy, or what else would we be looking for?" I did not know that great misery lay in this very fact: that, sunk and blinded as I was, I could not conceive of that light of virtue and of beauty to be embraced for its own sake, which the eye of the flesh does not see and which is seen from within. Nor did I, wretched as I was, consider where it came from that I could enjoy discussing even these things, foul as they were, with my friends. Nor could I be happy without friends, even by the idea of happiness I had then, however great the abundance of fleshly pleasures. And these friends I certainly loved for their own sake alone, and I felt that they in turn loved me for my own sake alone.

What twisted paths! Woe to the reckless soul that hoped, by leaving You, to have something better! It has turned and turned again, onto its back, its sides, its belly, and everything is hard, and You alone are rest. And look, You are here, and You free us from our miserable wanderings, and set us on Your way, and comfort us, and say, "Run; I will carry you; yes, I will bring you through; and there too I will carry you."
