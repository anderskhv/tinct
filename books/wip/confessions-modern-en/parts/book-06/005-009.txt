I was glad, too, that the old writings of the Law and the Prophets were now set before me to be read no longer with the eye that had once found them absurd, when I used to attack Your holy ones for thinking things they did not in fact think. And I was glad to hear Ambrose in his sermons to the people often and very earnestly commend this text as a rule: The letter kills, but the Spirit gives life. Meanwhile he would draw aside the mystical veil and open up in a spiritual sense passages which, taken literally, seemed to teach something perverse. In this he taught nothing that offended me, though he did teach things whose truth I did not yet know. For I held my heart back from assenting to anything, afraid of falling headlong, but by hanging in suspense I was being killed all the more. I wanted to be as certain of the things I could not see as I was that seven and three make ten. I was not so mad as to think that even this could not be known; but I wanted other things to be just as clear, whether bodily things that were not present to my senses, or spiritual things, which I did not know how to conceive except in bodily terms. By believing I could have been cured, so that the eyesight of my soul, once cleared, might somehow be directed toward Your truth, which abides forever and fails in no part. But just as a person who has had a bad doctor is often afraid to trust himself even to a good one, so it was with the health of my soul. It could not be healed except by believing, and yet, for fear of believing falsehoods, it refused to be cured, resisting Your hands, You who have prepared the medicines of faith, applied them to the diseases of the whole world, and given them such great authority.

Even so, this led me to prefer the Catholic teaching. I felt that she acted more modestly and honestly in requiring belief in things that were not demonstrated (whether because they could be demonstrated in themselves, though not to certain people, or because they could not be demonstrated at all), whereas among the Manichees our credulity was mocked with a promise of certain knowledge, and then so many utterly fabulous and absurd things were imposed on us to be believed, precisely because they could not be demonstrated. Then, Lord, little by little, with a most gentle and merciful hand, You touched and settled my heart and persuaded me. I considered how countless were the things I believed that I had not seen, and had not been present for when they happened: so much in the history of nations, so many accounts of places and cities I had never seen, so much taken on trust from friends, so much from doctors, so much constantly from one person or another. Unless we believed these things, we would do nothing at all in this life. Last of all I considered how unshakably I believed who my parents were, something I could not know unless I had believed what I heard. Considering all this, You persuaded me that it was not those who believed Your Books, which You have established with such great authority among almost all nations, who deserved blame, but those who did not believe them. You persuaded me, too, that I should not listen to anyone who might say to me, "How do you know that those Scriptures were given to the human race by the Spirit of the one true and most truthful God?" For this was the very thing most to be believed. No combativeness in all the slanderous questions I had read among so many philosophers contradicting one another could ever wring from me the belief "that You are," whatever You might be (which I did not know), and "that the governing of human affairs belongs to You."

This I believed, sometimes more firmly, at other times more feebly. But I always believed both that You exist and that You care for us, even though I did not know what to think about Your substance, or what way led, or led back, to You. Since we were too weak to find the truth by clear reasoning, and for that very reason needed the authority of the holy Scriptures, I had now begun to believe that You would never have given that Scripture such outstanding authority throughout every land unless You had willed that through it You should be believed in, and through it sought. For now, having heard many of the passages that used to offend me by sounding absurd explained plausibly, I referred them to the depth of the mysteries. Its authority seemed to me all the more worthy of reverence and of holy faith because, while it lies open for everyone to read, it keeps the dignity of its secret within a deeper sense. It offers itself to all in the plainest words and the humblest style of speech, and yet it exercises the full attention of those who are not light-minded. In this way it welcomes everyone into its broad embrace, and through narrow openings carries a few across to You; yet these are many more than it would carry if it did not stand so high in authority, and did not draw the crowds into the lap of its holy humility. I was thinking these things over, and You were with me. I sighed, and You heard me. I wavered, and You steered me. I went along the broad road of the world, and You did not abandon me.

I gaped after honors, money and marriage, and You laughed at me. In these desires I suffered the bitterest hardships, and You were all the kinder to me the less You let anything that was not You grow sweet to me. Look at my heart, Lord, You who willed that I should remember this and confess it to You. Let my soul now cling to You, since You have freed it from the sticky birdlime of death that held it so fast. How wretched it was! And You kept prodding the sore of its wound, so that it might leave everything else and be turned to You, who are above all things and without whom all things would be nothing; so that it might be turned, and be healed. How miserable I was, then, and how You dealt with me to make me feel my misery on that day when I was preparing to deliver a speech in praise of the Emperor. In it I was to tell many lies, and my lying was to be applauded by people who knew I was lying. My heart was panting with these worries and seething with the fever of consuming thoughts. As I was passing through one of the streets of Milan, I noticed a poor beggar, already full, I suppose, joking and in high spirits. I sighed and spoke to the friends who were with me about the many sorrows that come from our madness. With all our strivings, like the ones I was then laboring at, dragging the burden of my own unhappiness under the goad of desire and making it heavier by dragging it, we were still aiming at nothing but to reach that carefree joy where that beggar had already arrived ahead of us, and where perhaps we would never arrive. What he had gained with a few coins he had begged, I was scheming for through so many miserable twists and turns: the joy of a passing happiness. He certainly did not have true joy; but with my ambitions I was seeking a joy far less true. And he was certainly glad while I was anxious; he was carefree, I was afraid. If anyone had asked me whether I would rather be merry or fearful, I would have answered that I would rather be merry. But if he had asked again whether I would rather be such a man as he was or such as I then was, I would have chosen myself, worn out with cares and fears as I was. But I would have chosen out of perversity; was it out of truth? For I had no business preferring myself to him because I was more learned, since my learning gave me no joy. I used it to seek to please people, not to teach them, but simply to please them. That is why You broke my bones with the rod of Your discipline.

@@ 9
Away from my soul, then, with those who say to her, "It makes a difference where a person's joy comes from. That beggar found his joy in drunkenness; you wanted to find yours in glory." What glory, Lord? A glory that is not in You. For just as his was not true joy, neither was mine true glory, and it overturned my mind all the more. That very night he would sleep off his drunkenness; but I had gone to sleep with mine and woken up with it, and I would go to sleep and wake up with it again, how many days You know, God. "But it does make a difference where a person's joy comes from." I know it, and the joy of faithful hope is incomparably beyond such vanity. Yet even then there was a difference between him and me, for he was without doubt the happier man. It was not only that he was soaked in cheerfulness while I was gutted by cares. It was also that he had got his wine by wishing people well, while I was seeking empty, puffed-up praise by lying. I said much to this effect to my friends at that time. I often noticed in them how things stood with me, and I found that things were bad with me, and I grieved, and so doubled that very badness. And if any good fortune smiled on me, I could not be bothered to reach for it, because it flew away almost before I could grasp it.
