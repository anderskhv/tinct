But look: it is out of my memory that I bring it when I say that there are four perturbations of the mind, desire, joy, fear and sorrow. And whatever I can say about them in argument, dividing each into the species that belong to its kind and defining it, I find there in my memory what to say, and I bring it out from there. Yet I am not disturbed by any of these perturbations when I call them to mind and speak of them. Indeed, even before I recalled and went over them, they were there; that is why they could be brought out from there by recollection. Perhaps, then, just as food is brought up from the stomach when an animal chews the cud, so these things are brought up from the memory by recollection. Why, then, does the one who argues about them, that is, the one who recollects them, not taste in the mouth of his thinking the sweetness of joy or the bitterness of sorrow? Is the comparison unlike in this, because it is not like in every respect? For who would willingly speak about such things, if every time we named sorrow or fear we were forced to feel sad or afraid? And yet we could not speak of them at all unless we found in our memory not only the sounds of their names, according to the images impressed by the senses of the body, but also notions of the things themselves. These we never received through any doorway of the flesh; rather the mind itself, perceiving them through the experience of its own passions, entrusted them to the memory, or else the memory retained them by itself, even without their being entrusted to it.

But whether this happens by means of images or not, who can easily say? For I name a stone, I name the sun, when the things themselves are not present to my senses; but their images are certainly present in my memory. I name bodily pain, and yet it is not present to me when nothing hurts; but unless its image were present in my memory, I would not know what I was saying, nor could I tell it apart from pleasure in discussion. I name bodily health when I am healthy in body, and the thing itself is present to me. Yet unless its image were also in my memory, I could in no way recall what the sound of this name meant. Nor would sick people understand what was being said when health was named, unless that same image were held by the power of memory, even though the thing itself was absent from the body. I name the numbers by which we count, and there they are in my memory, not their images but the numbers themselves. I name the image of the sun, and that image is present in my memory; for I do not recall the image of its image, but the image itself is present to me when I call it to mind. I name memory, and I recognize what I name. And where do I recognize it, if not in memory itself? Is memory, too, present to itself by means of its image, and not by itself?

What about when I name forgetfulness and likewise recognize what I name? How could I recognize it if I did not remember it? I do not mean the sound of the name, but the thing it signifies; if I had forgotten that, I would certainly not be able to recognize what the sound means. So when I remember memory, memory itself is present to itself through itself; but when I remember forgetfulness, both memory and forgetfulness are present: memory, by which I remember, and forgetfulness, which I remember. But what is forgetfulness, except the absence of memory? How, then, is it present so that I remember it, when, if it is present, I cannot remember? But if what we remember we hold in memory, and yet unless we remembered forgetfulness we could never recognize the thing signified when we hear the name, then forgetfulness is held in the memory. So it is present so that we do not forget it, and yet when it is present, we forget. Or are we to understand from this that forgetfulness, when we remember it, is present in the memory not by itself but by its image, since if forgetfulness were present by itself, it would make us not remember but forget? And who, in the end, will search this out? Who will grasp how it is?

As for me, Lord, I truly labor over this, and I labor over myself. I have become to myself a hard soil that demands too much sweat of the brow. For we are not now exploring the regions of the sky, or measuring the distances of the stars, or investigating how the earth is balanced. It is I who remember, I, the mind. It is not so surprising if what I am not is far from me. But what is nearer to me than myself? And look, the power of my own memory is beyond my understanding, even though without it I cannot even speak of myself. For what am I to say, when I am certain that I remember forgetfulness? Am I to say that what I remember is not in my memory? Or am I to say that forgetfulness is in my memory for this purpose, so that I do not forget? Both are utterly absurd. What third way is there? How can I say that it is the image of forgetfulness that my memory holds, not forgetfulness itself, when I remember it? How can I say this either, since when the image of anything is impressed on the memory, the thing itself must first be present, so that the image can be impressed from it? For this is how I remember Carthage, and all the places I have been, and the faces of people I have seen, and what the other senses have reported; this is how I remember the health or the pain of the body itself. When these things were present, my memory took images from them, which I could look at as present and go over in my mind when I remembered the things themselves in their absence. If, then, forgetfulness is held in the memory through its image and not through itself, then it must itself have been present once, so that its image could be taken. But when it was present, how did it write its image in the memory, since forgetfulness by its presence wipes out even what it finds already recorded there? And yet, in whatever way it happens, though that way is beyond grasping and beyond explaining, I am certain that I also remember forgetfulness itself, by which what we remember is buried.

Great is the power of memory, a fearful thing, O my God, a deep and boundless multiplicity; and this is the mind, and this is I myself. What, then, am I, O my God? What is my nature? A life varied and manifold, and utterly immense. Look at the plains and caves and caverns of my memory, countless and countlessly full of countless kinds of things, present either through images, as all bodies are, or in person, as the arts are, or through certain notions or impressions, as the affections of the mind are, which the memory retains even when the mind is not feeling them, though whatever is in the memory is also in the mind. Through all these I run and fly this way and that; I dive in as far as I can, and there is no end anywhere. So great is the power of memory, so great the power of life, even in man living this mortal life! What, then, shall I do, O You, my true life, my God? I will pass beyond even this power of mine that is called memory; yes, I will pass beyond it, so as to reach You, O sweet Light. What do You say to me? See, I am climbing up through my mind toward You who dwell above me. I will pass beyond this power of mine that is called memory too, wanting to reach You where You can be reached, and to cling to You where one can cling to You. For even cattle and birds have memory; otherwise they could not find their way back to their lairs and nests, or to the many other things they grow used to; indeed they could not grow used to anything except through memory. So I will pass beyond memory too, to reach Him who set me apart from the four-footed beasts and made me wiser than the birds of the air. I will pass beyond memory too; and where shall I find You, You who are truly good, You sure and certain sweetness? Where shall I find You? If I find You outside my memory, then I do not keep You in mind. And how shall I find You, if I do not remember You?

For the woman who had lost her coin and searched for it with a lamp would never have found it unless she had remembered it. For once it was found, how would she know whether it was the same coin, unless she remembered it? I remember having looked for and found many things; and from this I know that when I was looking for any of them and someone asked me, "Is this it?" "Is that it?" I kept saying, "No," until the thing I was looking for was shown to me. If I had not remembered it, whatever it was, then even if it had been shown to me, I would not have found it, because I could not have recognized it. And it is always like this when we look for and find something lost. Still, when something is lost by chance from sight but not from memory, such as any visible body, its image is still held within, and it is looked for until it is restored to sight; and when it is found, it is recognized by the image that is within. And we do not say we have found what was lost unless we recognize it, and we cannot recognize it unless we remember it. But this thing was lost to the eyes while it was kept in the memory.
