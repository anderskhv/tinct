Yet these are not the only things that the immeasurable capacity of my memory holds. Here too is everything I have learned of the liberal sciences and not yet forgotten, set apart, as it were, in some inner place that is yet no place; and what I carry here are not images of them, but the things themselves. For what literature is, what the art of debate is, how many kinds of questions there are, whatever I know of these, is in my memory in such a way that I have not kept the image and left the thing outside. Nor is it as if it had sounded and passed away, like a voice that leaves its impress on the mind through the ear, by which it can be recalled as though it were sounding when it no longer sounds; or like a smell which, while it passes and fades into the air, affects the sense of smell and so conveys an image of itself into the memory, which we renew by remembering; or like food, which certainly has no taste any longer in the stomach and yet in the memory still tastes, in a way; or like anything the body perceives by touching, which the memory still pictures even when it has been taken away from us. For those things are not brought into the memory themselves; only their images are caught up with marvelous speed and stored away, as it were, in marvelous compartments, and then marvelously brought out again by the act of remembering.

But now, when I hear that there are three kinds of questions, "Whether a thing is? What it is? Of what kind it is?", I do hold the images of the sounds that make up those words, and I know that those sounds passed noisily through the air and now no longer exist. But the things themselves that those sounds signify I never reached with any sense of my body, and I never saw them anywhere except in my mind; yet what I have stored in my memory is not their images but the things themselves. How they got into me, let them say if they can. For I run through all the doorways of my flesh and cannot find by which one they came in. The eyes say, "If they had color, we reported them." The ears say, "If they made a sound, we made them known." The nostrils say, "If they had a smell, they passed through us." The sense of taste says, "If they have no flavor, do not ask me." Touch says, "If it has no bodily bulk, I did not handle it; and if I did not handle it, I reported nothing about it." Where did these things come from, and how did they get into my memory? I do not know how. For when I learned them, I did not take them on trust from someone else's mind; I recognized them in my own, and, approving them as true, I entrusted them to it, laying them up, as it were, where I could bring them out whenever I wished. So they were in my heart even before I learned them, but they were not in my memory. Where were they, then? Or why, when they were spoken, did I recognize them and say, "That is so, it is true," unless they were already in the memory, but pushed so far back and buried, as it were, in such deep hollows that, if someone else's prompting had not drawn them out, I might perhaps have been unable to think of them?

So we find that learning these things, whose images we do not take in through the senses but which we perceive inwardly by themselves, without images, as they actually are, is nothing other than this: by thinking, we gather together, so to speak, the things the memory already held scattered and in disorder, and by paying attention we see to it that they are placed ready to hand, as it were, in that same memory where before they lay hidden, scattered and neglected, so that now they readily come to a mind that has grown familiar with them. And how many things of this kind my memory carries which have already been found and, as I said, placed ready to hand, things we are said to have learned and to know! Yet if I stop calling them to mind for even a short space of time, they sink down again and slip away, as it were, into the more remote recesses, so that they have to be thought out again from there as if they were new, for they have no other dwelling place; and they have to be driven together again so that they can be known, that is, they have to be collected, as it were, out of a kind of scattering. That is where the word "cogitation" comes from. For cogo (collect) and cogito (re-collect) are related to each other as ago and agito, facio and factito. But the mind has claimed this word for itself, so that it is not what is "collected" just anywhere, but what is "recollected," that is, gathered together, in the mind, that is properly said to be cogitated, or thought.

The memory also contains the principles and countless laws of numbers and dimensions, none of which any bodily sense has impressed on it, since they have no color, sound, taste or smell, and cannot be touched. I have heard the sounds of the words by which they are signified when people discuss them, but the sounds are one thing and the principles another. For the sounds are different in Greek and in Latin, but the principles themselves are neither Greek nor Latin nor any other kind of language. I have seen lines drawn by craftsmen, even the very finest, like a spider's thread; but those lines are different: they are not images of the lines that the eye of the flesh reported to me. Anyone who recognizes them within himself, without thinking of any body whatsoever, knows them. I have also perceived with all the senses of my body the numbers of the things we count; but the numbers by which we count are different, and they are not images of these, and for that very reason they truly are. Let anyone who does not see them laugh at me for saying these things, and I will pity him while he laughs at me.

All these things I remember, and I remember how I learned them. I have also heard, and remember, many arguments raised against them with great falsity; and though these are false, it is not false that I remember them. And I also remember that I have distinguished between those truths and the falsehoods raised against them. And I see that distinguishing these things now is one thing, and remembering that I have often distinguished them, when I often thought about them, is another. So I remember that I have often understood these things; and what I now distinguish and understand I store in my memory, so that later on I may remember that I understand it now. So I also remember that I have remembered; just as, if later on I recall that I have been able to remember these things now, it will be by the power of memory that I recall it.

The same memory also contains the affections of my mind, not in the way the mind itself holds them when it is experiencing them, but in a very different way, according to a power that belongs to memory. For I remember that I once rejoiced without rejoicing now, and I recall my past sadness without being sad; I remember without fear that I once was afraid, and I call to mind a past desire without desire. Sometimes, on the contrary, I remember my past sadness with joy, and my past joy with sadness. This is not surprising in the case of the body, for the mind is one thing and the body another. So if I remember some past pain of the body with gladness, it is not so surprising. But this memory is itself the mind (for when we tell someone to keep something in memory, we say, "See that you keep it in mind," and when we forget, we say, "It did not come to mind," and "It slipped my mind," calling the memory itself the mind). Since this is so, how is it that when I remember my past sadness with joy, the mind has joy and the memory has sadness, and the mind is joyful because of the joy that is in it, yet the memory is not sad because of the sadness that is in it? Does the memory perhaps not belong to the mind? Who would say that? The memory, then, is like the stomach of the mind, and joy and sadness are like sweet and bitter food: when they are entrusted to the memory, they have, as it were, passed into the stomach, where they can be stored but cannot be tasted. It is ridiculous to think these are alike; and yet they are not entirely unlike.
