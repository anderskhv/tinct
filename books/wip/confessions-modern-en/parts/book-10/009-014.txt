Is this bodily form not plain to everyone whose senses are sound? Why, then, does it not say the same thing to everyone? Animals, small and great, see it, but they cannot question it, because no reason has been set over their senses to judge what the senses report. But human beings can question it, so that the invisible things of God are clearly seen, being understood through the things that are made; but by loving these things they become subject to them, and those who are subject cannot judge. Nor do created things answer those who question them unless the questioners can judge. Nor do they change their voice (that is, their appearance) if one person merely sees while another both sees and questions, so as to appear one way to the first and another way to the second; rather, appearing the same way to both, it is mute to the one and speaks to the other. Or rather, it speaks to everyone, but only those understand who compare the voice they receive from outside with the truth within. For truth says to me, "Neither heaven nor earth nor any other body is your God." Their very nature says this to anyone who sees them: "They are a mass; a mass is less in any part of it than in the whole." Now I speak to you, my soul: you are my better part, for you quicken the mass of my body, giving it life, which no body can give to a body; but your God is, even to you, the Life of your life.

@@ 10
What, then, do I love when I love my God? Who is He, above the very summit of my soul? By means of my soul itself I will climb up to Him. I will pass beyond the power by which I am joined to my body and fill its whole frame with life. It is not by that power that I find my God; for then the horse and the mule, which have no understanding, might find Him too, since it is the same power by which their bodies also live. But there is another power: not only the one by which I give life to my flesh, but the one by which I give it sense, the flesh that the Lord fashioned for me. He commanded the eye not to hear and the ear not to see, but gave me the eye to see through and the ear to hear through, and to each of the other senses its own proper seat and function; and though these are different, I, the one mind, carry them out through them. I will pass beyond this power of mine as well, for the horse and the mule have this too, since they also perceive through the body.

@@ 11
So I will pass beyond this power of my nature too, climbing by steps toward Him who made me. And I come to the fields and spacious palaces of memory, where the treasures of countless images are kept, brought in from things of every kind that the senses have perceived. Stored there too is whatever we think up besides, by enlarging or shrinking or in any other way altering what the senses have touched; and whatever else has been entrusted to it and laid up there, which forgetfulness has not yet swallowed and buried. When I go in there, I ask for whatever I want to be brought out, and some things come at once; others have to be searched for longer and are dug out, as it were, from some more hidden storeroom; others come rushing out in crowds, and while I am asking and searching for something else, they leap out into the middle as if to say, "Is it us, perhaps?" These I brush away with the hand of my heart from the face of my remembrance, until the thing I want comes out of the mist and steps into view from its hiding place. Other things come up easily and in unbroken order, just as they are called for; those in front make way for those behind, and as they make way they are put back out of sight, ready to come out again when I want them. All of this happens when I recount something from memory.

@@ 12
There all things are kept distinct and sorted by kind, each having come in by its own entrance: light, and all the colors and shapes of bodies, by the eyes; all kinds of sounds by the ears; all smells by the entrance of the nostrils; all tastes by the mouth; and by the sense of the whole body, what is hard or soft, hot or cold, rough, heavy or light, whether outside the body or inside it. All these the great harbor of memory takes in, in its countless hidden and indescribable recesses, to be available and brought out again when needed; each enters by its own door and is laid up there. Yet the things themselves do not go in; only the images of the things perceived are there, ready for thought to call them to mind. And who can say how these images are formed, even though it is plain by which sense each was caught and stored away inside? For even when I sit in darkness and silence, I can bring out colors in my memory if I want, and tell black from white and any others I choose; and sounds do not break in and disturb the image drawn in through my eyes that I am looking over, though they too are there, lying hidden and stored away, as it were, by themselves. For I can call for them as well, and they are there at once. And with my tongue at rest and my throat silent, I can sing as much as I like; and those images of colors, which are nonetheless there, do not push in and interrupt when I am handling another store, the one that flowed in through the ears. In the same way I recall at will the other things brought in and heaped up by the other senses. I can tell the scent of lilies from that of violets while smelling nothing at all; and I prefer honey to sweet wine, and smooth to rough, while at that moment I am neither tasting nor touching anything, but only remembering.

@@ 13
I do these things inside, in the vast hall of my memory. For there heaven, earth and sea are present to me, along with everything in them that I have been able to perceive, except what I have forgotten. There I also meet myself and recall myself, what I have done, when and where, and how I felt when I did it. There is everything I remember, whether from my own experience or on the word of others. From the same store I myself keep weaving new and further likenesses of things, either things I have experienced or things I have believed on the basis of what I have experienced, into what is past; and from them I also infer future actions, events and hopes, and I think over all of these again as if they were present. "I will do this or that," I say to myself in that great recess of my mind, full of the images of so many and such great things, "and this or that will follow." "Oh, if only this or that could be!" "God forbid this or that!" I say these things to myself, and as I say them, the images of everything I speak of are there, from the same treasury of memory; and I could not speak of any of them at all if the images were missing.

@@ 14
Great is this power of memory, exceedingly great, O my God, a vast and boundless inner chamber! Who has ever reached the bottom of it? Yet this is a power of my mind and belongs to my nature, and I myself cannot grasp all that I am. So the mind is too narrow to contain itself. Then where is the part of it that it does not contain? Is it outside the mind and not in it? Then how does the mind not contain it? Great wonder rises up in me at this, and amazement takes hold of me. And people go out to marvel at the heights of mountains, the huge waves of the sea, the broad currents of rivers, the sweep of the ocean and the circling of the stars, and they pass themselves by. They do not wonder that when I spoke of all these things I was not seeing them with my eyes, and yet I could not have spoken of them unless I was actually seeing, inwardly in my memory, the mountains, waves, rivers and stars that I have seen, and the ocean that I believe in, and with the same vast distances between them as if I were seeing them outside. Yet when I saw them with my eyes, I did not swallow them into myself by seeing them; nor are the things themselves with me, only their images. And I know by which sense of the body each one was impressed on me.
