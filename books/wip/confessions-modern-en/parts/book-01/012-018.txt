Moving on from infancy, I came to boyhood; or rather, boyhood came to me and took the place of infancy. Yet infancy did not leave (for where would it have gone?), and still it was there no longer. I was no longer an infant without speech; I was now a boy who could talk. This I remember, and later I worked out how I learned to talk. It was not that my elders taught me words by setting them before me in some fixed method, as they taught me my letters a little later. Instead I wanted to express what was in my heart by cries, by broken sounds and by all sorts of movements of my limbs, so that I could get my way, and yet I could not express everything I wanted, or to everyone I wanted. So, with the understanding You gave me, my God, I myself practiced the sounds in my memory. When people named something, and turned toward it as they spoke, I saw and held on to the fact that the sound they made was their name for the thing they meant to point out. That they meant this thing and not another was clear from the movement of their bodies, which is a kind of natural language shared by all peoples: the expression of the face, the glance of the eyes, the gestures of the limbs and the tone of the voice, which show how the mind feels as it seeks, holds, rejects or avoids things. In this way, by hearing words over and over in their places in different sentences, I gradually gathered what they stood for; and once I had trained my mouth to these signs, I used them to say what I wanted. So I exchanged with the people around me these everyday signs of what we wanted, and ventured further into the stormy company of human life, while still depending on my parents' authority and at the beck and call of my elders.

@@ 13
O God, my God, what misery and what mockery I went through then! As a boy I was told that the right way to live was to obey my teachers, so that I might get on in this world and excel in the arts of the tongue, which serve to win the "praise of men" and deceitful riches. Next I was sent to school to learn to read and write, and I had no idea, poor wretch, what use any of it was; yet if I was lazy about learning, I was beaten. Our forefathers thought this was right, and many who had gone through the same life before us had laid out the weary paths we were made to walk, piling up toil and grief for the sons of Adam. But we discovered, Lord, that people prayed to You, and from them we learned to think of You, as well as we were able, as someone great, who could hear us and help us even though You were hidden from our senses. So, as a boy, I began to pray to You, my help and my refuge. I broke the knots that tied my tongue in order to call on You, and small as I was, I begged You with no small feeling not to let me be beaten at school. And when You did not hear me (not that this was to hand me over to folly), the grown-ups, even my own parents, who wanted no harm to come to me, laughed at my beatings, which were then my great and heavy misfortune.

@@ 14
Is there anyone, Lord, so great of soul, clinging to You with such intense love (for a kind of stupidity can do the same, after a fashion); is there anyone, I say, whom devout attachment to You has filled with so great a spirit that he can think as lightly of the rack and the hooks and other such tortures (which people all over the world beg You in great dread to spare them), while loving those who fear them most bitterly, as our parents made light of the torments our teachers inflicted on us as boys? For we feared those torments no less than they, and prayed to You no less to escape them. And yet we did sin, by writing or reading or studying less than was demanded of us. It was not, Lord, that we lacked memory or ability; by Your will we had enough of both for our age. But what we loved was play, and for that we were punished by people who were doing exactly the same thing themselves. Only, the idleness of grown-ups is called "business," while that of boys, which is really the same, is punished by those grown-ups; and no one feels sorry for the boys, or for the men either. Would any sound judge approve of my being beaten as a boy because I played ball, and so made slower progress in the studies I was to learn only so that as a man I could play a more disgraceful game? And what else was the man who beat me doing? If some fellow teacher got the better of him in a trivial dispute, he was more eaten up with bitterness and envy than I was when a playmate beat me at ball.

@@ 15
And yet I sinned in this, O Lord God, Creator of all things in nature and the One who orders them, but of sin only the One who orders it: O Lord my God, I sinned by going against the orders of my parents and of those teachers. For whatever their motive in wanting me to learn, I could have put that learning to good use later. I disobeyed them not because I had chosen something better, but because I loved play: I loved the proud thrill of winning my contests, and I loved having my ears tickled by lying stories, which only made them itch the more; and the same curiosity shone more and more from my eyes, eager for the shows and games of my elders. Yet the people who put on these shows are held in such esteem that nearly everyone wants the same for their own children; and still they are quite willing to see those children beaten if the same shows keep them from the studies by which they hope they will grow up to put on such shows themselves. Look on these things with pity, Lord, and deliver us who call on You now; deliver also those who do not yet call on You, so that they may call on You and You may deliver them.

@@ 16
So, as a boy, I had already heard of eternal life, promised to us through the humility of the Lord our God, who stooped down to our pride; and from my mother's womb, since she put great hope in You, I was already being signed with the mark of His cross and seasoned with His salt. You saw, Lord, how once, when I was still a boy, I was suddenly seized by a pain in my stomach and came close to dying. You saw, my God, since You were already my guardian, how eagerly and with what faith I begged the devotion of my mother, and of Your Church, the mother of us all, for the baptism of Your Christ, my God and Lord. The mother of my flesh was deeply alarmed, since with a heart pure in Your faith she was laboring even more lovingly to bring my salvation to birth. She would have rushed to have me consecrated and cleansed by the health-giving sacraments, confessing You, Lord Jesus, for the remission of sins, if I had not suddenly recovered. So my cleansing was postponed, as though I would have to be soiled again if I lived, because after that washing the stains of sin would bring a greater and more dangerous guilt. At that time I already believed, as did my mother and the whole household, apart from my father; yet he did not overcome the power of my mother's piety in me, so that I should not believe just as he did not yet believe. For she was eager that You, my God, rather than he, should be my father; and in this You helped her to win out over her husband, whom she obeyed although she was the better of the two, since in this too she was obeying You, who have commanded it.

@@ 17
I ask You, my God, and I would like to know, if You would like it too, why my baptism was put off at that time. Was it for my good that the reins were, so to speak, loosened on me so that I could sin? Or were they not loosened? If they were not, why do we still hear it said on every side, of one person or another, "Leave him alone, let him do what he likes; he is not baptized yet"? Yet where the health of the body is concerned, no one says, "Let him be wounded even more; he is not healed yet." How much better it would have been, then, if I had been healed at once, and if my family and I had taken care that my soul, once its health was restored, was kept safe in the keeping of You who gave it. Better, truly. But how many great waves of temptation seemed to hang over me once boyhood was past! My mother foresaw them, and she chose to expose to them the clay from which I might later be shaped, rather than the finished likeness itself.

@@ 18
Even in boyhood, though, when far less was feared for me than in my youth, I did not love my studies, and I hated being forced into them. Yet I was forced, and that was good for me; but I did not do good, for I would not have learned unless I had been made to. No one does good against his will, even if what he does is good. Nor did those who forced me do good; the good I received came from You, my God. For they gave no thought to what use I would make of what they forced me to learn, beyond satisfying the insatiable desires of a wealthy poverty and a shameful glory. But You, by whom the very hairs of our head are numbered, used the error of all those who pressed me to learn for my benefit; and my own error, in refusing to learn, You used for my punishment, a fitting penalty for one who was so small a boy and so great a sinner. So by means of those who did not do good, You did good to me, and by means of my own sin You justly punished me. For You have commanded, and so it is, that every disordered affection should be its own punishment.
