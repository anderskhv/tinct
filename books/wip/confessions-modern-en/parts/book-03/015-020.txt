It is the same with crimes of violence, where there is a desire to hurt, whether by insult or by injury. These are done either for revenge, as one enemy against another; or to gain some advantage that belongs to someone else, as the robber against the traveler; or to escape some evil, as against someone who is feared; or out of envy, as the less fortunate against the more fortunate, or as someone who has done well in some matter against a person he fears may become his equal, or resents already being his equal; or simply for the pleasure of another's pain, as with those who watch gladiators, or who laugh at and mock others. These are the heads of wickedness that sprout from the lust of the flesh, of the eye, or of power, whether one of them alone, or two together, or all three at once. And so people live badly, against the three and the seven, that psaltery of ten strings, Your Ten Commandments, O God most high and most sweet. But what shameful acts can there be against You, who cannot be defiled? Or what acts of violence against You, who cannot be harmed? Yet You punish what people commit against themselves, because even when they sin against You, they act wickedly against their own souls, and iniquity lies to itself. It does this by corrupting and perverting their nature, which You made and ordered, or by using permitted things without restraint, or by burning for forbidden things, for that use which is against nature. Or they are found guilty of raging against You in heart and word, kicking against the goads. Or they break through the boundaries of human society and boldly delight in private alliances or divisions, depending on what has pleased or offended them. These things happen when You are abandoned, O Fountain of Life, who are the one and true Creator and Ruler of the universe, and when, out of self-willed pride, some single false thing is picked out from the whole and loved. So it is by humble devotion that we return to You. And You cleanse us of our evil habits, and are merciful to the sins of those who confess, and hear the groaning of the prisoners, and free us from the chains we have made for ourselves, provided that we no longer raise against You the horns of a false freedom, losing everything through greed for more, by loving our own private good more than You, the Good of all.

Among these shameful acts, these crimes of violence and all these many sins, there are also the sins of people who are, on the whole, making progress. Those who judge rightly condemn these sins by the standard of perfection, yet praise the people themselves in hope of the harvest to come, as with the green blade of growing corn. And there are some acts that look like shameful acts or crimes of violence and yet are not sins, because they offend neither You, our Lord God, nor human society. This happens, for example, when things suited to a particular time are acquired for the needs of life, and we cannot tell whether it was done out of a lust for possessing; or when wrongs are punished by lawful authority for the sake of correction, and we cannot tell whether it was done out of a lust for hurting. So many actions that people disapprove of are approved by Your testimony, and many that people praise are condemned with You as witness, because the outward look of the deed, the mind of the one who does it, and the hidden needs of the moment often differ. But when You suddenly command something unusual and unforeseen, even something You once forbade, and for the time being keep the reason for Your command hidden, and even if it goes against the agreement of some human society, who can doubt that it must be done, since only that human society is just which serves You? But blessed are those who know that You have commanded it! For all things were done by Your servants either to show something needed for the present, or to foretell things to come.

@@ 17
Knowing nothing of this, I mocked those holy servants and prophets of Yours. And what did I achieve by mocking them, except to be mocked by You? Little by little, without noticing, I was led on into such nonsense as believing that a fig weeps when it is picked, and that the tree, its mother, sheds milky tears. Yet if some Manichaean saint ate that fig (picked, of course, by someone else's guilt, not his own), it would be mixed into his insides, and he would breathe angels out of it; indeed, particles of God would burst out of him with every sigh and belch as he prayed. Those particles of the most high and true God would have stayed bound up in that fig, unless they had been set free by the teeth and belly of some "Elect" saint! And I, wretched as I was, believed that more mercy should be shown to the fruits of the earth than to human beings, for whose sake they were made. For if someone who was hungry, and not a Manichaean, asked for some, the mouthful given to him would seem, as it were, condemned to capital punishment.

And You reached out Your hand from above and drew my soul out of that deep darkness, while my mother, Your faithful one, wept to You for me more than mothers weep over the bodily deaths of their children. For by the faith and the spirit she had from You, she saw the death in which I lay; and You heard her, O Lord. You heard her and did not despise her tears when they poured down and watered the ground beneath her eyes in every place where she prayed; yes, You heard her. For where else did that dream come from by which You comforted her, so that she agreed to live with me and eat at the same table in the house? She had begun to refuse this, turning away from and detesting the blasphemies of my error. For she saw herself standing on a kind of wooden rule, and a radiant young man coming toward her, cheerful and smiling at her, while she was grieving and worn out with grief. He asked her the reasons for her sorrow and her daily tears (in order to teach, as such beings usually do, not to learn), and she answered that she was mourning my ruin. Then he told her to set her mind at rest, and urged her to look closely and see "that where she was, there I was too." And when she looked, she saw me standing beside her on the same rule. Where did this come from, if not from the fact that Your ears were turned toward her heart? O You, the Good and all-powerful, who care for each one of us as though You cared for that one alone, and for all as though each were the only one!

@@ 19
And where did this come from too? When she had told me about this vision, and I tried to twist it to mean "that she should rather not despair of one day being what I was," she replied at once, without any hesitation: "No. I was not told, 'Where he is, there you are too,' but, 'Where you are, there he is too.'" I confess to You, O Lord, as far as I can remember (and I have often spoken of this), that Your answer through my mother when she was awake moved me even then more than the dream itself. She was not thrown off by the plausibility of my false interpretation, and she saw so quickly what there was to see, which I certainly had not seen before she said it. And it was by that dream that a joy for this holy woman, to be fulfilled so long afterward, was foretold so long beforehand, to console her in her present distress. For almost nine years followed, in which I wallowed in the mud of that deep pit and in the darkness of falsehood, often trying to rise but being dashed down all the harder. All that time that chaste, devout and sober widow, the kind You love, now more cheerful in hope but no less earnest in her weeping and mourning, never stopped, at every hour of her prayers, lamenting over me to You. And her prayers entered Your presence; yet You still let me be tangled and tangled again in that darkness.

@@ 20
Meanwhile You gave her another answer, which I remember. For I pass over much, hurrying on to the things that press me more urgently to confess to You, and much I do not remember. You gave her, then, another answer through a priest of Yours, a certain bishop brought up in Your Church and well trained in Your books. When this woman begged him to be so kind as to talk with me, refute my errors, unteach me what was bad and teach me what was good (for he used to do this when he happened to find people suited to receive it), he refused. That was wise of him, as I later came to see. For he answered that I was still unteachable, being puffed up with the novelty of that heresy, and that I had already unsettled a number of untrained people with tricky little questions, as she herself had told him. "But leave him alone for a while," he said. "Just pray to God for him. By reading he will find out for himself what that error is, and how great its impiety." At the same time he told her how he himself, as a little boy, had been handed over to the Manichees by his misled mother, and had not only read almost all their books but had even copied them out many times; and how it had become clear to him, without anyone arguing with him or convincing him, how much that sect ought to be avoided; and so he had avoided it. When he had said this and she still would not be satisfied, but pressed him harder, pleading with him and weeping many tears that he would see me and talk with me, he became a little irritated with her persistence and said, "Go now, and God bless you; it is impossible that the son of these tears should perish." She often recalled in her conversations with me that she had taken this answer as if it had sounded from heaven.
