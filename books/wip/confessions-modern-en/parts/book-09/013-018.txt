Then, when the time came for me to hand in my name, we left the country and went back to Milan. Alypius also decided to be born again in You along with me. He was already clothed in the humility that suits Your Sacraments, and he was so tough a master of his body that, with unusual daring, he walked the frozen ground of Italy in bare feet. We also took with us the boy Adeodatus, born of my sin according to the flesh. You had made him well. He was not quite fifteen, and in intelligence he outdid many serious and learned men. I confess Your gifts to You, O Lord my God, Creator of all, who are abundantly able to reshape what is misshapen in us; for I had no part in that boy except the sin. As for our bringing him up in Your discipline, it was You, and no one else, who inspired us to do it. I confess Your gifts to You. There is a book of ours called The Teacher; it is a dialogue between him and me. You know that all the ideas given there to the person talking with me were his, when he was in his sixteenth year. I found many other things in him, still more remarkable. His gifts filled me with awe. And who but You could be the craftsman of such wonders? Soon You took his life from the earth, and I now remember him without anxiety, fearing nothing for his childhood or his youth or for him at all. We took him with us, the same age as ourselves in grace, to be brought up in Your discipline. And we were baptized, and the anxiety about our past life fled from us. In those days I could not have enough of the wonderful sweetness of reflecting on the depth of Your counsels for the salvation of the human race. How I wept at Your Hymns and Canticles, moved to the core by the voices of Your sweetly singing Church! The voices flowed into my ears, and the Truth was distilled into my heart. From it the feelings of my devotion overflowed, and tears ran down, and I was happy in them.

@@ 14
The Church of Milan had not long before begun to use this kind of consolation and encouragement, with the brothers singing together eagerly, in harmony of voice and heart. It was a year, or not much more, since Justina, mother of the boy Emperor Valentinian, had persecuted Your servant Ambrose for the sake of her heresy, into which she had been led by the Arians. The devout people kept watch in the church, ready to die with their Bishop, Your servant. There my mother, Your handmaid, who carried a leading share of those anxieties and vigils, lived on prayer. We ourselves, not yet warmed by the heat of Your Spirit, were still stirred by the sight of the city in alarm and turmoil. It was then that hymns and psalms first began to be sung after the manner of the Eastern Churches, so that the people would not lose heart through the weariness of grief. From that day to this the custom has been kept, and many, indeed almost all, of Your congregations in other parts of the world have followed it.

@@ 15
Then in a vision You revealed to the Bishop I have named the place where the bodies of the martyrs Gervasius and Protasius lay hidden. You had kept them uncorrupted for so many years in Your secret treasury, so that at the right time You could bring them out to check the fury of a woman, though an Empress. For when they had been found and dug up, and were being moved with due honor to the Ambrosian Basilica, not only were people tormented by unclean spirits healed, with the demons themselves confessing it, but a certain man who had been blind for many years, a citizen well known in the city, asked what the people's excited joy was about. When he heard, he leapt up and asked his guide to lead him there. When he had been led there, he begged to be allowed to touch the bier of Your saints, whose death is precious in Your sight, with his handkerchief. He did so, and put it to his eyes, and at once they were opened. From that point the news spread; from that point Your praises glowed and shone; from that point the mind of that enemy, though not brought to the health of believing, was at least turned back from her fury of persecution. Thanks be to You, my God! From where, and to where, have You led my memory, so that I should confess these things to You as well? Great as they are, I had forgotten them and passed them by. And yet even then, when the scent of Your ointments was so fragrant, we did not run after You. That is why I wept all the more while Your Hymns were being sung: I had long sighed for You, and now at last I was breathing You in, as far as breath can come into this house of grass of ours.

@@ 16
You who make people of one mind to live in one house also brought Euodius to join us, a young man from our own town. He had been an officer at court, had been converted to You and baptized before us, and had left the service of the world to enlist in Yours. We were together, and meant to live together in our holy purpose. We looked for the place where we could serve You most usefully, and we were returning to Africa together. On the way, when we had come as far as Ostia, my mother departed this life. I leave out much, because I am hurrying on. Accept my confessions and my thanks, O my God, for countless things I pass over in silence. But I will not leave out whatever my soul brings forth about that handmaid of Yours, who brought me forth both in the flesh, so that I might be born into this temporal light, and in her heart, so that I might be born into eternal Light. It is not her gifts I want to speak of, but Yours in her; for she neither made nor brought herself up. You created her, and her father and mother did not know what kind of person would come from them. It was the scepter of Your Christ, the discipline of Your only Son, in a Christian household, a good member of Your Church, that brought her up in Your fear. Yet for her good training she used to praise not so much her mother's care as that of a certain very old maidservant, who had carried her father about when he was a baby, the way little ones used to be carried on the backs of older girls. Because of this, and because of her great age and excellent character, she was highly respected by the heads of that Christian household. So she was also given charge of her master's daughters, and she looked after them carefully. When it was necessary she restrained them firmly with a holy strictness, and she taught them with serious good sense. Except at the times when they ate their very moderate meals at their parents' table, she would not let them drink even water, however thirsty they were. She was guarding against a bad habit, and she added this sound advice: "You drink water now because you have no wine at your disposal. But when you are married and become mistresses of storerooms and cellars, you will turn up your noses at water, but the habit of drinking will stay with you." By this way of teaching, and by the authority she had, she held back the greediness of childhood and shaped the girls' very thirst to such a fine moderation that what they ought not to want, they did not want.

@@ 17
And yet, as Your handmaid told me, her son, a love of wine had crept up on her. For when her parents, as was the custom, would tell her, as a sober girl, to draw wine from the cask, she would hold the cup under the opening and, before pouring the wine into the jug, take a small sip with the tip of her lips; her natural feelings would not let her take more. She did this not from any craving for drink, but from the high spirits of youth, which boil over into playful mischief, and which in young people are usually kept in check by the seriousness of their elders. And so, by adding a little each day to that little (for whoever despises small things will fall little by little), she had slipped into such a habit that she would greedily drain her small cup when it was almost full of wine. Where was that wise old woman then, and her strict forbidding? Would anything be any use against a hidden disease, if Your healing hand, O Lord, did not watch over us? Her father, her mother and her nurses were absent, but You were present, You who create, who call, and who even through those set over us work something toward the salvation of our souls. What did You do then, O my God? How did You cure her? How did You heal her? Did You not draw out of another soul a hard, sharp taunt, like a surgeon's knife from Your secret store, and with one stroke cut away all that rotten matter? For a maidservant who used to go down to the cellar with her got into a quarrel, as happens, with her young mistress when they were alone, and threw this fault in her face with the most bitter insult, calling her a drunkard. Stung to the quick by that taunt, she saw how ugly her fault was, and at once condemned it and gave it up. Just as flattering friends corrupt us, so insulting enemies often correct us. Yet You repay them not for what You do through them, but for what they themselves intended. For the servant in her anger wanted to hurt her young mistress, not to cure her, and she did it in private, either because the time and place of the quarrel happened to find them alone, or because she was afraid she herself would be blamed for having revealed it so late. But You, Lord, Ruler of all things in heaven and on earth, who turn to Your own purposes the deepest currents of the torrent, the ordered turbulence of the flow of the ages, healed one soul through the very sickness of another. You did it so that no one who notices this should credit his own power when someone else he wanted to see reformed is reformed through his words.

@@ 18
So she was brought up modestly and soberly, made subject to her parents by You rather than to You by her parents. As soon as she was old enough to marry, she was given to a husband, and she served him as her lord. She did all she could to win him for You, preaching You to him by the way she lived. By this You adorned her, and made her someone her husband respected, loved and admired. She bore his unfaithfulness to her bed in such a way that she never had a quarrel with her husband about it. For she was waiting for Your mercy upon him, hoping that he would believe in You and become chaste. Besides this, he was hot-tempered, as warm in his anger as in his affection. But she had learned not to oppose an angry husband, not only in what she did but not even in what she said. Only when he had calmed down and was quiet, and in a mood to listen, would she explain what she had done, if it happened that he had taken offense too hastily. In short, many married women whose husbands were gentler than hers still bore shameful marks of blows on their faces, and when they talked among friends they would blame their husbands' behavior. She would blame their tongues, and give them serious advice as if in jest: "From the time you heard the marriage contract read out to you, you should have regarded it as a deed of service, by which you became servants. So, remembering your position, you ought not to set yourselves up against your lords." And they knew what a violent-tempered husband she put up with. So they were amazed that no one had ever heard, or seen any sign, that Patricius had beaten his wife, or that there had been any quarrel at home between them for even a single day. When they asked her privately how this could be, she taught them the practice I have just described. The wives who followed it found it good and thanked her; those who did not follow it found no relief and went on suffering.
