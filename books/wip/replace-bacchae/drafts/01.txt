Dionysus. (Dionysus enters.) I have arrived here in Thebes: Dionysus, the son of Zeus, whom Semelê, Kadmus' daughter, bore when lightning drove her into labour. I have exchanged my divine appearance for a human one and stand beside the springs of Dirkê and the waters of Ismenus. Near the palace I can see my mother's grave, where the thunderbolt struck her. The wreckage of her house still smoulders with Zeus's fire, the enduring mark of Hera's violence against my mother. Kadmus has rightly made this ground, his daughter's burial place, a sanctuary. I myself have enclosed it in the thick green growth of vines. I left the gold-rich fields of Lydia and Phrygia and travelled across the sun-scorched Persian country, past Bactrian fortresses and the storm-beaten land of Media, onwards through blessed Araby and all Asia beside the salt sea, with its crowds of lofty-towered cities where Hellenes live among foreign peoples. Of the cities of Hellas, this is the first I visit. In those other countries I established my dances and ceremonies to reveal myself to people as a God. Here in Hellas, Thebes was the first place I stirred with my triumphant cry, dressing her in fawnskin and putting the ivy-covered thyrsus into her hand. For my mother's sisters—shame upon them!—declared that Zeus had never fathered Dionysus. Semelê, they said, had slept with a mortal and blamed Zeus for her own lust, a clever deception devised by Kadmus! That was why, they boasted, Zeus killed the lying woman who claimed him as her lover. So I have driven them from home with the sting of madness. They live distracted in the mountains, compelled to wear the clothing of my celebrations. Every woman of Kadmus' people, every one, I drove raving out of her house. They sit among Kadmus' sons beneath green pine trees on exposed rocks. Thebes must learn, however unwillingly, what it means to remain outside my sacred mysteries. I must defend Semelê's honour, revealing to mortals the God she bore to Zeus. Now Kadmus has handed his crown and royal authority to Pentheus, another daughter's son. In attacking me he fights heaven itself: he excludes me from his offerings of wine and leaves my name out of his prayers. I will therefore demonstrate my divinity to him and to all Thebes. Once I have triumphed here, I will move to another country and reveal myself there. If the angry Thebans take up weapons to drive their Bacchants from the mountains, I will lead my Maenads against them in battle. That is why I have assumed a mortal body and changed my appearance to a man's. Women who left Tmolus, Lydia's wall of rock, my company of revellers whom I brought from foreign homes to share my travels and my rest, raise the cymbals invented by great Mother Rhea and myself, the instruments of Phrygian towns. Surround Pentheus' royal palace and strike them so that Kadmus' city may witness it. I will go into Kithairon's valleys, where my Bacchanals are living, and dance with them there. (He leaves. The Chorus enters, waving thyrsus-wands and striking their timbrels.)
