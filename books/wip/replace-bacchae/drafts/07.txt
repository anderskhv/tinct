Dionysus. Women of Asia, has pure terror overwhelmed you so completely that you have collapsed on the ground? I suppose you saw Pentheus' palace sway when Bacchus shook it. Come, get up from the earth and shake this bewildering fear out of your bodies.

Chorus. Welcome, brightest light of our Evian celebrations! I was so lonely and abandoned; what joy it is to see you now!

Dionysus. Did fear make your hearts fail when I went inside? Did you think I would sink into darkness, trapped in Pentheus' prison?

Chorus. Why wouldn't we? What protection would I have if disaster overtook you? But how did you escape from that godless tyrant's power?

Dionysus. I set myself free, quite easily, without any labour or struggle.

Chorus. But didn't he fasten your hands in layer upon layer of chains?

Dionysus. I made him ridiculous. He imagined he was binding me, but never touched or held me; he was feeding on an empty illusion. Beside the stalls where he meant to lock me up he found a bull and began looping his ropes round its knees and hoofs. He panted with rage, sweat pouring from his whole body, biting his lips, while I sat calmly beside him and watched at my leisure. Just then our Bacchus arrived. He shook the house as if with an earthquake and kindled a sudden fire upon his mother's grave. The king thought his palace was burning. He rushed everywhere, frantically ordering his servants to fetch water. Every slave was busy with that useless task. Then he abandoned it, thinking I had escaped his trap, and rushed into the building with his deadly sword drawn. Bromius, I believe—I can only tell you what I think—created a phantom inside the house. Pentheus immediately threw himself upon it, charging and stabbing the luminous air, convinced he was killing me. Then Bacchus dealt his pride another humiliation by throwing the building to the ground. It lies there now in ruins, a sight to make him bitterly regret my chains! Exhausted by his efforts, he drops his sword and collapses. A mortal dared to challenge a God in battle! I then walked out unhindered, without troubling myself about Pentheus, and have come from his palace to you. But I think he will be out here soon: I can hear footsteps inside. What will he say about this? However fiercely he rages, I shall take his blustering lightly. Wisdom means keeping anger under sober control. (Pentheus enters.)

Pentheus. What an outrage! The stranger has escaped, although a moment ago his chains held him as firmly as fate. Ha! There he is! What is going on? How did you get out to stand in front of my own palace?

Dionysus. Stay where you are, and slow the pace of your anger.

Pentheus. How did you get out here after escaping from your chains?

Dionysus. Didn't you hear me say, "Someone will set me free"?

Pentheus. Who? Your answers grow stranger every time you speak.

Dionysus. The one who makes the grape-bearing vine grow for humankind.

Pentheus. Yes, who sends women raving out of their houses!

Dionysus. What you ridicule is the very glory of Dionysus.

Pentheus (to attendants). My order is to bar every tower around us.

Dionysus. What for? Can the Gods not cross over walls?

Pentheus. Clever, so clever—except in the things where you need wisdom.

Dionysus. I have wisdom where it matters most. But first listen to that man and hear his account. He has come with news from the mountains. I will wait for you; don't worry that I might run away. (A Herdman enters.)

Herdman. Pentheus, ruler of this country of Thebes, I have come from Kithairon, where the glittering silver arrows of the snow never fail.

Pentheus. What important news have you come here to bring?

Herdman. I have seen the frenzied Bacchanals whose white feet carried them away from this country. King, I want to tell you and Thebes what extraordinary and astonishing things they are doing. But first tell me whether I may speak freely about what I saw or must shorten the sail of my story. I fear your hasty temper, your passion and your anger that exceeds even a king's measure.

Pentheus. Speak; you will leave unharmed by me. Honest men must not be met with anger. The more dreadful your account of the Bacchanals, the more severely I will punish the man who taught our women this wickedness.

Herdman. Your grazing cattle were climbing the steep hillside just as the sun first sent out its warming rays, when I saw three companies of Bacchant women. Autonoë led one, your mother Agavê another, and Ino the third. They all lay sleeping, their bodies resting easily. Some leaned back on leafy pine branches; others lay carelessly on the ground with oak leaves under their heads. They were modest, not drunk on wine, as you claim, nor chasing desire alone through the woodland shade to the sighing of flutes. When the lowing of our horned cattle reached her, your mother leapt up among the Bacchanals and shouted, "Shake off your sleep!" Brushing rosy sleep from their eyelids, they sprang up, wonderfully graceful to see: young girls, elderly women and unmarried maidens. First they let their hair fall over their shoulders. Those whose fawnskins had come unfastened tied them up, girdling the spotted hides with snakes that licked their cheeks. Some held fawns or wolf cubs in their arms and fed these wild creatures with their own white breast milk. They were young mothers who had left their babies, their breasts still full. Then they crowned their heads with ivy, oak leaves and star-flowered bryony. One woman struck the rock with her thyrsus and a fountain burst out in a shower of spray. Another drove her reed-wand into the earth and the God sent a spring of wine up through it. Those who wanted a white, foaming drink scratched the ground with their fingertips, and milk poured out without limit. Sweet streams of honey meanwhile dripped from their ivy staffs. Had you seen it, you would have prayed to the God you now reject. Then we, your cattlemen and shepherds, gathered to discuss these marvellous, awe-inspiring deeds. One man, who often strayed into town and had a ready tongue, called to us all: "You who live on the terraces of these holy mountains, shall we do our master a favour and chase his mother Agavê out of Bacchus' revels?" We thought that good advice and hid in ambush among the leafy thickets. At the appointed time the women waved their thyrsi for the ceremonies, calling together on Iacchus, King of Clamour, child of Zeus. The mountains and every wild creature were swept into ecstasy; nothing remained unshaken as they rushed on. Agavê happened to leap close to me. I sprang from the thicket where I was hiding and tried to seize her. She shouted, "Ho, my swift-footed hounds, these men are hunting us! Follow me, follow, carrying your thyrsi as spears!" We ran and barely escaped being torn apart by the Bacchanals. Without swords they swooped on our grazing cattle. You would have seen your mother clutching a loudly bellowing heifer with heavy udders, while other women tore calves into bloody strips. Ribs and cloven hoofs flew in every direction; pieces of flesh hung dripping blood beneath the pines. Bulls that had been snorting and glaring fiercely along their horns were tripped and thrown down, dragged to earth by countless clutching hands of girls. The flesh was stripped from their bones faster than you could close your royal eyes. Then they swept on, racing like birds in flight towards the lowland fields along Asopus that yield rich harvests for Thebes: Hysiae and Erythrae, lying beneath Kithairon's cliffs. They descended like an enemy army, throwing possessions everywhere and snatching babies out of houses. Whatever they put on their shoulders stayed there without being tied; nothing fell to the dark ground, neither bronze nor iron. They carried fire on their hair without burning. Furious at being plundered, the inhabitants took up weapons against the Bacchanals. Then, King, we saw something astonishing. The men's iron-pointed javelins drew no blood from them, but the women hurled their thyrsus-staves and inflicted wound after wound. Women drove men into flight, for a God's power was at work. Afterwards they returned to the place they had left, to the springs the God had provided, and washed off the gore. The snakes licked the drops of blood from their cheeks. So, my lord, whoever this God may be, welcome him into the city. Besides his other powers, I hear people say he gave humanity the vine that soothes sorrow. Where wine is absent, Love too disappears, and no other joy remains for human beings.

Chorus. I am afraid to speak freely before the king, but I will still say what I think: Dionysus stands equal to every God.

Pentheus. This Bacchanal outrage is spreading over us like fire, making us a disgrace throughout Greece! There is no time to waste. Go to Electra's gate and summon all my shield-bearing soldiers, the riders of swift horses, those who wield bucklers and those who draw the bow. They are to assemble here with me. We shall march against the Bacchanals. Enduring what these women are doing to us would exceed every limit.

Dionysus. You hear my words, Pentheus, yet you will not give way at all. Even though you insult me, I warn you: remain here and do not take up arms against a God. Bromius will not tolerate your driving his Bacchanals from the mountains where they celebrate.

Pentheus. Don't lecture me. Be satisfied that you escaped your chains, or I shall punish you again.

Dionysus. Better offer him sacrificial victims than rage and kick against the goads, a mortal fighting a God.

Pentheus. Victims? Yes, women, slaughtered as they deserve! Savage killing in the valleys of Kithairon!

Dionysus. You will all run away. What a disgrace when bronze shields retreat before the Bacchanals' wands.

Pentheus. Wrestling with this stranger gets us nowhere. Whether acting or suffering, he refuses to be quiet.

Dionysus. My friend, even now this evil could become something good.

Pentheus. How? By making myself my slave-women's slave?

Dionysus. I can bring the women here without using weapons.

Pentheus. Ha! You are laying a treacherous trap for me here!

Dionysus. A trap? I am trying to save you with my skill!

Pentheus. You have all agreed on this so that your revels can continue forever.

Dionysus. No; understand that I made this agreement with the God.

Pentheus (to attendant). Bring me my weapons! And you, stop talking.

Dionysus. Listen! Would you like to see their camp up in the hills?

Pentheus. Yes, even if the sight cost me an immeasurable fortune in gold.

Dionysus. Why has this longing taken such a powerful hold on you?

Pentheus. To see them drunk on wine—what a bitter spectacle!

Dionysus. Yet a bitter spectacle is something you would enjoy seeing?

Pentheus. Indeed, crouching silently beneath the pine trees to watch them.

Dionysus. However secretly you approach, they will track you down.

Pentheus. Then openly! Yes, that was a useful thing to tell me.

Dionysus. Shall I guide you there? Will you attempt the journey?

Pentheus. Hurry and lead me; I resent every moment of delay!

Dionysus. First you must dress yourself in robes of fine linen.

Pentheus. Why? Am I to stop being a man and become a woman?

Dionysus. Otherwise they may see a man there and kill you.

Pentheus. Good advice. Yes, you have been shrewd about this before.

Dionysus. It was Dionysus who taught me that knowledge.

Pentheus. How do I put this good advice of yours into practice?

Dionysus. I will go inside your palace and dress you there.

Pentheus. Dress me in what? Women's clothes? No, that would shame me.

Dionysus. Have you lost your desire to watch the Maenads now?

Pentheus. Tell me exactly what clothing you intend to put on my body.

Dionysus. I shall adorn your head with long, flowing hair.

Pentheus. And after that, what else will I be wearing?

Dionysus. Long robes, and a coif to cover your head as well.

Pentheus. Is there nothing else you mean to add to my outfit?

Dionysus. A thyrsus to hold, and a spotted fawnskin to wear.

Pentheus. I cannot put a woman's dress around my body!

Dionysus. Then fighting the Maenads will mean bloodshed for you.

Pentheus. Yes, that's true. I must go and observe them first.

Dionysus. Certainly wiser than chasing one disaster into another disaster.

Pentheus. But how can I cross Kadmus' city without being noticed?

Dionysus. We will take deserted paths; I will guide you myself.

Pentheus. Anything would be better than the Bacchants laughing at me.

Dionysus. I will go inside and arrange what is needed.

Pentheus. Very well. My decision is made; I see my course clearly. I am going in. Either I must march out carrying my sword or I must follow the advice you have given. (He leaves.)

Dionysus. Women, he is stepping into the trap. He will find the Bacchants, and death as his punishment. Dionysus, you are close at hand; do your part now and let us take revenge. First strike his mind with a sudden madness. While sane, he would never agree to wear a woman's dress; driven out of his reason, he will put it on. I also long to make him Thebes' laughingstock, led through the city looking like a woman after all his terrifying threats. I am going to put on Pentheus the clothes he will wear to Hades when his mother's hands have killed him. Then he will know Dionysus, Zeus's son, at last revealed as a God most terrible, yet most gentle to humankind. (He leaves.)
