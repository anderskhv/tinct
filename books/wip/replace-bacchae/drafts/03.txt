Teiresias. Ho, gatekeeper! Call Kadmus out of the house, Agenor's son who came from Sidon and surrounded this Theban city with towers. Someone go and tell him Teiresias wants him. He knows why I am here: the agreement one old man made with another still older, to wind ivy round our thyrsi, put on fawnskins and crown our heads with ivy wreaths. (Kadmus enters.)

Kadmus. Dear friend, I heard your voice inside the house and recognised it: wisdom speaking with a wise man's voice. Here I am, ready and dressed in the God's clothing. Dionysus is my own daughter's son, and he has revealed his divinity to mankind. We must do everything in our power to honour him. Where shall we dance, where set our feet and shake our grey heads? Teach me, Teiresias; let one old man guide another, for you are wise. Night and day I can strike the ground with my thyrsus without growing tired. Joy makes us forget how old we are.

Teiresias. My heart feels exactly as yours does. I too feel young; I will try the dance.

Kadmus. Then let us ride in a chariot to the mountain.

Teiresias. No, that would lessen the honour we give the God!

Kadmus. One old man leading another, I will take you along.

Teiresias. We will feel no weariness; the God will guide us there.

Kadmus. Will we be the only Thebans dancing for Bacchus?

Teiresias. Yes; wisdom belongs to us alone. All the others are fools.

Kadmus. We have delayed long enough. Come, take hold of my hand.

Teiresias. Here it is; link your hand firmly with mine.

Kadmus. Mortal as I am, I will never look down on the Gods!

Teiresias. The Gods are beyond our reasoning. We hold to the traditions of our fathers, ancient as time, and no argument will overthrow them, however subtle the minds that produce it. Someone may say I show no respect for my age by putting on ivy and preparing to dance. But the God has made no distinction between the dancing of the young and of those bent by years. From everyone equally he demands the honour owed to him. He has no wish to receive only half his due.

Kadmus. Since your eyes cannot see the daylight, Teiresias, I will speak for you as circumstances require. Here comes Pentheus, Echion's son, hurrying towards the palace; I gave him the throne. How agitated he is! What strange news can he have? (Pentheus enters.)

Pentheus. While I was away from this country I heard of extraordinary misconduct in my own city. Our women have left their homes, pretending to be possessed by Bacchus, and wander wildly over the wooded hills, dancing in honour of this new God Dionysus, whoever he is. In the middle of each company stand bowls filled to the brim with wine. They slip away, some here and some there, to secluded places where they commit shameful acts with men. They claim to be Maenad priestesses, but it is Aphroditê they honour more than Bacchus. My servants are keeping all those I have caught securely chained in the public prison. I will hunt the rest down from the hills: Ino, Agavê who bore me to Echion, and Autonoê, Aktaion's mother. Once iron bonds have caught them, this poisonous Bacchic revelry will soon stop. I hear that a stranger has arrived from Lydia, a conjurer and sorcerer with perfumed golden curls tossed about his head, flushed with wine, his eyes carrying Love's seductive charm. Day and night he keeps company with the girls, pretending to perform Evian mysteries. If I can imprison him inside these walls, that will end his thyrsus-beating and his flowing locks: I will cut his head from his shoulders. He calls Dionysus a God! He says Zeus once sewed him into his thigh, although lightning destroyed him and his mother because she lied about Zeus loving her. Doesn't blasphemy like that deserve the terrible punishment of hanging, whoever this stranger may be? But here is another marvel: Teiresias the prophet, dressed in spotted fawnskins! And my mother's father too—what a laughable spectacle! Shaking a reed-wand! Grandfather, I am ashamed to see grey heads emptied of all sense. Throw away that ivy, drop the thyrsus and free your hand, my mother's father. Teiresias, you persuaded him to do this. You want to force this new God on people so that you can earn money from augury and prophecy! If your white hair did not protect you, you would sit chained among the Bacchanals for introducing these pestilent ceremonies. When the glory of the grape joins a women's feast, I say nothing good comes from their celebrations.

Chorus. What blasphemy! Stranger, have you no respect for heaven or for Kadmus, who sowed the earth-born seed? Echion's son, you disgrace your ancestry!

Teiresias. A wise man readily finds eloquence when his subject is noble. Your tongue runs smoothly enough to sound wise, but nothing you have said makes sense. A reckless man with power and a ready tongue is a bad citizen because he lacks judgement. As for this new God you ridicule, words cannot express the greatness he will attain in Hellas. Prince, two powers stand foremost among humanity. There is divine Demeter: she is Earth, whichever name you choose to give her, and she feeds mortals with solid food. Then comes Semelê's son. To complement her gift he discovered the flowing drink of the grape and gave it to human beings. When that stream of wine fills sorrow-worn people, it rests them from their grief. He gives them sleep too, forgetfulness of everyday suffering; there is no other salve for their toil. Though himself a God, he is poured out as an offering to the Gods, and through him people receive blessings. You mock the tale of his being sewn into Zeus's thigh? Let me show you the beauty of that story. After Zeus rescued the baby from the lightning fire and carried him to Olympus, Hera wanted to cast the divine child out of heaven. Zeus devised a God's countermeasure. He broke off a piece of the air surrounding the earth and shaped it into a hostage, keeping Dionysus safe from Hera's malice. Later people said he had been nursed in Zeus's thigh. They changed the name and made a myth from it, because the God had once served as a hostage to Hera. This God also foretells the future: Bacchic frenzy and ecstasy overflow with prophecy. When his full power floods our bodies, he makes his maddened followers tell what is to come. He shares some of Ares' powers as well. Armoured armies drawn up for battle are struck with panic before they touch a spear. That too is madness sent by Dionysus. You will yet see him on Delphi's cliffs, leaping across the cleft summit with pine torches and waving Bacchus' branch high above him. Yes, he will be great throughout Hellas. Listen to me, Pentheus. Do not boast that sheer force commands humanity, or imagine yourself wise because your distorted vision tells you so. Welcome the God into your country. Pour wine, join his dances and put on a wreath. Dionysus will not impose chastity on women. Restraint in every matter always rests in the nature of true womanhood. Remember this: a woman with a virtuous heart will not be corrupted by his rites. You enjoy it when thousands gather at your gates and all Thebes glorifies the name of Pentheus. He too, I believe, enjoys being honoured. Kadmus and I, the pair you mock, will crown ourselves with ivy and dance. We are two old men, but dance we must. Nothing you say will persuade me to fight the Gods. Your madness is severe; no spell can cure you, even if spells have driven you mad.

Chorus. Old man, your words do honour to Phœbus, and your wisdom pays proper reverence to the mighty God Bromius.

Kadmus. My son, Teiresias has given you good advice. Stay within our customary way of life, beside us. Your mind is wandering in the clouds; your wisdom amounts to nothing. Even if, as you claim, he is no God, call him one. Let a splendid deception give Semelê the reputation of having borne a God, and our whole house will share the honour. Do you remember Aktaion's miserable end? The dogs he had raised tore him apart in the meadows and devoured his raw flesh because he boasted that he hunted better than Artemis. To save you from the same fate, let me crown your head with ivy. Join us in honouring the God.

Pentheus. Take your hand away! Go and play the Bacchanal without smearing me with your foolishness. I will punish this prophet who has taught you to be senseless. Someone go quickly. (To an attendant.) You, hurry to his place of divination. Use levers to wrench it up and throw it down. Turn the whole thing upside down; scatter his holy ribbons to the winds and storms. That will wound him most deeply. Others of you search the city for that stranger with the girl's face, who infects our wives with strange madness and dishonours our beds. If you find him, drag him here in chains. He will be stoned to death and regret ever celebrating his rites in Thebes.

Teiresias. Poor wretch, you have no idea what you have said! You lacked sense before; now you are utterly mad. Kadmus, let us go and pray for him, savage brute though he is, and for Thebes, so that no extraordinary vengeance from the God strikes them. Come with me, carrying your ivy staff. Try to support my body as I support yours. What a disgrace if two greybeards fell! Still, what does that matter? We must serve Bacchus, Zeus's son. Kadmus, take care that Pentheus does not bring his name's echo, repentance, upon your house. It is his own behaviour speaking here, no prophecy. Only a fool speaks foolishly. (They leave.)
