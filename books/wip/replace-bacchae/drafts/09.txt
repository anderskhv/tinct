Dionysus. Pentheus, burning to see forbidden things, so eager in your wrongful eagerness, come outside your palace. Let me see you dressed as a woman, in a frenzied Bacchant's clothes, ready to spy on your mother and her company. (Pentheus enters.) There! You have the appearance of one of Kadmus' daughters.

Pentheus. Ah! I seem to be looking at two suns, two versions of Thebes, our city with seven gates! You look like a bull leading me onwards, and horns have grown upon your head. Were you an animal before? You are certainly a bull now!

Dionysus. The God is accompanying us. He was unfriendly before, but now he is our ally; you see as you should at last.

Pentheus. Who do I look like? Don't I have Ino's bearing, or the way my mother Agavê carries herself?

Dionysus. When I look at you, I seem to see the women themselves. But what's this? A lock has slipped out of position, away from where I braided it beneath your coif.

Pentheus. I disarranged it inside, shaking my head backwards and forwards as I whirled in Bacchic frenzy.

Dionysus. Since I have taken on the work of your lady's maid, I will put it right. Lift your head.

Pentheus. There you are; smooth it into place. I am relying on you.

Dionysus. Your belt is loose now, and the folds of your dress do not hang evenly below your ankles.

Pentheus. Yes, it looks wrong beside my right foot. On the left the robe hangs straight beside my ankle tendon.

Dionysus. When you find the Bacchants sober and your expectations disappointed, you will surely call me your best friend.

Pentheus. Should I carry this thyrsus in this hand or the other, to look more like a proper Bacchanal?

Dionysus. Carry it on the right, moving it in time with your right foot. How admirable your change of heart is!

Pentheus. Could I lift the valleys of Mount Kithairon onto my shoulders, with all the Bacchanals as well?

Dionysus. You could, if you wished. Before, your mind was crooked; now it has the shape it should have.

Pentheus. Using levers? Or could I tear it up with my hands, pushing an arm or shoulder beneath its peaks?

Dionysus. No, don't destroy the Nymphs' shrines or the places where Pan lives and the sound of his pipes echoes.

Pentheus. True, true. We must not overpower the women by force. I shall hide among the pine trees.

Dionysus. You will have the hiding place fate appoints, coming as you do in deceit to spy upon the Bacchanals.

Pentheus. I think I can see them in the thickets, caught like birds in the snares of their sweet love-play.

Dionysus. That is precisely what you are appointed to watch for. Perhaps you will catch them, unless they catch you first.

Pentheus. Take me straight through the centre of Thebes. I alone among them dare to undertake this deed.

Dionysus. You alone are labouring for Thebes, entirely alone. That is why your appointed strain and struggle await you. Follow me; I will guide you there in safety. Someone else will bring you back.

Pentheus. Yes, my own mother will.

Dionysus. Everyone will see you then.

Pentheus. That is why I am going.

Dionysus. You will return carried high.

Pentheus. What luxurious comfort for me!

Dionysus. Your mother's hands will carry you.

Pentheus. You mean to lavish splendour on me!

Dionysus. Only the appropriate splendour for you.

Pentheus. The splendour that I deserve.

Dionysus. Strange man, strange indeed! What happens to you will be strange too. In this way you will gain fame reaching to the heavens. (Pentheus leaves.) Agavê, reach out your hands; you sisters, daughters of Kadmus, reach out yours! I am bringing this prince to a mighty contest. The victory will belong to me and to Bromius. Its outcome will reveal the rest. (He leaves.)
