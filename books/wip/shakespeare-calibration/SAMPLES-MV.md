# The Merchant of Venice: calibration samples

Three passages, one for each calibration category. Each paragraph shows the **original** (Gutenberg #1515, Gutenberg lineation), the **current** live Tinct Modern English and the **proposed** Modern English, with the reasons for every change. Paragraph ids are `chapter.paragraph` in Tinct's served editions.

## MV-A: Act 3, Scene 2: Bassanio chooses the lead casket

*Category: difficult imagery or rhetoric. Paragraphs 14.13–14.17.*

### 14.13 BASSANIO.

**Original**

> So may the outward shows be least themselves.  
> The world is still deceiv’d with ornament.  
> In law, what plea so tainted and corrupt  
> But, being season’d with a gracious voice,  
> Obscures the show of evil? In religion,  
> What damned error but some sober brow  
> Will bless it, and approve it with a text,  
> Hiding the grossness with fair ornament?  
> There is no vice so simple but assumes  
> Some mark of virtue on his outward parts.  
> How many cowards, whose hearts are all as false  
> As stairs of sand, wear yet upon their chins  
> The beards of Hercules and frowning Mars,  
> Who inward search’d, have livers white as milk,  
> And these assume but valour’s excrement  
> To render them redoubted. Look on beauty,  
> And you shall see ’tis purchas’d by the weight,  
> Which therein works a miracle in nature,  
> Making them lightest that wear most of it:  
> So are those crisped snaky golden locks  
> Which make such wanton gambols with the wind  
> Upon supposed fairness, often known  
> To be the dowry of a second head,  
> The skull that bred them in the sepulchre.  
> Thus ornament is but the guiled shore  
> To a most dangerous sea; the beauteous scarf  
> Veiling an Indian beauty; in a word,  
> The seeming truth which cunning times put on  
> To entrap the wisest. Therefore thou gaudy gold,  
> Hard food for Midas, I will none of thee,  
> Nor none of thee, thou pale and common drudge  
> ’Tween man and man: but thou, thou meagre lead,  
> Which rather threaten’st than dost promise aught,  
> Thy palenness moves me more than eloquence,  
> And here choose I, joy be the consequence!  

**Current**

> BASSANIO. So outward shows may be the least like themselves. The world is still deceived with ornament. In law, what plea so tainted and corrupt that, being seasoned with a graceful voice, doesn't obscure the appearance of evil? In religion, what damned error, but some sober brow will bless it, and approve it with a text, hiding the grossness with fair ornament? There's no vice so simple that it doesn't assume some mark of virtue on its outward parts. How many cowards, whose hearts are as false as staircases of sand, still wear upon their chins the beards of Hercules and frowning Mars—who, inwardly examined, have livers white as milk—and these put on the mere outward show of valor to make themselves feared. Look on beauty, and you'll see it's bought by weight, which therein works a miracle in nature, making them lightest who wear most of it. So those curled snaky golden locks which make such wanton play with the wind upon supposed fairness, are often known to be the dowry of a second head—the skull that bred them lying in the sepulchre. Thus ornament is just the deceptive shore to a most dangerous sea; the beautiful scarf veiling an Indian beauty; in a word, the seeming truth which cunning times put on to entrap the wisest. Therefore, you gaudy gold, hard food for Midas, I'll have none of you. Nor any of you, pale and common drudge between man and man. But you, you meager lead, which threatens rather than promises anything—your paleness moves me more than eloquence, and here I choose. Joy be the consequence!

**Proposed**

> BASSANIO. So outward shows may be the least like what they really are. The world is always deceived by ornament. In law, what plea is so tainted and corrupt that, seasoned with a charming voice, it can't hide its look of evil? In religion, what damnable error is there that some solemn-faced man won't bless and confirm with a text from scripture, hiding its grossness with fair ornament? There's no vice so simple-minded that it doesn't assume some mark of virtue on its outward parts. How many cowards, whose hearts are as treacherous as stairs of sand, still wear on their chins the beards of Hercules and frowning Mars, though, searched inside, they have livers as white as milk; and these put on only valor's outgrowth, a brave man's beard, to make themselves feared. Look at beauty, and you'll see it's bought by weight, and there it works a miracle in nature, making those who wear the most of it the lightest: light in weight, and light in virtue. In the same way, those curled, snaking golden locks that frolic so wantonly with the wind on the head of some supposed beauty often turn out to be the dowry of a second head: a wig, grown by a skull that now lies in the tomb. Thus ornament is only the treacherous shore of a most dangerous sea; the beautiful scarf that veils a dark Indian face and passes it off as a beauty; in a word, the seeming truth which cunning times put on to entrap the wisest. Therefore, you gaudy gold, hard food for Midas, whose every bite turned to gold, I'll have none of you. Nor any of you, silver, you pale and common drudge that passes between man and man as money. But you, you meager lead, which threatens rather than promises anything, your paleness moves me more than eloquence, and here I choose. May joy be the consequence!

**Why**

- L1 'least themselves' = least like what they really are; current 'the least like themselves' is opaque by ear. Johnson (in Furness, New Variorum Merchant of Venice (1888), note on III.ii.79): he begins abruptly, the first part of the argument having passed in his mind; 'So' kept.
- L2 false friend fixed: 'still' = always (Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), still adv.: 'always, ever, continually'); current 'is still deceived' is heard as 'even now'.
- L3-5 unnatural syntax fixed: current 'what plea so tainted and corrupt that ... doesn't obscure' has no main verb. 'season'd' continues the spoiled-meat metaphor of 'tainted' (Clarendon in Furness, note on 82), so both words are kept; 'gracious' = attractive, lovely (Onions, gracious 2, citing this line) -> 'charming'; 'Obscures the show of evil' -> 'hide its look of evil'.
- L5-8 hard idiom and metonymy made clear: 'What damned error but some sober brow / Will bless it' (archaic 'what ... but') -> 'what damnable error is there that some solemn-faced man won't bless'; 'approve' = prove, confirm (Onions approve 1, citing 'approve it with a text'; Dyce in Furness: 'ratify, confirm') and 'text' = a text from scripture; current left 'sober brow' and 'approve it with a text' untranslated. 'grossness' = flagrant character, enormity (Onions, citing this line): kept, since modern 'grossness' carries it.
- L9-10 kept from current word for word ('vice' is the F1 reading followed by all editors from Rowe; the Quartos read 'voice', per Furness's collation).
- L11-13 'false' = treacherous, giving way under the foot (Knight in Furness, note on 90: stairs of sand 'betray the feet of those who trust to them') -> 'as treacherous as stairs of sand'; current 'as false as staircases of sand'.
- L14 hard image made clear: a white, bloodless liver is the sign of cowardice (Onions s.v. liver: 'white or pale l., symbolical of cowardice', citing this line). Current 'have livers white as milk' leaves the point to the listener, and its dashed aside attaches 'who' to Hercules and Mars; 'though ... they' fixes the reference.
- L15-16 mis-gloss fixed: 'valour's excrement' = the beard, valour's outgrowth (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon (Perseus) s.v. Excrement 2: 'that which grows out of the body, hair, beard ... assume but valour's e. (the beard of Hercules) Merch. III, 2, 87'; Onions: 'valour's e. (= a brave man's beard)'; Malone and Staunton in Furness). Current 'the mere outward show of valor' drops the beard image; 'redoubted' = feared.
- L16-19 flattened pun restored: 'lightest' = least heavy and most wanton (Onions light adj. 3 'wanton, unchaste', 'quibbles are frequent'; Heath in Furness: 'the more it is laid on, the more lightness it indicates in the wearer'). Current keeps 'lightest' but the moral sense is inaudible; 'therein' archaic. Beauty bought 'by the weight' = cosmetics and false hair (Heath).
- L20-24 hard image made clear: the golden locks are a wig made from a dead woman's hair (Malone in Furness on the periwig fashion); 'dowry' = property (Schmidt s.v. Dowry 2 'property (of women) in general', citing this line), kept with the gloss 'a wig'; 'crisped' = curled (Onions; Steevens); 'wanton gambols' = frolics (Onions wanton adj. 1); 'supposed' = pretended (Onions supposed 2); 'Upon supposed fairness' = surmounting a counterfeit beauty (Clarendon in Furness). Current left 'dowry of a second head' untranslated.
- L25-26 'guiled' = treacherous, full of guile (Onions 'guiled: treacherous (S.)', citing this line; Schmidt s.v. Guiled 'furnished or armed with deceit, treacherous'; Steevens and Clarendon in Furness); current 'deceptive shore to a ... sea' is close but unidiomatic.
- L26-27 hard image made clear, prejudice kept at the original's strength: Qq and F1 read 'an Indian beautie' (kept by most editors; Hanmer's 'dowdy', Keightley's 'feature' etc. rejected). 'Indian' is derogatory: a dark-skinned face that the speaker's world did not count as beauty (Brae and White in Furness; Furness: 'An Indian beauty is assuredly not an English beauty'). Current 'the beautiful scarf veiling an Indian beauty' leaves the listener unable to hear why the scarf deceives. The rendering neither softens nor sharpens: it says only what 'Indian beauty' carried.
- L27-29 kept from current word for word ('seeming truth', 'cunning times' are clear).
- L29-30 allusion made clear: Midas's food turned to gold at his touch; a five-word gloss added. 'I will none of thee' -> 'I'll have none of you' (current).
- L31-32 hard image made clear: the 'pale and common drudge' is silver coin, a menial passing from hand to hand (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon (Perseus) s.v. Drudge: 'one employed in mean service, a slave', citing Merch. III, 2, 103); current 'pale and common drudge between man and man' leaves both the metal and the money image unstated.
- L32-34 textual crux recorded: Gutenberg's 'palenness' is a misprint. Chose 'paleness', the reading of Q1 ('palenes'), Q2 and F1 ('palenesse'), kept by Cambridge/Globe (not among Furness's 'plainness' editors); Warburton's emendation 'plainness' (favoured by Furness) is the alternative. Current dash removed.
- L35 archaic optative 'joy be the consequence' -> 'May joy be the consequence!'.
- Conventions: current's unspaced em dashes removed; American spelling kept as in the edition (valor, meager).

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-MV should-fix): "There's no vice so simple that" → "There's no vice so simple-minded that". 'simple' = silly, witless (Schmidt, Simple 5, citing this line); modern 'simple' is heard as minor.*

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-MV note): "a miracle of nature" → "a miracle in nature". Restores the paradox against nature that sets up 'lightest'.*

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-MV note): "livers as white as milk, the coward's mark;" → "livers as white as milk;". The sentence already names them cowards; the least necessary of eight added glosses in the speech.*

### 14.15

**Original**

> [_Aside._] How all the other passions fleet to air,  

**Current**

> [Aside.] How all the other passions fly to air—

**Proposed**

> [Aside.] How all the other passions vanish into air —

**Why**

- 'fleet' = pass away, vanish (Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), fleet: 'the sense "pass away, vanish" is freq.'); current 'fly to air' is not idiomatic modern English.
- Stage direction '[Aside.]' kept exactly as the current modern text has it.
- Convention: current ends with an unspaced dash; the spaced em dash now also does the work of the original's 'As' (= namely) at the start of 14.16.

### 14.16

**Original**

> As doubtful thoughts, and rash-embrac’d despair,  
> And shudd’ring fear, and green-ey’d jealousy.  
> O love, be moderate; allay thy ecstasy,  
> In measure rain thy joy; scant this excess!  
> I feel too much thy blessing, make it less,  
> For fear I surfeit.  

**Current**

> as doubtful thoughts, and rash-embraced despair, and shuddering fear, and green-eyed jealousy. Oh love, be moderate; calm your ecstasy. In measure rain your joy; restrain this excess! I feel too much of your blessing—make it less, for fear I'm overwhelmed.

**Proposed**

> anxious thoughts, and despair embraced too rashly, and shuddering fear, and green-eyed jealousy. Oh, love, be moderate; calm your ecstasy. Rain your joy in due measure; restrain this excess! I feel too much of your blessing — make it less, for fear I overindulge and sicken.

**Why**

- L1 'As' = to wit, namely (Furness, note on III.ii.115, citing Walker and Abbott §113). Current 'as doubtful thoughts' is heard as 'while' or 'because'; the list now follows the dash that ends 14.15.
- L1 false friend fixed: 'doubtful' = apprehensive, suspicious (Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), doubtful, citing Mer.V. III.ii.109) -> 'anxious thoughts'; current 'doubtful thoughts' is heard as 'uncertain'.
- L1 compound unpacked: 'rash-embrac'd despair' -> 'despair embraced too rashly'. 'green-eyed jealousy' kept (clear, recognizable).
- L3-4 unnatural syntax fixed: current 'In measure rain your joy' keeps the inverted order; 'measure' = moderation (Onions measure 4, citing 'In measure rain thy joy'). Textual crux recorded: 'rain' (F1 'raine'; Rowe and most editors after Malone) vs 'rein' (Singer, Collier, Cambridge, Globe; preferred by Clarendon and Furness). The served text reads 'rain' and the words sound the same aloud, so 'rain' is kept.
- L5-6 image restored: 'surfeit' = sicken from too much food or drink; current 'for fear I'm overwhelmed' drops the feast image -> 'for fear I overindulge and sicken'. Dash spaced.

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-MV should-fix): "Let your joy rain down in due measure;" → "Rain your joy in due measure;". 'rain down' closed the rain/rein crux that the current wording left open to the ear (F1 'raine', Q3 'reine'; Furness collation).*

### 14.17 BASSANIO.

**Original**

> What find I here? [_Opening the leaden casket_.]  
> Fair Portia’s counterfeit! What demi-god  
> Hath come so near creation? Move these eyes?  
> Or whether, riding on the balls of mine,  
> Seem they in motion? Here are sever’d lips,  
> Parted with sugar breath, so sweet a bar  
> Should sunder such sweet friends. Here in her hairs  
> The painter plays the spider, and hath woven  
> A golden mesh t’entrap the hearts of men  
> Faster than gnats in cobwebs. But her eyes!—  
> How could he see to do them? Having made one,  
> Methinks it should have power to steal both his  
> And leave itself unfurnish’d. Yet look how far  
> The substance of my praise doth wrong this shadow  
> In underprizing it, so far this shadow  
> Doth limp behind the substance. Here’s the scroll,  
> The continent and summary of my fortune.  

**Current**

> BASSANIO. What do I find here? [Opening the leaden casket.] Fair Portia's likeness! What demi-god has come so near to creation? Do these eyes move? Or, riding on the balls of mine, do they only seem in motion? Here are parted lips, separated by sugared breath—so sweet a barrier should ever sunder such sweet friends. Here in her hair the painter plays the spider, and has woven a golden mesh to trap the hearts of men faster than gnats in cobwebs. But her eyes!—how could he see to do them? Having made one, I'd think it should have power to steal both of his own and leave itself unfinished. Yet look how far the substance of my praise wrongs this shadow by underprizing it, just so far does this shadow limp behind the substance. Here's the scroll, the content and summary of my fortune.

**Proposed**

> BASSANIO. What do I find here? [Opening the leaden casket.] Fair Portia's likeness! What demi-god of a painter has come so close to creating life? Do these eyes move? Or do they only seem to be in motion because they're riding on my own moving eyeballs? Here are parted lips, held apart by sugar-sweet breath: it's fitting that so sweet a barrier should part such sweet friends. Here in her hair the painter plays the spider, and has woven a golden mesh to trap the hearts of men faster and more firmly than gnats are caught in cobwebs. But her eyes! How could he see to paint them? Having painted one, I'd think it would have the power to steal both of his own eyes and leave itself without its partner. And yet, look: just as far as the substance of my praise wrongs this shadow, this painted image, by undervaluing it, so far does this shadow limp behind the substance, the living woman. Here's the scroll, which holds and sums up my fortune.

**Why**

- L1-2 kept from current ('counterfeit' = likeness, portrait: Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), citing this line; Steevens in Furness 'a likeness, without comprehending any idea of fraud'). Inline stage direction kept exactly as the current modern text has it.
- L2-3 hard line made clear: the demi-god is the painter, who came near to creating a living woman; current 'has come so near to creation' is opaque by ear.
- L3-5 image made clear: the painted eyes seem to move because Bassanio's own eyeballs carry them; 'Or whether' = or (Abbott §136, via Furness). Current 'riding on the balls of mine' is left close to the original and is opaque (and open to an unintended hearing) aloud.
- L5-7 mis-gloss fixed: 'should' = ought (Furness, note on III.ii.127: 'In the sense of ought'); it is fitting that so sweet a bar parts such sweet friends. Current 'so sweet a barrier should ever sunder such sweet friends' turns the compliment into a reproach.
- L7-10 false friend fixed: 'fast' in Shakespeare commonly = firmly fixed (Onions fast adj. 2 'firmly adhering'; he also notes the 'rapid, quick' sense occurs). Current 'faster than gnats in cobwebs' is heard only as 'more quickly'; both senses kept.
- L10-11 'do them' = paint them; current's unspaced '!—' removed.
- L11-13 mis-gloss fixed: 'unfurnish'd' = unmatched with its fellow, i.e. without the second eye (Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), unfurnish'd, citing this line; Monck Mason and Henley in Furness). Current 'unfinished' reproduces Rowe's emendation (followed by Pope, Theobald, Warburton), not the Q/F word the original shows. 'should have power' = would have the power.
- L13-16 wordplay made clear: shadow = the painted image, substance = the real Portia (the stock substance/shadow antithesis); 'how far ... so far' = 'as far as ... so far' (Abbott §46, via Furness). Current leaves 'shadow' and 'substance' untranslated; 'underprizing' -> 'undervaluing'.
- L16-17 false friend fixed: 'continent' = that which contains (Dyce in Furness; Onions continent sb. 1) and, per Onions sb. 4, 'summary, sum' (citing this line); 'which holds and sums up' covers both. Current 'the content and summary' is a near miss.

- 14.14-14.16: the parser split Portia's aside into three paragraphs: a lone speaker prefix 'PORTIA.' (14.14), the first verse line with its [Aside.] direction and no prefix (14.15), and the remaining five lines with no prefix (14.16). Fragment structure kept. 14.15 now ends with a spaced em dash, which stands for the original's 'As' (= namely); 14.16 continues in lower case.
- 14.17: the inline direction [Opening the leaden casket.] is kept exactly as the current modern text has it, after the first sentence.
- 14.13 line 34: Gutenberg prints 'palenness', a misprint. Proposed follows 'paleness' (Q1 'palenes', Q2/F1 'palenesse', kept by Cambridge/Globe per Furness's collation); Warburton's emendation 'plainness', which Furness (1888) favours, is the recorded alternative.
- 14.16 line 4: textual crux 'rain' (F1 'raine'; Rowe and most editors after Malone) vs 'rein' (Singer, Collier, Cambridge, Globe; preferred by Clarendon and Furness). Proposed follows the served text's 'rain'; the words sound the same aloud.
- 14.13 line 27: 'an Indian beauty' is the Qq/Ff reading, kept by most editors; Furness records many emendations (Hanmer 'dowdy', Keightley 'feature', Walker 'gipsy', etc.). Read with Brae, White and Furness as derogatory; see the reasons for 14.13.
- Unit maps: o_lines are 1-based within the paragraph's Gutenberg lines; where a sentence boundary falls mid-line, adjacent units share that line, and the 'o' field quotes the exact original span.

#### Defects found in the current text

- 14.13 L2: false friend left: 'still deceived' (still = always).
- 14.13 L3-5: ungrammatical sentence ('what plea so tainted and corrupt that ... doesn't obscure' has no main verb).
- 14.13 L5-8: archaic 'what damned error, but some sober brow will bless it' construction left; 'sober brow' and 'approve it with a text' (approve = confirm, with a scripture text) untranslated.
- 14.13 L14: 'livers white as milk' left without its point (cowardice); the dashed aside attaches 'who' to Hercules and Mars.
- 14.13 L15-16: mis-gloss: 'valour's excrement' (= the beard) rendered 'the mere outward show of valor'; the beard image is lost.
- 14.13 L19: flattened pun: 'lightest' (light = unchaste) kept as a word, but the second sense is inaudible.
- 14.13 L20-24: 'the dowry of a second head' left untranslated; the listener cannot tell the curls are a wig of dead woman's hair.
- 14.13 L27: 'an Indian beauty' left untranslated; the derogatory point, and so the whole image, is lost to a modern listener.
- 14.13 L29-32: Midas allusion and 'pale and common drudge between man and man' (silver coin) left opaque.
- 14.16: 'as' (= namely) and 'doubtful' (= apprehensive) are false friends left in place; 'In measure rain your joy' keeps the inverted order; 'overwhelmed' drops the surfeit (overeating) image.
- 14.17: 'riding on the balls of mine' left close to the original; 'should ever sunder' misreads 'should' (= ought); 'faster' (= more firmly) left as a false friend; 'unfinished' reproduces Rowe's emendation instead of the Q/F 'unfurnish'd' (= without its fellow); substance/shadow wordplay untranslated; 'come so near to creation' opaque.
- Conventions: unspaced em dashes throughout ('Mars—who', 'breath—so', 'eyes!—how', 'anything—your', 'air—').

### 2.9 PORTIA.

**Original**

> Ay, that’s a colt indeed, for he doth nothing but talk of his horse,  
> and he makes it a great appropriation to his own good parts that he can  
> shoe him himself. I am much afeard my lady his mother play’d false with  
> a smith.  

**Current**

> PORTIA. Ah, that one is a colt indeed—he does nothing but talk about his horse, and he counts it a great point in his favor that he can shoe it himself. I'm rather afraid his mother had a fling with a blacksmith.

**Proposed**

> PORTIA. Yes, that one is a colt indeed, as giddy and witless as a young horse, for he does nothing but talk about his horse, and he counts it a great point in his favor that he can shoe it himself. I'm very much afraid her ladyship his mother was unfaithful with a blacksmith.

**Why**

- Mis-gloss fixed: 'Ay' = yes; current 'Ah' turns agreement into an interjection.
- Flattened pun restored: 'colt' = a young horse and 'a witless, heady, gay youngster' (Johnson, in Furness, New Variorum Merchant of Venice (1888), note on I.ii.39); the horse-mad prince is both. Current keeps the word but only the horse sense is audible.
- 'appropriation to his own good parts' = special excellence added to his accomplishments (Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), appropriation, citing I.ii.45; Singer in Furness): current 'a great point in his favor' kept.
- Register restored: current 'had a fling' is modern slang (rule 6) and drops the mock-formal 'my lady his mother'; 'much afeard' = very much afraid, which current 'rather afraid' weakens; 'play'd false' = was unfaithful. The joke (he shoes horses because his real father was a smith) stays implicit, as in the original.
- Convention: current's unspaced dash removed.

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-MV note): "my lady his mother" → "her ladyship his mother". Keeps the mock formality and is clear by ear.*

### 2.10 NERISSA.

**Original**

> Then is there the County Palatine.  

**Current**

> NERISSA. Then there's the County Palatine.

**Proposed**

> NERISSA. Then there's the Count Palatine.

**Why**

- False friend fixed: 'County' = Count (Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), 'county: count (freq.) Mer.V. i. ii. 48'; Malone in Furness: 'County and Count were synonymous'). Current 'County Palatine' is heard as a territory; Portia herself says 'the Count Palatine' in 2.13.

### 2.11 PORTIA.

**Original**

> He doth nothing but frown, as who should say “And you will not have me,  
> choose.” He hears merry tales and smiles not. I fear he will prove the  
> weeping philosopher when he grows old, being so full of unmannerly  
> sadness in his youth. I had rather be married to a death’s-head with a  
> bone in his mouth than to either of these. God defend me from these  
> two!  

**Current**

> PORTIA. He does nothing but frown, as if to say, "If you don't want me, suit yourself." He hears funny stories and doesn't smile. I'm afraid he'll turn into the weeping philosopher when he gets old, being so full of unmannerly sadness already in his youth. I'd rather be married to a skull with a bone in its mouth than to either of those two. God protect me from them both!

**Proposed**

> PORTIA. He does nothing but frown, as if to say, “If you won't have me, choose as you please.” He hears funny stories and doesn't smile. I'm afraid he'll turn into the weeping philosopher when he gets old, being so full of ill-mannered gloom in his youth. I'd rather be married to a skull with a bone in its mouth than to either of those two. God protect me from them both!

**Why**

- Flattened double meaning restored: 'choose' = do as one likes (Onions, A Shakespeare Glossary (1911), choose 1, citing 'An you will not have me, choose'; Schmidt via Furness: 'to do at one's pleasure'). Current 'suit yourself' has the sense but drops the word 'choose' on which the scene turns (Portia: 'O me, the word "choose"!'); 'choose as you please' keeps both.
- False friend fixed: 'sadness' = seriousness (Onions, sadness); current 'unmannerly sadness' is heard as sorrow. 'already' in current was an addition and is removed.
- Kept from current: 'funny stories', 'weeping philosopher' (Heraclitus, Clarendon in Furness; the phrase explains itself), 'skull with a bone in its mouth', 'God protect me from them both!'.
- Convention: straight double quotes in current -> curly double quotes.

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-MV should-fix): "ill-mannered solemnity" → "ill-mannered gloom". Schmidt files this line under Sadness 1 'sorrow, dejection', which makes him the future 'weeping philosopher'.*

### 2.13 PORTIA.

**Original**

> God made him, and therefore let him pass for a man. In truth, I know it  
> is a sin to be a mocker, but he! why, he hath a horse better than the  
> Neapolitan’s, a better bad habit of frowning than the Count Palatine.  
> He is every man in no man. If a throstle sing, he falls straight

#### Structure notes

- 13.22, 13.25 and 13.27 are stage directions, reproduced exactly as the current modern text has them (13.27 reads 'Exit' where the original has 'Exeunt'; left unchanged per the brief).
- 13.31/13.33: Tubal's sentence is interrupted by Shylock (13.32); the paragraph-final and paragraph-initial dashes are kept.
- Edition-wide convention: the live MV modern-en uses unspaced em dashes ('Genoa—', 'Ha, ha—heard', 'usurer—let', 'execute—and', 'tribe—a', 'now—I', 'you—loss', '—had') and, in 2.11, straight double quotes; rule 8 requires spaced em dashes and curly double quotes. Every rewritten paragraph here follows rule 8. Kept paragraphs are reproduced word for word; 13.31 and 13.36 need only mechanical dash spacing ('Genoa —', 'Ha, ha — heard').
- 13.36: 'heard in Genoa?' is the served (Gutenberg) reading; Qq and Ff read 'here in Genoa', Rowe and most editors 'where? in Genoa?' (Furness). Kept, since the sense is the same.
- 13.30: 'Why so?' (a question in F1 and the served text, preferred by Furness) kept; Onions reads 'why, so!' as acquiescence. 'Why, thou—loss upon loss!' (Q/F1 'thou', Collier's dash) kept; the later Folios' 'then' is Furness's preference.
- 13.19-13.38 are prose; unit maps are sentence-level (o_lines [1, 1], exact original span in 'o').

#### Defects found in the current text

- 13.16: flattened pun and coarsening: 'Are you still in heat at your age?' drops 'rebel' (Solanio's twist on Shylock's word) and swaps 'old carrion ... rebels it' for an animal-in-heat image.
- 13.18: 'jet and ivory', 'red wine and Rhenish' left, so the black/white colour contrast carrying the insult is inaudible.
- 13.19: mis-gloss 'bad match' (= bargain); false friend 'smug' (= spruce, trim); 'Christian courtesy' (lending without interest) left unexplained; 'dares scarcely show' unnatural order.
- 13.21: archaic idiom 'it shall go hard but I will' left untranslated.
- 13.26: 'a third one can't be matched' unclear by ear; subjunctive 'turn Jew' left.
- 13.28: archaic 'How now' left.
- 13.29: 'came where I heard of her' unnatural; 'couldn't find her' changes present 'cannot'.
- 13.30: 'there, there, there, there' left (heard today as comfort); 'would she were hearsed' left archaic after 'I wish'; 'no sighs but my breathing, no tears but my shedding' garbled; 'even' added.
- 13.33: 'argosy' (largest merchant ship) reduced to 'a merchant ship'; 'had ... lost' shifts 'hath ... cast away' into the past.
- Conventions: unspaced em dashes in 13.19, 13.21, 13.26, 13.30, 13.31, 13.33, 13.36.
