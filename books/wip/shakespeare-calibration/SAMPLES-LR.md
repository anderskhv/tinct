# King Lear: calibration samples

Three passages, one for each calibration category. Each paragraph shows the **original** (Gutenberg #1532, Gutenberg lineation), the **current** live Tinct Modern English and the **proposed** Modern English, with the reasons for every change. Paragraph ids are `chapter.paragraph` in Tinct's served editions.

**Status:** these are proposals, drafted to test the standard in `STANDARD.md`. They are not a replacement, and nothing is published.

## LR-A: Act 3, Scene 2: the storm

*Category: difficult imagery or rhetoric. Paragraphs 11.0–11.12.*

### 11.0 (stage direction, unchanged)

> [Storm continues. Enter LEAR and FOOL.]

### 11.1 LEAR.

**Original**

> Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! Rage! blow!  
> You cataracts and hurricanoes, spout  
> Till you have drench’d our steeples, drown’d the cocks!  
> You sulphurous and thought-executing fires,  
> Vaunt-couriers to oak-cleaving thunderbolts,  
> Singe my white head! And thou, all-shaking thunder,  
> Strike flat the thick rotundity o’ the world!  
> Crack nature’s moulds, all germens spill at once,  
> That make ingrateful man!  

**Current**

> LEAR. Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! Rage! Blow! You cataracts and hurricanes, spout till you have drenched our steeples and drowned the weathercocks! You sulphurous and thought-swift fires, advance guards to oak-cleaving thunderbolts, singe my white head! And you, all-shaking thunder, strike flat the thick rotundity of the world! Crack nature's moulds, spill all seeds at once, that make ungrateful man!

**Proposed**

> LEAR. Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! Rage! Blow! You cloudbursts and waterspouts, spout till you have drenched our steeples and drowned the weathercocks! You sulfurous fires of lightning that kill as quick as thought, forerunners of the thunderbolts that split oaks, singe my white head! And you, all-shaking thunder, strike flat the thick round globe of the world! Crack the molds that nature casts us in, and spill and destroy at once all the seeds that make ungrateful man!

**Why**

- Mis-gloss fixed: 'hurricanoes' = waterspouts (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'Hurricano, a water-spout', citing this line; Dyce in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current 'hurricanes' loses the image of water spouting over the steeples.
- Hard word made clear: 'cataracts' = mighty falls of water from the sky (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'mighty fall of water, a waterspout', citing this line). A listener hears 'cataracts' as waterfalls or the eye disease; 'cloudbursts'.
- Mis-gloss fixed: 'thought-executing' = doing execution (killing) as fast as thought (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'doing execution in the same moment as it is thought of; rapid like thought'; Johnson in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current 'thought-swift' keeps the speed and drops the killing. Moberly's reading ('executing the thought of Him who casts you', Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)) noted; the standard Schmidt/Johnson reading chosen.
- Hard image made clear: 'fires' are lightning, so 'fires of lightning' is added as a short gloss; 'Vaunt-couriers' = forerunners (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'Vaunt-courier, forerunner'), replacing the military 'advance guards'.
- Untranslated phrase fixed: 'the thick rotundity o' the world' = the solid round globe (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: rotundity 'roundness, spherical form'). Current keeps 'thick rotundity' untranslated (too close). Delius's secondary 'roundness of gestation' (Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)) is not forced into the line.
- Flattened double sense restored: 'all germens spill' = destroy all seeds (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: germens 'germs, seeds'; spill 2) 'to destroy', citing this line; Steevens in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current 'spill all seeds' keeps only the modern 'pour out'; proposed 'spill and destroy' carries both.
- 'Crack nature's moulds' made unambiguous by ear ('the molds that nature casts us in', so no listener hears fungus); American spelling as in Tinct's calibrated Hamlet ('molds', 'sulfurous'). Rest of the wording kept from current.

### 11.2 FOOL.

**Original**

> O nuncle, court holy-water in a dry house is better than this  
> rain-water out o’ door. Good nuncle, in; and ask thy daughters  
> blessing: here’s a night pities neither wise men nor fools.  

**Current**

> FOOL. Oh nuncle, holy water at court, in a dry house, is better than this rainwater out of doors. Good nuncle, go in, and ask your daughters' blessing: here's a night that pities neither wise men nor fools.

**Proposed**

> FOOL. Oh, nuncle, the holy water of court flattery in a dry house is better than this rainwater out of doors. Good nuncle, go in, and ask your daughters' blessing: here's a night that pities neither wise men nor fools.

**Why**

- Mis-gloss fixed: 'court holy-water' = flattery, fair words (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'court h. ... the Fr. eau bénite de la cour, i.e. flattery'; Steevens and Malone citing Cotgrave, 'Court holy water; complements, faire words, flattering speeches', in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current 'holy water at court' takes the idiom literally and loses the Fool's point: go back and flatter your daughters. The holy-water/rainwater contrast is kept.
- Kept from current: 'ask your daughters' blessing' (the irony of a father kneeling to his children) and 'a night that pities neither wise men nor fools'. Punctuation 'Oh, nuncle,'.

### 11.3 LEAR.

**Original**

> Rumble thy bellyful! Spit, fire! spout, rain!  
> Nor rain, wind, thunder, fire are my daughters;  
> I tax not you, you elements, with unkindness.  
> I never gave you kingdom, call’d you children;  
> You owe me no subscription: then let fall  
> Your horrible pleasure. Here I stand your slave,  
> A poor, infirm, weak, and despis’d old man:  
> But yet I call you servile ministers,  
> That will with two pernicious daughters join  
> Your high-engender’d battles ’gainst a head  
> So old and white as this! O! O! ’tis foul!  

**Current**

> LEAR. Rumble your bellyful! Spit, fire! Spout, rain! Neither rain, wind, thunder, nor fire are my daughters. I do not accuse you, elements, of unkindness. I never gave you a kingdom, called you my children — you owe me no allegiance. So let fall your horrible pleasure. Here I stand, your slave, a poor, infirm, weak, and despised old man. But yet I call you servile ministers, who will join with two pernicious daughters in your high-engendered battles against a head as old and white as this! Oh! Oh! It's foul!

**Proposed**

> LEAR. Rumble your bellyful! Spit, fire! Spout, rain! Neither rain, wind, thunder, nor fire are my daughters. I do not accuse you, elements, of unkindness. I never gave you a kingdom, called you my children — you owe me no allegiance. So let fall on me whatever horror you please. Here I stand, your slave, a poor, infirm, weak, and despised old man. But yet I call you servile lackeys, who will join your battalions, bred high in the heavens, with two wicked daughters against a head as old and white as this! Oh! Oh! It's foul!

**Why**

- Hard idiom made clear: 'let fall Your horrible pleasure' = drop on me whatever dreadful thing you please ('pleasure' = will; Heath's paraphrase 'Do with me what you please' in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current 'let fall your horrible pleasure' leaves it untranslated.
- False friend fixed: 'servile ministers' = slavish servants, agents (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: minister 1) 'instrument, executor', 3) 'servant in general'). A listener hears government ministers; 'servile lackeys' keeps the contempt.
- Untranslated phrase fixed: 'high-engender'd battles' = armies bred in the sky (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: high-engendered 'created in the sky', citing this line; battle 3) 'an army prepared for or engaged in fight'). Current 'high-engendered battles' is opaque, and 'battles' is a false friend.
- 'pernicious' = wicked (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'mischievous, malicious, wicked', citing 'two p. daughters'); modern 'pernicious' means subtly harmful.
- Kept from current: 'Rumble your bellyful' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: bellyful 'as much as satisfies the appetite'), 'I do not accuse ... of unkindness' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: tax vb. 3, citing this line), 'allegiance' for 'subscription' (Upton in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)), and 'It's foul' (foul weather and a foul deed).

### 11.4 FOOL.

**Original**

> He that has a house to put’s head in has a good head-piece.  
> The codpiece that will house  
> Before the head has any,  
> The head and he shall louse:  
> So beggars marry many.  
> The man that makes his toe  
> What he his heart should make  
> Shall of a corn cry woe,  
> And turn his sleep to wake.  
> For there was never yet fair woman but she made mouths in a glass.  

**Current**

> FOOL. He that has a house to put his head in has a good headpiece. The codpiece that will house itself before the head has any, both the head and he shall have lice — so beggars marry many. The man that makes his toe what he should make of his heart shall, of a corn, cry woe, and turn his sleep to waking. For there was never yet a fair woman who didn't make faces at herself in a mirror.

**Proposed**

> FOOL. Anyone who has a house to put his head in has a good headpiece: a roof over his head, and a good head on his shoulders. The man who finds a home for his codpiece before he has one for his head will end up with lice on both, head and codpiece: that's the way many beggars marry. The man who cherishes his toe as he should cherish his heart will cry out in pain over a corn and lie awake when he should be sleeping. For there was never yet a fair woman who didn't make faces at herself in a mirror.

**Why**

- Flattened pun restored: 'head-piece' = helmet, a covering for the head, and also the skull as the seat of thought (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: head-piece 'armour for the head, helmet', citing this line; also 'used of the skull as containing the brain'). Current 'has a good headpiece' keeps only the word; proposed carries both senses (a roof over his head / a good head on his shoulders).
- Bawdy made understandable at the original's strength: the man who houses his codpiece (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'a part of the male dress, very indelicately conspicuous') before he has a roof gets lice (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: louse vb. 'to have lice', citing 'the head and he shall l.'). Current 'The codpiece that will house itself before the head has any, both the head and he shall have lice' is opaque ('he' = the codpiece).
- Crux: 'So beggars marry many' read as 'so many beggars marry' (Mason in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)); Johnson's 'a beggar marries a wife and lice' noted.
- Hard image made clear: 'makes his toe What he his heart should make' = cherishes a lowly member in place of a vital one (Capell; Furness's own note: 'A man who prefers or cherishes a mean member in place of a vital one shall suffer enduring pain', in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current keeps the opaque construction and archaic 'of a corn, cry woe' and 'turn his sleep to waking'.
- Last sentence kept from current ('made mouths in a glass' = made faces in a mirror; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'to make mouths == to make faces', citing this line). Song lines rendered as plain speakable sentences, as in Tinct's calibrated Hamlet songs.

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-LR should-fix): "that's how many beggars marry" → "that's the way many beggars marry". Heard aloud as a number ('how many beggars'); Mason's reading, as recorded in Furness.*

> LEAR. No, I will be the pattern of all patience; I will say nothing.

**Original**

**Current, kept as is**

### 11.8 FOOL.

**Original**

> Marry, here’s grace and a codpiece; that’s a wise man and a  
> fool.  

**Current**

> FOOL. Why, here's grace and a codpiece; that's a wise man and a fool.

**Proposed**

> FOOL. Why, here's grace — His Grace the King — and a codpiece; that's a wise man and a fool.

**Why**

- Hidden title made audible: 'grace' = His Grace, the King (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: grace 11) 'used as an appellation of persons of the highest rank; of kings and queens'; Steevens in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880): 'the king's grace' was the usual expression). The 'codpiece' is the Fool himself (Douce in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current 'here's grace' reads as the virtue only. Which of the two is the wise man stays open, as in the original.
- 'Marry' (oath) kept as 'Why,' as in current.

*Reviewer fix applied (CF-LR note): "His Grace" → "grace — His Grace the King —". 'His Grace' alone drops the virtue sense of 'grace' and today suggests a duke or archbishop.*

### 11.9 KENT.

**Original**

> Alas, sir, are you here? Things that love night  
> Love not such nights as these; the wrathful skies  
> Gallow the very wanderers of the dark,  
> And make them keep their caves. Since I was man,  
> Such sheets of fire, such bursts of horrid thunder,  
> Such groans of roaring wind and rain I never  
> Remember to have heard. Man’s nature cannot carry  
> Th’affliction, nor the fear.  

**Current**

> KENT. Alas, sir, are you here? Things that love the night don't love such nights as these. The wrathful skies frighten the very wanderers of the dark, and make them keep to their caves. Since I became a man, I do not remember ever to have heard such sheets of fire, such bursts of horrid thunder, such groans of roaring wind and rain. Human nature cannot bear the affliction, nor the fear.

**Proposed**

> KENT. Alas, sir, are you here? Things that love the night don't love such nights as these. The wrathful skies frighten the very wanderers of the dark, and make them keep to their caves. Since I became a man, I cannot remember ever hearing such sheets of fire, such bursts of dreadful thunder, such groans of roaring wind and rain. Human nature cannot bear the affliction, nor the fear.

**Why**

- Register restored: 'horrid' = dreadful, hideous (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: horrid 'dreadful, shocking, hideous', citing this line). In current English 'horrid' has weakened to 'nasty', which deflates Kent's awe; 'dreadful'.
- Unnatural syntax fixed: current 'I do not remember ever to have heard' -> 'I cannot remember ever hearing'.
- Rest kept from current: 'frighten' for 'Gallow' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: gallow 'to frighten', citing this line; Wright in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)) and 'Human nature cannot bear the affliction, nor the fear' (Hudson in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)).

### 11.10 LEAR.

**Original**

> Let the great gods,  
> That keep this dreadful pudder o’er our heads,  
> Find out their enemies now. Tremble, thou wretch,  
> That hast within thee undivulged crimes  
> Unwhipp’d of justice. Hide thee, thou bloody hand;  
> Thou perjur’d, and thou simular of virtue  
> That art incestuous. Caitiff, to pieces shake  
> That under covert and convenient seeming  
> Hast practis’d on man’s life: close pent-up guilts,  
> Rive your concealing continents, and cry  
> These dreadful summoners grace. I am a man  
> More sinn’d against than sinning.  

**Current**

> LEAR. Let the great gods, who keep this dreadful uproar over our heads, find out their enemies now. Tremble, you wretch, who has within you undivulged crimes unwhipped by justice. Hide yourself, you bloody hand; you perjurer, and you false imitator of virtue who are incestuous. Caitiff, shake to pieces, you who under cover and convenient seeming have plotted against a man's life. Close pent-up guilts, rive your concealing containers, and cry for grace from these dreadful summoners. I am a man more sinned against than sinning.

**Proposed**

> LEAR. Let the great gods, who keep up this dreadful uproar over our heads, find out their enemies now. Tremble, you wretch, with crimes hidden inside you that justice has never whipped. Hide yourself, you bloody hand; you perjurer, and you who counterfeit virtue and yet commit incest. You villain, shake yourself to pieces, you who have plotted against a man's life behind a hidden, respectable front. You guilty secrets, shut up tight, split open the coverings that conceal you, and beg for mercy from these dreadful summoners who call you to judgment. I am a man more sinned against than sinning.

**Why**

- 'undivulged' = secret, hidden (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, citing this line): current keeps the rare Latinate word, hard by ear, and mixes persons ('you wretch, who has within you').
- 'simular of virtue' = hypocritical pretender to virtue (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: simular subst. 'simulator, hypocritical pretender', citing this line). Current 'you false imitator of virtue who are incestuous' is clumsy; proposed 'you who counterfeit virtue and yet commit incest'.
- Archaic word left untranslated: 'Caitiff' = wretch, villain (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'wretch, slave ... used as a term of reproach', citing III, 2, 55).
- False friend fixed: 'covert and convenient seeming' = a secret, proper-looking appearance (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: covert adj. 'secret, hid, disguised' and convenient 3) 'proper, becoming, decent', both citing this line). Current 'under cover and convenient seeming' leaves 'convenient' meaning 'handy'. Johnson's alternative ('appearance such as may promote his purpose', Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)) noted; 'practis'd on' = plotted against (Dyce in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880); Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon).
- Hard line made clear: 'close pent-up guilts, Rive your concealing continents' = secret guilts shut up tight, split open the coverings that hide you (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: close adv. 'in strict confinement', rive 'to split, to cleave', continent 'that which contains and encloses ... the cover', all citing this passage). Current keeps 'close' (a false friend) and archaic 'rive'.
- Opaque allusion glossed briefly: 'summoners' = officers who summon offenders to court (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: 'persons employed to warn offenders to appear in court'; Steevens in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)); 'cry ... grace' = beg for mercy (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: grace 5) 'mercy', citing this line).
- Kept from current: 'uproar' for 'pudder' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: pother 'turmoil'; 'keep this uproar' -> idiomatic 'keep up') and the famous 'I am a man more sinned against than sinning'.

### 11.11 KENT.

**Original**

> Alack, bareheaded!  
> Gracious my lord, hard by here is a hovel;  
> Some friendship will it lend you ’gainst the tempest:  
> Repose you there, whilst I to this hard house,—  
> More harder than the stones whereof ’tis rais’d;  
> Which even but now, demanding after you,  
> Denied me to come in,—return, and force  
> Their scanted courtesy.  

**Current**

> KENT. Alas, bareheaded! Gracious my lord, close by here is a hovel; it will lend you some shelter against the tempest. Rest there, while I go to this hard house — harder than the stones it's built of, which even just now, when I inquired after you, denied me entry — return, and force their stinted courtesy.

**Proposed**

> KENT. Alas, bareheaded! My gracious lord, close by here is a hovel; it will lend you some friendship against the tempest. Rest there, while I go back to this hard house — harder than the stones it's built of, which even just now, when I inquired after you, denied me entry — and force them to give the courtesy they grudge.

**Why**

- Image restored (current omission): 'Some friendship will it lend you' personifies the hovel as the only friend Lear has left; current 'it will lend you some shelter' drops it.
- Unnatural syntax fixed: current 'while I go to this hard house — ... — return, and force their stinted courtesy' leaves 'return' dangling after 'go to'; proposed puts 'go back' first. 'scanted courtesy' = courtesy afforded grudgingly (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: scant vb. 2) 'to afford sparingly and with reluctance, to grudge', citing 'force their --ed courtesy'); current 'stinted courtesy' is opaque. Kept from current: 'when I inquired after you' ('demanding' = asking, Wright in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)) and 'denied me entry'.
- Archaic inversion fixed: 'Gracious my lord' -> 'My gracious lord' (Abbott, cited in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). 'hard house' pun (hard-hearted, and stone) kept.

### 11.12 LEAR.

**Original**

> My wits begin to turn.  
> Come on, my boy. How dost, my boy? Art cold?  
> I am cold myself. Where is this straw, my fellow?  
> The art of our necessities is strange,  
> That can make vile things precious. Come, your hovel.  
> Poor fool and knave, I have one part in my heart  
> That’s sorry yet for thee.  

**Current**

> LEAR. My wits begin to turn. Come on, my boy. How are you, my boy? Are you cold? I'm cold myself. Where is this straw, my fellow? The art of our necessities is strange, that can make vile things precious. Come, lead on to your hovel. Poor fool and knave, I have one part in my heart that's sorry yet for you.

**Proposed**

> LEAR. My wits begin to turn. Come on, my boy. How are you, my boy? Are you cold? I'm cold myself. Where is this straw, my fellow? Our need has a strange art: it can make vile things precious. Come, lead on to your hovel. Poor fool, poor lad, I have one part in my heart that's still sorry for you.

**Why**

- False friend fixed: 'knave' here = lad, a term of endearment (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: knave 1) 'a young fellow, a boy ... even as a term of endearment', citing 'poor fool and k.' III, 2, 72). Current keeps 'knave', which a listener hears as 'rogue', turning tenderness into insult. Archaic 'sorry yet' -> 'still sorry'.
- Hard phrasing made clear: 'The art of our necessities is strange, That can make vile things precious' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: art 1) 'skill'; vile 'originally == low, mean'). Current keeps the construction, which is hard to parse by ear; proposed keeps 'art', 'vile' and 'precious' so the line stays recognizable.
- Rest kept from current.

## LR-B: Act 1, Scene 4: the Fool's first entrance

*Category: fast dialogue, comedy or wordplay. Paragraphs 4.62–4.99.*

### 4.62 (stage direction, unchanged)

> Enter Fool.

### 4.63 FOOL.

**Original**

> Let me hire him too; here’s my coxcomb.  

**Current**

> FOOL. Let me hire him too. Here's my coxcomb.

**Proposed**

> FOOL. Let me hire him too. Here's my coxcomb, my fool's cap.

**Why**

- False friend glossed at first mention: 'coxcomb' = a fool's cap (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: coxcomb 1) 'a fool's cap', citing this passage; Minsheu in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880): fools wore caps topped with a cock's comb). Today 'coxcomb' means a vain dandy; the short appositive lets every later coxcomb joke land.

### 4.64 (stage direction, unchanged)

> [Giving Kent his cap.]

### 4.65 LEAR.

**Original**

> How now, my pretty knave, how dost thou?  

**Current**

> LEAR. Well, my pretty knave, how are you doing?

**Proposed**

> LEAR. Well, my pretty lad, how are you doing?

**Why**

- False friend fixed: 'knave' = boy, used as a term of endearment (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon: knave 1), citing 'my pretty k.' I, 4, 46; Lear's fondness noted by White and Lloyd in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)). Current 'my pretty knave' reads as 'rogue'. Rest kept from current.

### 4.66 FOOL.

**Original**

> Sirrah, you were best take my coxcomb.  

**Current**

> FOOL. Sirrah, you'd best take my coxcomb.

**Proposed**

> FOOL. Fellow, you'd best take my coxcomb.

**Why**

- Archaic address translated: 'Sirrah' = form of address to an inferior, here 'used between equals of low degree' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, citing Lr. I, 4, 109), so 'Fellow' (Fool to Kent). Tinct's calibrated Macbeth likewise translates 'Sirrah' by function. 'you were best' = you had best (Abbott in Furness, New Variorum King Lear (1880)); current 'you'd best' kept.

### 4.67 KENT. (kept)

**Original**

> Why, fool?  

**Current, kept as is**

> KENT. Why, fool?

### 4.68 FOOL.

**Original**

> Why, for taking one’s part that’s out of favour. Nay, an thou  
> canst not smile as the wind sits, thou’lt catch cold shortly:  
> there, take my coxcomb: why, this fellow has banish’d two on’s  
> daughters, and did the third a blessing against his will; if  
> thou follow him, thou must needs wear my coxcomb. How now,  
> nuncle! Would I had two coxcombs and two daughters!  

**Current**

> FOOL. Why, for taking the part of one out of favor. No, if you can't smile as the wind sits, you'll catch cold shortly. There, take my coxcomb. **Proposed**
