# Twelfth Night: calibration samples

Three passages, one for each calibration category. Each paragraph shows the **original** (Gutenberg #1526, Gutenberg lineation), the **current** live Tinct Modern English and the **proposed** Modern English, with the reasons for every change. Paragraph ids are `chapter.paragraph` in Tinct's served editions.

**Status:** these are proposals, drafted to test the standard in `STANDARD.md`. They are not a replacement, and nothing is published.

## TN-A: Act 1, Scene 5: Viola unveils Olivia; the willow cabin

*Category: difficult imagery or rhetoric. Paragraphs 5.113–5.129.*

### 5.113 VIOLA. (kept)

**Original**

> Good madam, let me see your face.  

**Current, kept as is**

> VIOLA. Good madam, let me see your face.

### 5.114 OLIVIA.

**Original**

> Have you any commission from your lord to negotiate with my face? You  
> are now out of your text: but we will draw the curtain and show you the  
> picture. [_Unveiling._] Look you, sir, such a one I was this present.  
> Is’t not well done?  

**Current**

> OLIVIA. Do you have any commission from your lord to negotiate with my face? You are now off your text. But we will draw the curtain and show you the picture. [Unveiling.] Look, sir, just like this I was at this present time. Is it not well done?

**Proposed**

> OLIVIA. Do you have any commission from your lord to negotiate with my face? Now you've strayed from your text. But we will draw the curtain and show you the picture. [Unveiling.] Look, sir: a portrait of me as I was just this moment. Isn't it well done?

**Why**

- Unnatural syntax fixed and joke restored: 'such a one I was this present' = 'this is how I looked at this very moment' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, present subst. 2: 'such a one I was this p.', Tw. I,5,253, '== this moment'; Onions 1911, present sb. 2: 'adv. (?) = just now', same line). Olivia labels her unveiled face like a dated portrait inscription, continuing 'draw the curtain and show you the picture'. Current 'Look, sir, just like this I was at this present time' keeps the inverted order, and the listener cannot hear the portrait joke.
- Unidiomatic phrase fixed: 'out of your text' continues the sermon joke ('what is your text?', 'chapter', 'heresy'; Onions 1911, text 2 'quotation, quoted saying', citing this scene): Viola has left the text she was preaching on. Current 'You are now off your text' is not idiomatic; 'strayed from your text' keeps the image.
- Archaic word order fixed: 'Is't not well done?' -> 'Isn't it well done?' (current 'Is it not'). 'well done' is kept because Viola's reply picks it up.
- Kept from current: the first sentence ('commission' = warrant, authority: Onions 1911, commission 2) and 'draw the curtain and show you the picture'. The stage direction stays exactly as the current modern text has it.

### 5.115 VIOLA.

**Original**

> Excellently done, if God did all.  

**Current**

> VIOLA. Excellently done, if God did all of it.

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. Excellently done — if God did all the painting.

**Why**

- Joke made audible: 'if God did all' = if it is all natural, with no cosmetic paint. Viola stays inside Olivia's portrait conceit and hints that the 'picture' may be painted, which is why Olivia answers that the colour is 'in grain'. Current 'if God did all of it' leaves the cosmetics jab implicit; 'all the painting' carries both senses (painting a portrait, painting a face) without adding an explanation.

### 5.116 OLIVIA.

**Original**

> ’Tis in grain, sir; ’twill endure wind and weather.  

**Current**

> OLIVIA. It is fast-dyed, sir; it will endure wind and weather.

**Proposed**

> OLIVIA. It's dyed in the grain, sir, and won't wash off; it will endure wind and weather.

**Why**

- Technical gloss made clear: 'in grain' = dyed fast with grain (kermes), hence indelible and natural, not cosmetic (Onions 1911, grain 1, citing ''Tis in g., sir; 'twill endure wind and weather'; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, grain: 'in g. == innate, natural, not factitious (used of colours)', Tw. I,5,255). Current 'fast-dyed' is a textile term many listeners will not decode, so the point (her colour is her own, not makeup) is lost. 'dyed in the grain ... won't wash off' keeps the dye image and states the point.

### 5.117 VIOLA.

**Original**

> ’Tis beauty truly blent, whose red and white  
> Nature’s own sweet and cunning hand laid on.  
> Lady, you are the cruel’st she alive  
> If you will lead these graces to the grave,  
> And leave the world no copy.  

**Current**

> VIOLA. It is beauty truly mixed, whose red and white were laid on by Nature’s own sweet and cunning hand. Lady, you are the cruellest woman alive if you would lead these graces to the grave and leave the world no copy.

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. It's beauty truly blended; its red and white were laid on by Nature's own sweet and skilful hand. Lady, you are the cruellest woman alive if you lead these graces to the grave and leave the world no copy, no child to carry them on.

**Why**

- False friend fixed: 'cunning' = skilful, knowing (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, cunning adj. 1, citing 'whose red and white nature's own sweet and c. hand laid on', Tw. I,5,258; Onions 1911, cunning adj. 1 'skilful, clever'). Current keeps 'cunning', which a listener hears as 'sly', turning Viola's praise of natural beauty into a hint of trickery.
- Pun set-up made audible: 'copy' = a reproduction, 'properly and tropically' (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, copy subst. 3, citing 'and leave the world no c.', Tw. I,5,261). Viola means a child who would reproduce these graces; Olivia's reply deliberately takes 'copy' and 'leave' as paperwork and a will. Current leaves 'no copy' unexplained; a short gloss states Viola's sense while the word stays for Olivia to twist.
- 'blent' = blended (Onions 1911, blent; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, blend 1, Tw. I,5,257); current 'mixed' is acceptable, 'blended' keeps the painter's palette. 'If you will lead' -> 'if you lead': current 'if you would lead' turns Viola's warning into a remote hypothesis.

### 5.118 OLIVIA.

**Original**

> O, sir, I will not be so hard-hearted; I will give out divers schedules  
> of my beauty. It shall be inventoried and every particle and utensil  
> labelled to my will: as, item, two lips indifferent red; item, two grey  
> eyes with lids to them; item, one neck, one chin, and so forth. Were  
> you sent hither to praise me?  

**Current**

> OLIVIA. Oh, sir, I will not be so hard-hearted. I will issue several inventories of my beauty. It shall be itemized and every particle and feature labelled by my will — as, item, two lips, fairly red; item, two grey eyes with lids on them; item, one neck, one chin, and so on. Were you sent here to praise me?

**Proposed**

> OLIVIA. Oh, sir, I won't be so hard-hearted. I'll issue several inventories of my beauty. It will be itemized, and every particle and utensil labelled and attached to my will, like this: item, two lips, fairly red; item, two grey eyes, complete with lids; item, one neck, one chin, and so on. Were you sent here to praise me — or to appraise me?

**Why**

- Mis-gloss fixed: 'labelled to my will' = added to my will as a label or codicil (Onions 1911, label vb. 'to add as a "label" or codicil', citing 'every particle and utensil labelled to my will'; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, label: 'a seal appended to a deed'). Current 'labelled by my will' reads as 'by my choice' and loses the joke that she will bequeath her beauty item by item, answering Viola's 'leave the world no copy'.
- Flattened joke restored: 'utensil' = a household implement (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, utensil, citing Tw. I,5,264). Olivia lists her face like pots and pans in a probate inventory, hence eyes 'with lids to them'. Current 'feature' removes the joke; 'utensil' and 'complete with lids' (inventory language) restore it.
- Pun restored: 'praise' also = appraise, value (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, praise vb. 4 'to appraise, to estimate: were you sent hither to p. me?', Tw. I,5,268; Onions 1911, praise vb. 'to appraise, value', same line). Current keeps only 'praise'; the listener now hears the valuer's sense that caps the inventory joke.
- Kept from current: 'several inventories' ('divers schedules'; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, schedule '== inventory' at Tw. I,5,263) and 'fairly red' ('indifferent red'; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, indifferent adv. 'pretty, rather, somewhat', citing 'two lips i. red').

### 5.119 VIOLA.

**Original**

> I see you what you are, you are too proud;  
> But, if you were the devil, you are fair.  
> My lord and master loves you. O, such love  
> Could be but recompens’d though you were crown’d  
> The nonpareil of beauty!  

**Current**

> VIOLA. I see what you are — you are too proud. But even if you were the devil, you are fair. My lord and master loves you. Oh, such love could only be repaid even if you were crowned the unrivalled queen of beauty!

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. I see you for what you are: you are too proud. But even if you were the devil, you are beautiful. My lord and master loves you. Oh, even if you were crowned the unrivalled queen of beauty, you could do no more than repay such love!

**Why**

- Misreading-prone syntax fixed: 'such love / Could be but recompens'd though you were crown'd / The nonpareil of beauty' = even if you were the peerless beauty, returning such love would merely repay it (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, recompense vb. 2 'to requite, to reward', Tw. I,5,272; Onions 1911, nonpareil 'one that has no equal', citing 'The n. of beauty'). Current 'such love could only be repaid even if you were crowned...' is heard as 'could only be repaid by something', and Viola's plea (love him back) is lost.
- False friend fixed by ear: predicative 'you are fair' = you are beautiful; a listener hears 'fair' as 'just' or 'pale'. (The formulaic address 'fair cruelty' in 5.129 is kept.)
- Idiom: 'I see you what you are' -> 'I see you for what you are' (current drops 'you').

### 5.120 OLIVIA. (kept)

**Original**

> How does he love me?  

**Current, kept as is**

> OLIVIA. How does he love me?

### 5.121 VIOLA. (kept)

**Original**

> With adorations, fertile tears,  
> With groans that thunder love, with sighs of fire.  

**Current, kept as is**

> VIOLA. With adoration, abundant tears, with groans that thunder love, with sighs of fire.

### 5.122 OLIVIA.

**Original**

> Your lord does know my mind, I cannot love him:  
> Yet I suppose him virtuous, know him noble,  
> Of great estate, of fresh and stainless youth;  
> In voices well divulg’d, free, learn’d, and valiant,  
> And in dimension and the shape of nature,  
> A gracious person. But yet I cannot love him.  
> He might have took his answer long ago.  

**Current**

> OLIVIA. Your lord knows my mind — I cannot love him. Yet I think him virtuous, know him to be noble, of great estate, of fresh and stainless youth, well reported, free-spirited, learned, and brave, and in stature and physical form, a graceful person. But still I cannot love him. He might have had his answer long ago.

**Proposed**

> OLIVIA. Your lord knows my mind: I cannot love him. Yet I believe he is virtuous, and I know he is noble, of high rank and fortune, of fresh and stainless youth; well spoken of, generous, learned and brave; and in build and natural shape a handsome figure of a man. But still I cannot love him. He could have taken no for an answer long ago.

**Why**

- Mis-gloss fixed: 'free' = noble-minded, generous (Onions 1911, free adj. 1 'of noble or honourable character, generous, magnanimous', citing Tw.N. I.v.281; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, free 11 'of a pure and generous mind', citing 'in voices well divulged, f., learned and valiant'). Current 'free-spirited' means unconventional or uninhibited, which is not the praise Olivia gives.
- Meaning shift fixed: 'He might have took his answer' = he could have accepted my answer as final. Current 'He might have had his answer' says only that he received it; 'taken no for an answer' is the exact modern idiom.
- Archaic words made clear: 'Of great estate' = of high rank (Onions 1911, estate sb. 2 'status, rank, dignity'), left as 'estate' (heard as landed property) in current; 'In voices well divulg'd' = of good repute (Onions 1911, divulge, and voice sb. 3 'general talk, rumour, report', both citing this line); 'dimension' = bodily frame (Onions 1911); 'A gracious person' = an attractive figure (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, gracious 5 'lovely, attractive, beautiful', citing 'a g. person', Tw. I,5,281; Onions 1911, person 1 'bodily figure'). Current 'a graceful person' weakens beauty to deportment.

### 5.123 VIOLA.

**Original**

> If I did love you in my master’s flame,  
> With such a suff’ring, such a deadly life,  
> In your denial I would find no sense,  
> I would not understand it.  

**Current**

> VIOLA. If I loved you with my master’s passion, with such suffering, such a dying life, I would find no sense in your denial — I would not accept it.

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. If I loved you with my master's flame, with such suffering, such a living death, I would find no sense in your refusal; I would not understand it.

**Why**

- Meaning restored: 'I would not understand it' (the refusal would be meaningless to me). Current 'I would not accept it' substitutes defiance for incomprehension. 'sense' = meaning (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, sense, listing Tw. I,5,285 with 'he in the worst s. construes their denial').
- Unidiomatic phrase fixed: 'such a deadly life' = a death-like life (Onions 1911, deadly adj. 'death-like, deathly', citing 'such a d. life'); current 'a dying life' is not English idiom; 'a living death' keeps the paradox.
- Image restored: 'in my master's flame'; current 'with my master's passion' drops the fire.

### 5.124 OLIVIA. (kept)

**Original**

> Why, what would you?  

**Current, kept as is**

> OLIVIA. Why, what would you do?

### 5.125 VIOLA.

**Original**

> Make me a willow cabin at your gate,  
> And call upon my soul within the house;  
> Write loyal cantons of contemned love,  
> And sing them loud even in the dead of night;  
> Hallow your name to the reverberate hills,  
> And make the babbling gossip of the air  
> Cry out Olivia! O, you should not rest  
> Between the elements of air and earth,  
> But you should pity me.  

**Current**

> VIOLA. Make me a willow cabin at your gate, and call upon my soul within the house. Write loyal songs of rejected love, and sing them loud even in the dead of night. Halloo your name to the echoing hills, and make the babbling gossip of the air cry out ‘Olivia!’ Oh, you should find no rest between the elements of air and earth, until you pitied me.

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. I'd make myself a willow cabin at your gate — willow, the tree of rejected lovers — and call out to you, my soul, inside the house. I'd write faithful songs of scorned love and sing them loud even in the dead of night. I'd shout your name to the echoing hills and make the babbling gossip of the air cry out “Olivia!” Oh, you would find no rest anywhere between the elements of air and earth until you pitied me.

**Why**

- Misleading by ear, fixed: 'Make me a willow cabin' = make myself a hut, answering 'what would you do?' (Onions 1911, cabin sb. 1 'temporary shelter of slight materials', citing 'a willow cabin'). Current keeps 'Make me...', which a listener hears as an order to Olivia. 'I'd make myself a willow cabin at your gate' keeps the famous line recognizable.
- Opaque allusion clarified: willow = emblem of disappointed love (Onions 1911, willow 'w. garland, emblem of disappointed love'); a few words added. 'call upon my soul within the house': the soul is Olivia, so 'call out to you, my soul' unpacks it.
- 'cantons' = songs (Onions 1911, canton, citing 'Write loyal cantons'); 'contemned' = scornfully refused (Onions 1911, contemn). Current 'loyal songs of rejected love' is acceptable; 'faithful songs of scorned love' is closer and more natural.
- Archaic word fixed: 'Hallow' = shout (Onions 1911, hallow 'to shout ... once trans. Tw.N. I.v.293'; Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon lists this line under Holla vb. 2 'to cry loudly' and Hollo vb. 'to utter loudly' (O. Edd. hallow), not under Hallow 'to make holy', so no 'holy' pun is imported). Current 'Halloo' is a hunting cry most listeners will not parse. 'reverberate' = resounding (Onions 1911); 'babbling gossip' = tattler (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, gossip subst. 3, citing 'the babbling g. of the air'); after 'echoing hills' the echo is clear, so no gloss is added.
- False friend fixed: 'you should not rest ... But you should pity me', where 'should' = would. Current 'you should find no rest' is heard as 'ought to'. 'the elements of air and earth' kept (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, element 2, citing this line).
- Convention fixed: current quotes the cry in single curly quotes; Standard rule 8 requires curly double quotes.

### 5.126 OLIVIA. (kept)

**Original**

> You might do much.  
> What is your parentage?  

**Current, kept as is**

> OLIVIA. You might do much. What is your parentage?

### 5.127 VIOLA.

**Original**

> Above my fortunes, yet my state is well:  
> I am a gentleman.  

**Current**

> VIOLA. Above my fortunes, yet my situation is good. I am a gentleman.

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. Above my present fortunes, yet my standing is good: I am a gentleman.

**Why**

- Opaque answer made clear: to 'What is your parentage?', 'Above my fortunes' = my birth is higher than my present lot (a servant's), and 'state' = rank, standing. Current 'Above my fortunes, yet my situation is good' leaves the first phrase opaque and blurs rank into circumstances; 'present' and 'standing' make the class point audible.
- Olivia repeats this line word for word at 5.131 (outside the passage); the echo there must use whatever wording 5.127 adopts (see structure_notes).

### 5.128 OLIVIA.

**Original**

> Get you to your lord;  
> I cannot love him: let him send no more,  
> Unless, perchance, you come to me again,  
> To tell me how he takes it. Fare you well:  
> I thank you for your pains: spend this for me.  

**Current**

> OLIVIA. Get yourself back to your lord. I cannot love him. Let him send no more — unless, perhaps, you come to me again to tell me how he takes it. Farewell. I thank you for your trouble. Spend this for me.

**Proposed**

> OLIVIA. Get yourself back to your lord. I cannot love him. Let him send no more — unless, perhaps, you come to me again to tell me how he takes it. Farewell. I thank you for your trouble. Take this, and spend it for my sake.

**Why**

- Ambiguity fixed: 'spend this for me': she offers money (Viola answers 'keep your purse'); 'spend' = lay out, expend (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, spend 5, citing Tw. I,5,302). Current 'Spend this for me' sounds like an errand on her behalf; 'spend it for my sake' makes it a tip. The rest of the current paragraph is kept word for word.

### 5.129 VIOLA.

**Original**

> I am no fee’d post, lady; keep your purse;  
> My master, not myself, lacks recompense.  
> Love make his heart of flint that you shall love,  
> And let your fervour like my master’s be  
> Plac’d in contempt. Farewell, fair cruelty.  

**Current**

> VIOLA. I am no paid messenger, lady. Keep your purse. It is my master, not myself, who lacks recompense. May Love make hard as flint the heart of whomever you fall in love with, and let your passion, like my master’s, be met with contempt. Farewell, fair cruelty.

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. I am no paid messenger, lady; keep your purse. It is my master, not I, who goes unrewarded. May Love give the man you come to love a heart of flint, and may your passion, like my master's, be met with contempt. Farewell, fair cruelty.

**Why**

- Unnatural syntax fixed: 'Love make his heart of flint that you shall love' = may Love harden to flint the heart of the man you will love. Current 'May Love make hard as flint the heart of whomever you fall in love with' inverts the clause, is hard to follow by ear, and drops the original's 'his' (a man).
- Stiff phrase fixed: 'lacks recompense' = goes without requital or reward (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, recompense subst. 2 'requital, reward', citing Tw. I,5,304). 'fee'd post' = paid messenger (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, post subst. 2 'a messenger', citing Tw. I,5,303), kept from current.
- 'Farewell, fair cruelty' kept: famous, and 'fair' in a formulaic address is heard as 'beautiful'.

#### Structure notes

- o_lines use the packet's '|' lines: Gutenberg verse lines, with each prose run counted as one line. Prose paragraphs (5.114, 5.118) therefore map every unit to [1, 1]. Where a sentence ends mid-line (5.119, 5.122, 5.125, 5.128, 5.129), adjacent units share that line number. Each unit carries an extra field 'o' quoting the exact original words it renders.
- 5.114: the stage direction [_Unveiling._] sits inside Olivia's prose paragraph. It is kept in place exactly as the current modern text has it ('[Unveiling.]').
- 5.126 holds two half-lines of verse ('You might do much. / What is your parentage?'); mapped as two units.
- 5.127 is quoted word for word by Olivia at 5.131 (outside this passage). Current 5.131 quotes the untranslated original ('Above my fortunes, yet my state is well: I am a gentleman.') in single curly quotes, while current 5.127 modernizes it, so the echo is already broken. Whatever wording 5.127 adopts must be reused verbatim, in curly double quotes, at 5.131.
- Convention: the live modern text uses curly apostrophes (U+2019) throughout. All proposed text, including 'keep' paragraphs, uses straight apostrophes (Standard rule 8); 'keep' means no word changes.

## TN-B: Act 3, Scene 1: Viola and Feste

*Category: fast dialogue, comedy or wordplay. Paragraphs 11.0–11.23.*

### 11.0 (stage direction, unchanged)

> [Enter Viola and Clown with a tabor.]

### 11.1 VIOLA. (kept)

**Original**

> Save thee, friend, and thy music. Dost thou live by thy tabor?  

**Current, kept as is**

> VIOLA. God save you, friend, and your music. Do you live by your tabor?

### 11.2 CLOWN. (kept)

**Original**

> No, sir, I live by the church.  

**Current, kept as is**

> CLOWN. No, sir, I live by the church.

### 11.3 VIOLA. (kept)

**Original**

> Art thou a churchman?  

**Current, kept as is**

> VIOLA. Are you a churchman?

### 11.4 CLOWN. (kept)

**Original**

> No such matter, sir. I do live by the church, for I do live at my  
> house, and my house doth stand by the church.  

**Current, kept as is**

> CLOWN. No such thing, sir. I live by the church, because I live at my house, and my house stands by the church.

### 11.5 VIOLA.

**Original**

> So thou mayst say the king lies by a beggar, if a beggar dwell near  
> him; or the church stands by thy tabor, if thy tabor stand by the  
> church.  

**Current**

> VIOLA. So you may say the king lies by a beggar, if a beggar dwells near him. Or that the church stands by your tabor, if your tabor stands by the church.

**Proposed**

> VIOLA. So you might as well say the king sleeps beside a beggar, if a beggar lives near him; or that the church stands by your tabor, if your tabor stands by the church.

**Why**

- Wordplay made audible: 'lies by' = lodges, sleeps beside (Schmidt, Shakespeare-Lexicon, lie vb., the lodging sense, citing 'the king --s by a beggar', Tw. III,1,8; Onions 1911, lie 4 'to dwell, sojourn, lodge, esp. to sleep or pass the night somewhere'). Current keeps 'lies by', which a listener now hears as 'tells lies', so Viola's absurd picture (the king in bed beside a beggar) is lost. 'stands by' keeps both senses in modern English (is next to / supports) and is unchanged.
- Register: 'So thou mayst say' -> 'So you might as well say', so the listener hears Viola mocking Feste's logic with his own trick.

### 11.6 CLOWN. (kept)

**Original**

> You have said, sir. To see this age! A sentence is but a chev’ril glove  
> to a good wit. How quickly the wrong side may be turned outward!  

**Current, kept as is**

> CLOWN. You have said it, sir. What an age this is! A sentence is just a kidskin glove to a sharp wit — how quickly the wrong side can be turned outward!

### 11.7 VIOLA.

**Original**

> Nay, that’s certain; they that dally nicely with words may quickly make  
> them wanton.  

**Current**
