# Blind comprehension review: TN

**Reader disclosure.** I recognise this play and know it well. The only files I opened were the brief and my packet. I use two tags throughout:

- **[play knowledge]** means I understood something only because I know the play.
- **[outside knowledge]** means I needed period knowledge, such as an archaic sense of a word or an Elizabethan idea.

A first-time listener would have neither.

The packet never states two facts that I know from the play.

1. Viola is a young woman disguised as a man ("Cesario"). She serves Orsino and loves him.
2. Olivia is veiled and is refusing suitors.

A careful *reader* can infer the disguise from clues:

- the speaker label VIOLA, with that character called "sir";
- in TN-B, the beard lines;
- in TN-C, "were I a woman", "I am all the daughters of my father's house" and "my boy".

A *listener* to TN-A alone gets no textual signal of the disguise.

## Passage TN-A: Olivia unveils; the willow-cabin speech

### Version X

**1. Speakers**

- `5.114`: Olivia means that the envoy's job is to deliver his lord's message and that looking at her face is not part of it; then she unveils anyway, presents her face as a freshly painted portrait and asks whether it isn't good work. / She wants to stay in control of the meeting by teasing the envoy, and she fishes for his verdict. The joke treats her face as a picture behind a curtain; "negotiate with my face" is mock-diplomatic.
- `5.117`: Viola means that Olivia's beauty is natural and perfectly blended by Nature, and that it would be cruel to take it to the grave without a child to carry it on. / She wants to turn the compliment into her master's argument: marry and have children, implicitly with Orsino. She persuades with flattery plus a guilt-trip ("the cruellest woman alive").
- `5.118`: Olivia means she will leave "copies" after all: itemised inventories of her features, attached to her will like goods in an estate. Then she asks whether he came to praise her or to value her like an appraiser. / She wants to deflate both the flattery and the marriage pitch by taking "copy" literally, and to keep the upper hand. The jokes are the mock-legal inventory and the praise/appraise pun.
- `5.119`: Viola means that she sees Olivia is proud, but that even a devil this beautiful would be beautiful; her master loves Olivia, and even the greatest beauty alive could do no more than repay such love. / She wants to rebuke Olivia's pride and press Orsino's suit, part accusation and part plea.
- `5.122`: Olivia means that Orsino knows her answer. She grants him every virtue (noble, rich, young, well spoken of, generous, learned, brave, handsome) and still cannot love him; he should have taken no for an answer long ago. / She wants to close the matter firmly without insulting Orsino.
- `5.125`: Viola means that in Orsino's place she would camp at Olivia's gate in a cabin of willow, the rejected lover's tree. She would sing songs of scorned love all night and shout Olivia's name until the echo repeated it, and give her no rest until she pitied him. / She wants to show Olivia what real devotion looks like and to shame the refusal. The passion sounds personal, not delegated.
- `5.126`: Olivia means that wooing like that might get somewhere, and asks who the envoy's family are. / She wants to know Cesario's rank. This is the first sign that she is interested in the messenger rather than the master.
- `5.127`: Viola means she was born above her present position but is still well placed: a gentleman. / She wants to claim dignity without saying more. This is one sentence in X; I include it to pair with Y.
- `5.128`: Olivia tells the envoy to go back to his lord: she cannot love him, and he should send no more messengers, unless this one returns to report how Orsino took the news. She offers money for the trouble. / She wants to end the suit while inviting Cesario back on a transparent pretext. The tip is a mark of favour.
- `5.129`: Viola means she is not a hired messenger and Olivia should keep her money; it is her master who goes unrewarded. She wishes that the man Olivia falls for will have a heart of flint and scorn her as she scorns Orsino. / She wants to keep her dignity and punish Olivia with a curse; "fair cruelty" is a parting barb.

**2. Unclear points**

- "Now you've strayed from your text." This is clear as "you've gone off-script". I know Olivia is extending an earlier sermon joke **[play knowledge]**, but the line does not need it.
- "But we will draw the curtain and show you the picture." The sudden royal or playful "we" might make a listener wonder who else is involved. Minor.
- "a portrait of me as I was just this moment". The past tense "was" makes me stop: why "was" if this is her face now? I took it as a joke on portrait labels ("the sitter as she was at such a date") **[outside knowledge]**. The gist gets through (her face shown as a fresh portrait), but the joke only half lands.
- "It's dyed in the grain, sir, and won't wash off". The phrase "dyed in the grain" is less familiar than "dyed in the wool", but "won't wash off" rescues it: the colour is natural, not makeup.
- "every particle and utensil labelled and attached to my will". Calling a body part a "utensil" (a kitchen tool) is jarring. It could be deliberate absurdity in a mock inventory, but by ear it sounds like the wrong word. "particle" is also odd.
- "fairly red". This could mean "moderately red" (deadpan understatement) or "beautifully red". Either works; I heard understatement.
- "you could do no more than repay such love". This took a second beat. I took it as "even the greatest beauty in the world would only break even by returning his love".
- "willow, the tree of rejected lovers". This is perfectly clear, but it is an explanatory aside dropped into the middle of a passionate outburst, and it sounds like a footnote in Viola's mouth. It is tonally jarring, though it is the only thing that tells a listener why the cabin is willow.
- "call out to you, my soul, inside the house". This is clear (Olivia is "my soul"), though the phrase in apposition needs a moment.
- "You might do much." (same in Y). This is slightly cryptic. I took it as "you might get somewhere with me", which the question about parentage confirms. I also know that she is falling for Cesario **[play knowledge]**, but the text hints at it well enough ("unless, perhaps, you come to me again").
- "Above my present fortunes, yet my standing is good". This is elliptical: the subject ("my parentage is") carries over from Olivia's question. Clear on a second beat.
- "I am a gentleman" and "May Love give the man you come to love a heart of flint". The double edge is invisible without **[play knowledge]**: Viola is a woman, and Olivia will fall for her. X's "the man you come to love" gives the curse a sharper hidden point.
- "With adoration, abundant tears, with groans that thunder love, with sighs of fire." (same in Y). This is clear, but the tone is ambiguous: an earnest report, or Viola gently sending up Orsino's excess? Probably intended.
- "He could have taken no for an answer long ago." This is clear. It is a modern idiom, but it is not jarring.

**4. Voice**

- **Olivia** is arch, quick and in command: mock-diplomatic, then mock-legal, then a brisk refusal, then a pretext so thin it is funny. She is distinct and alive.
- **Viola** is earnest and direct, and she can be blunt ("you are too proud", "even if you were the devil"). She is lyrical and urgent in the willow speech, where the repeated "I'd ..." drives it, and proud at the end. She is distinct and alive. Only the willow gloss dents her.

### Version Y

**1. Speakers**

- `5.114`: Olivia means and wants the same as in X. "just like this I was at this present time" makes the portrait joke hard to follow, so the tease blurs.
- `5.117`: Viola makes the same argument, but says only "no copy", with no gloss. The point about children is implicit.
- `5.118`: Olivia makes the same inventory joke but ends with a plain jab, "Were you sent here to praise me?", meaning "is flattery your commission?". There is no pun.
- `5.119`: Viola makes the same rebuke and plea, but the logic of the final sentence is scrambled (see below).
- `5.122`: Olivia gives the same refusal and list of virtues, but "free-spirited" misdescribes Orsino.

- `5.126`: Olivia's line is identical to X.

- `5.128`: Olivia gives the same dismissal and invitation, ending "Spend this for me."

**2. Unclear points**

- "You are now off your text." This is understandable but not an idiom (we say "off-script"), and it sounds slightly foreign.
- "Look, sir, just like this I was at this present time." By ear this is the hardest line in the passage. The inverted word order plus "at this present time" sounds like broken English, and the portrait-label joke cannot be recovered without **[outside knowledge]**.
- "Excellently done, if God did all of it." "all of it" is vague. The hint of makeup is fainter than X's "all the painting", though still inferable.
- "It is fast-dyed, sir". This is a dyer's term, and by ear it sounds exactly like "fast died" (quickly died). "endure wind and weather" helps.
- "It is beauty truly mixed". "mixed" can suggest impure or a mixed bag. For paint, "blended" is clearer.
- "sweet and cunning hand". To a modern ear "cunning" means sly, which tilts the compliment toward a dig. I read it as "skilful" only because I know the old sense **[outside knowledge]**.
- "leave the world no copy." After all the talk of painting, "copy" can be heard as a painted replica. I took it as children only because of "lead these graces to the grave" and because I know the argument **[outside knowledge]**. On the other hand, the bare word keeps the double sense that Olivia's inventory joke plays on.
- "labelled by my will". "by my will" can mean "as I wish" rather than "attached to my testament". "inventories" and "item" point toward the testament, but the phrase is muddy.
- "as, item, two lips, fairly red". The archaic "as," (meaning "for example") trips the ear.
- "Oh, such love could only be repaid even if you were crowned the unrivalled queen of beauty!" The logic is scrambled. It can be heard as "only if you were the queen of beauty could you repay it", which is close to the opposite of the point. I recovered the intended sense ("even the greatest beauty could do no more than repay it") only by comparing with X.
- "of great estate", "well reported". These are slightly archaic but gettable: rich and high-ranking; well spoken of.
- "free-spirited". This is misleading, because the modern sense is unconventional or bohemian. A listener would think Olivia is praising Orsino as carefree, and the sense "generous, noble-minded" is lost.
- "in stature and physical form, a graceful person". This is flat and clinical. "a graceful person" undersells a list that should build to "handsome".
- "He might have had his answer long ago." The sense is "he could have accepted his answer". It can also be heard as "he probably received it". Minor.
- "such a dying life". This is an awkward twist on the idiom "living death", though understandable.
- "Make me a willow cabin at your gate". Without "I'd", a modern ear hears an order to Olivia: "(you) make me a willow cabin". There is a real risk of mishearing. Why the cabin is willow is also unexplained **[outside knowledge]**.
- "call upon my soul within the house". This can sound like invoking one's own soul, as in prayer. I took "my soul" to mean Olivia inside the house only because I know the line **[play knowledge]**.
- "Halloo your name to the echoing hills". "Halloo" is an archaic hunting shout. It is gettable as "shout" and vivid, but it can be heard as "hello".
- "Oh, you should find no rest". "should" here means "would", but a modern ear may hear "ought to".
- "Above my fortunes, yet my situation is good." This is elliptical, and "situation" sounds bureaucratic.
- "Spend this for me." This sounds like an errand (spend it on my behalf) rather than "keep it as a token". Viola's "Keep your purse" clears it up afterwards.
- "who lacks recompense", "whomever you fall in love with". Both are clear but stiff.
- "Is it not well done?", "It shall be itemized". Both are slightly stiff and archaic.
- Y has the same **[play knowledge]** ironies as X ("I am a gentleman" and the curse).

## Passage TN-B: Viola and the Clown

### Version X

**1. Speakers**

- `11.1`: Viola greets the Clown and his music and asks whether he earns his living by his drum. / She wants to open some friendly banter, and, it turns out later, to reach Olivia.
- `11.4`: The Clown means he "lives by" the church because his house stands next to it, not because he is a clergyman. / He wants to score with a pun on "live by" (earn a living from / live next to).
- `11.5`: Viola means that by his logic the king "lies by" a beggar if a beggar lives near him, and the church "stands by" (is kept up by) his drum if his drum stands next to the church. / She wants to beat him at his own game by pushing his logic to absurdity.
- `11.6`: The Clown means "exactly": to a sharp wit a sentence is like a soft glove, easily turned inside out. / He wants to concede her point and sigh, mock-grandly, over the times and over slippery language.
- `11.10`: The Clown means that his sister's name is a word, and anyone who "dallies" with it might make her "wanton". Besides, words have been scoundrels ever since bonds (contracts) disgraced them. / He wants a bawdy laugh plus a cynical aside.
- `11.14`: The Clown means he cares about something, just not about Viola; if not caring for her counts as caring for nothing, he wishes that would make her vanish. / He wants to tease and insult, cheekily.
- `11.16`: The Clown means he is not Olivia's fool, because she is too sensible to keep one until she marries. Husbands are just bigger fools, as herrings are bigger than pilchards. He is her "corrupter of words". / He wants to mock husbands and define his job wittily.
- `11.18`: The Clown means that folly goes everywhere, like the sun, and that it would be a pity if the fool did not visit Viola's master as often as his own mistress; in other words, her master is a fool too. He thinks he saw "your wisdom" there. / He wants to needle Viola and her master.
- `11.19`: Viola means that if he is going to mock her she is done with him, and she gives him a coin. / She wants to end the teasing, tip him and move on.
- `11.21`: Viola means that she almost pines for a beard, though not on her own chin, and asks whether his lady is at home. / She wants to voice a private longing obliquely, then get to Olivia.

**2. Unclear points**

- "tabor" (same in Y). This is unfamiliar by ear. The stage direction and "your music" tell a reader it is an instrument; I know it is a small drum **[outside knowledge]**.
- "So you may say the king lies by a beggar, if a beggar dwells near him." "lies by" means sleeps or lodges beside, but it can also be heard as "tells lies". After a moment I heard "sleeps beside".
- "Or that the church stands by your tabor, if your tabor stands by the church." (same in Y). The reversal sounds absurd, which lands. The second sense of "stands by" (is supported by) I caught only on reflection.
- "those who dally cleverly with words can quickly make them wanton". "dally" and "wanton" are dated, and "wanton words" is not idiomatic. The sexual sense only snaps into place with the sister joke.
- "words are great rascals, ever since promissory bonds disgraced them". This is opaque: "promissory bonds" sounds like financial jargon, and it is not clear why bonds would disgrace words. I know the sense only from prior acquaintance with the line **[play/outside knowledge]**: once written contracts were required, a spoken word was worth nothing. The tense also slips ("are ... ever since").
- "I am loath to argue reason with them". Odd phrasing, but gettable.
- "If that means caring for nothing, sir, I wish it would make you invisible." (same in Y). The insult lands, but the logic (you are nothing, so vanish) takes a beat.
- "till she be married". An archaic subjunctive; fine.
- "as pilchards are to herrings: the husband is the bigger one" (same in Y). "pilchards" is unfamiliar to many listeners, but "the husband is the bigger one" rescues the joke.
- "By my troth". A dated oath, gettable as "honestly".
- "I’m almost sick for one, though I wouldn’t want it to grow on my chin." "sick for" is slightly ambiguous: longing for it, or sick of it? More important, the second half only makes sense if you know Viola is a woman in love with a bearded man **[play knowledge]**. At this point the packet has not told the listener that.
- "Is your lady within?" Dated, but clear.
- "Wouldn’t a pair of these have bred, sir?" By ear it is unclear what "these" are. A listener must infer "coins" from Viola's handover, and the begging joke (give me a second coin so they can breed) depends on that.
- "Yes, if kept together and put to use." The sense of "use" as interest is lost on a modern ear **[outside knowledge]**, and "put to use" sounds like "employed". The line still half-works as a joke about breeding animals.

**3. Images, puns and jokes**

| Item | What I took it to mean | First hearing? |
|---|---|---|
| "live by your tabor" / "live by the church" | Earn a living by / live next to | Yes; he explains it |
| "the king lies by a beggar" | By his logic the king sleeps beside a beggar | Partly |

**4. Voice**

- **The Clown** is nimble, cheeky, faintly insolent and angling for a tip. He is distinct and alive; "What an age this is!" is a good grumble.
- **Viola** is quick and good-humoured. She matches him, draws a line when the teasing gets personal, then shows a flash of private feeling. She is alive.
- The period colour ("By my troth", "till she be married", "within", "dally/wanton") adds flavour and some friction.

**The two biggest remaining obstacles in Y:**

1. "ever since written bonds disgraced them" is still opaque.
2. The beard aside ("I'm almost sick with longing for one, though I wouldn't want it to grow on my chin") only makes sense if you know Viola is a woman who loves a man, and nothing in the passage says so.

Runners-up: "tabor", and the logic of "caring for nothing ... invisible".

## Passage TN-C: Orsino and Viola ("Patience on a monument")

### Version X

**1. Speakers**

- `9.34` + `9.36`: The Duke sends everyone out and tells Cesario to go again to that queen of cruelty. His love is above worldly things: he does not care about Olivia's lands, and he values the wealth Fortune gave her as lightly as Fortune does. What draws him is the jewel Nature gave her, her beauty. / He wants Cesario to press the suit again, framed as pure love with no interest in money.
- `9.39`: Viola means that he must accept Olivia's answer. Suppose a lady loved him as much as he loves Olivia, and he could not love her and told her so: wouldn't she have to accept that? / She wants him to see the symmetry and accept Olivia's no. "as perhaps there is" covertly points at herself.
- `9.40`: The Duke means that no woman's body could withstand a passion like his, and no woman's heart could hold it. Women's love is appetite, of the palate rather than the liver, which gorges, sickens and turns away; his is as hungry as the sea, so there is no comparison. / He wants to brush off her analogy and assert that his love is supreme, with open contempt for what women can feel.
- `9.43`: Viola means she knows all too well how women can love: they are as faithful as men. Her father's daughter loved a man as she herself might love the Duke, if she were a woman. / She wants to rebut him and half-confess her own love.
- `9.45`: Viola means that her sister's story is a blank because she never spoke. Hiding her love ate away her bloom, and she wasted away, sickly and sad, like a statue of Patience smiling at grief. Wasn't that love? Men say and swear more, but they show more than they feel. / She wants to move him and to prove that women are constant. She also takes a glance at men's performance, including his, and grieves for herself behind the disguise.
- `9.47`: Viola means she is the only daughter and the only son left in her family, and yet she does not know whether her sister died. Then she asks whether she should go to Olivia. / She wants to stop before she gives herself away. The riddle half-confesses that she is the "sister"; the question changes the subject.
- `9.48`: The Duke means "yes, that's the business": she should hurry, give Olivia this jewel and tell her his love will not retreat or accept a refusal. / He wants action, and closes the reverie.

**2. Unclear points**

- "get yourself to that same sovereign cruelty". This is gettable as a mock title for Olivia (the supremely cruel one), after a beat.
- "doesn’t prize quantities of common land". "common land" misleads, because it sounds like public commons rather than her worldly estates; alternatively "common" might mean "vulgar". I took it as "doesn't care how much land she owns".
- "The parts that fortune has bestowed on her". "parts" can be heard as body parts, an unintended double meaning, or as talents. The meaning is her wealth and rank.
- "I value as fickly as fortune itself". "fickly" is a rare, odd-sounding word. The sense, "as lightly as fickle Fortune", depends on knowing Fortune as the fickle goddess **[outside knowledge]**.
- "But it’s that miracle and queen of jewels which nature decks her in that draws my soul." This sentence has two "that"s and is hard to parse by ear. It never says "beauty", so a listener may picture a real jewel. That picture gets muddled when the Duke hands over an actual jewel in 9.48.
- "Must she not then be given her answer?" This sounds like "shouldn't she get a reply?" rather than "shouldn't she accept the no?", so the parallel blurs.
- "There is no woman’s body that can endure the beating of so strong a passion". "beating" flickers toward violence until "as love gives my heart" resolves it.
- "They lack capacity." Clear, though faintly modern (it suggests mental capacity).
- "no movement of the liver, only the palate". This is opaque: why the liver? It depends on the old idea that the liver is the seat of passion **[outside knowledge]**.
- "which suffers gluttony, satiation, and revolt". This is a list of abstract nouns, and "revolt" sounds political before it resolves into nausea.
- "as it might be perhaps, were I a woman, I should love your lordship". This is tangled and archaic ("should" meaning "would"), though gettable on a second pass.
- "Too well, what love women may bear men." Elliptical, but fine after "What do you know?".
- "a green and yellow melancholy" (same in Y). The colours suggest a sickly pallor, though "green" may suggest envy. Gettable as an ill-looking sadness.
- "like Patience on a monument, smiling at grief". The capital P is not audible, so a listener may hear the quality "patience". "on a monument" suggests a statue, and it works after a beat.
- "Was not this love, indeed?" Archaic word order, but clear.
- "our shows are more than our will". This is opaque by ear: "shows" means displays and "will" means desire. The next sentence helps.
- "I am all the daughters of my father’s house, and all the brothers too. And yet I know not." This is a riddle. That her brother is lost, presumed drowned, is **[play knowledge]**; that the "sister" is Viola herself the text implies fairly clearly. "And yet I know not" leaves open what she does not know: whether the sister died, or whether the brother is dead. Possibly intended.
- "Yes, that’s the matter." A modern ear hears "that's the problem", but the intended sense is "that's the business".
- "To her in haste." Elliptical but clear.

**3. Images, puns and jokes**

| Item | What I took it to mean | First hearing? |
|---|---|---|
| "sovereign cruelty" | A mock title with a built-in contradiction | Mostly yes |
| "my love, more noble than the world" | Hyperbole | Yes |
| Fortune's gifts valued "as fickly as fortune itself" | He values her wealth as lightly as Fortune does | Partly |
| "that miracle and queen of jewels which nature decks her in" | Her beauty as Nature's jewel | Partly, on reflection |

| Love as appetite: palate versus liver | Women's love is shallow; true passion lives in the liver | Partly: the palate yes, the liver no |
| "as hungry as the sea, and can digest as much" | His love is endless | Yes; strong |
| "concealment, like a worm in the bud, feed on her rosy cheek" | Hidden love eating her bloom from inside, like a worm in a rosebud | Yes; "rosy" makes the rose link |
| "a green and yellow melancholy" | A sickly pallor | Partly |

**4. Voice**

- **The Duke** is grand, imperious and self-absorbed ("Let all the rest withdraw", "To her in haste"). His hyperbole is alive, though "fickly" and the knotted jewel sentence make him stiff.
- **Viola** is gentle, persistent and reasoning, then quietly heartbroken. The archaisms ("were I a woman, I should love", "Was not this love, indeed?", "I know not") give her lines a formal, elegiac colour that suits the moment but costs some clarity.

### Version Y

**1. Speakers**

- `9.34` + `9.36`: The Duke's meaning is the same, but he spells out what draws him: "her beauty".
- `9.39`: Viola's meaning is the same, and the logic is sharp: "Then mustn't she accept that answer?"
- `9.40`: The Duke's meaning is the same, with the liver glossed and the palate's decline made concrete.
- `9.43`: Viola's meaning is the same, in clean syntax ("as I might perhaps love your lordship, if I were a woman").
- `9.45`: Viola's meaning is the same, with "pined away in silent longing", "carved on a monument" and "our show is greater than what we really feel".
- `9.47`: identical to X apart from "I don't know".
- `9.48`: The Duke's meaning is the same, with "the business at hand" and "Go to her quickly".

**2. Unclear points**

- "go to that same queen of cruelty". Clear, and the echo with "queen of jewels" is pleasant.
- "sets no value on any quantity of dirty land". "dirty" here means mere earth, base property. For a moment it can sound like polluted land, but the contempt for property comes through.
- "The gifts that Fortune has bestowed on her, tell her I hold as carelessly as Fortune does." This is mostly clear, because "carelessly" does the work. A listener still needs a sense of Fortune as a careless giver.
- "that queen of jewels Nature has decked her out in: her beauty". This is clear. The tacked-on ": her beauty" makes the Duke explain his own metaphor, which is flat, but it removes the confusion with a literal jewel.
- "not a stirring of the liver, the seat of passion, but of the palate". This is clear, but the gloss sounds like a footnote in the Duke's mouth.
- "they cannot retain it". Clear.
- "a green and yellow melancholy" is the same as in X.
- "I am all the daughters of my father's house, and all the brothers too. And yet I don't know." This is the same riddle, with the same dependence on **[play knowledge]**.
- "Yes, that's the business at hand." / "Go to her quickly." Clear, but a bit managerial.

**3. Images, puns and jokes**

The images are the same as in X and are generally clearer:

| Item | What I took it to mean | First hearing? |
|---|---|---|
| "queen of cruelty" | A mock title for Olivia | Yes |
| "dirty land" | Mere earth; he despises her property | Yes; odd but gettable |
| "hold as carelessly as Fortune does" | He values her wealth as lightly as Fortune does | Mostly yes |
| "queen of jewels ... her beauty" | Her beauty as Nature's jewel | Yes, because it is glossed |
| "No woman's ribs can bear the pounding" | A heartbeat no woman's body could withstand. More physical and vivid than X. | Yes |
| "overeats, grows sick of the taste, and revolts in disgust" | Women's love is shallow appetite | Yes |
| "every bit as hungry as the sea" | His love is endless | Yes |
| "the love a woman can bear me and the love I bear Olivia" | A neat balance on "bear" | Yes |
| Worm in the bud; Patience "carved on a monument" | As in X | Yes |
| The riddle in 9.47 | Viola is the "sister"; her brother is lost | Partly: the woman part yes, the brother part no without **[play knowledge]** |

**4. Voice**

- **The Duke** is still grand and self-regarding, and "ribs ... pounding" and "every bit as hungry as the sea" are vivid. But the embedded glosses (": her beauty", "the seat of passion") make him sound as if he is annotating himself, and "Go to her quickly" and "the business at hand" are plainer than a duke should sound.
- **Viola** is clear and still tender. "pined away in silent longing" is a little more conventional than X's "pined in her thoughts", but the speech still moves.

### TN-C: X vs Y

**Y is clearer by ear throughout.** In each pair below, Y comes first and is clearer:

| Y | X |
|---|---|
| "queen of cruelty" | "sovereign cruelty" |
| "dirty land" | the misleading "common land" |
| "hold as carelessly as Fortune does" | "value as fickly as fortune itself" |
| ": her beauty" | the knotted "that miracle and queen of jewels which nature decks her in that draws my soul" |
| "accept that answer" | "be given her answer" |
| "the seat of passion" explains the liver | the liver is unexplained |
| "overeats, grows sick of the taste, and revolts in disgust" | "suffers gluttony, satiation, and revolt" |
| "if I were a woman" | "were I a woman, I should love" |
| "carved on a monument" | "on a monument" |
| "what we really feel" | "our shows are more than our will" |
| "the business at hand" | "that's the matter" |

**Y is at least X's equal in vividness and voice.** It is more physical in the Duke's big speech ("ribs", "pounding", "overeats"). X keeps a more compressed, formal music in Viola's lines, and it never makes the Duke explain himself.

**What Y loses:** the pleasure of working out "queen of jewels" for oneself; some of the Duke's grandeur, because of the self-glosses and the managerial ending; and a touch of Viola's restraint.

**Preference: Y.**

**The two biggest remaining obstacles in Y:**

1. "I am all the daughters of my father's house, and all the brothers too. And yet I don't know." The riddle needs the backstory (a lost brother; she is the sister), and it is unclear what she does not know.
2. The pair in the Duke's opening, "sets no value on any quantity of dirty land" and "The gifts that Fortune has bestowed on her, tell her I hold as carelessly as Fortune does". This abstract contrast between wealth and beauty takes effort by ear, and "dirty land" is an odd phrase.

Runner-up: the footnote-like gloss "the seat of passion".

## Summary

| Passage | Preference | Biggest obstacle 1 (in the preferred version) | Biggest obstacle 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| TN-A | **X** | "a portrait of me as I was just this moment": the portrait joke half-lands and the "was" puzzles | "willow, the tree of rejected lovers": a clear but footnote-like aside that breaks the willow speech |
| TN-B | **Y** | "ever since written bonds disgraced them": still opaque | The beard aside, "sick with longing for one, though I wouldn't want it to grow on my chin", which needs the knowledge that Viola is a woman in love |
| TN-C | **Y** | "I am all the daughters of my father's house, and all the brothers too. And yet I don't know.": the riddle needs the backstory | "dirty land" and "hold as carelessly as Fortune does": an abstract opening contrast |

**Across the passages:** neither version is better everywhere. Y is clearer in B and C. In A, however, Y has the passage's worst snags: "just like this I was at this present time", "free-spirited", "such love could only be repaid even if ..." and "Make me a willow cabin". X wins A decisively and loses B and C.

**A minor note for readers:** TN-B says "Count Orsino's", but the speaker label in TN-C is "DUKE". A reader of the packet might wonder whether these are the same man; they are **[play knowledge]**. A listener never hears "Duke", so this only affects readers.
