**Disclosure.** I already know this play well. I have tried to judge each version the way a first-time listener would hear it. **PK** marks anything I understood only because I know the play. **1st** means "got it on first hearing": yes, partly or no.

# Passage LR-A: the storm

## LR-A, version X

### 1. Speakers
- **11.1**: Lear means: he orders the storm to do its worst. It should flood the church towers, let the lightning burn his white head, flatten the round earth and smash the molds and seeds that ungrateful people are made from. / Wants: every ungrateful person wiped out, even at the cost of the world and himself. The speech is a curse and an invitation at once.
- **11.2**: Fool means: flattery in a dry house beats a soaking outdoors. Lear should go in and ask his daughters for their blessing, because this night spares no one, wise or foolish. / Wants: to get Lear under a roof. He pleads through a joke: a father asking his children's blessing is a pointed reversal.
- **11.3**: Lear means: the storm owes him nothing, since he never gave it a kingdom or called it his child, so it may do what it likes to a helpless old man. Then he turns on it: the elements are servile lackeys who join his two wicked daughters against him. / Wants: to set the storm's blameless cruelty against his daughters' guilt. Self-pity turns into accusation.
- **11.4**: Fool means: a man with a house to put his head in has a good head. A man who finds a home for his codpiece (sex, marriage) before he has a roof ends up with lice, and that's how beggars marry. A man who treats his toe the way he should treat his heart will be kept awake by a corn. Then comes an unrelated line about pretty women pulling faces in mirrors. / Wants: to needle Lear, who has no house now, about getting his priorities wrong, and to lighten the scene with bawdy nonsense.
- **11.9**: Kent means: he's shocked to find Lear out in this. Even night creatures are hiding, he has never known such a storm, and nobody can bear it. / Wants, implicitly: Lear sheltered.
- **11.10**: Lear means: the storm is the gods hunting down the guilty. He calls on hidden sinners (murderers, perjurers, incestuous hypocrites, secret plotters) to tremble, burst out of hiding and beg for mercy. He himself is more victim than sinner. / Wants: hidden guilt exposed and punished, and himself declared the wronged party.
- **11.11**: Kent means: Lear is bareheaded, and a hovel nearby gives some protection. Lear should rest there while Kent goes back to the house that just turned him away (its people harder than its stones) and forces them to show the hospitality they begrudge. / Wants: Lear sheltered now, and the hosts shamed or forced into decency.
- **11.12**: Lear means: his mind is slipping. He suddenly notices that the Fool is cold, and that he is cold himself. Need makes worthless things like straw precious. He asks to be led to the hovel and tells the Fool that part of his heart still grieves for him. / Wants: shelter, and to comfort the Fool. This is the first tenderness in the passage.
- Short lines: 11.5, "No, I will be the pattern of all patience; I will say nothing." The irony is audible straight after his raging. 1st: yes. For 11.8, see section 3.

### 2. Unclear points
- **"nuncle"** (11.2, and throughout the Fool's speech). Never explained. I heard it as a pet form of "uncle", so a listener may wonder whether the Fool is a relative. That it is the Fool's name for Lear is PK.
- "the holy water of court flattery in a dry house". A slightly overloaded phrase, but because "flattery" is said aloud, the point lands.
- "forerunners of the thunderbolts that split oaks". To a modern listener lightning *is* the thunderbolt. I took "forerunners" to mean heralds. Minor.
- "who will join your battalions, bred high in the heavens, with two wicked daughters". The aside "bred high in the heavens" splits "join … with". I took it as "ally your sky-born armies with my daughters". A slight tangle by ear.
- "finds a home for his codpiece". You have to know what a codpiece is (a pouch over the crotch). I took it as "has sex or marries before he has a home". A listener who doesn't know the word loses the joke.
- "The man who cherishes his toe as he should cherish his heart". The general sense is clear: wrong priorities bring pain. How it applies to Lear (favouring the flattering daughters over the loving one) needs the plot, which is PK. No third daughter is mentioned in this passage.
- **"For there was never yet a fair woman who didn't make faces at herself in a mirror."** Opaque. "For" promises a reason that never comes. I took it as a jester's non sequitur, perhaps a jab at vanity or pretence. Baffling on first hearing.
- "here's His Grace and a codpiece; that's a wise man and a fool". "His Grace" is Lear, which is clear. "a codpiece" standing for the Fool himself needs you to know how fools dressed (PK). The joke that it's unclear which one is the wise man only half-lands.
- "behind a hidden, respectable front". A "hidden … front" is slightly self-contradictory, and "front" has a modern criminal-racket ring. I took it as "a hypocritical cover". A minor tonal slip.
- "this hard house". It is never said whose house this is. I inferred from "go in, and ask your daughters' blessing" that the daughters are inside. That works.
- "Where is this straw, my fellow?". Nobody has mentioned straw. I inferred bedding in the hovel, with "my fellow" addressed to Kent. Minor.
- "make vile things precious". Today "vile" means disgusting, but here it means lowly. The sense survives.

### 4. Voice
- **Lear**: huge and alive in 11.1, 11.3 and 11.10. The drop into broken tenderness in 11.12 ("Are you cold? I'm cold myself") is clearly heard. Distinct.
- **Fool**: sharp in 11.2. In 11.4 he becomes explained prose. The song-riddle loses its jingle and he sounds as if he is annotating himself. This is the most flattened voice in the passage.
- **Kent**: plain, practical and loyal, with a soldier's directness. Distinct.

## LR-A, version Y

### 1. Speakers (what a listener actually gets from Y)
- **11.1**: Lear means the same: flood the steeples, burn his head, flatten the world, smash nature's molds. / Wants the same annihilation. A listener gets much of it from the rhythm rather than from individual words ("cataracts", "rotundity").
- **11.2**: Fool means, as heard: court, with its "holy water", in a dry house is better than standing in the rain, so go in and ask your daughters' blessing. / Wants Lear indoors. A listener will probably miss that "holy water at court" means flattery, so the point about swallowing pride and flattering is lost.
- **11.3**: Lear means the same as in X, but here the elements are "servile ministers" who join his "pernicious daughters" in battles "high-engendered". / Wants the same.
- **11.4**: Fool means: a house means a good "headpiece" (the pun is unexplained). Then something about a codpiece housing itself before the head, about lice, and about beggars marrying "many". A man who puts his toe before his heart will cry over a corn. Then the mirror line. / Wants the same as in X, but by ear most of it is fog.
- **11.9**: Kent, the same as in X, a little stiffer.
- **11.10**: Lear, the same catalogue of hidden sinners, heard through archaisms: "Caitiff", "rive", "convenient seeming".
- **11.11**: Kent, the same, but the plan at the end is compressed: "return, and force their stinted courtesy".
- **11.12**: Lear, the same, but "Poor fool and knave" can sound harsher than intended.

### 2. Unclear points
- **"You cataracts and hurricanes, spout"**. Today "cataracts" first means the eye condition, and hurricanes don't "spout". I took them as waterfalls or downpours and storms; that they are waterspouts is PK. Jarring by ear.
- "You sulphurous and thought-swift fires". The word "lightning" never appears, but it can be inferred. "thought-swift" is compact and works.
- "the thick rotundity of the world". Latinate. "Rotundity" can sound comic, as in "rotund" meaning fat. I took it as "the round bulk of the earth".
- "spill all seeds at once, that make ungrateful man". The clause "that make" is stranded after "at once". I parsed it on second thought.
- "nuncle": as in X.
- **"holy water at court, in a dry house"**. Opaque. That "court holy water" means flattery is PK. By ear it sounds like "the comforts of court".
- "So let fall your horrible pleasure." It can be heard as "your pleasure is horrible". I took it as "do whatever horrible thing you like". Mildly unclear.
- "servile ministers". "Ministers" means agents here, but a modern ear hears government or church ministers. Mild.
- "in your high-engendered battles". An opaque compound. I guessed "battles bred in the sky".
- "has a good headpiece". Unexplained: it could be heard as a helmet, or as good brains. The roof-and-head pun half-lands.
- **"The codpiece that will house itself before the head has any, both the head and he shall have lice — so beggars marry many."** Very hard by ear. The codpiece is personified, and "he" means the codpiece. "so beggars marry many" invites the wrong reading, "beggars marry many wives". This is verse word order without the rhyme that would justify it.
- "shall, of a corn, cry woe, and turn his sleep to waking". Inverted word order. "of a corn" means because of a corn on the toe, but "corn" could be heard as grain. The toe/woe rhyme helps a little.
- The mirror line: as in X.
- **"here's grace and a codpiece"**. Without "His", "grace" doesn't obviously mean the king; it sounds like virtue or decorum. I connected it to Lear only through X and PK.
- "I do not remember ever to have heard". Stiff, but clear.
- "horrid thunder". "Horrid" now sounds weak and childish, as in "horrid weather". A tonal problem.
- "you wretch, who has within you undivulged crimes". "you … who has" jars grammatically, and "undivulged" is bookish.
- **"Caitiff, shake to pieces"**. An opaque archaism that could be heard as a name. That it means wretch or villain is PK.
- "under cover and convenient seeming". "convenient seeming" is opaque. I took it as "a suitable-looking front".
- **"Close pent-up guilts, rive your concealing containers"**. "Close" can be heard as the verb, as in "close the pent-up guilts!". "rive" is archaic for split, and "containers" sounds modern and domestic. I took the line as "secret guilts, burst out of your hiding places".
- "these dreadful summoners". Without X's "who call you to judgment", the word is vaguer. I guessed "those who summon you to trial".
- "while I go to this hard house — … — return, and force their stinted courtesy". The long aside separates "go" from "return", so "return" sounds like "come back to you". "force their stinted courtesy" is compressed. I took it as "go back and force their grudging hospitality".
- **"Poor fool and knave"**. "Knave" now means rogue, so it sounds like an insult in a tender moment. That it means "lad" here is PK.

### 4. Voice
- **Lear**: grander and more sonorous in 11.1, with compact compounds and Latinate weight. In 11.10 the archaisms make him sound like a period pageant rather than a man. "knave" risks souring the tenderness of 11.12.
- **Fool**: barely audible as a joker in 11.4. It is neither clear prose nor a clean song.
- **Kent**: more formal ("Gracious my lord", "I do not remember ever to have heard") and a little stiffer than in X.

## LR-A: X versus Y
- **Clearer by ear: X, decisively.** Compare "cloudbursts and waterspouts" with "cataracts and hurricanes"; "holy water of court flattery" with "holy water at court"; the codpiece riddle as plain prose with Y's inverted half-verse; "His Grace" with "grace"; "You villain … split open the coverings that conceal you" with "Caitiff … rive your concealing containers"; "force them to give the courtesy they grudge" with "return, and force their stinted courtesy"; and "Poor fool, poor lad" with "Poor fool and knave".
- **Vividness: split.** Y's 11.1 is more compact and musical: "thought-swift fires, advance guards to oak-cleaving thunderbolts" and "Crack nature's moulds, spill all seeds at once". It also keeps a scrap of the Fool's rhyme (toe/woe). But X is not flat. It has "thick round globe of the world" and "Rumble your bellyful!", and it keeps an image that Y dropped: "lend you some friendship against the tempest", where Y has plain "shelter".
- **What X loses:** the compression of Lear's first speech; the Fool's song shape in 11.4, where the headpiece joke comes with its footnote attached; and the double sense of "grace" in 11.8.
- **Preference: X.**
- **Two biggest remaining obstacles in X:**
  1. **Fool, 11.4.** The codpiece and toe/heart riddles never show how they apply to Lear, and "For there was never yet a fair woman who didn't make faces at herself in a mirror" is a non sequitur that "For" makes sound like a conclusion.
  2. **11.8, "Why, here's His Grace and a codpiece; that's a wise man and a fool."** The listener has to know that a codpiece stands for the Fool in his costume, and the "which one is wise?" joke only half-lands. "nuncle", used throughout and never explained, adds to the same fog around the Fool.

# Passage LR-B: the Fool and the coxcomb

## LR-B, version X

### 1. Speakers
- **4.63**: Fool means: he'll hire Kent too, and pays him with his fool's cap. / Wants: to brand Kent a fool for signing on with Lear. A joke-insult.
- **4.68**: Fool means: Kent earns the cap for backing a man out of favour. If you can't turn with the wind of favour you'll be left out in the cold. Lear has "banished" the two daughters he gave everything to and has blessed the third by accident, so anyone who follows him is a fool. Then, to Lear: he wishes he had two caps and two daughters. / Wants: to warn Kent, to tell Lear under cover of a jest that he's been a fool, and to set up a riddle.
- **4.70**: Fool means: if he gave two daughters everything, he'd deserve both fool's caps himself. Lear can have this cap and beg a second from his daughters. / Wants: to call Lear a double fool who now lives on his daughters' charity.
- **4.72**: Fool means: truth is a dog whipped out to the kennel, while a pampered bitch, "Lady Brach", warms herself by the fire and stinks. / Wants: to protest that truth is punished and flattery rewarded. It deflects the whip threat and jabs at the daughters.
- **4.76**: Fool means: a string of prudent maxims (hide your wealth, say less than you know, lend less than you have, ride rather than walk, don't believe all you hear, don't bet everything, give up drink and whores, stay home), and you'll come out ahead. / Wants: to mock Lear by listing everything he failed to do.
- **4.78**: Fool means: if it's nothing, it's like a lawyer's advice given free, since Kent paid nothing for it. Then: can Lear make any use of nothing? / Wants: payment, as a joke, and to lure Lear into saying "nothing".
- **4.81–82**: Fool means: tell him that's exactly what his land brings in now, which is nothing; he won't believe it from a fool. / Wants: to drive home that Lear has nothing.
- **4.85**: Lear plays along: "No, lad. Teach me."
- **4.86**: Fool means: bring here the lord who advised giving away the land, or stand in for him yourself. Then both fools appear together: the one in motley (me) and the other exposed (you). / Wants: to name Lear the bitter fool, returning Lear's "A bitter fool".
- **4.88**: Fool means: you've given away every other title, and "fool" is the one you were born with. / Wants: to sting. It's the best joke in the scene.
- **4.90**: Fool means: great men won't let him be "altogether" the fool. If he had a monopoly on folly, lords and ladies would demand a share. Then he offers two crowns for an egg. / Wants: to satirise the great and set up the next riddle.
- **4.92**: Fool means: the two halves of an eggshell are "crowns". Lear split his crown and gave both halves away, like a man carrying his donkey instead of riding it. There was little wit in his bald crown when he gave the golden one away. If anyone calls this folly, whip him. / Wants: to mock the division of the kingdom and to head off the whip.
- **4.94**: Song. Fools are out of favour because wise men have become fools and act like apes.
- **4.96–98**: Fool means: he's been singing ever since Lear made his daughters his mothers, handing them the rod and dropping his own breeches for a spanking. They wept for joy; he sang for sorrow that a king would play baby games and join the fools. He asks for a schoolmaster to teach him to lie. / Wants: to mock Lear for making himself a child, and to hint that lying is what gets rewarded.
- Short lines: 4.71 and 4.99 are Lear's whip threats. By the end the double bind (whipped for the truth, whipped for lies) is only half set up, because the passage stops first.

### 2. Unclear points
- "Let me hire him too." Who hired whom? "too" implies Lear has just taken Kent on, which can be inferred. That Kent is newly hired, in disguise, is PK.
- "nuncle": as in LR-A, used five times and never explained.
- **"this fellow has banished two of his daughters, the two he gave everything to, and done the third a blessing against his will"**. Even with the gloss, the paradox baffles. Why is giving them everything "banishing" them? What blessing did the third daughter get? I understood it only through PK, or through LR-C later in this packet.
- "I wish I had two coxcombs and two daughters!" / "If I gave them everything I own, I'd keep my coxcombs myself." The logic (two daughters given everything means two fool's caps) takes a second pass.
- "Fellow, I'll teach you a speech." (4.74) Who is addressed? The Fool used "Fellow" to Kent in 4.66, but Lear answers "Do." That it is addressed to Lear is PK.
- **"at dice stake less than you throw for"**. Unclear. I took it as "don't bet everything on one throw".
- **"and you shall have more than two tens to a score"**. An opaque punchline for anyone who doesn't know that a score is 20. Even then, "more than 20 in your 20", meaning you come out ahead, takes a moment.
- "the other found out there". "Found out" (exposed) could be heard as "found, out there". Nobody says which fool is sweet and which is bitter. I took it as: I'm the sweet fool in motley, and you're the bitter one, exposed.
- "you carried your ass, your donkey, on your back". The gloss prevents the "backside" reading, but it is clunky. Most people don't know the fable of the man who carried his donkey (PK). I took it as an absurd role reversal.
- "let the man be whipped who first finds it so". "Finds it so" is a little opaque. I took it as "whoever first calls this folly deserves the whip, because it's true".
- Layout, not an ear problem: 4.80–4.82 splits one speech across three ids ("the rent from his land / comes to — nothing"), so on the page it looks like a broken line.
- The songs in 4.94 and 4.98 are marked "[Singing.]" but are prose. By ear they don't sound like songs.

### 4. Voice
- **Fool**: quick and impudent, clearly the liveliest voice. But X keeps attaching footnotes to his punchlines: "my fool's cap", "the bitch-hound", "a jester's motley", "your ass, your donkey", "— like a fool". His songs are prose, so he sometimes sounds like an annotated edition of himself.
- **Lear**: indulgent at first ("my pretty lad", "boy", "lad"), then stung ("A bitter fool"). Without "sirrah" the edge between master and servant is softer, and "Take heed, boy — the whip" threatens less.
- **Kent**: dry and brief. Fine.

## LR-B, version Y

### 1. Speakers (same content as X; below is what the listener loses)
- **4.63**: Fool hires Kent too and hands over his "coxcomb". The word is unexplained; the stage direction explains it to a reader, not to a listener.
- **4.68**: Same as X, but "smile as the wind sits" and the unexplained "banished two of his daughters" make the point harder to follow.
- **4.70**: Same. "all my living" means all I own.
- **4.72**: Same, but "Lady Brach" is unexplained. The listener hears a pampered lady or dog, not the "bitch" insult.
- **4.76**: The same maxims, now as a rhyming jingle.
- **4.78, 4.81–82, 4.88**: The same.
- **4.86**: The same, in rhymed verse.
- **4.90**: The same, but elliptical: "won't let me".
- **4.92, 4.94, 4.96–98**: The same content. 4.94 rhymes; 4.98 keeps the inverted verse order.

### 2. Unclear points
- **"coxcomb"** (4.63 onward, eight times). Never explained in the spoken words. A listener who doesn't know it means a fool's cap, or who knows only "coxcomb" as a conceited fop, loses the running gag. PK.
- **"my pretty knave, how are you doing?"** "Knave" sounds like "rogue", and the modern "how are you doing?" beside it is a clash of registers.
- "Sirrah" (4.66, 4.71, 4.74, 4.95, 4.99). A recognisable Shakespearean form of address and fine by ear. But in 4.74 ("Sirrah, I'll teach you a speech") the addressee is unclear. That it is Lear is PK.
- "smile as the wind sits". Archaic, and "favor" is never named. Half clear, thanks to "catch cold".
- **"this fellow has banished two of his daughters and did the third a blessing against his will"**. The paradox is unexplained. With LR-C in mind, a listener would think the Fool has it backwards. PK.
- "Truth's a dog who must to kennel". Elliptical, but understandable.
- **"Lady Brach"**. Opaque; it sounds like the name of a lady at court. That a brach is a bitch-hound is PK.
- "A pestilent gall to me!" "Gall" now means cheek or impudence. I took it as "a damned irritant". Mildly ambiguous.
- **"Lend less than you owe"**. Misleading, because a modern listener hears "owe" as a debt. That it means "own" is PK.
- "Learn more than you trust". Ambiguous. I took it as "know more than you rely on or believe".
- "Wager less than you throw" and "Than two tens to a score": as in X.
- "Please tell him—that's just what the rent of his land comes to now". The listener must link "that's" back to "nothing", and the dash sounds like a break-off. Slightly less clear than X's "— nothing".
- **"Or do you stand for him."** It sounds like a question ("do you stand up for him?") rather than "or you stand in his place". A real ambiguity.
- "Will presently appear". "Presently" means at once here; a modern ear hears "soon". Mild.
- **"The one in motley here"**. "Motley" is unexplained. PK.
- "No, faith—lords and great men won't let me." Won't let him what? The listener has to reach back to Kent's "not altogether foolish". The "it" in "If I had a monopoly on it" is clarified only later.
- **"you carried your ass on your back across the mud"**. A modern ear hears "ass" as backside, and the fable is lost. It gets a laugh for the wrong reason. PK.
- "If I'm speaking like myself in this". That "like myself" means "like a fool" is never spelled out.
- "Fools had never less grace in a year". "Grace" means favour here, but can be heard as gracefulness.
- "Then they for sudden joy did weep, And I for sorrow sung". Inverted order and the archaic "sung". The rhyme partners are missing ("peek-a-boo" and "go among the fools" don't rhyme with weep or sung), so it sounds half-finished.
- "put down your own breeches". An odd verb, but understood through "the rod".

### 4. Voice
- **Fool**: sounds much more like a professional jester. He rhymes in 4.76, 4.86 and 4.94, says "Sirrah" to a king, and swears "faith". But the jokes that carry the scene (coxcomb, Lady Brach, motley, ass) often don't land by ear, so much of his liveliness is sound without sense.
- **Lear**: "sirrah" gives him a master's bite; "Take heed, sirrah—the whip" is sharper than X's "boy". But "my pretty knave, how are you doing?" is a collision of registers.
- **Kent**: the same as in X.

## LR-B: X versus Y
- **Clearer by ear: X.** The scene is a chain of jokes that depend on "coxcomb", "Lady Brach", "motley" and "ass". X explains each one, and Y leaves them to the listener's vocabulary. X also names the paradox ("the two he gave everything to") and spells out "— nothing", "won't let me be altogether foolish" and "— like a fool". It avoids Y's "Lend less than you owe" and "Or do you stand for him".
- **More vivid, more voice: Y.** In Y the maxims (4.76), the sweet/bitter fool (4.86) and "foppish/apish" (4.94) are songs; in X they are prose. Y's "sirrah" and "knave" keep the status game going, and "Sirrah, I'll teach you a speech", said to a king, is cheekier than "Fellow".
- **What X loses:** the Fool's music. X's "[Singing.]" cues introduce prose, and the glosses sometimes land right after the punchline and slow it down ("your ass, your donkey").
- **Preference: X, narrowly,** because the jokes have to land on first hearing for the Fool to work at all. I'd want Y's rhymes back in 4.76, 4.86 and 4.94 wherever they cost no sense. That means keeping "own", not "owe", and "or you stand in for him".
- **Two biggest remaining obstacles in X:**
  1. **4.68–4.70, the daughters/coxcomb paradox.** "has banished two of his daughters, the two he gave everything to, and done the third a blessing against his will" and "If I gave them everything I own, I'd keep my coxcombs myself" still need the plot.
  2. **The Fool's rhymes flattened into prose, so their payoffs misfire.** Above all "at dice stake less than you throw for … more than two tens to a score", and the "[Singing.]" passages that don't sound sung.

# Passage LR-C: the love test

## LR-C, version X

### 1. Speakers
- **1.20**: Lear means: he'll now reveal his hidden plan. The kingdom is divided in three; he's retiring and handing his burdens to younger people. He'll announce his daughters' dowries now to prevent quarrels later. France and Burgundy, rival suitors for his youngest, will get their answer. Each daughter must say how much she loves him, so the biggest share goes where "nature" and merit meet. / Wants: a public ceremony of love, an orderly succession, and flattery he can reward.
- **1.21**: Goneril means: she loves him beyond words, beyond sight, space and liberty, beyond any value, as much as life, as much as any child ever loved, so much that breath and speech fail. / Wants: the biggest share, through hyperbole. The irony that she has plenty of words is audible.
- **1.23**: Cordelia, aside, means: she can't compete in words and will love in silence. / Wants: to stay true.
- **1.24**: Lear means: Goneril gets the land from this line to that one (forests, plains, rivers, meadows), for her and Albany's heirs, forever. Now Regan. / Wants: to reward Goneril and keep the ceremony moving.
- **1.25**: Regan means: she is made of the same stuff as Goneril and just as worthy. Goneril described her love exactly but fell short, because Regan rejects every other pleasure and is happy only in Lear's love. / Wants: to outbid her sister.
- **1.27–28**: Cordelia means: poor me. No, not poor, because my love weighs more than my words. / Wants: to steady herself.
- **1.29**: Lear means: Regan gets a third equal to Goneril's. Now Cordelia, his joy and his youngest, courted by France and Burgundy: what will she say to win a third richer than her sisters'? / Wants: Cordelia's declaration. It also reveals that he has saved the best third for her.
- **1.33**: Lear means: nothing earns nothing, so speak again. / Wants: a proper answer. It is a warning.
- **1.34**: Cordelia means: she can't force her heart up into her mouth. She loves him exactly as her bond as a daughter requires. / Wants: to be honest without performing.
- **1.35**: Lear means: what's this? Improve your answer or ruin your prospects. / Wants: compliance. It is a threat.
- **1.36**: Cordelia means: she gives back what she owes (obedience, love, honour). Her sisters' claims are false, since they married. When she marries, her husband will get half her love, and she won't marry and still claim all her love is her father's. / Wants: to expose her sisters and to argue for proportion.
- **1.41**: Lear means: then her honesty is her only dowry. He swears by the sun, Hecate, the night and the stars that he renounces all fatherly care and kinship. She is now a stranger. A savage Scythian, or a cannibal who eats his own children, will be as dear to him as she is. / Wants: to punish her absolutely, through a formal curse and disowning.

### 2. Unclear points
- **"Meanwhile we'll reveal our deeper purpose."** Meanwhile what? The passage opens with no antecedent. That it means "while Gloucester fetches France and Burgundy" is PK.
- **"Our son of Cornwall, and you, our no less loving son of Albany"**. By ear, "son of Cornwall" means Cornwall's son. I took it as son-in-law only through PK and Y. A real risk of misparsing.
- "great rivals in our youngest daughter's love". "In" is slightly odd, but fine.
- **"where nature is matched by merit"**. Vague: is "nature" natural affection or birthright? I took it as "where natural love is matched by deserving".
- "dearer than eyesight, space, and liberty". "Space" is odd: room to move, or outer space? Mild.
- "I'm made of the same metal as my sister". Heard as metal or mettle. It works either way.
- **"I find she names my very deed of love"**. Opaque. Is a "deed" a legal title or an act? I took it as "she describes my love exactly".
- "all other joys that the most refined senses can possess". Vague. I took it as "all other pleasures of the senses".
- "no less in size, validity, and pleasure". "Validity" sounds legal. I took it as "value".
- "although the last and least". "Least" means youngest or smallest, but it could sound like a slight just after "our joy".
- **"the vines of France and milk of Burgundy compete to be chosen"**. "milk of Burgundy" puzzles a modern ear, for which Burgundy means wine. I took it as France (vineyards) and Burgundy (pasture) standing for the two suitors.
- "according to my bond". "Bond" is the tie between daughter and father, but it can sound financial or legal. Its coldness is partly the point.
- "How now, Cordelia?" Archaic, but widely known.
- **"Surely I'll never marry like my sisters, to love my father all."** Ambiguous: it can be heard as "I'll never marry" followed by a fragment. I took it as "I won't marry the way my sisters did and still claim all my love is my father's".
- "But do you mean what you say?" Clear, but flattened. It sounds like a modern teacher, not a stunned king.
- **"by the mysteries of Hecate and the night"**. "Hecate" is unexplained. Many won't know the goddess, and by ear "HEK-it" may not even register as a name. PK.
- "from which we exist and cease to be". The preposition is slightly odd. Fine.
- "The barbarous Scythian". An unexplained reference, though "barbarous" carries the meaning. PK.

### 3. Images, puns and jokes
| Item | What I took it to mean | 1st |
|---|---|---|
| Map, "from this line to that one" | He points out the shares | yes |
| "younger shoulders"; "crawl unburdened toward death" | Handing over the load; old age | yes |

### 4. Voice
- **Lear**: the regal "we", expansive and orderly, then brittle. The curse is strong. But his two key reactions ("But do you mean what you say?", "so unfeeling?") sound ordinary, so his hurt goes flat at the turning point.
- **Goneril**: gushing and over-stuffed. **Regan**: competitive and accountant-like ("rate myself at her worth", "names my very deed"). The two are distinct but close, which is the point.
- **Cordelia**: short and plain, clearly different from both sisters.

## LR-C, version Y

### 1. Speakers (same content as X; below is what Y changes for the listener)
- **1.20**: Lear's plan is spelled out: "the part of our purpose we have kept hidden", "son-in-law Cornwall" and "son-in-law Albany", the suitors who "have long stayed at our court", and "where natural love, joined with merit, claims it as its due". Every piece comes across, but the speech is long.
- **1.21**: Goneril's "a love that leaves breath too poor and speech too weak to tell it" is clearer and still an image (poverty).
- **1.25**: Regan's "she has spelled out the very deed of my love" is clearer.
- **1.29**: Lear's "value" and "the vineyards of France and the milk-rich pastures of Burgundy compete to stake their claim" are clearer.
- **1.34**: Cordelia's "according to my bond, my duty as your daughter" glosses "bond".
- **1.35**: Lear's "What? What, Cordelia?" is audible shock.
- **1.36**: Cordelia's "Perhaps, when I marry …" and "and still give all my love to my father" are clear.
- **1.37 / 1.39**: Lear's "But is your heart in this?" and "So young, and so untender?" are emotional.
- **1.41**: "Hecate, goddess of witchcraft", "every claim of blood that makes you mine" and "the man who makes meals of his own children" are clear and vivid.

### 2. Unclear points
- **"Meanwhile we'll reveal the part of our purpose we have kept hidden."** The same missing antecedent as in X (PK).
- **1.20 as a whole.** The listener must carry the dowries, the suitors and the divesting list before the question arrives. Each clause is clear; the whole is heavy by ear.
- "conferring them on younger strength". Abstract, but fine.
- "dearer than eyesight, space, and liberty": as in X.
- **"she has spelled out the very deed of my love"**. Better, though "deed" is still half legal. The gist is clear.
- **"all other joys that the most refined senses can possess"**: as in X, vague.
- "although the last and least": as in X.
- "compete to stake their claim". A modern, gold-rush idiom. Mild.
- "What? What, Cordelia?" It could sound as if he didn't hear her, but mostly it reads as spluttering disbelief.
- "Perhaps, when I marry … shall carry". "Perhaps" softens her and sits awkwardly beside "shall". Minor.
- "by the secret rites of Hecate, goddess of witchcraft, and of the night". The gloss helps. The comma leaves "and of the night" hanging, but it's fine.
- **"The barbarous Scythian"**: as in X, unexplained.

### 3. Images, puns and jokes
The list is as in X, with these differences:
- "younger strength" is plainer than X's "shoulders". 1st: yes.
- "leaves breath too poor and speech too weak to tell it". 1st: yes; clearer than X, with the poverty image intact.
- "spelled out the very deed of my love". 1st: partly to yes.
- "vineyards of France and the milk-rich pastures of Burgundy". 1st: yes.
- "untender" and "true". 1st: yes. The pun on tender (young, soft) is audible, and X loses it with "unfeeling".
- "But is your heart in this?" echoes "heave my heart into my mouth". 1st: yes.
- "the man who makes meals of his own children". 1st: yes; grotesque and vivid.
- "every claim of blood that makes you mine". 1st: yes; it hurts.
- Hecate, glossed. 1st: yes. The Scythian. 1st: partly.

### 4. Voice
- **Lear**: fuller and more emotional at the turn ("What? What, Cordelia?", "is your heart in this?", "untender"), so his hurt is heard before the curse. His opening is more explained and heavier.
- **Goneril and Regan**: as in X. Regan is a little clearer.
- **Cordelia**: slightly diluted by the gloss "my duty as your daughter" and by "Perhaps", but still plain and distinct.

## LR-C: X versus Y
- **Clearer by ear: Y.** Compare "son-in-law Cornwall" with "son of Cornwall"; "where natural love, joined with merit, claims it as its due" with "where nature is matched by merit"; "spelled out the very deed of my love" with "names my very deed of love"; "value" with "validity"; "milk-rich pastures of Burgundy" with "milk of Burgundy"; "and still give all my love to my father" with "to love my father all"; and "Hecate, goddess of witchcraft" with a bare "Hecate".
- **More vivid, more voice: also Y, at the emotional turn.** Compare "What? What, Cordelia?" with "How now, Cordelia?"; "But is your heart in this?" with "But do you mean what you say?"; "untender" with "unfeeling"; and "every claim of blood that makes you mine" with "all kinship and blood-tie with you". X keeps "younger shoulders", which is better than "younger strength", and a tighter opening.
- **What Y loses:** concision. Lear's opening is longer. Cordelia's "bond" gets a gloss she wouldn't say herself ("my duty as your daughter"), and "Perhaps" slightly softens her terseness.
- **Preference: Y.**
- **Two biggest remaining obstacles in Y:**
  1. **Lear's opening speech (1.20).** It starts with a "Meanwhile" that refers back to nothing. The listener must then carry the Cornwall/Albany address, the dowries, the France/Burgundy aside and the divesting list before the love question arrives.
  2. **Regan's bid (1.25).** "she has spelled out the very deed of my love" and "all other joys that the most refined senses can possess" are images that can't be unpacked on one hearing, though the gist survives.
  - A close third: "The barbarous Scythian" (1.41), an unexplained reference at the climax that "barbarous" and the cannibal parallel only partly rescue.
