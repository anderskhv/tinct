**Sources.** I read only `BLIND-BRIEF.md` and `blind-calib-MV.md`. I opened no other file and looked nothing up.

**What I already knew.** I recognise this play and know it well: the casket test, the suitors, Shylock's bond and the daughter's flight. I cannot un-know it. **[PK]** marks every point I understood only, or mainly, because I know the play. My clarity judgements are about what the text itself gives a first-time listener.

## MV-A: Bassanio chooses the lead casket; Portia's aside; the portrait

**What a first-time listener must infer.** There are several caskets, among them gold and lead. The right choice wins Portia, and the prize inside is her portrait and a scroll. Nothing states the rules. The listener has to rebuild them from "here I choose", the stage direction, "Fair Portia's likeness!" and "the scroll … of my fortune".

### Version X

**Speakers**
- 14.13 — Bassanio means that appearances lie. A sweet voice hides a corrupt plea in law. A solemn face blesses error "with a text" in religion. Cowards wear heroes' beards, and beauty is bought (cosmetics, dead people's hair). So ornament is a lure, and he rejects the gold casket and a second, "pale" one and takes lead, whose unpromising look moves him more than eloquence. / He wants to choose right and win Portia. He is arguing himself, and anyone listening, out of the showy choice, and ends with a prayer ("Joy be the consequence!").
- 14.14–14.16 — Portia (aside) means her other feelings (doubt, despair, fear, jealousy) have vanished in a rush of joy. She begs love to ease off because the joy is too much. / She wants to keep her composure; this is a plea to love. Her joy tells the listener that lead is the right casket and that she knew it.
- 14.17 — Bassanio means he has found Portia's portrait, and he marvels at how lifelike it is. The eyes seem to move, breath parts the lips, and the painted hair is a golden net for men's hearts. The eyes are so dazzling that the painter should have been blinded after painting the first one. Then he says his praise undervalues the picture as much as the picture falls short of the real woman, and turns to the scroll. / He wants to express his wonder and to learn his fate from the scroll. The conceits are playful and rapturous.

**Unclear points** (exact quotes, with what I took them to mean)
- "So outward shows may be the least like themselves." — "least like themselves" is paradoxical by ear. I took it as: appearances may be least like the reality underneath. The opening "So" hangs from nothing in the excerpt.
- "The world is still deceived with ornament." — By ear "still" means "even now", not "always".
- "In law, what plea so tainted and corrupt that, being seasoned with a graceful voice, doesn't obscure the appearance of evil?" — The verb is missing ("what plea [is] so…") and the rhetorical question is negative, so it takes a second pass. I took it as: no plea is so rotten that a pleasing delivery can't hide it.
- "In religion, what damned error, but some sober brow will bless it, and approve it with a text" — "what X, but Y will…" means "what X is there that Y won't…". The construction is archaic and hard to parse live. "sober brow" is a solemn face, meaning a cleric. **"approve it with a text": a modern ear first hears a phone text.** I only got "scripture" from "In religion".
- "There's no vice so simple" — Is "simple" pure/unmixed or unsophisticated? The gist survives either way.
- "How many cowards … still wear upon their chins the beards of Hercules and frowning Mars—who, inwardly examined, have livers white as milk—and these put on …" — The sentence starts as an exclamation and restarts with "and these"; loose but followable. "livers white as milk" means cowardice. I got it through "lily-livered"; the text doesn't gloss it.
- "Look on beauty, and you'll see it's bought by weight, which therein works a miracle in nature, making them lightest who wear most of it." — What is "bought by weight"? Cosmetics or false hair have to be inferred. "which therein" has a vague antecedent. The pun on *lightest* (light = loose, unchaste) isn't signalled, so a first-time listener hears only a weight paradox. I got the pun from knowing Shakespeare's use of "light" **[PK]**.
- "which make such wanton play with the wind upon supposed fairness" — "upon supposed fairness" is abstract. I took it as "on the head of a supposed beauty".
- "are often known to be the dowry of a second head—the skull that bred them lying in the sepulchre" — "dowry" is odd. From the dash clause I rebuilt "the hair grew on a now-dead head, so it's a wig". That works on reflection, not instantly, and "wig" is never said.
- **"the beautiful scarf veiling an Indian beauty"** — This is opaque, and it seems to argue backwards: veiling a *beauty* isn't deceptive, so the image contradicts the point. I know the period sense **[PK]**: the "beauty" under the scarf was not then considered beautiful. X hides that so completely that the image fails. "Indian" is also ambiguous: South Asian or American?
- "hard food for Midas" — This needs the myth that Midas's food turned to gold. Many listeners know "the Midas touch" but not the food detail.
- **"Nor any of you, pale and common drudge between man and man."** — Who is "you"? Silver is never named. I know it's the silver casket, silver coin passing from hand to hand **[PK]**. A first-time listener may not even know there's a third casket.
- "which threatens rather than promises anything" — What threat: lead's grim look, or an inscription? The text never says. **[PK]** The caskets carry inscriptions.
- "your paleness moves me more than eloquence" — Silver has just been dismissed as "pale", so praising lead for its "paleness" sounds contradictory.
- "How all the other passions fly to air— as doubtful thoughts" — "as", meaning "such as", is archaic. By ear it sounds like "as if" or "while". "rash-embraced despair" is a compressed compound that needs a beat.
- "In measure rain your joy" — The word order is inverted, and "rain", "reign" and "rein" sound identical. "Rein your joy" (restrain it) also fits, so the ambiguity still lands on the right sense.
- **"What demi-god has come so near to creation?"** — On first hearing, who is the demi-god: Portia, Bassanio, the painter? The painter is only named three sentences later ("the painter plays the spider"), so the listener has to reinterpret backwards. "so near to creation" means "so near to creating life".
- **"Or, riding on the balls of mine, do they only seem in motion?"** — "the balls of mine" means "my eyeballs", but by ear it invites a snigger at a rapturous moment. It is unintended and jarring.
- "so sweet a barrier should ever sunder such sweet friends" — This sounds like a lament ("that it should *ever* part them!"), not "it's fitting that…". "sunder" is archaic. The image survives: breath parts the lips.
- "faster than gnats in cobwebs" — "faster" is heard as speed, not firmness. Harmless.
- "Having made one, I'd think it should have power to steal both of his own and leave itself unfinished." — "both of his own" (eyes) is elliptical. "leave itself unfinished" is slightly off: it's the pair, or the portrait, that's left unfinished. The conceit, that the first painted eye would blind the painter, can be recovered with effort.
- **"Yet look how far the substance of my praise wrongs this shadow by underprizing it, just so far does this shadow limp behind the substance."** — This is the hardest sentence in the passage:
  - "substance" appears twice in different senses: what my praise says, then the real woman.
  - "shadow", meaning the picture, is never glossed.
  - "underprizing" is archaic.
  - The "look how far … just so far" frame is lopsided.

  I took it as: my praise falls as short of the picture as the picture falls short of her.
- "the content and summary of my fortune" — "content" can be heard as "contented". I took it as "contents".

**Voice.**
- **Bassanio (the casket speech):** dense, rhetorical and incantatory. He has the sweep of a set speech and sounds alive and elevated, but he is often archaic enough to lose the listener.
- **Portia:** the aside keeps its rhymes (air/despair, jealousy/ecstasy, excess/less), so it sounds like a breathless, heightened song. She is distinct and alive.
- **Bassanio (at the portrait):** rapturous and witty, but he trips on "balls" and on the shadow/substance knot.

### Version Y

**Speakers**

- "just as far as the substance of my praise wrongs this shadow, this painted image, by undervaluing it, so far does this shadow limp behind the substance, the living woman." — The glosses help. But it is still one long balanced sentence with "substance" in two senses. It remains the hardest line, and it is now also cluttered.

**Images, puns and jokes.** These are the same as in X. What changes is how many land on first hearing:

- The dead person's hair as "a wig": got on first hearing.
- The scarf over "a dark Indian face": understood on first hearing, but jarring.

- "Let your joy rain down in due measure": got. "rain down" removes the rain/reign/rein ambiguity.

- Eyes "riding on my own moving eyeballs": got, with no snigger.
- The spider-painter's golden mesh, trapping hearts "faster and more firmly": got; the image is intact.

- Shadow/substance: got with effort, thanks to the glosses.

**Voice.**
- **Bassanio (the casket speech):** clearer, but at times he sounds as if he's footnoting his own speech: "silver, you pale and common drudge that passes between man and man as money"; "this shadow, this painted image"; "the substance, the living woman"; "a brave man's beard"; "the coward's mark". He is still eloquent and alive, with less momentum and compression.
- **Portia:** the aside loses one rhyme (air/"rashly") and ends clinically ("overindulge and sicken"). It is slightly flatter than X, but still her own.
- **Bassanio (at the portrait):** fully alive, and easier to follow than in X.

### Compare MV-A

**Clearer by ear: Y, clearly.** It fixes X's real stoppers:
- "approve it with a text" (heard as texting) becomes "with a text from scripture".
- The unnamed silver casket is named: "silver … as money".
- "What demi-god has come so near to creation?" becomes "demi-god of a painter … creating life".
- "the balls of mine" becomes "my own moving eyeballs".
- "so sweet a barrier should ever sunder" becomes "it's fitting that…".
- The unheard "lightest" pun is made audible.
- "the dowry of a second head" gains "a wig".

**Vividness and voice: X keeps more.** It is more compressed and musical. The clearest case is Portia's rhymed aside ("fly to air … rash-embraced despair"), and the curt "hard food for Midas, I'll have none of you" is another. X is also clearer at "the mere outward show of valor" and more exact with "deceptive shore".

**What Y loses.** Its appositive glosses slow Bassanio's rhetoric and explain two jokes. Its "dark Indian face" line replaces X's self-contradicting image with an explicit, jarring one. Neither version handles that line well.

**Preference: Y.**

## MV-B: Portia and Nerissa review the suitors

**What a first-time listener must infer.** "If I married him" tells the listener that "them" are suitors. The casket test and the father's will only appear at 2.20.

### Version X

**Speakers**
- 2.7 (one sentence) — Portia sets up the game. Nerissa names them, Portia describes them, and Nerissa judges how she feels.
- 2.9 — Portia means the Neapolitan prince is an immature horse bore who is proud he can shoe his own horse, so perhaps his mother slept with a blacksmith. / She wants to amuse Nerissa; the mockery is cruel but affectionate.
- 2.11 — Portia means the Count Palatine only frowns, never smiles at a joke, and will end up a weeping philosopher. She'd rather marry a skull than either of these two. / Mockery, ending in a mock prayer ("God protect me from them both!").
- 2.13 — Portia means Monsieur Le Bon has no self of his own. He outdoes each rival at that rival's vice, dances at birdsong and fences with his shadow. Marrying him would mean marrying twenty men, and she could never love him back. / Mockery with mock contrition ("I know it's a sin to be a mocker, but him!").
- 2.15 — Portia means she and the English baron share no language. He's handsome but might as well be a silent picture, and he wears a patchwork of foreign fashions and borrowed manners. / Mockery of an Englishman who apes foreign fashion.
- 2.17 — Portia means the Scottish lord took a slap from the Englishman and vowed to "repay" it later. The Frenchman guaranteed that debt and pledged another. / An extended loan joke. **[PK]** The point is the French–Scottish alliance against England, which is not in the text.
- 2.19 — Portia means the German is a drunk: bad sober, worse drunk, barely human. She hopes to escape him. / Mockery.
- 2.20 (one sentence) — Nerissa warns that if he chooses the right casket, Portia must accept him under her father's will. / It is a practical warning. It reminds Portia of her constraint and tells the listener about it.
- 2.21 — Portia means she'll rig the test by putting wine on a wrong casket to lure the German. She'll do anything rather than marry a drunk. / A scheme plus a punchline.

**Unclear points**
- **"Then there's the County Palatine."**, then **"the Count Palatine"** (2.13) — "County" sounds like a place, and the switch suggests two different things. **[PK]** "County" is an old form of "Count".
- "that one is a colt indeed" — Not glossed, but clear enough from "he does nothing but talk about his horse": he is immature and horsey.
- "had a fling" — Modern and casual, but clear; it didn't jar me.
- "the weeping philosopher" — The reference is opaque (it's Heraclitus, **[PK]**). The gist, a gloomy old sage, explains itself.
- "unmannerly sadness" — Slightly archaic, but clear.
- "If he'd scorn me" — Slightly off grammar ("if he scorned me"), but clear.
- **"What about Falconbridge …?" / "You know I say nothing to him"** — Portia's answer plays on "say to", but Nerissa's question doesn't set it up, so the joke is invisible. The line reads only as "I don't talk to him."
- "you'd swear in court that my English is worth only a penny" — It takes a beat: why court? I took it as "you could testify that I have hardly any English".
- **"his round hose in France"** — "round hose" is an opaque garment.
- "he borrowed a box on the ear" — A box on the ear is a slap; the phrase is slightly old-fashioned. The borrow-and-repay frame is clear once you see it.
- **"I think the Frenchman went his guarantor, and signed on for another."** — "signed on for another" is opaque: another slap, or another loan? I took it as: the Frenchman backed the Scot's revenge and pledged another blow. The alliance is **[PK]**.
- **"And whatever the worst that could happen, I hope I can manage to do without him."** — The grammar is garbled: "whatever the worst that could happen" has no verb. The sense is recoverable: however things go, I hope to avoid him.
- "a deep glass of Rhenish wine" — "Rhenish" is mildly opaque; "wine" rescues it.
- "if there's a devil inside him and that temptation outside" — Clear: his inner demon plus the outward lure.
- "marry a sponge" — In context, a drunkard. But today "sponge" also means a freeloader. There is slight ambiguity, and the punchline is strong.

## MV-C: Shylock, Salarino and Solanio; then Tubal

**What a first-time listener must infer.**
- The excerpt opens mid-exchange, so "That's certain, if the devil may be her judge." has no antecedent here. **[PK]** Shylock has just said his daughter is damned for running off.
- The bond's penalty is only implied, by "if he forfeits, you won't take his flesh". A first-time listener can infer it; **[PK]** supplies the pound of flesh.

### Version X

**Speakers**
- 13.16 — Solanio twists Shylock's "flesh and blood to rebel" into a sex joke: is your flesh still rising with lust at your age? / He wants to humiliate Shylock ("old carrion").
- 13.18 — Salarino means the daughter is nothing like Shylock: as different as black jet from white ivory, or red wine from white. Then he pivots to ask for news of Antonio's losses at sea. / An insult (she is fair and fine, he is dark and base), then fishing for news.
- 13.19 — Shylock means Antonio is another bad deal: bankrupt, prodigal, afraid to show his face on the Rialto, once so dapper. Antonio called him usurer and lent free of interest, so now let him beware his bond. / A threat, gloating and grievance, hammered home by the refrain.
- 13.20 — Salarino means that surely Shylock won't really take Antonio's flesh if he defaults, since it's no use to him. / He is trying to wave the threat away, and mocks it.
- 13.21 — Shylock means he'll take the flesh for fish bait and for revenge.
  - Antonio has damaged him at every turn only because he is a Jew.
  - Yet Jews have the same bodies, senses and feelings as Christians, and, like Christians, will take revenge when wronged.
  - He will practise the villainy Christians taught him, and outdo them.

  / He is justifying revenge, arguing from shared humanity, turning Christian hypocrisy back on his mockers, and threatening.
- 13.26 — Solanio means Tubal is another Jew, and no third could match these two unless the devil became a Jew. / An insult as he leaves.
- 13.28 — Shylock greets Tubal and asks for news from Genoa: has he found the daughter? / He wants her back, and his property.

**Unclear points**
- "That's certain, if the devil may be her judge." — No antecedent in the excerpt **[PK]**.
- "black jet and white ivory" — Clear. It makes the mockers' colour-coded contempt explicit, which may jar. But it is plainly their contempt, not the text's.
- "red wine and white Rhine wine" — Clear, if slightly redundant.
- "show his head on the Rialto" — A proper noun, but "the marketplace" in the next clause helps.
- "Let him look to his bond." — "look to" means "beware"; repetition makes it clear. The "bond" is the loan contract, and its penalty is only implied.
- "Has not a Jew eyes? Has not a Jew hands, organs, dimensions …" — Archaic word order, but familiar. "dimensions" is slightly abstract.
- "If you prick us" — Context ("bleed") makes "pierce" unmistakable. No snag.
- "If a Jew wrongs a Christian, what is his humility? Revenge." — Compressed sarcasm: the Christian's so-called humility answers with revenge. "his" resolves to the nearest noun, "a Christian", and the next sentence's parallel confirms it. A minor beat.
- "and it will take a great deal to stop me from improving on the instruction." — Clear but wordy. The speech's climax sags.
- "I often came to places where I heard of her, but I can't find her." — A tense slip ("can't" for "couldn't"). Harmless.
- **"The curse never fell upon our nation until now — I never felt it until now."** — Which curse? I took it as a curse on the Jewish people that he only now feels personally. The reference is opaque to a first-time listener; the gist ("I feel cursed") survives.
- **"Why, you — loss upon loss!"** — It sounds as if Shylock starts to insult Tubal ("Why, you —") and then breaks off. Who he is addressing is unclear.
- "No news of them? Why so?" and "Ha, ha—heard in Genoa?" — Fine.

- "who dares scarcely show his head" — Archaic word order; fine.

- "it shall go hard but I will improve on the instruction" — An archaic idiom. A modern ear hears "it'll be hard, but I'll outdo you", which is close enough, and the line rings.
