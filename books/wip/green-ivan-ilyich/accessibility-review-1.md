# Accessibility Review 1 — The Death of Ivan Ilyich (candidate.json)

**Reviewer:** blind pass, candidate.json only. No source text or drafter notes consulted.

**Coverage:** All 12 chapters, all 298 paragraphs, read in full, start to finish, then
re-scanned paragraph by paragraph for specific wording. Nothing sampled or skipped.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** This reads as fluent, contemporary literary prose
throughout — clear sentence rhythm, natural dialogue, no archaic diction, no
mechanically-chopped syntax. The handful of items below are small, mostly
untranslated foreign-language fragments and period-specific terms that a general
reader could stumble on for a beat, not structural accessibility problems. Several
long paragraphs (the funeral scene in ch1, the career summary in ch2-3, the office
routine in ch3 §16) run 300-500+ words but read smoothly because they're built from
short, clear clauses in sequence — they are dense with incident, not hard to parse.

## Specific issues

1. **Ch1 [16]** — "Piccadilly side-whiskers." A general reader may not know this
   refers to a mutton-chop style of facial hair named for a London street/fashion
   association; it's a visual detail that could pass by unnoticed rather than land.
   Minor.

2. **Ch2 [1]** — "le phénix de la famille — the family's shining exception." This one
   is self-glossed in-line (the dash translation), so it works fine as written —
   flagging only because it's the first of several untranslated French phrases and
   sets the pattern.

3. **Ch2 [4]** — "bon enfant" left untranslated and unglossed. A reader without French
   has to guess from context (it's clear enough — "good-natured, well-mannered" — but
   requires inference).

4. **Ch2 [5]** — "Il faut que jeunesse se passe" is immediately glossed ("youth must
   have its fling") — no issue, works well.

5. **Ch2 [7]** — "comme il faut" used twice in this paragraph ("just as comme il faut
   and just as decorous") without gloss. Reader has to infer "properly correct/proper"
   from the adjacent "decorous."

6. **Ch2 [9]** — "the game of vint." Vint (a Russian precursor to bridge) is named but
   never explained; a reader may wonder whether it's a card game, a dance, or
   something else until "bridge" appears later in the text and retroactively clarifies
   it by association. Mild friction on first encounter.

7. **Ch2 [13]** — "de gaieté de coeur" left untranslated and unglossed ("Without any
   reason — de gaieté de coeur, as Ivan Ilych put it to himself — his wife began...").
   The surrounding "Without any reason" does the semantic work, so meaning survives,
   but the phrase itself is opaque to a non-French reader.

8. **Ch3 [3]** — "the Empress Marya's Institutions" is a proper-noun reference (a real
   Russian charitable/administrative body) with no gloss; a general reader will read
   past it as "some other bureaucratic option," which is close enough to the intended
   sense (Ivan Ilych doesn't care which), so this is low-severity.

9. **Ch6 [1]** — "There was a syllogism he had picked up from Kiesewetter's Logic."
   "Syllogism" is used without definition, and "Kiesewetter's Logic" names a specific
   19th-century textbook a reader won't recognize. The passage does immediately
   demonstrate what a syllogism is via the Caius example, so the term is functionally
   explained by what follows — but a reader who doesn't already know the word
   "syllogism" gets no direct definition, only the worked example.

10. **Ch8 [43]** — "his hair curled à la Capoul" — an unglossed reference to a specific
    19th-century hairstyle (named for singer Victor Capoul). A reader gets "curled" and
    can picture *something*, but the specific cultural reference is opaque. Low
    severity — doesn't affect comprehension of the scene.

11. **Ch10 [4]** — "Morocco is expensive, but it doesn't hold up well." "Morocco" here
    means morocco leather (a type of goatskin leather), established two sentences
    earlier ("the creases in the morocco leather"), so it is technically glossed by
    proximity — but a fast reader could momentarily read "Morocco" as the country and
    have to backtrack. Very low severity given the immediate prior sentence.

12. **Ch8 [19]** — Tense shift: "An hour goes by like that, and another. But now the
    doorbell rings... He is fresh, hearty, plump..." (paragraph 19 and the doctor
    exchange that follows shift from past to present tense for several paragraphs,
    then the narration returns to past). This is a deliberate device (the narration
    tightens into the immediate, trapped present of Ivan Ilych's dying days) and reads
    as intentional rather than an error, but a reader moving quickly could experience
    it as a jarring inconsistency rather than register the effect. Flagging for
    awareness, not as a defect — this is a stylistic/structural choice, not
    unglossed vocabulary.

## What's working well

- Dialogue throughout (the drawing-room scenes in ch1, the family scenes in ch8 and
  ch11) is natural and easy to follow, with clear speaker attribution.
- The extended free-indirect passages tracking Ivan Ilych's denial, bargaining, and
  rage (ch4 [10], ch6 [4]-[8], ch9 [17]-[21], ch12 throughout) are rendered in plain,
  direct sentences that carry the psychological content without softening it —
  "screamed without stopping," "he hated her with his whole soul," "he dropped into a
  soft armchair and stared in horror at his bare, withered thighs" all land with full
  force, nothing sanded down.
- Technical/legal vocabulary (examining magistrate, Public Prosecutor, the Code of
  1864, Court of Cassation) is used consistently and the surrounding sentences supply
  enough function ("he had the power to indict and imprison anyone he chose") that a
  reader doesn't need to know Russian civil-service ranks to follow the plot.
- The Gerasim scenes (ch7) read with real warmth and clarity — no accessibility
  issues at all there.

## Summary

The chapter — the whole book — is clear, well-paced, contemporary prose. The
issues above are all small: a cluster of untranslated French social-register phrases
(items 3, 5, 7) and a few unglossed period/cultural proper nouns (items 1, 8, 10,
11) that a reader will either infer from context or briefly skate over without losing
the thread. None of them block comprehension of a scene or a plot point. Recommend:
targeted fixes only where a gloss can be added briefly and naturally (vint, comme il
faut, de gaieté de coeur are the best candidates); the rest are non-blocking.
