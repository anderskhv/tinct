37|With passions so mild and so healthy a restraint, men who were more wild than wicked, and more concerned to protect themselves from harm than to inflict it on other animals, were not exposed to dangerous quarrels. They had no dealings with one another, and so knew nothing of vanity, respect, esteem or contempt. They had no notion of what we call mine and yours, and no true idea of justice. They regarded any violence done to them as an injury easily made good, not as an offense deserving punishment, and they never so much as dreamed of revenge, except perhaps mechanically and on the spur of the moment, like a dog that bites the stone thrown at it. Their disputes would seldom have ended in bloodshed if they had never been caused by anything more serious than food. But there is a more dangerous cause of conflict, which I must not leave unmentioned.
38|Of the many passions that move the human heart there is one of a hot and impetuous nature which makes the two sexes necessary to each other, a terrible passion that scorns every danger, sweeps aside every obstacle, and in its frenzy seems fit to destroy the very human race it is meant to preserve. What would become of men given over to this lawless and brutal rage, without modesty and without shame, fighting every day over the objects of their desire at the cost of their blood?
39|We must first admit that the fiercer the passions, the more necessary laws are to restrain them. But apart from the disorders and crimes these passions cause among us every day, which sufficiently prove that laws are not enough for the purpose, we would do well to look back a little further and ask whether these evils did not arise together with the laws themselves. On that view, even if laws were capable of repressing these evils, it would be the least we could expect of them, since it would be no more than stopping the spread of a harm they themselves had produced.
40|Let us begin by distinguishing what is moral from what is physical in the passion called love. The physical part is that general desire which impels the sexes to come together; the moral part is what directs this desire and fixes it on one particular object to the exclusion of all others, or at least gives it greater intensity toward its preferred object. It is not hard to see that the moral side of love is a manufactured feeling, arising from society and carefully and skillfully promoted by women in order to establish their rule and give the sex that ought to obey the power to command. This feeling rests on certain notions of beauty and merit that a savage cannot have, and on comparisons that he cannot make, so it can hardly exist in him. His mind was never able to form abstract ideas of regularity and proportion, and neither is his heart open to feelings of admiration and love, which, without our noticing it, arise from applying those ideas. He listens only to the instincts nature planted in him, not to a taste he never had the means to acquire, and any woman will serve his purpose.
41|Limited entirely to the physical side of love, and fortunate enough not to know the preferences that sharpen the appetite while making it harder to satisfy, men in the state of nature must be subject to fewer and less violent fits of this passion, and consequently to fewer and less violent quarrels because of it. Imagination, which causes so much havoc among us, never speaks to the hearts of savages. They calmly wait for nature's promptings and yield to them without choosing, with more pleasure than fury, and their desires do not outlast their need of the thing desired.
42|Nothing, then, is plainer than that it is society alone that has added to love, as to all the other passions, the impetuous ardor that so often makes it fatal to mankind. It is all the more ridiculous to portray savages as constantly murdering each other to satisfy their brutality, since this view directly contradicts experience. The Caribs, who of all people in the world have so far departed least from the state of nature, are in reality the most peaceful in their love affairs and the least subject to jealousy, although they live in a burning climate that seems always to add considerably to the intensity of these passions.
43|As for the conclusions that may be drawn, for various animal species, from the fights of the males, who at all seasons cover our poultry yards with blood and in spring make our forests echo with the noise of their struggles over females, we must begin by excluding every species in which nature has plainly established relations between the sexes' strength that differ from those among us. So from the fights of cocks we can draw no conclusion that touches the human species. In the species where the proportion is better kept, these fights must be due wholly to the shortage of females relative to males, or, what amounts to the same, to the intervals during which the females constantly refuse the males' advances. For if the female admits the male for only two months a year, it is just as if the number of females were five sixths smaller than it is. Neither case applies to the human species, where the females generally outnumber the males, and where nobody has ever observed, even among savages, that females have, like those of other animals, set seasons of passion and indifference. Besides, in several of these animals the whole species catches fire at once, and for some days there is nothing to be seen but confusion, uproar, disorder and bloodshed, a state unknown in the human species, where love is never periodical. So we cannot conclude from the fights of certain animals over their females that the same would happen to man in the state of nature. Besides, even supposing we could, since these contests do not destroy the other species, there is at least equal reason to think they would not be fatal to ours. Indeed it is very likely they would cause less havoc than they do in society, especially in those countries where, morality still being held in some esteem, lovers' jealousy and husbands' vengeance produce duels, murders and even worse crimes every day; where the duty of eternal fidelity serves only to breed adultery; and where the very laws of chastity and honor necessarily help to increase dissoluteness and multiply abortions.
44|Let us conclude that savage man, wandering in the forests without industry, without speech, without a fixed home, a stranger alike to war and to every social tie, with no need of his fellows and no desire to harm them, perhaps without even telling them apart as individuals, subject to few passions and finding in himself all he needs, had no knowledge or feelings except those that suit that condition. He sensed only his real needs, noticed only what it was in his interest to see, and his understanding made as little progress as his vanity. If he happened to make some discovery, he could the less pass it on because he did not even know his own children. The art died with its inventor; there was no education and no improvement; generations followed generations to no purpose; and since each started from the same point, whole centuries passed in the crudeness and barbarity of the first age. The species had grown old while the individual remained in a state of childhood.
