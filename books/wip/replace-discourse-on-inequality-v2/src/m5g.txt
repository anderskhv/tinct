53|If this were the place for details, I could easily explain how inequalities of influence and authority become unavoidable among individuals as soon as, united in one body, they are forced to compare themselves with one another and to notice the differences they find in the constant use each must make of his neighbors. These differences take several forms. But wealth, noble rank, power and personal merit are in general the main distinctions by which men in society measure each other, and I could prove that the harmony or conflict among these different forces is the surest sign of a state's good or bad original constitution. I could show that among these four sorts of inequality, personal qualities are the source of all the rest, and wealth is the one in which they end up, because, being the most immediately useful to individual prosperity and the easiest to pass on, it is used to buy every other distinction. This observation lets us judge fairly accurately how far any people has strayed from its original institution and how many steps it still has to take toward the extreme of corruption. I could show how much this universal craving for reputation, honors and preferment, which devours us all, exercises and compares our talents and forces; how much it excites and multiplies our passions; and how, by creating a universal competition, rivalry or rather enmity among men, it daily causes countless disappointments, successes and disasters of every kind among the innumerable contenders it draws into the same race. I could demonstrate that it is to this itch to be talked about, to this frenzy for distinction that hardly ever gives us a moment's rest, that we owe both the best and the worst things among us, our virtues and our vices, our sciences and our errors, our conquerors and our philosophers; that is, a great many bad things for a very few good ones. I could prove, in short, that if we see a handful of rich and powerful men at the peak of fortune and greatness while the crowd grovels in obscurity and want, it is only because the first value what they enjoy to exactly the extent that others lack it, and that, without any change in their situation, they would stop being happy the moment the people stopped being miserable.
54|But these details alone would supply enough material for a larger work, in which the pros and cons of every kind of government could be weighed against the rights of man in a state of nature, and in which could be unveiled all the different faces that inequality has worn to this day and may wear until the end of time, depending on the nature of those governments and on the revolutions that time must inevitably bring about in them. We would then see the multitude oppressed by domestic tyrants as a result of the very precautions they took against foreign masters. We would see oppression grow continually without the oppressed ever being able to learn where it would stop or what lawful means they had left to check its progress. We would see the rights of citizens and the liberties of nations extinguished by slow degrees, and the groans, protests and appeals of the weak regarded as seditious murmuring. We would see policy limit to a hired portion of the people the honor of defending the common cause. We would see taxes made necessary by such measures, and the disheartened farmer deserting his field even in peacetime and leaving the plow to take up the sword. We would see fatal and bizarre rules laid down about the point of honor. We would see the defenders of their country sooner or later become her enemies and constantly hold their daggers at the chests of their fellow citizens. And the time would come when they might be heard telling the oppressor of their country: "Were you to order me to bury my sword in my brother's breast, in my father's throat, or into the womb of my wife heavy with child, still I would carry out every command with a steady right hand."
55|From the vast inequality of conditions and fortunes, from the great variety of passions and talents, of useless arts, harmful arts and frivolous sciences, would come clouds of prejudices equally opposed to reason, happiness and virtue. We would see the chiefs encourage everything that tends to weaken men formed into societies by dividing them; everything that, while giving society an air of apparent harmony, sows in it the seeds of real division; everything that can inspire the different classes with mutual distrust and hatred by setting their rights and interests against each other, and so strengthen the power that holds them all in check.
56|It is from the heart of this disorder and these upheavals that despotism, slowly lifting its hideous head and consuming in every part of the state all that still remained sound and untainted, would at last emerge to trample the laws and the people and to set itself up on the wreckage of the republic. The times just before this final change would be times of calamity and trouble, but in the end everything would be swallowed up by the monster, and the people would no longer have chiefs or laws but only tyrants. At this fatal time all regard for virtue and morals would likewise vanish; for despotism, cui ex honesto nulla est spes, tolerates no other master wherever it reigns. The moment it speaks, honesty and duty lose all their influence, and the blindest obedience is the only virtue left for the miserable slaves to practice.
57|This is the final stage of inequality, the farthest point, which closes the circle and meets the point from which we started. Here all private individuals become equal again, because they are nothing; and since the subjects now have no law but their master's will, nor the master any law but his passions, all notions of good and principles of justice vanish once more. Here everything returns to the sole law of the strongest, and therefore to a new state of nature unlike the one we began with, in that the first was the state of nature in its purity and the last is the result of excessive corruption. In other respects there is so little difference between these two states, and despotism so thoroughly dissolves the contract of government, that the despot is master only as long as he remains the strongest, and as soon as his slaves can drive him out, they may do so without his having the slightest right to complain of their treating him badly. The uprising that ends in a sultan's death or deposition is as lawful an act as those through which, the day before, he disposed of the lives and fortunes of his subjects. Force alone upheld him; force alone overthrows him. Thus everything happens and follows in its natural order; and whatever the outcome of these quick and frequent revolutions, no one can complain of another's injustice, only of his own imprudence or bad luck.
58|By discovering and following the lost and forgotten tracks along which man must have traveled from the natural to the civil state, and by restoring, together with the intermediate stages I have just indicated, those that lack of time forces me to leave out or that my imagination has not supplied, every attentive reader must be struck by the immense distance between these two states. In this slow succession of things he may find the solution to an endless number of problems in morals and politics that philosophers cannot solve. He will see that, since the mankind of one age is not the mankind of another, the reason why Diogenes could not find a man was that he looked among his contemporaries the man of an earlier time. Cato, he will then see, fell with Rome and with liberty because he did not suit the age he lived in, and the greatest of men served merely to astonish a world that would have gladly obeyed him had he come into it five hundred years earlier. In a word, he will be in a position to understand how the soul and passions of men gradually change, as it were, their nature; why, in the long run, our needs and pleasures change their objects; why, as original man fades away by degrees, society offers us nothing but an assemblage of artificial men and manufactured passions, which are the work of all these new relations and have no foundation in nature. Reflection teaches us nothing on this point that experience does not completely confirm. Savage man and civilized man differ so deeply in inclinations and passions that what makes up the supreme happiness of one would drive the other to despair. The first longs only for rest and freedom; he wants only to live and to be free of labor; even the calm of the most dedicated Stoic falls short of his complete indifference to everything else. The citizen, by contrast, always on the move, is forever sweating and toiling and racking his brains to find occupations still more laborious. He remains a drudge to his last minute; he even courts death to be able to live, or gives up life to gain immortality. He cringes before powerful men he hates and rich men he despises; he balks at nothing for the privilege of serving them; he is not embarrassed to take pride in his own weakness and the protection they give him; and proud of his chains, he speaks with contempt of those who lack the honor of sharing his bondage. What a sight the painful and envied toils of a European minister of state must be in the eyes of a Carib! How many cruel deaths would this lazy savage prefer to such a dreadful life, which is very often not even sweetened by the pleasure of doing good? But to see the point of so many cares, his mind would first have to attach some meaning to the words power and reputation; he would have to learn that there are men who count for something the opinion of the rest of mankind, who know how to be happy and satisfied with themselves on the testimony of others rather than on their own. In truth, what really underlies every one of these differences is that the savage dwells inside himself, whereas the citizen, always outside himself, knows only how to live in other people's opinion, so that it is, so to speak, from their judgment alone that he draws the sense of his own existence. It is not my subject to show how this disposition breeds so much indifference to good and evil, despite so many fine speeches about morality; how everything, being reduced to appearances, becomes mere artifice and play-acting: honor, friendship, virtue, and often even vice itself, which we finally learn the secret of boasting about; how, in short, forever asking others what we are and never venturing to question ourselves on so delicate a point, amid so much philosophy, humanity, politeness and so many sublime maxims, we have nothing to offer for ourselves but a deceitful and frivolous exterior, honor lacking virtue, reason lacking wisdom and pleasure lacking happiness. It is enough that I have shown this is not the original condition of man, and that it is only the spirit of society, and the inequality that society breeds, which thus change and transform all our natural inclinations.
59|I have tried to present the origin and progress of inequality, the establishment and abuse of political societies, to the extent that these things can be derived from the nature of man by the light of reason alone, independently of those sacred doctrines that give sovereign authority the sanction of divine right. It follows from this picture that, since there is hardly any inequality among men in a state of nature, all the inequality we now see owes its force and growth to the unfolding of our faculties and the progress of our understanding, and at last becomes permanent and lawful through the establishment of property and laws. It also follows that moral inequality, authorized by any merely positive right, conflicts with natural right whenever it is not combined in the same proportion with physical inequality. This distinction sufficiently settles what we are to think, in that respect, of the kind of inequality found in all civilized nations. For it is plainly against the law of nature for a child to command an old man, a fool to guide a wise man, and a handful of people to gorge themselves on luxuries while the starving multitude lacks the barest necessities of life.
