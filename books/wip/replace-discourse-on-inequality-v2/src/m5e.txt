36|I know that several writers have assigned other origins to political society, for instance the conquests of the powerful or the union of the weak, and it makes no difference to what I am about to establish which of these causes we adopt. Still, the one I have just set out seems to me the most natural, for the following reasons. First, in the case of conquest, the right of conquest is in fact no right at all and so could not serve as the foundation of any other right; the conqueror and the conquered would always remain at war with one another, unless the conquered, restored to the full possession of their liberty, freely chose their conqueror as their chief. Until then, whatever agreements might have been made between them, since those agreements were founded on violence and therefore null and void in fact, there could not have existed under this hypothesis either a true society, or a political body, or any law other than that of the strongest. Second, in the case of the union of the weak, the words strong and weak are ambiguous. In the period between the establishment of the right of property or first occupation and that of political government, the meaning of these terms is better expressed by the words poor and rich, because before laws existed men in reality had no other means of subduing their equals than by attacking their property or by giving up some of their own to them. Third, since the poor had nothing to lose but their liberty, it would have been the height of madness for them to give up willingly the only good they had left without getting something in return. The rich, on the other hand, were vulnerable, so to speak, in every part of their possessions, so it was much easier to do them harm and they had more reason to guard against it. And finally, it is only reasonable to suppose that a thing was invented by the one to whom it could be useful rather than by the one to whom it must prove harmful.
37|Government in its infancy had no regular or permanent form. For lack of enough philosophy and experience, men could see no further than present troubles and thought of providing remedies for future ones only as they arose. In spite of all the efforts of the wisest legislators, the political state remained imperfect because it was largely the work of chance; and since its foundations were badly laid, time, though enough to reveal its defects and suggest remedies, could never repair its original flaws. Men were constantly patching, whereas to build a good structure they should have begun, as Lycurgus did at Sparta, by clearing the ground and removing the old materials. At first society consisted merely of some general agreements that all members bound themselves to observe, and for whose fulfillment the whole body became guarantor to each individual. Experience was needed to show the great weakness of such a constitution, and how easy it was for those who broke it to escape conviction or punishment for faults of which the public alone was to be both witness and judge. The laws could not fail to be evaded in a thousand ways, and troubles and disorders could not help multiplying, until at last it was found necessary to think of entrusting private persons with the dangerous trust of public authority, and magistrates with the duty of enforcing the people's obedience. To say that chiefs were chosen before associations were formed, and that the ministers of the laws existed before the laws themselves, is a supposition too ridiculous for me to refute seriously.
38|It would be equally unreasonable to imagine that men at first threw themselves into the arms of an absolute master, without conditions or any return from him, and that the first means devised by jealous, unconquered men for their common safety was to rush headlong into slavery. Indeed, why did they give themselves superiors if not to be defended by them against oppression, and protected in their lives, liberties and properties, which are in a way the essential components of their being? Now, in the relations between man and man, the worst that can happen to anyone is to find himself at another's mercy. Would it not have been contrary to good sense to begin by handing over to a chief the only things for the protection of which they needed his help? What equivalent could he have offered them for so fine a privilege? And if he had dared to demand it under the pretext of defending them, would he not at once have received the answer in the fable: "What worse can an enemy do to us?" It is therefore beyond dispute, and indeed a fundamental maxim of political law, that people gave themselves chiefs to defend their liberty and not to be enslaved by them. "If we have a prince," said Pliny to Trajan, "it is so that he may keep us from having a master."
39|Political writers argue about the love of liberty with the same sophistry that philosophers use about the state of nature. From the things they see, they judge very different things they have never seen, and they attribute to men a natural inclination toward slavery because of the patience with which the slaves they know carry the yoke. They do not realize that liberty is like innocence and virtue: its value is known only to those who possess it, and the taste for it is lost along with the thing itself. "I know the charms of your country," Brasidas said to a satrap who was comparing the life of the Spartans with that of the Persians, "but you cannot know the pleasures of mine."
40|As an unbroken colt bristles its mane, paws the ground and rages at the mere sight of the bit, while a trained horse patiently bears both whip and spur, so the barbarian will never bend his neck to the yoke that civilized man carries without complaint, but prefers the stormiest liberty to a peaceful subjection. So it is not by the servile attitude of enslaved nations that we should judge man's natural inclinations for or against slavery, but by the marvels every free people performs to protect itself from oppression. I know that the former are always praising the peace and quiet they enjoy in their chains, and that miserrimam servitutem pacem appellant. But when I see the others sacrifice pleasures, peace, riches, power and even life itself to preserve that single treasure so scorned by those who have lost it; when I see animals born free dash out their brains against the bars of their cage out of a natural horror of captivity; when I see crowds of naked savages scorn European pleasures and defy hunger, fire, sword and death itself to keep their independence, I feel that it is not for slaves to argue about liberty.
41|As for paternal authority, from which several writers have derived absolute government and every other form of society, it is enough, without turning to Locke and Sidney, to observe that nothing in the world is more unlike the cruel spirit of despotism than the gentleness of that authority, which looks more to the benefit of the one who obeys than to the advantage of the one who commands. By the law of nature the father remains master of his child only as long as the child needs his help; after that they are equals, and the son, entirely independent of his father, owes him no obedience but only respect. Gratitude is indeed a duty we must pay, but one that benefactors cannot demand. Instead of saying that civil society derives from paternal authority, we should say rather that it is from civil society that paternal authority gets its main strength. No individual was recognized as the father of several others until they gathered around him. The father's goods, which he can indeed dispose of as he pleases, are the ties that hold his children in dependence on him, and he may divide his property among them in proportion to how well they have earned his regard by constant deference to his wishes. The subjects of a despotic chief, on the other hand, far from having any such favor to expect from him, since they themselves and all they have are his property, or at least are considered by him as such, are obliged to accept as a favor whatever he leaves them of their own belongings. He does them justice when he strips them; he shows them mercy when he lets them live.
42|If we keep comparing fact with right in this way, we will find as little soundness as truth in the voluntary establishment of tyranny. It would be hard to prove the validity of a contract that binds only one side, in which one party stakes everything and the other nothing, and which could only turn out to the harm of the one who bound himself. This odious system is even today far from being that of wise and good monarchs, and especially of the kings of France, as can be seen from several passages in their edicts, and particularly from a celebrated document published in 1667 in the name and by the orders of Louis XIV: "Let it not be said, then, that the sovereign is not subject to the laws of his realm, since that he is, is a maxim of the law of nations which flattery has sometimes attacked but which good princes have always defended as the guardian deity of their realms. How much more reasonable is it to say, with the wise Plato, that the perfect happiness of a state consists in the subjects obeying their prince, the prince obeying the laws, and the laws being fair and always directed to the public good? I will not stop to consider whether, liberty being the noblest faculty of man, it is not degrading one's nature, reducing oneself to the level of the brutes that are slaves of instinct, and even offending the Author of one's being, to renounce without reserve the most precious of his gifts and to submit to committing all the crimes he has forbidden us merely to gratify a mad or cruel master; and whether this sublime Craftsman ought to be more angered at seeing his work destroyed than at seeing it dishonored. I will only ask what right those who were not afraid to degrade themselves in this way could have to subject their descendants to the same disgrace, and to renounce, in the name of their posterity, blessings that it does not owe to their generosity and without which life itself must seem a burden to all who are worthy to live."
43|Pufendorf says that, as we can transfer our property to others by contracts and agreements, we may likewise strip ourselves of our liberty in favor of other men. This, in my opinion, is very poor reasoning. In the first place, the property I give to another becomes by that transfer something quite foreign to me, whose misuse cannot affect me; but it matters greatly to me that my liberty is not misused, and I cannot, without taking on the guilt of the crimes I might be forced to commit, expose myself to becoming the instrument of any. Besides, the right of property being a matter of merely human convention and institution, every man may dispose as he pleases of what he owns. But the case is different for the essential gifts of nature, such as life and liberty, which every man is allowed to enjoy and which it is doubtful, at least, that anyone has the right to give up. By giving up the one we degrade our being; by giving up the other we destroy it as far as it is in our power to do so; and since no worldly enjoyment can compensate us for the loss of either, it would offend both nature and reason to renounce them for any price. But even if we could transfer our liberty as we do our property, the difference would be very great for our children, who enjoy our property only by a transfer of our right, whereas liberty, being a blessing they hold from nature as human beings, cannot be taken from them by their parents. So just as it took violence to nature to establish slavery, it took altering nature to perpetuate that right; and the jurists who have gravely pronounced that the child of a slave is born a slave have in other words decided that a man is not born a man.
44|It therefore appears to me incontestably true not only that governments did not begin with arbitrary power, which is but the corruption and extreme end of government and eventually brings it back to the law of the strongest, for which governments were at first the remedy, but also that, even supposing they had begun in this way, such power, being illegitimate in itself, could never have served as the foundation for the rights of society, nor consequently for the inequality of institution.
