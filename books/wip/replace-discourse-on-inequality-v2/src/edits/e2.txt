These investigations, hard to carry out and little thought of until now, are still the only means left to us for clearing away a thousand difficulties that hide the true foundations of human society. Our ignorance of man's nature is what blurs and darkens any correct definition of natural right. As Monsieur Burlamaqui puts it, the idea of right, and even more the idea of natural right, are plainly ideas that depend on the nature of man. This great philosopher continues: it is therefore from man's very nature, from his constitution and his condition, that the principles of this science must be drawn.

P2.6
=>
It is impossible to see, without surprise and scandal, how little agreement exists on this important point among the various authors who have dealt with it. Hardly any two of the gravest writers share one opinion. Leave aside the philosophers of antiquity, who appear to have competed in contradicting each other about the most basic principles. The Roman jurists place man and every other animal under one and the same natural law, either because by that name they mean the law nature imposes upon herself more than the one she lays down for others, or, more probably, because of the peculiar sense the word "law" had for these jurists, who here seem to understand by it nothing beyond the general relations nature sets up among all living creatures for the sake of their shared preservation. The moderns, who accept as "law" only a rule given to a moral being, one that is intelligent, free, and looked at in connection with other beings, restrict the competence of natural law to the single animal gifted with reason, namely man. But each defines this law in his own manner and builds it on such abstruse metaphysical foundations that, far from being able to discover those foundations themselves, very few among us can even grasp them. So every definition offered by these learned men, though they clash on everything else, comes down to the same claim: nobody can understand the law of nature, and therefore obey it, unless he is an extremely subtle reasoner and a profound metaphysician. This amounts to saying that men, when they set up society, must have drawn on a stock of knowledge that is very hard for most people to develop, indeed impossible, even inside society.

P2.7
=>
Given how little men know of nature and how poorly they agree on what "law" means, we can hardly expect them ever to settle on a good definition of natural law. Accordingly, all those found in books, apart from lacking uniformity, rest on many kinds of insight that men do not naturally possess and on benefits they cannot even imagine as long as they stay in the state of nature. The writers begin by asking which rules would be best, in the common interest, for men to agree among themselves; then, with no further ceremony, they apply the name natural law to a collection of such rules, offering as the only proof that the collection deserves the name the benefit that universal compliance with it would bring. This is certainly a very easy way to manufacture definitions and to explain the nature of things through a nearly arbitrary fitness.

But so long as we remain ignorant of the constitution of man as he first left the hands of nature, it is pointless to try to decide what law he was given or what law fits him best. The only thing we can clearly identify about that law is twofold: for it to count as law, the will of the person it binds must submit to it with knowledge of the obligation; and for it to count as natural, it must speak immediately in the voice of nature.

Viewed calmly and without self-interest, human society seems at first to show us only the violence of the strong and the oppression of the weak. The mind is horrified by the cruelty of the one and saddened by the blindness of the other. Nothing in human life is less stable than those external relations, produced by chance more often than by wisdom, that we call weakness or power, poverty or wealth; so human institutions look, at first glance, like castles built upon quicksand. Only by examining them closely, and by sweeping away the dust and sand that surround and hide the structure, do we notice the unshakable foundation on which it rests and learn to respect it. Yet unless we study man seriously, along with his natural abilities and how they successively developed, we shall never manage to draw these distinctions or to separate, in the world as it is, what comes from the divine will from what human art claims as improvement. The political and moral reflections that the question I am examining invites are therefore useful in every respect, and the hypothetical history of governments is an instructive lesson for man in every way. Reflecting on what we would have turned into had we been left to ourselves, we should learn to bless the One whose kindly hand, by amending our institutions and giving them a firm footing, has averted the disorders that would otherwise have followed, and has caused our happiness to spring from means that, to all appearances, would without his intervention have finished off our misery.
