1|Magnificent, most honored, and sovereign lords: I am convinced that only a virtuous man can offer his country tributes she would not blush to accept. For thirty years I have worked to make myself worthy of paying you public homage, and now that this fortunate occasion has arrived to make up in part for what my efforts could not achieve, I decided I could rely on the zeal that drives me rather than on any title that entitles me to approach you in this way. I was lucky enough to be born among you. How, then, could I reflect on the equality that nature has established among human beings, and on the inequality they have added themselves, without thinking of the deep wisdom with which both have been blended in your state? Together they work in a way that departs least from natural law while serving society best, preserving public order and guarding each person's private happiness. When I looked into the soundest principles that good sense could lay down for the constitution of a perfect government, I was so struck to find every one of them realized in yours that, even if I had not been born within your walls, I would have felt bound to offer this portrait of human society to the people who, in my judgment, enjoy its benefits most fully and have been best protected against its smallest abuses.
2|If I had been free to choose the place of my birth, I would have chosen a society of a size matched to the limits of human ability, that is, to the possibility of being well governed. Each member would be able to handle his own work so fully that he would never need to hand his responsibilities over to others. In such a state everyone would know everyone else so well that neither the hidden schemes of vice nor the quiet modesty of virtue could escape the notice and judgment of the public. And because people would be used to seeing and knowing one another, each citizen's love of his country would be a love of its people rather than of its soil.
3|I would have wanted to be born in a country where the ruler and the citizens share one and the same interest, so that every movement of the machine would necessarily serve the good of the whole. Since that can only happen when ruler and citizens are one and the same body, I would naturally have wanted to be born under a democratic government, sensibly moderated.
4|I would have chosen to live and die free, which means being subject to the laws in such a way that neither I nor anyone else in the society could throw off their honorable yoke. This is a healthy and gentle yoke, and the proudest necks bear it all the more meekly because they were made to carry no other.
5|So I would have wanted no member of the state to be able to boast of standing above its laws, and the state to have no reason to fear being forced to accept laws from outside. For whatever the constitution of a government may be, if even one man in it is exempt from the laws, everyone else is necessarily at his mercy. And where there are two heads, one native and one foreign, however well they divide sovereign authority between them, it is impossible for both to be properly obeyed or for the government to be run well.
6|I would not have wanted to belong to a recently founded republic, however good its laws. Its government might well have been shaped differently from what the present emergencies demand, and I could not be sure it would not be shaken and destroyed almost at birth, either because the new administration did not suit the citizens or the citizens did not suit the new administration. Liberty is like those rich and hearty foods, or those strong wines, that nourish and strengthen robust constitutions used to them, but overwhelm, upset and ruin weak and delicate bodies that have never had them. Once people are accustomed to masters, they can never again do without them. The harder they struggle to throw off their chains, the further they stray from the paths of liberty, because they mistake for liberty an unlimited license that is its exact opposite, and nearly always end up the slaves of impostors who, instead of lightening their chains, make them far heavier than before. Even the Romans, who later became the model for all free states, were unable to govern themselves when they first threw off the Tarquins. Degraded by slavery and by the shameful work those tyrants had forced on them, they were at first no better than a dull mob that had to be handled and governed with the greatest wisdom. Only so could they gradually grow used to breathing the healthy air of liberty, and these spirits, weakened or rather reduced to the level of beasts by the rod of oppression, slowly acquire the severity of character and nobility of courage that finally made them the most admirable people on earth. So I would have looked for a country that was a happy and peaceful republic, whose origin was lost in the night of time; one that had suffered only such shocks as revealed and strengthened the patriotism and courage of its members; and one whose citizens, long used to a prudent independence, were not only free but worthy of being free.
7|I would have chosen a country protected from the brutal rage of conquest by a fortunate inability to conquer, and, by an even more fortunate location, free from any fear of being conquered itself. It would be a free city set among nations none of which found it in their interest to attack her liberties, and all of which, on the contrary, felt it their own duty to defend those liberties when others attacked them. In a word, it would be a republic that not only held nothing to tempt its neighbors' ambition but could reasonably count on their help in case of need. A republic so well placed would have nothing to fear except from itself. If its members trained for war, it would be to keep alive that martial ardor and generous spirit which suit liberty so well and nourish a taste for it, not because they had any need to prepare their defense.
8|I would have sought a country where legislative power belonged to all its inhabitants; for who can know better than the members of a society on what terms it suits them to live together? But I would not have approved of plebiscites like those of Rome, where the leaders of the commonwealth, the very people most concerned for its preservation and welfare, were shut out of the deliberations on which its safety often depended, and where, by an absurd consequence, magistrates were deprived of rights that the humblest citizen enjoyed.
9|On the contrary, to put an end to self-interested, ill-considered projects and dangerous innovations, which in the end ruined the Athenians, I would have wanted no private citizen to have the right to propose whatever laws came into his head. That right would belong only to the magistrates, and they in turn would use it with such caution, and the people would approve their proposals with such reserve, and the announcement of laws would be carried out with such solemnity, that every member of the community would have time enough, before the constitution could be put at risk, to recognize that nothing makes laws more sacred and respected than great age. People soon despise laws they see altered from one day to the next, and governments that get into the habit of neglecting ancient customs in the name of imaginary improvements often bring in worse evils than those they meant to correct or prevent.
10|Above all, I would have avoided as inevitably ill governed a republic whose people, claiming they could do without magistrates, or at least giving them only a shaky authority, imprudently kept to themselves the administration of civil affairs and the carrying out of their own laws. Such must have been the crude constitution of the first governments just as they emerged from the state of nature, and it was another of the faults that helped bring down the republic of Athens.
11|Instead I would have chosen a society whose individual members were content with the right to confirm their laws and to decide together, on the report of their magistrates, the most important public matters. They would set up respected courts, take care to distinguish their separate functions, and each year elect the most knowledgeable, sensible and honest men among themselves to administer justice and govern the state. In such a society the integrity of the magistrates would bear witness to the wisdom of the people, and magistrates and people would honor one another. So if some dangerous misunderstanding ever disturbed public peace and harmony, even those periods of blindness and error would show clear signs of mutual restraint and esteem and of general respect for the laws, certain omens and pledges of a sincere and lasting reconciliation.
12|Such, magnificent, most honored and sovereign lords, are the advantages I would have looked for in the country I chose as my birthplace. And if Providence had added to these blessings a charming setting, a mild climate, fertile soil and the loveliest scenery under heaven, then to be perfectly happy I would only ask to enjoy all these gifts in the heart of this fortunate country, living peacefully in friendly company with my fellow citizens, practicing toward them, and following their example, the duties of humanity, friendship and every other virtue, so as to leave behind the name of an honest man and a worthy patriot.
