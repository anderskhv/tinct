1|The most useful and least developed of all human studies is, in my opinion, the study of man, and I dare say that the inscription on the temple at Delphi contained by itself a more important and difficult lesson than all the thick volumes of the moralists. I therefore regard the subject of this Discourse as one of the most interesting questions philosophy can raise and, regrettably for us, among the knottiest that philosophers can try to settle. For how can we discover the source of inequality among men without knowing men themselves? And how can man see himself as nature made him, in spite of all the changes that a long succession of years and events must have worked on his original constitution, and tell what belongs to his own essence from what circumstances and his own progress have added to or altered in his primitive condition? The human soul is like the statue of Glaucus, which time, sea and storms had so disfigured that it resembled a savage animal more than a deity. Altered in the heart of society by a thousand ever-changing causes, by countless discoveries and errors, by changes in the make-up of the bodies around it, and by the constant clash of its own passions, the human soul has lost so much of its original appearance that it can scarcely be recognized. Instead of a being that always acts on fixed and unvarying principles, in place of the heavenly, majestic simplicity its Author stamped on it, we find only the shocking contrast of passion that believes it is reasoning and an understanding in delirium.
2|What is still crueler is that every improvement made by the human species only carries it further from its primitive condition, so that the more new knowledge we accumulate, the more we cut ourselves off from acquiring the most important knowledge of all. In a sense it is by studying man that we have made ourselves unable to know him.
3|It does not take great insight to see that the first origin of the differences between men must be sought in these successive changes in the human frame. It is generally agreed that men are naturally as equal among themselves as the animals of every species were before various physical causes produced the varieties we now see in some of them. In fact, it is impossible to imagine how these first changes, whatever caused them, could have altered all the individuals of the species at once and in the same way. Clearly, while some improved or worsened their condition and picked up various good or bad qualities that were not part of their nature, the rest stayed longer in their original state. Such was the first source of inequality among men, which it is far easier to point out in general terms than to trace back precisely to its true causes.
4|So my readers should not suppose that I pride myself on having seen what I consider so hard to discover. I have opened up some arguments and ventured some conjectures, not so much in hope of settling the question as to throw some light on it and to state it truly. Others will easily be able to go further along the same road, but none will find reaching the end of it easy. For it is by no means a simple task to separate what is natural from what is artificial in man's present constitution, and to become well acquainted with a state that perhaps never existed, no longer exists and probably never will, yet of which we must have sound notions in order to judge our present state correctly. Indeed, a man must be more of a philosopher than most people think to take it upon himself to say exactly what precautions are needed for making reliable observations on this subject. In my opinion, a sound answer to the following problem would not be beneath the Aristotles and Plinys of our age: what experiments would be needed to know man as nature made him, and what are the best ways to carry out those experiments within society? Far from claiming to solve this problem, I believe I have thought enough about it to dare say in advance that the wisest philosophers would not be too wise to direct such experiments, nor the most powerful rulers too powerful to carry them out. That combination of circumstances is hardly to be expected, or at least not with the perseverance, or rather the continuing supply of knowledge, insight and goodwill on both sides, that success would require.
5|These investigations, hard to carry out and little thought of until now, are still the only means left to us for clearing away a thousand difficulties that hide the true foundations of human society. Our ignorance of man's nature is what blurs and darkens any correct definition of natural right. As Monsieur Burlamaqui puts it, the idea of right, and even more the idea of natural right, are plainly ideas that depend on the nature of man. This great philosopher continues: it is therefore from man's very nature, from his constitution and his condition, that the principles of this science must be drawn.
6|It is impossible to see, without surprise and scandal, how little agreement exists on this important point among the various authors who have dealt with it. Hardly any two of the gravest writers share one opinion. Leave aside the philosophers of antiquity, who appear to have competed in contradicting each other about the most basic principles. The Roman jurists place man and every other animal under one and the same natural law, either because by that name they mean the law nature imposes upon herself more than the one she lays down for others, or, more probably, because of the peculiar sense the word "law" had for these jurists, who here seem to understand by it nothing beyond the general relations nature sets up among all living creatures for the sake of their shared preservation. The moderns, who accept as "law" only a rule given to a moral being, one that is intelligent, free, and looked at in connection with other beings, restrict the competence of natural law to the single animal gifted with reason, namely man. But each defines this law in his own manner and builds it on such abstruse metaphysical foundations that, far from being able to discover those foundations themselves, very few among us can even grasp them. So every definition offered by these learned men, though they clash on everything else, comes down to the same claim: nobody can understand the law of nature, and therefore obey it, unless he is an extremely subtle reasoner and a profound metaphysician. This amounts to saying that men, when they set up society, must have drawn on a stock of knowledge that is very hard for most people to develop, indeed impossible, even inside society.
7|Given how little men know of nature and how poorly they agree on what "law" means, we can hardly expect them ever to agree on a sound definition of natural law. Accordingly, all those found in books, apart from lacking uniformity, rest on many kinds of insight that men do not naturally possess and on benefits they cannot even imagine as long as they stay in the state of nature. The writers begin by asking which rules would be best, in the common interest, for men to agree among themselves; then, with no further ceremony, they apply the label natural law to a set of such rules, offering as the only proof that the collection deserves the name the benefit that universal compliance with it would bring. This is certainly a very easy way to manufacture definitions and to explain the nature of things through a nearly arbitrary fitness.
8|But so long as we remain ignorant of the constitution of man as he first left the hands of nature, it is pointless to try to decide what law he was given or what law fits him best. The only thing we can clearly identify about that law is twofold: for it to count as law, the will of the person it binds must submit to it with knowledge of the obligation; and for it to count as natural, it must speak immediately in the voice of nature.
9|So I set aside all the scholarly treatises that teach us only to look at men as they have made themselves, and confine myself to the first and simplest workings of the human soul. In it I think I can distinguish two principles that come before reason. One makes us take a deep interest in our own survival and well-being, and the other fills us with a natural reluctance to see any other being, above all any being like ourselves, suffer or die. From the cooperation and combination that our mind is able to form between these two principles, without any need to add sociability as a third, flow, in my opinion, all the rules of natural right. Reason is later forced to rebuild these rules on other foundations, when by gradually developing its powers it has finally smothered the authority of nature.
10|By proceeding in this way we are freed from having to make a man a philosopher before he can be a man. His obligations are not laid down for him solely by the slow voice of wisdom. As long as he does not fight the inner prompting of compassion, he will never harm another man, or even any other sentient creature, except in those lawful cases where his own survival is at stake and it is therefore his duty to put himself first. This also puts an end to the ancient disputes over whether other animals share in the law of nature. It is clear that, lacking reason and free will, they cannot know that law. But since they share to some extent in our nature through the sensitivity with which they are endowed, we may well suppose they should also share in the benefit of natural law, and that man owes them certain kinds of duty. Indeed, if I am bound not to harm any being like myself, it seems to be less because he is a rational being than because he is a sentient one; and since this quality is common to men and animals, it should at least exempt the animals from needless ill treatment by men.
11|This same study of primitive man, of his actual needs and the basic principles of his duties, is also the only good method for overcoming an endless number of difficulties about the origin of moral inequality, the true foundations of political bodies, the mutual rights of their members, and a thousand similar questions that are as important as they are poorly understood.
12|Viewed calmly and without self-interest, human society seems at first to display nothing except the violence of the strong and the oppression of the weak. The mind is horrified by the cruelty of the one and saddened by the blindness of the other. Nothing in human life is less stable than those external relations, produced by chance more often than by wisdom, that we call weakness or power, poverty or wealth; so human institutions look, at first glance, like castles built upon quicksand. Only by examining them closely, and by sweeping away the dust and sand that surround and hide the structure, do we notice the unshakable base on which it stands and come to respect it. Yet unless we study man seriously, along with his natural abilities and how they successively developed, we shall never manage to draw these distinctions or to separate, in the world as it is, what comes from the divine will from what human art claims as improvement. The political and moral reflections that the question I am examining invites are therefore valuable in every way, and the hypothetical history of governments is an instructive lesson for man in every way. Reflecting on what we would have turned into had we been left alone, we should learn to give thanks to the One whose kindly hand, by amending our institutions and giving them a firm footing, has averted the disorders that would otherwise have followed, and has caused our happiness to spring from means that, to all appearances, would without his intervention have finished off our misery.
