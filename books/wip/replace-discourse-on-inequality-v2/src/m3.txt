1|What is the origin of inequality among mankind, and is such inequality authorized by the law of nature?
2|It is of man that I must speak, and the very question I am answering tells me I am speaking to men; for only those who are not afraid to honor truth have the right to raise discussions of this kind. So I will confidently defend the cause of mankind before the sages who invite me to stand up for it, and I will count myself fortunate if I can only behave in a way not unworthy of my subject and my judges.
3|I see two kinds of inequality among men. One I call natural or physical, because nature establishes it, and it consists in differences of age, health, bodily strength and qualities of mind or soul. The other may be called moral or political, because it depends on a sort of convention and is established, or at least authorized, by the common consent of mankind. It consists in the different privileges that some men enjoy at the expense of others, such as being richer, more honored or more powerful than they are, and even being able to demand their obedience.
4|It would be absurd to ask what causes natural inequality, since the bare definition of it answers the question. It would be more absurd still to wonder whether the two kinds of inequality might be essentially connected, for that would mean asking, put differently, whether people who give the orders are inevitably better than people who follow them, and whether physical or mental strength, wisdom and virtue are always found in people in proportion to their power or wealth. This is a question that might perhaps be debated by slaves within hearing of their masters, but it does not suit free and reasonable beings who are seeking the truth.
5|So what exactly is this Discourse about? It is to identify, in the course of events, the moment when right replaced violence and nature became subject to law. It is to lay out the chain of surprising events through which the strong came to serve the weak, and the people to buy an imaginary ease at the price of real happiness.
6|Every philosopher who has examined the foundations of society has felt the need to go back to a state of nature, but not one of them has ever got there. Some have not shrunk from attributing to man in that state ideas of justice and injustice, without bothering to prove that he really had such ideas, or even that they would have been useful to him. Others have talked about everyone's natural right to hold on to what is his, without saying what they understood by "his." Others again, simply handing the strongest an authority over the weakest, have immediately produced government, without considering the time men needed to form any notion of what the words "authority" and "government" mean. In short, all of them, constantly harping on needs, greed, oppression, desires and pride, have carried into the state of nature ideas collected in society. They claimed to speak of savages and described citizens. Indeed, few of our own writers seem even to have doubted that a state of nature once really existed. Yet it is plain from sacred history that even the first man, who had received instruction and commandments directly from God, never lived in that state. And if we give the books of Moses the authority that any Christian philosopher must grant them, we have to deny that such a state ever existed among men even before the Flood, unless they fell into it through some extraordinary event. That is a paradox very hard to defend and altogether impossible to prove.
7|Let us begin, then, by setting the facts aside, for they do not touch the question. The investigations we may undertake here are not to be understood as historical truths but only as hypothetical, conditional reasoning, better suited to explaining the nature of things than to showing their true origin, like the systems our natural scientists produce daily about how the world was formed. Religion commands us to believe that, since God himself drew men out of the state of nature, they are unequal because he wished them to be that way. Yet religion does not prevent us from making conjectures, based only on the nature of man and of the beings around him, about what would have become of mankind if it had been left to itself. That is the question I have to answer, and the one I mean to look into in this Discourse. Since my topic concerns mankind in general, I will try to use language that suits all nations. Or rather, setting aside time and place so as to think only of the people I am addressing, I will picture myself in the Lyceum at Athens, reciting the lessons of my masters before judges like Plato and Xenocrates, with the whole human race as my audience.
8|O man, whatever country you come from and whatever your opinions, listen to my words. You will hear your history as I believe I have read it, not in books written by people like you, who are liars, but in the book of nature, which never lies. Everything I repeat after her must be true, except where I may happen, without meaning to, to bring in my own ideas. The times I am going to speak of are very distant. How far you have changed from what you used to be! In a sense I am about to write the life of your species, from the qualities you received, which your education and your habits could corrupt but could not destroy. There is, I know, an age at which each of you would choose to stop, and you will look for the age at which, if you had your wish, your species would have stopped. Dissatisfied with your present condition for reasons that threaten your unhappy descendants with still greater dissatisfaction, you may wish you could go back. This feeling ought to be taken as praise of your first ancestors, condemnation of your contemporaries, and a source of terror to anyone unlucky enough to come after you.
