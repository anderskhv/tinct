45|I will not now enter into the inquiries that remain to be made about the nature of the fundamental pacts of each kind of government. Following the common opinion, I will limit myself here to treating the establishment of the political body as a real contract between the multitude and the chiefs it chooses. By this contract both sides commit themselves to observe the laws it stipulates, which form the bonds of their union. The multitude, having on the occasion of their social relations gathered all their wills into one person, makes every article on which that will declares itself into a fundamental law, binding without exception on all members of the state. One of these laws regulates the choice and powers of the magistrates appointed to oversee the carrying out of the rest. This authority extends to everything that can maintain the constitution but to nothing that could alter it. Honors are added to it, to make the laws and their ministers respected, and the persons of the ministers are distinguished by certain privileges to compensate them for the heavy labors that come with good administration. The magistrate for his part commits himself to use the power given to him only in accordance with the intentions of those who gave it, to maintain each of them in peaceful possession of his property, and on every occasion to prefer the public good to his own private interest.
46|Before experience had shown, or a thorough knowledge of the human heart had pointed out, the abuses inseparable from such a constitution, it must have seemed all the more perfect because those charged with preserving it were themselves most concerned in it. For since the magistracy and its rights rest solely on the fundamental laws, as soon as those ceased to exist the magistrates would cease to be legitimate, the people would no longer be bound to obey them, and, since the essence of the state did not lie in the magistrates but in the laws, its members would immediately regain their primitive and natural liberty.
47|A little reflection would give us new arguments confirming this truth, and the nature of the contract alone might convince us that it cannot be irrevocable. If there were no higher power able to guarantee the good faith of the contracting parties and force them to fulfill their mutual commitments, they would remain sole judges in their own case, and each would always have the right to withdraw from the contract once he found that the other had broken its terms, or that those terms had ceased to suit his private convenience. On this principle the right of abdication may probably be founded. Now, if we consider, as we do, only what is human in this institution, then if the magistrate, who has all the power in his own hands and who takes for himself all the advantages of the contract, nevertheless has the right to give up his authority, how much better a right must the people have, who pay for all the faults of their chief, to renounce their dependence on him. But the shocking dissensions and countless disorders that would necessarily follow from so perilous a privilege show, above all else, how much human governments needed a firmer basis than reason alone, and how necessary it was for public peace that the will of the Almighty should step in to give sovereign authority a sacred and inviolable character, depriving subjects of the harmful right to dispose of it as they pleased. If mankind had gained no other advantage from religion, this alone would be enough to make them adopt and cherish it, since it saves more blood than fanaticism has ever spilled. But let us pick up the thread of our hypothesis again.
48|The various forms of government owe their origin to the various degrees of inequality among the members at the moment they first merged into a political body. Where one man happened to stand out for power, virtue, wealth or influence, he became the only magistrate, and the state took a monarchical form. If several of fairly equal eminence rose above all the rest, they were elected jointly, and this election produced an aristocracy. Those between whose fortunes or talents there was no such disproportion, and who had strayed less far from the state of nature, kept the supreme administration in common and formed a democracy. Time showed which of these forms suited mankind best. Some remained wholly subject to the laws; others soon bowed their necks to masters. The former worked to preserve their liberty; the latter thought of nothing but taking their neighbors', jealous at seeing others enjoy a blessing they had lost themselves. In a word, riches and conquest fell to the share of one, and virtue and happiness to the other.
49|In these various forms of government, all offices were at first elective. When wealth did not predominate, preference was given to merit, which gives a natural authority, and to age, which is the parent of deliberation in council and experience in execution. The elders of the Hebrews, the Gerontes of Sparta, the Senate of Rome, and even the very origin of our word seigneur show how much gray hair was once respected. The more often the choice fell on old men, the more often it had to be repeated, and the more the trouble of these repetitions was felt. Electioneering set in; factions arose; the parties grew bitter; civil wars blazed up; the lives of citizens were sacrificed to the alleged happiness of the state; and things finally reached the point of nearly falling back into their original confusion. The ambition of the leading men led them to take advantage of these circumstances to make the hitherto temporary offices permanent in their families. The people, already used to dependence, accustomed to ease and the comforts of life, and too softened to break their fetters, agreed to an increase in their slavery in order to secure their peace. In this way chiefs became hereditary, and acquired the habit of regarding magistracies as a family estate and themselves as owners of the communities in which they were at first mere officers; of calling their fellow citizens their slaves; of counting them, like so many cows or sheep, as part of their property; and of styling themselves the peers of gods and kings of kings.
50|By following the progress of inequality through these different revolutions, we will find that the establishment of laws and the right of property was the first stage, the establishment of magistrates the second, and the third and last the change from legitimate to arbitrary power. So the different conditions of rich and poor were authorized by the first period, those of powerful and weak by the second, and by the third those of master and slave, which formed the last degree of inequality and the point at which all the rest finally end, until new revolutions either dissolve the government completely or bring it back closer to its legitimate constitution.
51|To understand the necessity of this progress, we should consider not so much the reasons for establishing political bodies as the forms these bodies take in their administration and the troubles that essentially come with them. For the very vices that make social institutions necessary also make the abuse of those institutions unavoidable. And since the laws, which are in general weaker than the passions, restrain men without changing them (Sparta alone excepted, whose laws mainly concerned the education of children, and where Lycurgus established such manners and customs as largely made laws unnecessary), it would not be hard to prove that every government which, guarding carefully against all alteration and corruption, scrupulously fulfilled the purposes of its institution, was instituted needlessly, and that a country where no one evaded the laws or misused the magistracy would need neither laws nor magistrates.
52|Political distinctions necessarily bring civil distinctions with them. The inequality between the people and the chiefs grows so fast that private members soon feel it, and it appears among them in a thousand forms according to their passions, talents and circumstances. The magistrate cannot usurp any illegitimate power without making dependents for himself, with whom he must share it. Besides, the citizens of a free state let themselves be oppressed only in proportion as blind ambition carries them away and, looking down rather than up, they come to love authority more than independence. When they submit to fetters, it is only to be better able to fetter others in their turn. It is hard to make a man obey who does not wish to command, and the most refined policy would find it impossible to subdue men who only want to be independent. But inequality easily gains ground among base and ambitious souls, always ready to run the risks of fortune and almost indifferent whether they command or obey, according to whether she favors or opposes them. So there must have been a time when the people's eyes were so bewitched that their rulers needed only say to the most pitiful wretch, "Be great, you and all your descendants," for him at once to look great in everyone's eyes as well as his own. His descendants took even more on themselves the further they were removed from him; the more distant and uncertain the cause, the greater the effect; the longer the line of idlers a family produced, the more illustrious it was thought.
