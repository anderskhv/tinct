45|If I have dwelt so long on this supposed primitive condition, it is because I felt it my duty, given how many ancient errors and deep-rooted prejudices I have to uproot, to dig down to the very roots and to show, with a true portrait of the state of nature, to what degree even natural inequality falls short, in that state, of the reality and influence our writers ascribe to it.
46|In fact, it is easy to see that among the differences that distinguish men, several pass for natural that are merely the work of habit and of the various ways of life that men take up in society. Thus a sturdy or fragile constitution, and the strength or weakness that goes with it, more often come from the hardy or soft way a man was brought up than from the original build of his body. The same is true of mental powers. Education not only creates a difference between minds that are cultivated and minds that are not, it even widens the difference among the cultivated ones in proportion to their training; for if a giant and a dwarf set out on the same road, the giant gains a new advantage over the dwarf at every step. Now compare the enormous variety in the schooling and lifestyle of the different classes in a civil state with the simplicity and uniformity of animal and savage life, where every individual eats the same food, lives the same way and does exactly the same things. We can easily see how much smaller the gap between one man and another must be in the state of nature than in the state of society, and how much every inequality of institution must increase the natural inequalities of the human race.
47|But even if nature, in handing out her gifts, really did show all the preferences ascribed to her, what advantage could the most favored gain from her partiality, at the expense of others, in a state of affairs that hardly allowed any relations between her pupils? What use is beauty where there is no love? What good is wit to people who do not talk, or cleverness to those who have no affairs to transact? Authors keep crying out that the strongest would oppress the weakest; but let them explain what they mean by the word oppression. One man will rule with violence, another will groan under constant subjection to his every whim. That is exactly what I observe among us, but I cannot see how it can be said of savage men, into whose heads it would be hard even to drive the meaning of the words domination and servitude. One man might indeed take the fruits another had collected, the game another had killed, the cave another had taken for shelter; but how could he ever demand obedience from him, and what chains of dependence can there be among men who own nothing? If I am driven from one tree, I only have to look for another; if one place is made unpleasant for me, what can stop me from settling elsewhere? But suppose I met a man so much stronger than me, and at the same time so wicked, so lazy and so savage, as to force me to provide for his food while he stays idle. He would have to resolve never to look away from me for even a moment, and to tie me up securely before he could take the briefest nap, for fear that I would kill him or escape while he slept. That is to say, he would have to expose himself willingly to far greater trouble than the trouble he wants to avoid, or any he causes me. And in the end, let him relax his vigilance ever so little, let him turn his head the other way at some sudden noise, and I am already lost in the forest, my fetters are broken, and he never sees me again.
48|But without insisting any longer on these details, anyone can see that since the ties of servitude are made only by men's mutual dependence on each other and by the reciprocal needs that bind them together, it is impossible to enslave a man without first putting him in a position where he cannot live without the enslaver's help. That position does not exist in a state of nature, which leaves every man his own master and makes the law of the strongest wholly vain and useless.
49|Having proved that the inequality that can exist between man and man in a state of nature is almost imperceptible and has very little influence, I must now go on to show its origin and follow its progress through the successive stages of the human mind's growth. I have shown that perfectibility, the social virtues and the remaining faculties that natural man had received only in potential could never develop by themselves; that for this a chance combination of several outside causes was needed, which might never happen, and without which he would have stayed forever in his primitive state. I must now consider and bring together the various accidents that may have perfected the human understanding by debasing the species, made a being wicked by making him sociable, and from so distant a starting point brought man and the world at last to the point where we now see them.
50|I must admit that, since the events I am about to describe might have happened in many different ways, my choice of those I will present can rest on nothing but conjecture. But conjectures become reasons when they are not only the most probable that can be drawn from the nature of things but the only means we have of discovering the truth. And the consequences I mean to draw from mine will not be merely conjectural, since on the principles I have just established it is impossible to form any other system that would not give me the same results and allow me to draw the same conclusions.
51|This entitles me to be briefer in my reflections on the way the passage of time makes up for the improbability of events; on the surprising power of very trivial causes when they act without interruption; on the impossibility, in one direction, of refuting certain hypotheses if, on the other, we cannot give them the degree of certainty that facts must be allowed to have; on the fact that when two facts are put forward as real, it is the business of history to link them by a chain of intermediate facts that are unknown or taken to be so, and to supply the facts that actually connect them, and the business of philosophy, when history is silent, to point out similar facts that can serve the same purpose; and finally on the privilege of resemblance, in regard to events, to reduce facts to a much smaller number of distinct classes than is commonly supposed. It suffices me to present these matters to my judges for their consideration; it is enough for me to have conducted my inquiry in such a way as to spare ordinary readers the trouble of considering them.
