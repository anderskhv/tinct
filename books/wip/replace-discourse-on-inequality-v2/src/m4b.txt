10|So let us take care not to confuse savage man with the men we see and talk with every day. Nature treats all animals left in her care with a favoritism that seems to prove how protective she is of that privilege. Horses, cats, bulls, even the donkey, are usually taller, and invariably sturdier, livelier, stronger and bolder in their forests than in our houses. They lose half those advantages by becoming domestic animals; it looks as though all our care to treat them kindly and feed them well merely degrades them. The same holds for man himself. As he becomes sociable and a slave to others, he becomes weak, timid and servile, and his soft, effeminate way of living completes the weakening of both his strength and his courage. We may add that the gap between man and man in the savage and domestic conditions must be even wider than between beast and beast. Since nature treats men and beasts alike, all the comforts with which men indulge themselves more than they do the animals they tame are so many particular causes that make them degenerate more noticeably.
11|Nakedness, then, the lack of houses, and of all those unnecessary things we think so necessary, are no great evils for these primitive men, and much less an obstacle to their survival. Their skin, it is true, has no hair, but in warm climates they have no need of such a covering, and in cold ones they soon learn to use the skins of the animals they have overcome. They have only two feet to run on, but they have two hands to defend themselves and supply all their needs. Teaching their children to walk may cost them a great deal of time and trouble, but the mothers carry them easily, an advantage not granted to other animal species, whose mothers, when chased, must either abandon their young or adjust their pace to theirs. In short, unless we admit those singular and chance combinations of circumstances that I will discuss later, and that may very well never have occurred, it is clear in every version of the question that the man who first made himself clothes and built himself a hut gave himself things he did not much need, since he had lived without them until then. And why should he not have been able to endure in his adult years the same kind of life he had endured since infancy?
12|Alone, idle and always surrounded by danger, savage man must like sleep and sleep lightly, like other creatures that think little and could be said to sleep all the time they are not thinking. Since self-preservation is nearly his only concern, the faculties he exercises most must be those most useful for attack and defense, whether to overcome prey or to avoid becoming another animal's. The organs that only softness and sensuality can refine, on the other hand, must remain crude and wholly incapable of delicacy. Since his senses are split in this fashion, his touch and his taste must be extremely coarse and dull, while his sight, hearing and smell are equally keen. Such is the animal state in general, and, if we believe travelers, it is the state of most savage nations. We should not be surprised, then, that the Hottentots at the Cape of Good Hope are able to make out ships at sea with the naked eye from as far away as the Dutch manage with their spyglasses; or that the savages of America tracked the Spaniards by scent as accurately as the finest hounds would have done; or that all these barbarous nations bear nakedness without pain, use huge quantities of pepper to flavor their food, and drink Europe's strongest liquors like water.
13|So far I have considered man only in his physical aspect; let us now try to examine him from a metaphysical and moral point of view.
14|In any mere animal I see only a clever machine, to which nature has given senses so that it can wind itself up and protect itself, to a certain extent, against anything that might destroy or disturb it. I see precisely the same in the human machine, with this difference: in all the operations of the beast, nature alone acts, whereas man, as a free agent, has a share in his own. One chooses by instinct, the other by an act of freedom. For that reason the beast cannot depart from the rules prescribed to it, even where departing from them would be useful, while man often departs from his rules to his own harm. Thus a pigeon would starve beside a dish of the finest meat, and a cat beside a heap of fruit or grain, though either could perfectly well live on the food it scorns if it ever thought of trying it. In the same way dissolute men give way to excesses that bring on fevers and even death, since the mind perverts the senses, and when nature falls silent the will goes on dictating.
15|All animals must be admitted to have ideas, since all have senses; they even combine their ideas to a certain degree, and in this respect the difference between man and beast is merely one of degree. Some philosophers have even claimed that the difference between one man and another is greater than that between some men and some beasts. Thus it is less his understanding that marks man off from other animals as his being a free agent. Nature speaks to every animal, and beasts obey her voice. Man feels the same impulse, but he also knows that he is free to resist or to give in, and it is above all in his consciousness of this freedom that his soul's spirituality shows itself. For natural science explains to some degree the workings of the senses and the formation of ideas, but in the capacity to will, or rather to choose, and in the awareness of that power, we find only acts that are purely spiritual and cannot be explained by the laws of mechanics.
16|But even if the difficulties that surround all these questions left some room to dispute this difference between man and beast, there is another very distinctive quality that separates them, and one that cannot be disputed. It is the capacity for self-improvement, which, as circumstances allow, develops all the other faculties in turn, and which resides in us not only as a species but in each individual. A beast, by contrast, is, after a few months, everything that it will ever be for the rest of its life, and its species after a thousand years is just what it had been in the first year of that long period. Why is man alone liable to senility? Is it not because he thereby returns to his primitive condition? While the beast, which has acquired nothing and so has nothing to lose, always keeps its instinct, man loses through old age or accident everything his perfectibility had gained him, and so falls lower still than the beasts themselves. That would be a sad necessity to have to concede that this distinguishing and nearly boundless faculty is the source of all man's misfortunes; that it is what, slowly, draws him out of that original condition in which his days would slip by in peace and innocence; that it is what, over the course of centuries, produces his discoveries and his mistakes, his virtues and his vices, and in the long run makes him a tyrant over himself and nature alike. It would be dreadful to have to praise as a benefactor whoever first thought of the boards the Orinoco Indians bind onto their children's temples, which preserve for them at least part of their natural simplicity and happiness.
17|Savage man, left by nature to pure instinct, or rather compensated for what she may have denied him by faculties able to make up for it at once and to raise him afterward far higher, would thus begin with purely animal functions. To see and to feel would be his first condition, shared with every other animal. To want and not to want, to wish and to dread, would be the first and in a way the only workings of his soul, until fresh circumstances caused new developments.
18|Whatever moralists may say, human understanding owes a great deal to the passions, which in their turn, as everyone agrees, owe a great deal to human understanding. Our reason improves through the activity of our passions; we seek knowledge only because we seek enjoyment, and it is impossible to see why a man free of fears and desires would bother to reason. The passions, for their part, arise from our needs and grow with our progress in knowledge, for we can desire or fear something only because of the ideas we hold of it, or from the plain impulses of nature. Savage man, lacking every kind of knowledge, feels only passions of this last kind. His wishes never extend past his bodily needs. The sole goods he knows are food, a female and rest; the only evils he dreads are pain and hunger. I mean pain, and not death, for no animal as such will ever know what it is to die, and awareness of death and its terrors is among the first things man acquired when he departed from the animal state.
