13|If Providence had been less kind to me, or if I had come to wisdom too late through my own reflection, and found myself dragging out a weak and languishing life in some other climate, sighing in vain for the peace and rest I had thoughtlessly given up in my youth, I would at least have kept alive in my heart the very feelings I have just described, even though I could not have put them to use in my own country. Filled with a tender and disinterested affection for my distant fellow citizens, I would have addressed them from the bottom of my heart, in words as close as possible to these.
14|My dear fellow citizens, or rather my dear brothers, since ties of blood bind us no less than our common obedience to the same laws: I take endless pleasure in being unable to think of you without thinking of all the blessings you enjoy. Perhaps not one of you values them more than I do, who have lost them. The more I consider your political and civil situation, the harder it is for me to imagine that the nature of things could allow a better one. Other governments, even when they are debating their own survival, must rely on imaginary schemes or, at best, mere possibilities. As for you, your happiness is already made and placed in your hands; you have only to enjoy it, and to be perfectly happy you need only be content with being so. Your sovereignty, won or won back at sword point and maintained for two centuries by courage and wisdom, is now fully and universally recognized. The most honorable treaties have fixed your borders, confirmed your rights and secured your peace. Your constitution is excellent and fortunate, not only dictated by the deepest wisdom but guaranteed by the most respected powers. Your state enjoys the greatest calm; you have no wars or conquerors to fear; you have no masters except the wise laws you have made yourselves, enforced by upright magistrates of your own choosing. You are not so rich as to be softened by luxury and lose, in the pursuit of empty pleasures, your taste for real happiness and solid virtue, nor so poor as to need foreign help to supplement the fruits of your own labor. In short, it hardly costs you anything to keep that precious liberty which great nations can preserve only by submitting to the most extravagant taxes.
15|Long may such a wisely and happily constituted republic flourish, for the happiness of its citizens and as an example to mankind! This is the only wish left for you to make, the only thing left for you to think about. From now on it depends on you alone, not to make yourselves happy, since your ancestors have spared you that trouble, but to make your happiness last by using it wisely. Your survival depends on your unbroken unity, your obedience to the laws, and your respect for those who administer them. If the smallest seeds of bitterness or distrust remain among you, hurry to root them out like an accursed yeast that would sooner or later bring misfortune and ruin on the commonwealth. I urge you all to withdraw into the depths of your own hearts and consult the secret voice of your conscience. Can any one of you point to a more upright, wiser or more respectable body of men anywhere in the world than your own magistrates? Do not all of them set you a perfect example of moderation, plain living, respect for the laws and sincere reconciliation? Why, then, for your own sake, will you not give such wise superiors the unreserved confidence that virtue has a right to expect from reason? Remember that you chose them, that they justify your choice, and that the honors due to those you have put in authority necessarily reflect on you. Is any of you so inattentive as not to see that when the laws lose their force and their defenders lose their authority, no one can have either safety or liberty? What is asked of you, then, except to do cheerfully and without hesitation what your interest, your duty and sound reason ought to lead you to do? Never let a blameworthy and dangerous indifference toward upholding the constitution make you neglect, whenever you need it, the prudent advice of your most knowledgeable and devoted fellow citizens. Let fairness, moderation and the most respectful firmness continue to guide everything you do, and show the whole world the example of a bold and modest people, equally jealous of its glory and its freedom. Above all, and this is the last advice I will give you, beware of malicious interpretations and poisoned talk, whose hidden motives are often more dangerous than the actions they attack. A whole household will wake from sleep and take the alarm from a faithful, watchful dog that barks only when thieves approach; but we hate the nuisance of those noisy, troublesome curs that are forever disturbing the public peace, and whose endless, ill-timed warnings stop us from listening to the ones they occasionally give at the right moment.
16|And you, magnificent and most honored lords, you worthy and respectable magistrates of a free people, allow me to offer you my own particular duty and homage. If there is any rank among men that can lend distinction to those who hold it, surely it is the one that talent and virtue together confer, the one you have made yourselves worthy of and to which your fellow citizens have raised you. Their merit adds greatly to the brilliance of yours. Since men who are themselves capable of governing others have chosen you to govern them, I place you as far above other magistrates as a free people, and above all the one you have the honor to preside over, is above the common mass of other states in knowledge and wisdom.
17|Let me mention an example that should have left better traces, one my heart will never stop thinking of. I cannot recall without the most pleasant emotion the memory of the virtuous citizen to whom I owe my life, and who often taught me, in my childhood, the respect that is due to you. I seem to see him still, earning his bread by the work of his hands while feeding his mind with the loftiest truths. I see Tacitus, Plutarch and Grotius lying among the tools of his trade. Beside him I see a beloved son receiving, alas, with far too little profit, the tender instruction of the best of fathers. Though the follies of a thoughtless youth made me forget those wise lessons for a time, I have at last the happiness of knowing that however inclined to vice a pupil may be, it is almost impossible that he will not one day or another benefit from the education given him by a truly affectionate teacher!
18|Such, magnificent and most honored lords, are the citizens, and even the ordinary natives, of the country you govern. Such are these knowledgeable and sensible men, of whom, under the names of tradesmen and common people, other nations are used to forming the meanest and falsest ideas. My father, I admit it gladly, was in no way distinguished among his fellow citizens; he was only what they all are. Yet such as he was, there is no country where his conversation would not have been sought after, and where men of the highest rank and fortune would not have cultivated his acquaintance, to their considerable benefit. It is not for me to speak to you, and thank heaven there is no need, of the regard you owe to men of this kind. By education, nature and birth they are your equals; by their own choice they are your inferiors, since they gave your merit that preference, and you in turn owe them some gratitude for it. I am delighted to hear with what kindness and condescension you soften, in all your dealings with them, the gravity that suits ministers of the law; how well you repay the respect and obedience they give you with your esteem and your attention to their welfare. This conduct is full of justice and wisdom, and it increasingly erases the memory of those unfortunate events that ought to be buried in eternal silence and oblivion! It is all the more sensible because this fair and generous people takes pleasure in its duty, is naturally fond of honoring you, and finds that those most eager to defend their own rights and privileges are also the most inclined to respect yours.
19|It is not strange that the heads of a civil society should care for its glory and welfare. But it is an uncommon happiness when those who regard themselves as the magistrates, or rather the masters, of a holier and loftier country show some affection for the earthly country that supports them. How pleasing it is to be able to make so rare an exception in our favor, and to count among our best citizens those devoted keepers of the sacred articles of faith that the laws have authorized, those venerable shepherds of souls, whose gentle and lively eloquence is all the better suited to planting the maxims of the Gospel in people's hearts because they are the first to show the fruits of them! Everyone knows how successfully the great art of the pulpit is practiced at Geneva. But we are so used to hearing clergymen say one thing and do another that few people stop to consider how far the spirit of Christianity, holiness of life, strictness with themselves and indulgence toward their neighbors prevail throughout our body of ministers. Geneva is perhaps the only city in the world that can boast so perfect and edifying a union between its men of letters and its clergy! It is largely on their acknowledged wisdom and moderation, and on their zeal for the prosperity of the commonwealth, that I rest my hopes of its lasting tranquillity. I observe with pleasure, surprise and respect how much they abhor the appalling maxims of those sacred and barbarous men of whom history offers more than one example, who, to defend the supposed rights of the Almighty, that is, their own interests, spilled human blood the more freely because they flattered themselves that their own would always be spared.
20|Can I forget here that precious half of the republic which makes the happiness of the other half, and whose gentleness and prudent conduct do so much to secure for it the blessings of peace and good morals? Lovable and virtuous women of my city, it will always be the fate of your sex to govern ours. Happy are we as long as your chaste power, confined within the bounds of married life, is exercised only for the glory and happiness of your country! This is how women commanded at Sparta, and this is how you deserve to command at Geneva. What man is brutal enough to resist the voice of honor and reason in the mouth of a loving wife? Who would not despise the empty charms of luxury on seeing your simple and modest dress, which, because of the luster it takes from you, looks the most becoming to beauty? It is your task to keep harmony alive among citizens, with a proper love and respect for the laws of their country, through your lovable and innocent rule and your winning conversation; to reunite divided families through happy marriages; and above all to correct, through the persuasive gentleness of your lessons and the modest graces of your speech, the mistaken notions of things that our young people pick up in other countries. From there, instead of any of the many useful things within reach of their observation and practice, they bring home nothing but a boyish tone and ridiculous airs learned among loose women, together with admiration for some supposed grandeur, a poor compensation for slavery, which cannot compare with the true and native greatness of august liberty. So always remain what you are now, the chaste guardians of our morals and the gentle bonds of our peace, and keep exercising on every occasion the privileges of nature and the heart in the service of duty and virtue!
21|I hope no event will ever make me a liar for guaranteeing, on the strength of your powerful support, eternal glory to the republic and happiness to its citizens. I must admit, however, that with all these advantages our state can never claim the brilliant and dazzling appearance that catches most people's eyes, and a childish, deplorable taste for which is the deadliest enemy of prosperity and freedom. Let dissolute young men run off to other countries in search of easy pleasures and long repentance. Let pretenders to good taste admire elsewhere the grandeur of palaces, the beauty of carriages, the sumptuousness of furniture, the pomp of spectacles, and all the refinements of luxury and softness. At Geneva you find nothing but men. Yet that sight has its own value, and those who look for it are well worth the admirers of everything else.
22|Please, magnificent, most honored and sovereign lords, accept all of you with the same goodness these respectful expressions of the share I take in your common prosperity. If, in this lively outpouring of my heart, I have unhappily given way to any indiscreet transports, I humbly beg you to forgive it in view of the tender love of a true patriot and the ardent, lawful zeal of one who wishes for himself no greater happiness than to see you all happy.
23|With the deepest respect, magnificent, most honored and sovereign lords, I am your most humble and most obedient servant and fellow citizen, John James Rousseau. Chambéry, 12 June 1754.
