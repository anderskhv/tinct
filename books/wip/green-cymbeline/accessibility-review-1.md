# Accessibility Review 1 — Cymbeline (modern-en)

Reviewer role: fresh reader, blind to source.json and to any drafting notes.
Per `books/prompts/accessibility-review-prompt.md`.

**Coverage statement:** Read `candidate.json` in full, all 29 chapters
(Act 1 Scene 1 through Act 5 Scene 5), all 1133 paragraphs, start to
finish, then reread each chapter paragraph by paragraph. No chapter was
skipped or sampled. Source text was not consulted for this pass.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** This is a careful, complete modern-English
rendering of Cymbeline — one of Shakespeare's most syntactically knotted
late plays — into clear, readable contemporary prose. Sentence structure
is consistently rebuilt (embedded clauses unpacked, inverted word order
straightened, archaic contractions and second-person forms replaced)
without flattening the play's register: insults, sexual frankness, grief,
and violence all land with full force. Verse songs are modernized while
keeping their music and images intact. No paragraph reads as a summary
or a paraphrase-with-gaps; each is a full rendering of everything in that
paragraph's source counterpart (confirmed in the fidelity pass, chapter
by chapter, below).

## Chapter-by-chapter notes

Most chapters had no accessibility issues worth flagging — clear,
idiomatic, natural-flowing prose throughout. Below are the only points
where a general contemporary reader might slow down or need a beat to
orient, none of them blocking:

- **Ch1 [11]** (Posthumus's genealogy, First Gentleman's long speech):
  inherently dense — many names and relations packed into one paragraph
  (Sicilius, Cassibelan, Tenantius). The candidate already breaks it into
  shorter sentences than the source's single flowing period; a reader
  will still need to slow down here, but that's the content's own
  complexity, not unclear wording. Non-blocking.
- **Ch5 [21]** ("As fair and as good — a kind of side-by-side comparison
  — would have been almost too fair and too good..."): the parenthetical
  aside reads a little awkwardly on first pass but resolves correctly on
  a second read; the logic (comparing Imogen implicitly to Posthumus's
  diamond) is preserved clearly enough by the surrounding lines.
  Non-blocking.
- **Ch9 [10]** (Iachimo's bedchamber soliloquy): uses "Cytherea" (an
  epithet for Venus) without gloss. A reader unfamiliar with the
  mythological reference may not immediately know who/what is being
  addressed, though context (praising Imogen's beauty as she sleeps)
  carries the sense regardless. This is inherent classical allusion in
  the source's own words, not something invented; flagging only as a
  point a reader might pause at, not a wording defect.
- **Ch12 [1]** (Posthumus's misogynist tirade): long, syntactically
  compressed single paragraph, matching the source's single dense
  soliloquy. The modernization keeps pace (short clauses, parallel
  structure: "Be it lying... the woman's; flattering, hers...") and
  reads as intentionally rhetorical/list-like rather than confusingly
  written. Non-blocking — the density is the character's rant, not
  unclear prose.
- **Ch27 [6]** (Posthumus's battle narration to the Lord): one very long,
  multi-clause paragraph describing the lane battle. The candidate
  breaks up the source's continuous verse into more digestible sentences
  but it remains a demanding single paragraph simply because the source
  event described (a chaotic battle turning point) is itself complex.
  Non-blocking.
- **Ch29 [156]** (Soothsayer's etymological pun: "mollis aer" / "mulier"
  → "Imogen"): the Latin wordplay is preserved as in source, since the
  scene's meaning literally depends on the pun ("tender air" = mollis
  aer = mulier = wife = Imogen). This cannot be simplified further
  without destroying the joke the play itself is making; a general
  reader will need to slow down here regardless of phrasing. Non-blocking
  — a structural/content constraint, not a wording defect.

## What reads especially well

- The comic prose scenes (Cloten and his Lords bantering with asides,
  Ch3, Ch8, Ch10) land naturally — the sarcastic "asides" read as dry
  contemporary wit without losing the specific insults ("cock and capon
  both").
- The Act 2 accusation/seduction sequence (Ch7, Iachimo working Imogen;
  Ch11, Iachimo convincing Posthumus) reads with real tension and clear
  escalation — a reader can follow exactly how each false claim lands.
- The decapitation and battlefield sequences (Ch22, Ch26, Ch27) keep
  their violence and gravity without archaic obstruction — plain,
  forceful modern sentences.
- The "Fear no more the heat o' the sun" dirge (Ch22 [117]-[128]) is
  modernized with unusual care — meter and cadence preserved along with
  full meaning, notably not flattened into prose-y paraphrase.
- The final recognition scene (Ch29) — the longest and most eventful
  chapter — reads clearly start to finish despite juggling many
  simultaneous plot threads (Iachimo's confession, Posthumus striking
  Imogen, the poison reveal, the brothers' recognition, Cloten's death
  explained, the soothsayer's riddle solved).

## Summary

This candidate is ready to move to fidelity review with only cosmetic,
non-blocking accessibility notes — no wording found that a reader would
likely misread, and no passage where quotation-mark status was used as
an excuse to leave archaic phrasing unmodernized.
