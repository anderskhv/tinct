# Symposium: blind comprehension review, reader BL3

**Packet:** chapters 1 and 3 of the modern English reading edition (“The Gathering”, “Pausanias's Speech”).

**Reader:** a curious sixteen-year-old, or an adult hearing the text read aloud, with no background in Greek philosophy. I take as known only who Socrates and Plato were, Zeus, Aphrodite as goddess of love, and Homer as the poet of the Trojan War. Everything else I judge from the text alone.

**How to read this report**
- Exact quotes from the packet are in curly double quotes. My paraphrase of what I understood is in *italics*.
- **[PRIOR]** marks each point I understood only because of knowledge from outside the text.
- Each unclear point has a type and a weight. The types are *ear* (hard to follow aloud), *opaque*, *misleading*, *archaic*, *tone* and *logic* (a step that does not follow). The weights are **high** (a listener loses the thread), **med** (a listener stumbles, then recovers) and **low** (a snag).
- My packet does not include chapter 2 (Phaedrus's speech). I do not count [3.0]'s references back to it against chapter 3.

## Chapter 1: The Gathering

### 1. Argument summary

This chapter is a scene, not a single speech, so I summarize each speaker's claim and its support in turn.

**Apollodorus, who narrates the frame story ([1.0]–[1.11])**
- He can retell the speeches about love from Agathon's supper because two days ago he told them to Glaucon on the road into Athens.
- He was not there himself. The supper happened *when they were boys*, the day after Agathon's first tragedy won the prize. Agathon left Athens years ago, and Apollodorus has followed Socrates for less than three years.
- His source is Aristodemus, a barefoot admirer of Socrates who was present, and Socrates has confirmed parts of the story. The chain of reports is hard to hold by ear. Aristodemus told Phoenix, Phoenix told "someone", and that person told Glaucon. Aristodemus also told Apollodorus, who then told Glaucon and is now telling an unnamed companion.
- A side claim: talk about philosophy is the greatest pleasure and benefit, and the talk of rich businessmen is worthless. He pities such men. *They only think he is wretched, but he knows they are.* His only support is his own before-and-after story: “a thoroughly wretched creature — no better than you are now”. He asserts, rather than argues, the step from *I was wretched* to *you are doing nothing*. I read this as a picture of his zeal, not as a flaw in the edition.
- The companion calls him “Apollodorus the madman”, and he accepts the name with irony.

**Socrates on the road ([1.12]–[1.24])**
- Socrates has bathed and put on sandals, which is unusual for him, because he is going to Agathon's. He skipped yesterday's crowded victory party and promised to come today instead. There is a pun: he wears “finery” for “such a fine man”.
- He talks Aristodemus into coming uninvited by playing with a proverb. The proverb says the good go uninvited to the feasts of inferior men. Agathon's name means "good", so the proverb can become *the good go uninvited to the feasts of the good*.
- He says Homer supports the change and goes even further. In Homer, Menelaus, “a fainthearted warrior”, comes uninvited to the feast of Agamemnon, “the bravest of men”, so *the worse goes to the better*. I could only work this step out on a second reading (Chapter 1, unclear point 16).
- Aristodemus worries that he will be the inferior guest, so he will say that Socrates invited him. Socrates replies that two people walking together will think of an excuse.

**At Agathon's house ([1.25]–[1.35])**
- Socrates falls behind, lost in thought, and Aristodemus arrives alone. Agathon welcomes him warmly. Socrates is found standing still in a neighbor's porch. Aristodemus says this is a habit of his and asks the others to leave him alone. Agathon tells the servants to run the dinner as if they were the hosts.
- Socrates arrives halfway through the meal. Agathon asks him to recline beside him, so that he can take in by touch whatever wise thought Socrates found in the porch.
- Socrates replies that it would be wonderful if wisdom flowed by touch from a fuller person to an emptier one, like water along a woollen thread. Then Agathon would fill *him*, because his own wisdom is “no better than a dream” while Agathon's shone “before more than thirty thousand Greeks”. This is ironic flattery. Agathon calls it mockery and says they will settle who is wiser later, with Dionysus as judge.

**Pausanias, Aristophanes, Eryximachus and Phaedrus on drinking ([1.36]–[1.42])**
- Pausanias says everyone is hung over from yesterday and asks how they can drink with the least harm. Aristophanes agrees, and Agathon admits he is not up to hard drinking either.
- Eryximachus is a physician. He says the weak drinkers (himself, Aristodemus and Phaedrus) are lucky that the strong drinkers are not in the mood. Socrates doesn't count, because he can drink or not as he likes. Speaking as a doctor, he says heavy drinking is harmful. Phaedrus defers to him.
- They agree that each man will drink only as much as he likes.

**Eryximachus's proposal, which passes on Phaedrus's complaint ([1.43]–[1.46])**
- He proposes sending the flute-girl away and making conversation the evening's entertainment.
- Phaedrus's complaint: other gods have hymns, professional teachers have written prose praising Heracles, and someone has even praised salt. Yet no poet has ever praised Love as he deserves. The conclusion, that Love has been neglected, follows clearly.
- The proposal: each guest in turn, from left to right, gives the best speech he can in praise of Love. Phaedrus goes first, because he sits first on the left and the idea is his.

**Socrates agrees, and Apollodorus adds a warning ([1.47]–[1.48])**
- Socrates says love is “the only subject I claim to understand”. He adds that Agathon, Pausanias and Aristophanes will not object either. The last speakers, himself included, are at a disadvantage, but they will be content if the early speeches are good.
- Apollodorus warns that neither Aristodemus nor he remembers everything, so he will give only the main speeches.

### 2. Unclear points

1. **[1.0]** *ear · high*: “As for what you're asking about, I believe I'm well prepared to answer.” The chapter starts in the middle of a conversation. We don't know who is speaking, who "you" is, or what was asked. The speaker's name first appears inside a shout he remembers, and the person he is talking to, later labelled COMPANION, is never named. *Understood: someone has asked the narrator to repeat some speeches, and he explains why he is able to.*
2. **[1.0]** *ear · med*: “Someone told me what he had heard from Phoenix, the son of Philip, but his account was very vague. He did say, though, that you knew about them”. This adds another link to the chain of reports and a name, "Phoenix, the son of Philip", that plays no further part. "He" could mean the unnamed someone or Phoenix. *Understood: Glaucon heard a muddled secondhand version and was told to ask Apollodorus.*
3. **[1.0]** *opaque · low*: “'Apollodorus! You there — you Phalerian! Halt!'” The text says he “called out playfully”, but only the mock-official "Halt!" carries the joke. *Understood: a teasing shout, with "Phalerian" meaning a man from Phalerum.* If there is more wit in it, the text doesn't show it.
4. **[1.0]** *opaque · low*: “Who better than you to report your friend's words?” We aren't yet told who "your friend" is. [1.3] makes it clear that it is Socrates.
5. **[1.0]** *opaque · low*: “the speeches in praise of love that Socrates, Alcibiades, and others gave at Agathon's supper”. Alcibiades is named as one of the speakers, but he is not among the guests in chapter 1, and the text never says who he is. Within my packet he is a loose end. **[PRIOR]** I expect him to appear later only because I know the book.
6. **[1.5]** *opaque · med*: “at the time Agathon won the prize with his first tragedy. It was the day after he and his chorus had offered their victory sacrifice.” What prize was it, and who awarded it? What is a "chorus" in a tragedy? A modern listener will think of a choir. Why would a playwright offer a sacrifice? *Understood: Agathon wrote a play that won a competition, and he celebrated with a religious ceremony and a party.* **[PRIOR]** I know that plays competed for prizes at religious festivals and that the chorus was part of the play.
7. **[1.7]** *opaque / archaic · med*: “Aristodemus, of the deme of Cydathenaeum”. Most listeners won't know "deme", and it is easy to mishear as "deem" or "dream". "Cydathenaeum" is hard to say and tells the listener nothing. *Understood only as "from some district".* **[PRIOR]** I know a deme was a local district of Athens.
8. **[1.7]** *ear · high*: “So we walked along and talked about the speeches on love. And that is why, as I told you at the start, I'm well prepared to do what you ask”. In the middle of a paragraph the story jumps from the remembered walk with Glaucon back to the present conversation. "You" silently changes from Glaucon to the unnamed companion. Only "as I told you at the start" signals the jump. Listening, I would have assumed he was still talking to Glaucon.
9. **[1.7]** *ear / tone · med*: “especially the talk of rich men and businessmen like you — it annoys me; and I pity you, my friends”. Only here do we learn that his listener is a businessman. Then "my friends", in the plural, suggests a group we have not been told about. The sudden contempt sounds simply rude until [1.8] shows that he always talks this way.
10. **[1.8]** *ear · high*: “COMPANION: I see you're just the same as ever, Apollodorus”. For four paragraphs the text switches to script-style speaker labels (COMPANION:, APOLLODORUS:), then drops them. "Companion" has never been introduced. Read aloud, the label sounds like a new character arriving. *Understood: this is the person Apollodorus has been talking to since the first line.*
11. **[1.8]** *ear · low*: “I really do believe you pity all mankind except Socrates, starting with yourself.” When heard aloud, "except Socrates, starting with yourself" can briefly sound like a second exception. *Understood: you pity everyone except Socrates, beginning with yourself.*
12. **[1.9]** *tone · low*: “the reason people say I'm crazy and out of my mind is precisely because I hold these views about you and about myself. No other evidence is needed.” Is he admitting he is mad, or being proudly sarcastic? *Understood as ironic self-mockery*, but the tone is uncertain.
13. **[1.12]** *archaic · low*: “all dressed up like a dandy”. The word is old-fashioned, but the meaning is easy to guess.
14. **[1.13]** *ear · low*: “I turned down his invitation yesterday — his victory sacrifice — because I was afraid of a crowd”. The phrase between the dashes makes it sound as if the invitation itself was a sacrifice. *Understood: he turned down an invitation to yesterday's victory celebration.*
15. **[1.14], [1.26], [1.30], [1.48]** *ear · high*: “I replied, Aristodemus said, 'I'll do whatever you tell me.'” and “I turned around, Aristodemus said”. Here "I" means Aristodemus, not the narrator. A listener hears "I replied" and has to reassign it once "Aristodemus said" follows. At [1.12] the text also switches from indirect report (“He said that he had met Socrates”) to Aristodemus speaking directly in the first person. By [1.48] (“I do not remember everything he told me”) "I" means Apollodorus again. In [1.30], “our friend Socrates” leaves it unclear whose "our" is meant. It doesn't help that the two narrators have similar names, Apollodorus and Aristodemus, with Aristophanes as a third Aristo- name. *Understood, thanks to the speaker tags, but slowly.*
16. **[1.15]–[1.19]** *ear · high*: “'To the feasts of inferior men the good go uninvited;'”, then “'To the feasts of the good the good go uninvited;'”, then Homer “who not only demolishes the proverb but does outright violence to it”, then “So in Homer it is not the better who comes uninvited to the worse, but the worse to the better.” The passage gives three versions of one sentence and a chain of better/worse reversals, cut into five short paragraphs. The gloss on Agathon's name rescues the first step. I could only work out the Homer step on a second reading. Homer's case, where the worse man goes to the better, is even further from the original proverb, where the good go to the inferior, than Socrates's version is. A listener would lose this. Agamemnon and Menelaus get just enough description for the joke to work. **[PRIOR]** I know who they are, and that they were brothers, which is why Menelaus could come unasked. The text doesn't need that fact, but without it Homer's example feels arbitrary.
17. **[1.20]–[1.21]** *ear / format · med*: “I shall be the inferior person who',” and then “'To the feasts of the wise goes uninvited.'” The sentence is split across two paragraphs. The dangling "who'," looks like a typo in print and forces a pause in mid-sentence when read aloud. The proverb also changes from "good" to "wise" without comment. *I took it as Aristodemus's own twist*, but a listener may think they missed something.

18. **[1.22]** *ear · low*: “'But I'll say that it was you who invited me, and then you'll have to come up with the excuse.'” The line after the verse quotation has no speaker tag. *I had to infer that Aristodemus is still speaking.*
19. **[1.23]–[1.24]** *ear / opaque · med*: “'Two going together,'” followed by “he replied, in Homeric style, 'one or the other of them may think up an excuse on the way.'” This is another quotation split across paragraphs. "In Homeric style" is unexplained: what makes the line Homeric? *Understood: two heads are better than one.* **[PRIOR]** I recognize it as a line from Homer. The text doesn't say so.
20. **[1.25]** *ear · low*: “a comical thing happened”. The sentence doesn't describe anything comical. The joke, that the uninvited guest arrives without the man who invited him, only lands in [1.26].

22. **[1.33]** *misleading · low*: “Serve whatever you like, since there's no one to give you orders — I've never left you to yourselves before.” This sounds as if nobody is in charge at all, yet Agathon, the master of the house, is standing right there. *Understood: "tonight, for the first time, I won't give you orders".*

Socrates missed half the meal, so "did not last long" jars. *Understood: short by his usual standard.* In the same paragraph, “'so that I can touch you,' he said” sounds odd, even flirtatious, when heard on its own. ### 3. What works

- **[1.0]** “'Apollodorus! You there — you Phalerian! Halt!' So I did as I was told”. A lively, funny opening, even without knowing the joke behind it.
- **[1.7]** “But what you only think about me, I know for certain about you. That's the difference.” This one line sums up Apollodorus.
- **[1.8]–[1.9]** The nickname “Apollodorus the madman”, and his cheerful acceptance of it, show his character through dialogue.
- **[1.13]** “So I've put on my finery, because he's such a fine man.” The pun works in English.
- **[1.17]** “since our host's name, Agathon, means "good"”. The gloss sits in the line exactly where it is needed. Without it the proverb joke is lost.
- **[1.25]** Agathon's welcome sounds natural and gracious, and it is easy to follow aloud.
- **[1.30]–[1.32]** “'He's standing there, rooted to the spot,'” and “He has a way of stopping anywhere and losing himself for no reason.” This is the most vivid picture of Socrates in the chapter.
- **[1.33]** “But this time, imagine that you are our hosts, and that I and the company are your guests.” A charming and clear reversal of roles.
- **[1.34]–[1.35]** “the way water creeps along a thread of wool from a fuller cup into an emptier one” and “before more than thirty thousand Greeks” are concrete images. Agathon's “You're mocking me, Socrates” confirms the irony straight away.
- **[1.35]** “Dionysus, god of wine and the theater, will be our judge”. A short, well-placed gloss that pays off again in [1.47].
- **[1.36]–[1.42]** The hangover conversation is relatable, comic and easy to follow aloud. “I was one of the people who got drowned in drink yesterday” and Eryximachus's pompous “I may be forgiven for saying, as a physician, that heavy drinking is a bad practice” each sketch a character in a line.
- **[1.46]** “I once came across a philosophical book in which the usefulness of salt was made the subject of an eloquent speech”. Funny, and it makes Phaedrus's complaint concrete. The rules of the game are completely clear: each man speaks in turn, “going from left to right”, with Phaedrus first “because he is sitting first on the left, and because he is the father of the idea”.
- **[1.47]** “Aristophanes, the comic poet, whose whole concern is with Dionysus and Aphrodite — that is, with wine and the theater, and with love”. The glosses do their job and set up chapter 3's Aphrodite.
- **[1.48]** “Aristodemus did not remember everything that was said, and I do not remember everything he told me”. A clear, honest reminder of the frame story that closes the chapter well.

### 4. Rating: 3 / 5

*What limits it most is the story-within-a-story narration.* The difficulty comes at the start. On their own, [1.0]–[1.11] would score about 2. The listener is never named, the chain of reports is long, [1.7] switches listeners without warning and script labels suddenly appear. The proverb-and-Homer exchange ([1.15]–[1.24]) is the other weak stretch. From [1.25] to the end the chapter is close to a 4. The arrival, the trance, the hangovers and the proposal are lively, concrete and easy to follow aloud. The number of names works against it throughout. More than twenty people and gods are named, and Pausanias, who speaks next, never gets a single identifying detail.

## Chapter 3: Pausanias's Speech

### 1. Argument summary

**Pausanias ([3.0]–[3.9])**
1. **There are two Loves, so we must say which one we praise.** Pausanias says Phaedrus set the task wrongly. Love always goes with Aphrodite, and there are two Aphrodites. The elder has no mother, is the daughter of Uranus, and is called “the heavenly Aphrodite”. The younger is the daughter of Zeus and Dione and is called “Common”. So there are two Loves, Heavenly and Common. The premise that Love and Aphrodite always go together is simply assumed (“we all know”).
2. **Nothing is good or bad in itself.** No action, including drinking, singing and talking, is good or bad in itself. It depends on how it is done. So only a love with a noble purpose deserves praise.
3. **Common Love is low.** It cares for the body more than the soul, is aimed at women and youths alike, and does good and bad without distinction. It chooses “the most foolish people” because it cares only about getting what it wants. Its goddess is younger and was born of both a male and a female parent. Two steps are left unexplained: why this love picks fools, and why a mixed birth produces love of both sexes (unclear points 7–8).
4. **Heavenly Love is high.** Its goddess is older and was born of the male only, so there is “nothing lewd” in it. It turns to males, prefers those with “the more valiant and intelligent” nature, and chooses young men whose minds are developing rather than boys. It aims to be faithful for life. The link from *older* to *not lewd* is asserted, not argued (unclear points 8–9).
5. **The law must restrain bad lovers.** The law should forbid love of young boys, because no one knows how they will turn out and the lover's effort may be wasted. Coarse lovers must be held back by force, because they are the ones who give love a bad name. But nothing done in an orderly and lawful way can fairly be blamed.
6. **Other places have simple rules, for bad reasons.** Where people are poor speakers (Elis, Boeotia), the practice is simply allowed. Where barbarians rule (Ionia), it is condemned, along with philosophy and gymnastics, because tyrants fear the bonds it creates. Athens's own tyrants learned this from Harmodius and Aristogeiton. So condemnation comes from the rulers' selfishness and the subjects' cowardice, and blanket approval comes from lazy thinking. The text never explains why philosophy and gymnastics threaten tyrants (unclear points 16–20).
7. **Athens's custom only looks contradictory.** A lover is praised and excused almost anything: begging, sleeping at the door, even breaking oaths. Yet fathers have their sons watched, and the sons' friends taunt them. Pausanias resolves this: the practice is honorable when it is done honorably. The custom sets up a chase, with lovers pursuing and beloveds fleeing. The chase tests who loves the body, and so flies off when youth fades, and who loves character, and so stays for life. This is the clearest step in the speech.
8. **The only honorable reason to yield is virtue.** Yielding in haste is dishonorable, because time is the true test. Yielding for money or power is dishonorable, because these things don't last and never produce generous friendship. The only honorable reason for a beloved to yield is virtue, meaning service given in order to become a better person. When the lover's wish to serve meets the beloved's wish to learn, yielding is honorable. Two test cases show this. Yield to a man you think is rich and you are disgraced even if he turns out to be poor, because you showed you would sell yourself. Yield to a man you think is good and you are noble even if he turns out to be a scoundrel. **Conclusion:** Heavenly Love makes both partners work at improving themselves, and it is valuable to individuals and to cities. Every other love belongs to the Common goddess.

**What follows the speech ([3.10]–[3.11]).** The narrator makes a pun on Pausanias's name. Aristophanes has hiccups, so Eryximachus speaks in his place and prescribes a cure: hold your breath, then gargle, then sneeze.

### 2. Unclear points

1. **[3.0]** *ear · low*: “Phaedrus, I don't think the question has been framed correctly.” No question was asked, only a task: to praise Love. *Understood: "the topic was set up wrongly".*
2. **[3.0]** *ear · high*: “since there are more Loves than one, we should have started by deciding which of them we are praising”. A listener cannot hear the capital L. "More loves than one" sounds like "more than one kind of love", which is half the point. But “praise him” and “the Love who works alongside her” treat Love as a person. Heard aloud, this double meaning (god and feeling) runs through the whole speech and is never flagged. *Understood: there are two love-gods, standing for two kinds of love.*
3. **[3.0], [3.1]** *opaque · high*: “For we all know that Love is inseparable from Aphrodite”. The whole two-Loves argument rests on this "we all know", and the text never says why it is true. Is Love her son or her helper? The text wavers: in [3.0] Love “works alongside her”, but in [3.1] he is “the offspring of the Common Aphrodite”. **[PRIOR]** I only know from outside the text that Love (Eros, Cupid) is traditionally Aphrodite's son or companion.
4. **[3.0]** *opaque · med*: “And am I not right that there are two goddesses? The elder, who has no mother, is called the heavenly Aphrodite”. Two Aphrodites is news to a modern listener, yet it is presented as common knowledge. A goddess with no mother is left unexplained. The gloss on Uranus helps. The name "Dione" means nothing to a listener, but that does no harm. **[PRIOR]** I know the birth story behind "no mother". The text doesn't tell it, and it matters only because [3.1] builds on “she is from the male only”.
5. **[3.0], [3.1]** *misleading · low*: “we call Common, the Aphrodite of all the people”, later followed by “essentially common and makes no distinctions. It is the love the baser sort of men feel”. The gloss "of all the people" sounds neutral, even democratic. Then "Common" turns out to mean vulgar. That is good wordplay if you catch it, but it may mislead on first hearing.
6. **[3.1]** *opaque / misleading · high*: “It is the love the baser sort of men feel, directed equally at women and at youths”. This is the first sign that the speech is about adult men's love for young males. "Youths" is an old-fashioned word, and a listener may hear it as young people of either sex. The text never states the arrangement behind the whole speech: an older "lover" courts a younger "beloved", who may "yield". It has to be pieced together from [3.1], [3.2], [3.5] and [3.6]. **[PRIOR]** I understood the arrangement, with an adult man as lover and a teenage boy as beloved, from outside knowledge of Greek customs.
7. **[3.1]** *logic · med*: “Its objects are the most foolish people, since this love cares only about getting what it wants and never about achieving its end nobly”. As stated, the step doesn't follow. Why would caring only about the goal make someone pick fools? *I rebuilt it as "fools are the easiest to win"*, but the text doesn't say that.
8. **[3.1]** *logic · med*: “This is the love that is directed toward youths, and because the goddess is older, there is nothing lewd or unruly in her.” Why would being older make a goddess free of lewdness? The speech asserts this without arguing it. "In her" is also ambiguous: does it mean the goddess or the love? In the same way, “she was born of the union of male and female, and so has something of both” leaves the listener to supply the conclusion, which is that her love goes to both sexes.
9. **[3.1]** *ambiguous / tone · med*: “Those inspired by this love turn to the male, delighting in whoever has the more valiant and intelligent nature”. "The more valiant" than whom? Pausanias could mean that males as a sex are braver and cleverer than females, which would explain "turn to the male". Or he could mean that these lovers pick the braver, cleverer young man. Heard aloud, the two readings blur. If he means the first, a modern listener will bristle. It is clearly Pausanias's view, but nothing marks it as his. **[PRIOR]** My reading that he means males as a sex comes partly from prior knowledge.
10. **[3.1], [3.2], [3.8]** *ear / misleading · high*: “For they love not boys, but young people whose minds are beginning to develop — around the age when their beards begin to grow.” The argument depends on an age line, yet the words for the beloved keep changing: “youths”, “boys”, “young people”, “young men”, “young boys” and, in [3.8], “the love of youth”. The gender-neutral "young people" also hides the fact that these are males. By ear it is hard to keep track of which group is acceptable and which is banned. *Understood: older teenage males are acceptable; children are not.*
11. **[3.2]** *tone · med*: “The love of young boys, however, ought to be forbidden by law, because their future is uncertain: they may turn out good or bad, in body or soul, and much noble enthusiasm may be wasted on them.” The reason given for the ban is that the lover's effort may be wasted, not that the child may be harmed. I assume that is what Pausanias means. A modern listener, though, will think they misheard, and nothing signals that this is the view of his time.
12. **[3.2]** *opaque · med*: “just as we try to restrain them from fixing their affections on freeborn women”. "Freeborn" implies that some women were not free, and the text doesn't say which law is meant. *Understood: there were rules against pursuing respectable women.* **[PRIOR]** I know about slavery and about the Athenian laws that protected citizens' wives and daughters.
13. **[3.2]–[3.6]** *ear · med*: “some have been led to deny that such attachments can ever be proper”. The euphemisms for the relationship keep changing: “such attachments”, “these connections”, “the custom”, “these attachments”, “these practices” and “such practices”. Only once, in [3.3], does the text say plainly “love between males”. A listener has to keep supplying what these phrases refer to.
14. **[3.3]** *opaque · med*: “Now here in Athens, and in Lacedaemon, the rules about love are perplexing”. "Lacedaemon" is not explained, and the speech never mentions it again. **[PRIOR]** I know that Lacedaemon is Sparta.
15. **[3.3]** *opaque · low*: “In Elis and Boeotia, and in countries that have no gift for eloquence”. The places are unknown, but the rest of the sentence gives enough to carry on with. **[PRIOR]** I know what and where these places are.
16. **[3.3]** *logic · med*: “The reason, I suppose, is that the men there are not eloquent, so lovers prefer not to take the trouble of pleading their case.” The reasoning is compressed. *I rebuilt it as: they can't win someone over with words, so the custom simply says yes, which saves lovers the work of persuading.* The phrase "lovers prefer" makes it sound like a personal choice rather than the reason for a rule.
17. **[3.3]** *opaque / misleading · high*: “In Ionia, on the other hand, and in other places generally subject to the barbarians”. Listeners won't know Ionia. They will hear "barbarians" as savage tribes, but here it seems to mean foreign rulers who govern through tyrants. **[PRIOR]** I know that "barbarians" meant non-Greeks, here the Persian empire, and that Ionia was a group of Greek cities under its rule.
18. **[3.3]** *misleading · high*: “love between males shares the evil reputation attached to philosophy and gymnastics, because all three are hostile to tyranny”. Listeners will hear "gymnastics" as the modern sport and wonder why a tyrant would fear somersaults. The text explains why love threatens tyrants (it creates strong bonds) but not why philosophy or exercise does. **[PRIOR]** I know that the gymnasium was a place where free men trained and talked together.
19. **[3.3]** *misleading · low*: “Rulers' interests require that their subjects be poor in spirit”. The phrase sounds like the familiar religious saying, where it means humble and blessed. Here it means cowed and spiritless.
20. **[3.3]** *opaque · high*: “as our own Athenian tyrants learned by experience. For Aristogeiton's love and Harmodius's constancy proved strong enough to bring down their power.” Athens had tyrants? Many listeners know Athens only as a democracy. The text doesn't say who Aristogeiton and Harmodius were, what they did, or whether they were a couple. Both names are also hard to catch by ear. *Understood: two lovers whose loyalty to each other helped overthrow a ruler.* **[PRIOR]** I know the story of these two tyrant-killers.

21. **[3.4]** *ambiguous · low*: “the love of the noblest and highest is especially honorable, even when the beloved is less beautiful than others”. Noblest by birth, or by character? *Understood: love aimed at the best people.*
22. **[3.4]** *misleading · med*: “he is not thought to be doing anything dishonorable — if he succeeds he is praised, and if he fails he is blamed”. "Blamed" seems to contradict "not thought to be doing anything dishonorable". On reflection, *I read it as: failing to win the beloved is treated as a defeat, like losing a contest, not as a wrong.* Heard aloud, most listeners will stumble. Note too that "succeeds" is the only hint, before [3.6], of what the pursuit is actually for.
23. **[3.4]** *opaque · med*: “custom allows him to do many strange things that philosophy would bitterly condemn if they were done from any motive of profit, or desire for office, or power”. Why does "philosophy" do the condemning, rather than people in general? Philosophy has just appeared as a force against tyranny, so its sudden role as a moral judge is confusing. *Understood: this is behavior that people would despise if it were done for gain.*
24. **[3.5]** *archaic · low*: “He may pray, beg, supplicate, swear oaths, sleep on a mat at the door”. "Supplicate" is archaic. "Pray" here means pleading with the beloved, but a listener may hear it as praying to the gods. Otherwise the list is vivid.
25. **[3.5]** *misleading · low*: “put them in the care of a tutor whose job is to prevent any such thing”. To a modern listener a tutor teaches, but here the tutor is a chaperone. The rest of the sentence explains the job, so this is only a snag. **[PRIOR]** I know this was a household attendant who escorted boys.
26. **[3.6]** *ear · med*: “There is dishonor in yielding — that is, in a beloved's granting his lover physical favors — to a bad lover, or in yielding in a bad way”. The gloss is valuable, because it is the only place where the text says what "yielding" means. But the gloss splits "yielding" from "to a bad lover", so when heard, the grammar falls apart. Before this point, the physical side of the relationship is only hinted at: “of the body rather than the soul” ([3.1]), “if he succeeds” ([3.4]).
27. **[3.6]** *ear · low*: “because his love becomes one with what is everlasting”. This is slightly mystical. What is everlasting: the beloved's character, or something divine? *Understood: he is attached to something that lasts.*
28. **[3.6]** *ear · low*: “and wants us to yield to the one sort and avoid the other”. "Us" puts Pausanias among the beloveds. *Understood: "people", or "the young".*
29. **[3.7]** *ear · med*: “whether a man is frightened into surrender by the fear of losing them, or, having tasted the benefits of money and political corruption, cannot rise above their seductions”. Nothing signals that "a man" here is the beloved, the one who yields. The sentence before, “being overcome by the love of money, wealth, or political power”, says "money" twice over. "Political corruption" is vague: whose corruption? *Understood: yielding because you fear losing money or position, or because you have been bought with money or favors, is disgraceful.*
30. **[3.7]** *misleading · med*: “so the beloved has one, and only one, form of voluntary service that is not dishonorable: namely, virtuous service”. "Service" now stands for the beloved's yielding, but the text never makes that link clear. "Virtuous service" could be heard as chores or acts of kindness. In older usage "virtue" can also mean chastity, which makes “for the beloved to yield — the way of virtue” sound like a contradiction. *Understood: giving yourself to a lover is honorable only if you do it to become a better person.*
31. **[3.8]** *archaic · low*: “whether in wisdom or in some other particular of virtue”. Archaic phrasing. *Understood: "or some other good quality".*
32. **[3.8]** *ear · med*: “These two customs — the love of youth, and the practice of philosophy and virtue generally — ought to meet in one”. Heard aloud, "the love of youth" is ambiguous: love felt when young, or love of the young? The second custom was described a moment earlier as serving someone in order to be improved. Now it is called "the practice of philosophy and virtue", so the listener has to match the new label to the old description. Two sentences later the "customs" become "laws".
33. **[3.8]** *ear · high*: “For when lover and beloved come together, each following his own law” and “then, when the two laws of love are fulfilled and meet in one, and only then, may the beloved yield honorably to the lover”. These two quotes open and close a single sentence of about 100 words, with two insertions between dashes. The main clause arrives only at the end. The sentence also contains “his gracious loved one”, where "gracious" means one who grants favors, though a listener will hear "polite". *Understood: when the lover wants to teach and the beloved wants to learn, yielding is honorable.*
34. **[3.8]** *misleading · med*: “And when love is of this disinterested sort”. Many listeners hear "disinterested" as "uninterested", which makes nonsense of love. Here it means unselfish, not done for gain.
35. **[3.8]** *ear · med*: “but in every other case there is equal disgrace in being deceived or not”. Heard aloud, this is a logic knot. The example that follows untangles it.
36. **[3.8]** *archaic · low*: “and is then disappointed of his gains”. *Understood: "disappointed of" means cheated out of.*
37. **[3.8]** *archaic / ear · high*: “he would give himself up to anyone's 'uses base' for the sake of money”. A listener can't hear the quotation marks, and "anyone's uses base" sounds garbled aloud. The reversed, archaic phrase looks like a quotation, but the text doesn't say where it comes from. *Understood: he would let anyone use him for money.* **[PRIOR]** I can make sense of it only because I am familiar with old-fashioned English.
38. **[3.9]** *misleading · low*: “he will do anything for anyone with a view to virtue and self-improvement”. "Do anything for anyone" sounds as indiscriminate as the money case he has just condemned. *I understood it as a deliberate mirror of that case.*
39. **[3.10]** *ear / opaque · med*: “Pausanias came to a pause — there's a neat little jingle for you, in the balanced style the wise teachers of speaking have taught me.” Who is "me"? After ten paragraphs of Pausanias saying "I", the narrator suddenly jokes in his own voice. Only the next sentence, “Aristodemus said that Aristophanes was due to speak next”, suggests that the narrator is Apollodorus. "The wise teachers of speaking" and "balanced style" are unexplained. A listener might link the teachers to [1.46]'s “professional teachers”. The pun itself works (see What works). **[PRIOR]** I read the line as Apollodorus teasing the fashionable teachers of public speaking because I know the period. The text doesn't say so.
40. **[3.10]** *opaque · low*: “who was reclining on the couch below him”. It is unclear what "below" means in a seating order. *Understood: on the next couch, and so next in turn.* **[PRIOR]** A little background on dining custom helped here.
41. **[3.0]–[3.9]** *format · low*: “Pausanias began:”. The speech has no quotation marks, unlike all the dialogue in chapter 1, and it starts in the middle of a paragraph. That is fine when heard, but in print the change of speaker is easy to miss. It is also why the "me" in [3.10] is confusing.

### 3. What works

- **[3.0]** “If there were only one Love, what you said would be fine — but since there are more Loves than one, we should have started by deciding which of them we are praising.” A clear opening that starts an argument. The listener knows at once what Pausanias is going to do.
- **[3.0]** The quick glosses “she is the daughter of Uranus, god of the heavens” and “the Aphrodite of all the people” are just enough to keep the myth moving.
- **[3.1]** “Take, for example, what we are doing right now — drinking, singing, and talking.” and “Done well, they are good; done badly, they are evil.” The principle is tied to the scene the listener has just been in. This is the easiest-to-grasp idea in the speech.
- **[3.2]** “not to catch them in their inexperience, deceive them, make fools of them, or run from one to another”. The bad lover is summed up in four concrete verbs.
- **[3.4]** “the self-seeking of the rulers and the cowardice of the ruled” is set against “the mental laziness of those who hold that opinion”. A balanced, memorable diagnosis.
- **[3.5]** “sleep on a mat at the door, and endure a servitude worse than any slave's” and “for there is no such thing as a lover's oath” are vivid and funny. The turn to the other side, “Fathers forbid their sons to talk with their lovers”, sets up the paradox cleanly. This is the clearest stretch of the chapter.
- **[3.6]** “They are honorable to whoever pursues them honorably, and dishonorable to whoever pursues them dishonorably.” and “That is why it encourages lovers to pursue and the beloved to flee”. The paradox is resolved as a test, and it is easy to follow aloud. “when the bloom of youth he desired is over, he takes wing and flies away, despite all his words and promises” is the most vivid image in the speech.
- **[3.7]** “time is the true test, here as in most other things” and “no generous friendship ever sprang from them” are plain and quotable.
- **[3.8]–[3.9]** The paired test cases (the lover thought rich who turns out poor, and the man thought good who turns out a scoundrel), capped by “If he is deceived, his error is a noble one.”, make the argument concrete at its most abstract point.
- **[3.9]** The close, “All other loves are the offspring of the other goddess, the Common one.”, loops back to the two goddesses. “To you, Phaedrus, I offer this contribution of mine in praise of Love” echoes [1.46].
- **[3.10]** “Pausanias came to a pause”. The pun works by ear and shows the "balanced style" rather than just naming it.
- **[3.10]–[3.11]** The hiccups and Eryximachus's cure (“tickle your nose with something and sneeze”, “'Now get on with it.'”) are comic, concrete and modern, a welcome breather after a dense speech.

### 4. Rating: 3 / 5

*What limits it most is a premise the text never states.* Repetition and good examples carry the main line through: there are two Loves, one for the body and one for the soul; Athens's mixed signals are a test; and a beloved should yield only for the sake of virtue. But the text never says outright that "lover" and "beloved" are an adult man and a teenage boy (**[PRIOR]**, see point 6). It also never ties "service" back to "yielding", and "yielding" itself is explained only once, in the middle of a sentence ([3.6]). Four more problems add to this: listeners can't hear the difference between Love and love; the two-Aphrodites premise is taken for granted; the survey of regions in [3.3] is crowded; and [3.7]–[3.8] is a run of archaic, ear-hostile phrasing ("disinterested", "uses base", the 100-word sentence). On their own, [3.4]–[3.6] would score 4, and [3.3] and [3.8] would score 2.

## The five biggest obstacles

1. **The social arrangement behind Pausanias's speech is never stated (ch. 3).** The text never says that "lover" and "beloved" mean an adult man and an adolescent male. Nor does it say plainly what the pursuit is for, beyond the single mid-sentence gloss on "yielding" in [3.6]. The age words keep shifting (“youths”, “boys”, “young people”, “young men”, “young boys”, “the love of youth”, [3.1]–[3.8]), and so do the euphemisms (“these connections”, “such attachments”, “such practices”, “virtuous service”, [3.2]–[3.7]). The jolt [3.2] gives a modern listener is left unframed: young boys should be off-limits because “much noble enthusiasm may be wasted on them”. **[PRIOR]** I understood the arrangement only from outside knowledge.
2. **The story within a story and the shifting "I" (ch. 1).** The opening line “As for what you're asking about” ([1.0]) has an unnamed addressee. The chain of reports has four links. [1.7] jumps from past to present in mid-paragraph without warning. Script labels (“COMPANION:”) appear suddenly in [1.8]–[1.11] and then vanish. In “I replied, Aristodemus said” (at [1.14]), "I" is suddenly Aristodemus, and an unidentified "me" turns up in [3.10]. The two narrators also have similar names, Apollodorus and Aristodemus.
3. **Love the god and love the feeling, and the two Aphrodites, are taken as given (ch. 3, set up in [1.46]).** In [3.0]–[3.1], a listener can't hear the capital L in “more Loves than one” or “the Love who works alongside her”. The claim “Love is inseparable from Aphrodite” is simply asserted, and so is the idea of a goddess “who has no mother”. The reasoning from birth to behavior (born of the male only, so she loves males; older, so “nothing lewd”) is myth-logic with no explanation.
4. **Names, places and period terms go unexplained, and several actively mislead (both chapters).** Unexplained names and places: “the deme of Cydathenaeum”, “Phoenix, the son of Philip”, “Melanippe”, Agamemnon and Menelaus, “Lacedaemon”, “Elis and Boeotia”, “Ionia”, and “Aristogeiton's love and Harmodius's constancy”. Misleading terms: “barbarians” will be heard as savages, “gymnastics” as the sport, and “tutor” as a teacher. Other terms stay opaque: “our own Athenian tyrants”, “freeborn women”, “the flute-girl”, “victory sacrifice” and “a hymn to the god”. Chapter 1 names more than twenty people and gods, and Pausanias gets no introduction at all.
5. **Archaic wording and syntax that defeat the ear (both chapters, worst in [3.7]–[3.8]).** Examples: “anyone's 'uses base'”, “disappointed of his gains”, “some other particular of virtue”, “this disinterested sort”, “his gracious loved one”, “supplicate” ([3.5]) and “dandy” ([1.12]). The main offenders are the 100-word sentence in [3.8] and the proverb-and-Homer passage in [1.15]–[1.24]. That passage gives three versions of one sentence and a chain of better/worse reversals, and "good" quietly becomes "wise". Its quotations are split across paragraphs, leaving a stray “who',”, and the same split happens again in [1.44]–[1.46]. Other logic knots make things worse: “if he fails he is blamed”, “philosophy would bitterly condemn”, “equal disgrace in being deceived or not”.
