## Chapter 1: The Gathering

### Argument summary

**Apollodorus, the frame story (¶1.0–1.11)**
- He can retell the speeches because two days earlier he went through them with Glaucon on the road into the city.
- The dinner was long ago: "When we were still boys", the day after Agathon's first win with a tragedy. So he wasn't there.
- His source is Aristodemus, an eyewitness and devoted follower of Socrates, and Socrates confirmed parts of the account.
- Talking about philosophy is the only talk worth anything. Rich men and businessmen "think you're doing something when in reality you're doing nothing."
- They pity him and he pities them: "But what you only think about me, I know for certain about you."
- The Companion calls him "Apollodorus the madman". He accepts the name, saying his views are exactly why people call him mad.
- **Can't reconstruct:** in "No other evidence is needed", I can't tell whether he is being sarcastic or conceding the point.

**Socrates on turning up uninvited (¶1.13–1.24)**
- **Claim:** Aristodemus may come to Agathon's dinner without an invitation.
- **Step 1:** the proverb says good men go uninvited to the feasts of inferior men. Socrates changes it to "the good go uninvited to the feasts of the good".
- **Step 2:** Homer broke the proverb even more. The "fainthearted" Menelaus came uninvited to the feast of Agamemnon, "the bravest of men", which is the worse man going to the better.
- Aristodemus fears he'll be the Menelaus of the evening, so he will say Socrates invited him.
- Socrates replies that two people together will think of an excuse.
- **Doesn't quite follow for me:**
  - Why "the good" is the right label for Agathon's house. The pun on his name can't be seen in the text.
  - How Homer's example supports Socrates's version, rather than just breaking the proverb in a different way.

**Socrates on wisdom passing by touch (¶1.34)**
- If wisdom flowed from a fuller person to an emptier one by touch, like water creeping along a wool thread, he would treasure the seat next to Agathon.
- His own wisdom is like a dream. Agathon's shone out before thirty thousand Greeks.
- It is irony, and the text says so outright. Agathon answers, "You're mocking me."

**Eryximachus's proposal, Phaedrus's complaint (¶1.36–1.46) and Socrates's support (¶1.47)**
- Most of the guests are hungover (Pausanias, Aristophanes). Agathon can't drink much. The weak drinkers are relieved, and Socrates doesn't mind either way.
- Speaking as a physician, Eryximachus says heavy drinking is harmful, so each guest drinks only as much as he likes.
- He asks for the flute-girl to be sent away so they can talk instead.
- Phaedrus's complaint: poets write hymns to other gods, sophists praise Heracles, and someone even praised salt, but no one has properly praised Love.
- So each guest, going left to right, gives the best speech he can in praise of Love. Phaedrus goes first, because he sits first on the left and is "the father of the idea".
- Socrates agrees, since love is "the only subject I claim to understand". He expects the others to agree too, though it is hard on the last speakers.

## Chapter 2: Phaedrus's Speech

### Argument summary
- **Claim:** Love is a great god: the eldest, the noblest and the mightiest. He is the chief giver of virtue in life and of happiness after death.
- **Why he is the eldest:**
  - No poet or prose writer records his parents.
  - Hesiod (Chaos came first, then Earth and Love), Parmenides and Acusilaus all place him first.
  - **Logic:** "no record of his parents" is an argument from silence. And the Parmenides line itself gives Love a "maker" (Generation), which seems to undercut the "no parents" point.
- **His greatest gift:** nothing is better for a young man than a virtuous older lover, and nothing better for the lover than the young man he loves. Love teaches a sense of honor and shame better than family, public honors or wealth can.
- **Shame before the beloved:**
  - A lover caught doing something shameful is more pained to be seen by his beloved than by anyone else.
  - So a city or army made of lovers would be the best of all, and even a handful of them could conquer the world.
- **Love gives the courage to die, and not only to men:** Alcestis died for her husband when his own parents would not, and the gods let her come back from the dead.
- **The contrast, Orpheus:**
  - He didn't dare to die. Instead he slipped into Hades alive and was given only a phantom of his wife.
  - Later he was killed by women, as a punishment.
- **Achilles:**
  - He was the beloved: younger and more beautiful than Patroclus. Aeschylus was wrong to make Patroclus the beloved.
  - He died to avenge Patroclus, his lover, knowing it meant his own death.
  - So the gods ranked him above Alcestis and sent him to the Islands of the Blessed, because they honor a beloved's devotion even more than a lover's.
  - **Logic, can't reconstruct:** "for the lover is more divine, since he is inspired by a god". Why would that make the beloved's love more admirable?

### Unclear points

**¶2.0 → ¶2.4**
- **ear:** "Phaedrus began by declaring that Love is a mighty god" … "For I know of no greater blessing for a young man"
  - Reported speech slides into first-person speech with no quotation marks or other cue.
  - By ear, this "I" could be Apollodorus.

**¶2.1**
- **archaic (mild):** "'First Chaos came, and then broad-bosomed Earth, the everlasting seat of all that is, and Love.'"
  - Poetic, but the next sentence rescues it: "In other words, after Chaos, these two came into being: Earth and Love."

**¶2.2**
- **logic:** "the philosopher-poet Parmenides sings of Generation, the power that brings things into being, as the maker of Love"
  - If Generation made Love, isn't that a parent? This seems to contradict "there is no record of his parents" just three sentences earlier.

**¶2.3**
- **misleading / ear:** "'First in the train of gods, he fashioned Love.'"
  - By ear, "train" suggests a railway; here it means a procession or sequence.
  - "He" has no clear referent. Is it Parmenides, or Generation, which was just called "the power"?

**¶2.4**
- **custom, understood but not framed:** "a virtuous lover — an older man who loves and courts him"
  - The gloss is clear and necessary.
  - But the custom itself, an older man courting a youth with public approval, arrives with no framing at all. A modern teenager will be jolted.
- **vague:** "or letting another man dishonor him because he is too cowardly to resist"
  - Dishonor him how: by insult, or by assault? I took it as "being humiliated without fighting back".

**¶2.6**
- **opaque:** "She was willing to lay down her life for her husband when no one else would, although he had a father and a mother."
  - Why did anyone have to die for him?
  - The premise, that he could escape death if someone died in his place, is **[prior knowledge]**. Without it, the story is puzzling.
- **opaque / tone:** "because he showed no spirit. He was only a harp-player"
  - The contempt for a musician is never explained.
  - A reader who knows the familiar version, where Orpheus "looked back", will think the edition got the myth wrong. This is Phaedrus's own version, but nothing signals that.
- **opaque:** "the gods later had him put to death at the hands of women"
  - Which women? And why is dying at their hands a punishment?
  - Both answers are **[prior knowledge]**: the Maenads, and the shame of being killed by women.

**¶2.7**
- **ear:** "his true love toward Patroclus, who was his lover and not his beloved"
  - By ear, the run of "his" words is a tangle.
  - The gloss that follows fixes it: "that is, Patroclus was the older man who loved him, and Achilles the younger man he loved."
- **ear (mild):** "The idea that Patroclus was the beloved is a foolish mistake made by the tragic poet Aeschylus"
  - Why their ages and roles matter only becomes clear a sentence later. It's a small detour.
- **logic:** "for the lover is more divine, since he is inspired by a god"
  - This step doesn't follow for me; see the argument summary above.
- **misleading:** "and dared to die for him — not only to defend him, but even after Patroclus was already dead"
  - This sounds as if Achilles died defending Patroclus.
  - The real point is that he died after his friend was already dead, when his death could no longer save anyone. The sentence muddles this.
- **opaque (mild):** "sent him to the Islands of the Blessed"
  - "Honored him" lets me infer a paradise for heroes, but the place itself is never explained.

### What works
- **¶2.4:** "What do I mean? The sense of honor and dishonor, without which neither states nor individuals ever do any good or great work." A perfect signpost for a listener.
- **¶2.4–2.5:** the army of lovers. Concrete and persuasive:
  - "even a mere handful of them would overcome the world"
  - "what lover would not rather be seen by the whole of mankind than by his beloved when he was deserting his post or throwing away his weapons?"
  - "even the worst coward would become an inspired hero"
- **¶2.6:** Alcestis "made them seem like strangers to their own son". A vivid image.
- **¶2.6:** Orpheus is a clear contrast to Alcestis: he "schemed to find a way into Hades while still alive."
- **¶2.7:**
  - The explicit gloss of the lover and beloved roles.
  - Hector glossed as "the Trojan who had killed Patroclus".
  - The closing sentence sums up the whole speech neatly.
- **Short glosses throughout** are exactly right for a listener: "the poet Hesiod", "the philosopher-poet Parmenides", "Acusilaus, an early prose writer on the origins of the gods", "the tragic poet Aeschylus".

### Rating: 4/5
A strong speech that is easy to follow, with vivid examples. It is held back by:
- the Parmenides lines
- the unmarked switch into "I"
- two steps in the Achilles passage that I can't reconstruct
- myths that assume background the text doesn't give (Alcestis, Orpheus)

## Chapter 3: Pausanias's Speech

### Argument summary
- **Correction to Phaedrus:**
  - You can't praise Love as one thing, because there are two Loves.
  - Love is inseparable from Aphrodite, and there are two Aphrodites. The Heavenly one is older, has no mother and is the daughter of Uranus. The Common one is younger, the daughter of Zeus and Dione.
- **Method:**
  - No action is good or bad in itself, whether drinking, singing or talking. It depends on how it's done.
  - So only love with a noble purpose deserves praise.
- **Common Love:**
  - It is of the body rather than the soul, and aims at women and youths without distinction.
  - It picks foolish partners and wants only to get what it wants, so it does good and evil at random.
  - Its mother was born of both male and female.
- **Heavenly Love:**
  - Its mother came from the male only, and because she is older she has no wantonness. So this Love turns to males: young men whose minds are developing, not boys.
  - It means to stay faithful for life.
  - Love of young boys should be banned by law, because their future is uncertain and devotion may be wasted on them.
  - **Logic:** "older, so no wantonness" and "born from the male, so loves males" are asserted, not argued.
- **Customs elsewhere:**
  - Elis and Boeotia simply allow these relationships. People there can't speak well, so lovers don't want the trouble of persuading.
  - Ionia, and lands under "the barbarians", condemn them, because tyrants fear the bonds they create. Harmodius and Aristogeiton brought down Athens's own tyrants.
  - Both extremes have bad causes: in Ionia, the self-interest of rulers and the cowardice of their subjects; in Elis and Boeotia, mental laziness.
- **The puzzle of Athenian custom:**
  - A lover is encouraged and forgiven everything: begging, sleeping at the beloved's door, even broken oaths.
  - Yet fathers guard their sons, and friends mock them.
  - **Resolution:** the custom is a test. Lovers pursue and the beloved flees, and time shows which kind of lover each one is.
- **Rules for the beloved:**
  - Giving in quickly is shameful, and so is giving in for money or power.
  - The only honorable way to yield is for the sake of virtue: when the lover's readiness to serve and the beloved's wish to become wise and good "meet in one".
- **The deception test and the conclusion:**
  - A young man who yields for money and is fooled is disgraced anyway, because he has shown he would sell himself.
  - One who yields for virtue and is fooled is still noble.
  - **Conclusion:** Heavenly love is precious to individuals and to cities. All other loves come from the Common Aphrodite.

### Unclear points

**¶3.0**
- **opaque:** "The elder, who has no mother, is called the heavenly Aphrodite — she is the daughter of Uranus."
  - Uranus is never explained, so the reason she is called "heavenly" is lost. Uranus means Heaven, or the Sky: **[prior knowledge]**.
  - By ear, a teenager will think of the planet.
  - How a goddess can have no mother isn't explained here.
- **opaque:** "The younger, the daughter of Zeus and Dione, we call Common"
  - Dione is unknown.
  - "Common", meaning ordinary or vulgar, works, and ¶3.1 confirms it.
- **archaic (mild):** "then I'll try to hymn that praiseworthy one in a manner worthy of him"

**¶3.0 and ¶3.1**
- **inconsistency (mild):** "the Love who works alongside her" versus "The Love who is the offspring of the Common Aphrodite"
  - Is this Love her companion or her son?

**¶3.1**
- **misleading:** "It is the love the meaner sort of men feel"
  - A modern ear hears "meaner" as "crueler". It means "baser" or "lower".
- **vague / euphemism:** "The most foolish beings are the objects of this love, which cares only about getting what it wants and never about achieving its end nobly"
  - "Beings" is an odd word here.
  - "Getting what it wants" and "achieving its end" are coy; I assume they mean sex.
  - The text doesn't say why such lovers would choose foolish partners.
- **ear:** "But the offspring of the Heavenly Aphrodite comes from a mother in whose birth the female had no part — she is from the male only."
  - Hard to untangle aloud. It means this Love's mother, Aphrodite, was born without a female parent.
- **archaic / logic:** "and because the goddess is older, there is nothing of wantonness in her"
  - "Wantonness" is archaic.
  - Why being older removes it is never explained.
- **archaic:** "partaking of both"
- **tone / opaque:** "anyone can recognize the pure enthusiasts by the very character of their attachments"
  - "Enthusiasts" sounds like hobbyists. It means people inspired by the Heavenly Love.

**¶3.2**
- **tone:** "For they love not boys, but intelligent beings whose reason is beginning to develop"
  - "Intelligent beings" sounds like science fiction, though the meaning (young men starting to think for themselves) is clear.
- **misleading:** "not to catch them in their inexperience, deceive them, play the fool with them, or run from one to another"
  - Today "play the fool with them" means "act silly together". The meaning needed is "make fools of them".
- **opaque:** "just as we try to restrain them from fixing their affections on freeborn women"
  - Which restriction does he mean? And the implied contrast with women who are not free is left hanging.
  - That there were laws protecting citizens' wives and daughters is **[prior knowledge]**.
- **misleading (mild):** "some have been led to deny the lawfulness of such attachments altogether"
  - "Lawfulness" suggests legal status. It seems to mean "decency".
- **archaic (mild):** "bring reproach upon love" and "nothing done decorously and lawfully can justly be censured"

**¶3.3**
- **opaque:** "Now here in Athens, and in Lacedaemon"
  - Lacedaemon is Sparta, which I know only from **[prior knowledge]**. "Sparta" would be understood instantly.
- **opaque (mild):** "In Elis and Boeotia, and in countries that have no gift for eloquence"
  - The names are unknown, but the text describes these places, so it's fine.
  - The joke (they can't talk, so they skip the persuading) is a stretch, but it lands.
- **opaque / misleading:** "In Ionia, on the other hand, and in other places generally subject to the barbarians"
  - Ionia is unknown.
  - To a modern ear, "barbarians" means savages. Here it means non-Greek rulers, namely the Persians: **[prior knowledge]**.
- **misleading:** "shares the evil reputation attached to philosophy and gymnastics"
  - "Gymnastics" suggests the modern sport. It means athletic training in the gymnasium, where free men met, and that is why tyrants would fear it. The text doesn't say any of this.
- **tone:** "poor in spirit"
  - A biblical echo that jars.
- **opaque:** "For Aristogeiton's love and Harmodius's constancy proved strong enough to bring down their power."
  - Who were these two, and what did they do?
  - All a listener can infer is "a pair of lovers overthrew the tyrants". Some listeners will also be surprised that Athens had tyrants at all.
  - The history is **[prior knowledge]**.

**¶3.4**
- **misleading (mild):** "if he succeeds he is praised, and if he fails he is blamed"
  - "Blamed" seems to contradict the "enormous encouragement" of the same sentence.
  - *I understood:* society treats winning the beloved like a victory, and failing like a defeat.
- **tone / opaque:** "many strange things that philosophy would bitterly condemn"
  - Why philosophy, and not public opinion? An odd choice of word.
- **archaic (mild):** "is owing to the mental laziness"

**¶3.5**
- **misleading:** "no enemy will accuse him of meanness or flattery"
  - "Meanness" here means servility, not unkindness.
- **archaic:** "he alone may swear and forswear himself"
  - A teenager won't know "forswear". The next clause rescues it: "there is no such thing as a lover's oath".
- **ear:** "But then again — when fathers forbid their sons to speak with their lovers and place them under a tutor whose job is to prevent any such thing, and when their companions and peers taunt them for anything of the sort they observe, and their elders refuse to silence these reproaches or rebuke them — anyone reflecting on all this would, on the contrary, conclude that we hold these practices to be utterly disgraceful."
  - The main clause only arrives after three stacked "when" clauses.
  - "Rebuke them" is ambiguous; presumably it means the friends doing the taunting.
  - "But then again" and "on the contrary" save it.

**¶3.6**
- **opaque, and central to the whole speech:** "There is dishonor in yielding to the bad, or yielding in a bad way; but there is honor in yielding to the good"
  - "Yield" is the key word of the speech. It comes with "gratify", "service", "show any kindness" and "gives himself to a lover", and none of them is ever explained.
  - What exactly the beloved grants is left to guesswork, so the moral argument can't really be weighed.
  - I read it as including a sexual relationship, but only from the "love of the body" context plus **[prior knowledge]**.
- **misleading (mild):** "and wants us to yield to the one sort and avoid the other"
  - Why "us"? Pausanias is a lover, not a beloved. Perhaps he means "the young".

**¶3.7**
- **ear / misleading:** "there is dishonor in being overcome by the love of money, wealth, or political power — whether a man is frightened into surrender by the fear of losing them, or, having tasted the benefits of money and political corruption, cannot rise above their seductions"
  - This reads like a general sermon against greed.
  - The actual point is buried: a beloved who gives in to a lover because he is bullied or bribed.
  - "Fear of losing them": losing what?
  - "Political corruption" sounds jarringly modern.

**¶3.8**
- **archaic:** "whether in wisdom or in some other particular of virtue"
- **ambiguous:** "These two customs — the love of youth, and the practice of philosophy and virtue generally — ought to meet in one"
  - "The love of youth" could mean love felt when you are young. Here it means love of youths.
  - The same paragraph then brings in a second pair, the lover's law and the beloved's law ("each following his own law").
  - It then says "the two laws of love are fulfilled and meet in one". I couldn't tell which pair is meant.
- **ear, the hardest sentence in the packet:** "For when lover and beloved come together, each following his own law: the lover thinking he is right to do any service he can for his gracious loved one, and the beloved thinking he is right to show any kindness he can to the one who is making him wise and good — the first capable of communicating wisdom and virtue, the second seeking to acquire them for the sake of his education and wisdom — then, when the two laws of love are fulfilled and meet in one, and only then, may the beloved yield honorably to the lover."
  - A colon and two dashes come before the main verb, which arrives at the very end.
- **tone / opaque:** "his gracious loved one"
  - "Gracious" means the one who grants favors, but that isn't apparent.
- **misleading:** "And when love is of this disinterested sort"
  - "Disinterested" is often heard as "uninterested". Here it means unselfish.
- **archaic:** "and is then disappointed of his gains"
- **archaic / opaque:** "he would give himself up to anyone's 'uses base' for the sake of money"
  - The word order is inverted, and the quotation marks can't be heard, so aloud it sounds garbled.
  - *I understood:* he would let anyone use him for anything, for money.

**¶3.10**
- **ear:** "there's a neat little jingle for you, in the balanced style the wise teachers of speaking have taught me"
  - The narrator suddenly says "me"; I assume it's Apollodorus.
  - The joke works, but the switch of voice is abrupt.
- **opaque (mild):** "who was reclining on the couch below him"
  - This relies on the seating custom. I understood it as "next in order".

**¶3.11**
- **punctuation / ear:** "I'll do both, said Eryximachus. … I'll do as you prescribe, said Aristophanes. Now get on with it."
  - There are no quotation marks here, although ¶3.10 used them.
  - Two speakers share one paragraph, so on the page the change of speaker is easy to miss.

### What works
- **¶3.0:** the opening objection is crisp: "If there were only one Love, what you said would be fine — but since there are more Loves than one…"
- **¶3.1:** "Take, for example, what we are doing right now — drinking, singing, and talking." A perfect, concrete example.
- **¶3.3:** why tyrants hate these bonds is clear political logic: "Rulers' interests require that their subjects be poor in spirit and that no strong bond of friendship or fellowship form among them".
- **¶3.5:** vivid details: "sleep on a mat at the door, and endure a servitude worse than any slave's" and "there is no such thing as a lover's oath".
- **¶3.6:**
  - "So when the bloom of youth he desired is over, he takes wing and flies away, despite all his words and promises." The best image in the chapter.
  - "it encourages lovers to pursue and the beloved to flee: it tests both" resolves the Athenian puzzle cleanly.
- **¶3.7:** "time is the true test, here as in most other things."
- **¶3.9:** "If he is deceived, his error is a noble one." A memorable turn.
- **¶3.10–3.11:**
  - "Pausanias came to a pause" is a pun that works in English. Because the text calls it a jingle, the listener gets it.
  - The hiccups are a great comic reset.
  - The hiccup cures are funny and sound modern ("Now get on with it").

### Rating: 3/5
The outline of the argument is easy to follow: two Loves; honor depends on how love is pursued; Athenian custom tests lovers; yield only for virtue. What limits it:
- The key act, "yield/gratify", is never named.
- ¶3.3 is packed with places and people who get no gloss.
- Old words that now mean something else: "meaner", "meanness", "play the fool", "disinterested", "wantonness", "forswear", "'uses base'".
- The two very long sentences in ¶3.5 and ¶3.8.

## Chapter 4: Eryximachus's Speech

### Argument summary
- **Claim:**
  - Pausanias was right that love is double, but it is not found only in human souls. It is in all bodies, all plants, everything.
  - So Love is universal and all-powerful.
- **Medicine:**
  - The body has a healthy love and a diseased love.
  - Pausanias said we should gratify good men and not bad ones. In the same way, a doctor gratifies the healthy parts of the body and discourages the diseased ones.
  - Medicine is knowledge of the body's loves. The best doctor separates them, turns the bad into the good, and reconciles opposites: hot and cold, bitter and sweet, moist and dry.
  - Asclepius founded medicine this way, and physical training and farming fall under the same rule.
- **Music:**
  - Heraclitus said the One is "united by disunion", like the bow and the lyre.
  - Eryximachus says that is wrong as stated, because harmony can't be discord.
  - His correction: high and low notes that once disagreed have been reconciled by the art of music. Rhythm is the same, with short and long beats.
  - **Logic:** how notes "once disagreed" is asserted, not argued.
- **Theory versus practice:**
  - In harmony and rhythm considered purely, love is not yet double.
  - In composing, performing and teaching (which is education), you must choose the heavenly love of Urania. The common love of Polyhymnia must be used with caution, the way a doctor lets a gourmet enjoy food without falling ill.
- **The seasons:**
  - When hot, cold, moist and dry are in balance, they bring health and plenty.
  - "The unruly love" brings plague, frost, hail and blight.
  - Astronomy is the study of these loves in relation to the heavens and the seasons.
- **Religion:**
  - Sacrifice and divination exist to keep the good love and cure the bad.
  - Impiety comes from honoring the wrong love, whether toward gods or parents, the living or the dead.
  - Divination is the peacemaker between gods and men.
- **Conclusion:** the love that aims at the good, "perfected in company with temperance and justice", is the mightiest. It is the source of our happiness and of friendship with the gods and with each other. Aristophanes can fill in anything he left out.
- **Coda:**
  - Aristophanes mocks the theory: does the body's harmony love sneezing?
  - Eryximachus threatens to heckle his speech.
  - Aristophanes hopes to be laughed with, not laughed at.

### Unclear points

**¶4.0**
- **ear / logic:** "The human body contains these two kinds of love. Everyone agrees that they are different and unlike, and because they are unlike, what they love and desire is unlike too: what is healthy desires one thing, and what is diseased desires another."
  - "They" drifts from the two kinds of love to the healthy and diseased parts of the body, without saying so.
  - *I understood:* the good love is the desire of the healthy parts, and the bad love is the desire of the diseased parts.

**¶4.0 and ¶4.3**
- **euphemism carried over from ch. 3:** "the good and healthy elements should be gratified" and "our duty to gratify the temperate"
  - This inherits Pausanias's unexplained "gratify".
  - It's fine for the body. In music I can't picture what it means; see ¶4.3 below.

**¶4.1**
- **mild:** "The best physician is the one who can separate the good love from the bad, or turn one into the other."
  - Taken literally, this works in both directions. Surely he only means turning bad into good.
- **misleading:** "And not only medicine in all its branches, but also the arts of physical training and farming, are under his dominion."
  - The nearest "he" is Asclepius, so it sounds as if Asclepius rules over farming.
  - Surely Love is meant. I worked that out only from the logic of the speech and **[prior knowledge]**.
- **opaque:** "I suppose that must have been what Heraclitus meant, although he did not put it accurately. For he says that the One is united by disunion"
  - Heraclitus gets no introduction, although Parmenides did.
  - "The One" means nothing to a newcomer.
  - The bow-and-lyre gloss helps a lot: "string and frame pull against each other and so hold together".
- **ear (mild):** "like the harmony of the bow and the lyre"
  - By ear, "bow" could be a violin bow. The gloss makes it clear.

**¶4.2**
- **logic:** "harmony is made of differing notes, of higher and lower pitch, that once disagreed but have now been reconciled by the art of music"
  - How can fixed notes "once disagree"?
  - It is offered as the correction to Heraclitus, but it is asserted rather than argued.
- **opaque (mild):** "which is what we call education, since that is how the young are trained"
  - Is composing and performing songs really education? The gloss helps.
  - That Greek schooling centered on music is **[prior knowledge]**.

**¶4.3**
- **misleading:** "At that point we must retell the old story of the beautiful and heavenly love"
  - "Old story" suggests a myth is coming. It means that Pausanias's argument applies again.
- **opaque:** "the love that comes from Urania, the beautiful and heavenly Muse" and "Polyhymnia, the common Muse of many songs"
  - What a Muse is, is **[prior knowledge]**.
  - The glosses "heavenly" and "of many songs" carry some of the meaning.
- **ear / abstract:** "It tells of our duty to gratify the temperate, and to gratify those who are not yet temperate only so that they may become temperate, and to preserve their love."
  - In music, who are "the temperate": listeners, performers, or the tunes themselves?
  - A teenager may not know the word "temperate" at all.
  - The hardest sentence in the chapter.
- **archaic:** "without breeding licentiousness"

**¶4.4**
- **mild:** "from the excesses and disorders of these elements of love"
  - "Elements of love" is an odd phrase.
- **tone:** "is called astronomy"
  - The sentence is clear, but it sounds strange to modern ears: astronomy as the study of love?
  - It is plainly Eryximachus's own theory, so it's acceptable.

**¶4.9**
- **ear / archaic:** "'Do you expect to shoot your bolt and escape, Aristophanes? Well, perhaps if you are very careful and bear in mind that you will be called to account, I may be induced to let you off.'"
  - There is no speaker tag; it is Eryximachus, by elimination.
  - "Shoot your bolt" is an old idiom a teenager won't know.
  - "I may be induced to let you off" is stiff.

### What works
- **¶4.0:** "Since Pausanias made a fine beginning but a lame ending" and "I will begin with medicine, to honor my own art". His self-regard gives him character and a light comic touch.
- **¶4.1:**
  - "a physician who knows how to root out love and how to plant it" is a clear gardening metaphor.
  - Hot and cold, bitter and sweet, moist and dry are concrete opposites.
  - "That is what our poet friends here tell us, and I believe them." A nice nod to the poets in the room.
- **¶4.2:**
  - "harmony is consonance, that is, notes sounding together; and consonance is a kind of agreement … you cannot harmonize what disagrees." A model gloss for a listener: slow and repetitive in a good way.
  - "there love has not yet become double: it has not yet split into a good kind and a bad kind". A helpful restatement.
- **¶4.3:** "just as in my own art it is no small task to regulate a gourmet's appetites". A modern analogy that is instantly clear.
- **¶4.4:**
  - Frost, hail, blight and plague make "the unruly love" concrete.
  - Divination is well glossed: "the reading of signs from the gods, the art through which gods and men communicate".
- **¶4.5:** the conclusion is clear, and "for I see that you are rid of your hiccups" is a neat handover to Aristophanes.
- **¶4.6–4.8:** the banter lands:
  - "I wonder whether the harmony of the body has a love for such noises and ticklings"
  - "instead of people laughing with me … they will only laugh at me"

### Rating: 4/5
This is the most orderly speech so far: medicine, then music, then the seasons, then religion, with good glosses along the way. What limits it:
- "the One is united by disunion"
- "his dominion", which points to Asclepius instead of Love
- the abstract passage about the Muses and "gratify the temperate"
- the final line, which has no speaker tag and uses archaic phrasing

## Notes across all four chapters

- **The register is uneven.**
  - Natural modern English ("Now get on with it", "a lame ending", "gotten better") sits next to much older phrasing: "I move in the next place", "do not therefore disturb him", "disappointed of his gains", "'uses base'", "partaking of both", "is owing to".
  - The old-fashioned patches cluster in ¶1.37–1.48, ¶3.1–3.8, ¶4.3 and ¶4.9.
- **Speakers are marked inconsistently.**
  - Most speech has quotation marks, but some has none:
    - ¶1.17 and ¶1.19
    - Phaedrus from ¶2.4, where he speaks in the first person
    - Pausanias from ¶3.0
    - ¶3.11
  - ¶1.8–1.11 use script labels, and ¶4.9 has no speaker tag at all.
  - Someone reading aloud can cover for some of this, but a reader of the page stumbles.
- **Hard names for the ear:** Apollodorus, Cydathenaeum, Eryximachus, Acumenus, Myrrhinusian, Melanippe, Prodicus, Acusilaus, Oeagrus, Lacedaemon, Boeotia, Aristogeiton, Harmodius, Asclepius, Heraclitus, Polyhymnia.
- **Glosses that work (keep doing this):** sophists, Dionysus, Aphrodite, Hesiod, Parmenides, Acusilaus, Generation, Aeschylus, Hector, lover and beloved, consonance, divination, gourmet.
- **Glosses that are missing:**
  - deme and Myrrhinusian
  - the pun on Agathon's name in the proverb
  - why Alcestis had to die, and how Orpheus was killed
  - the Islands of the Blessed
  - Uranus and Dione
  - Lacedaemon, Ionia, "barbarians", "gymnastics"
  - Harmodius and Aristogeiton
  - Heraclitus and "the One"
  - what a Muse is
  - above all, what "yield" and "gratify" mean
- **By ear, "Love" the god and "love" the feeling sound the same.**
  - The text establishes "the god Love" early (¶1.46), so this mostly works.
  - Pausanias's "two Loves" will be heard as "two kinds of love", which is roughly right anyway.

### Understood only through prior knowledge (consolidated)
- **¶1.7 and ¶1.41:** "deme" and "Myrrhinusian" as names of districts.
- **¶1.16–1.18:** that Agathon's name means "good", which is what makes the proverb a pun.
- **¶1.0:** that Alcibiades, named as a speaker, only arrives later.
- **¶1.43:** three drinking-party customs: a set drinking pace, hired flute-girls, and the women kept in separate rooms.
- **¶1.47:** that Agathon and Pausanias were a couple.
- **¶2.6:**
  - why Alcestis had to die: someone could die in her husband's place
  - the familiar "looked back" version of Orpheus, which conflicts with this one
  - that the women who killed Orpheus were the Maenads
- **¶2.7:** the Islands of the Blessed (partly inferable from the text).
- **¶3.0:** that Uranus means Heaven.
- **¶3.2:** the laws protecting "freeborn" women.
- **¶3.3:**
  - Lacedaemon is Sparta
  - Ionia was under Persian rule, and "barbarians" means non-Greeks
  - the social life of the gymnasium
  - who Harmodius and Aristogeiton were
  - that Athens had tyrants at all
- **¶3.6–3.9:** that "yield" and "gratify" mean sexual favors.
- **¶4.1:** that "his dominion" means Love's; who Heraclitus was; what "the One" is.
- **¶4.2:** that Greek education centered on music and poetry.
- **¶4.3:** what the Muses are.

## Five biggest obstacles

1. **It's hard to tell who is speaking, and to whom.**
   - The frame story in chapter 1:
     - the listener in ¶1.0 is never named
     - chains of hearsay
     - ¶1.7 jumps from Glaucon to the present-day listener
     - "I" switches between Apollodorus and Aristodemus (¶1.14, ¶1.26)
     - COMPANION and APOLLODORUS script labels appear without warning (¶1.8–1.11)
   - Later chapters have missing quotation marks or speaker tags: ¶1.17 and ¶1.19, ¶2.4, ¶3.11, ¶4.9.
2. **"Yield" and "gratify" are never explained.**
   - This is the central act in Pausanias's speech (¶3.6–3.9). The same vagueness runs through "service", "show any kindness", "gives himself to a lover" and "'uses base'".
   - Eryximachus reuses "gratify" in ¶4.0 and ¶4.3.
   - A newcomer can't tell what is being allowed or forbidden.
3. **Names, places and myth premises that carry the argument have no gloss.**
   - ¶3.3: Lacedaemon, Elis and Boeotia, Ionia, "the barbarians", Harmodius and Aristogeiton.
   - ¶3.0: Uranus and Dione. The link between Uranus and "heavenly" is lost.
   - ¶1.7 and ¶1.41: deme and Myrrhinusian.
   - ¶4.1: Heraclitus's "the One".
   - ¶4.3: Urania and Polyhymnia.
   - ¶2.6: why Alcestis had to die.
   - ¶1.16–1.18: the pun on Agathon's name ("good") in the proverb.
4. **Old words, and words whose meaning has shifted, mislead modern ears.**
   - **Shifted meanings:** "meaner" and "meanness" (¶3.1, ¶3.5), "play the fool with them" (¶3.2), "disinterested" (¶3.8), "gymnastics" and "barbarians" (¶3.3).
   - **Simply archaic:** "wantonness", "forswear", "disappointed of his gains", "'uses base'", "licentiousness", "shoot your bolt", "unbidden", "the women who are within", "I move in the next place".
   - On top of this, the register is uneven.
5. **Very long sentences, and steps in the argument that don't follow.**
   - **Long sentences:** ¶3.5 ("But then again — when fathers forbid…") and ¶3.8 ("For when lover and beloved come together… and only then…"). ¶3.8 also has two different pairs that both "meet in one".
   - **Steps that don't follow:**
     - ¶2.7: "for the lover is more divine, since he is inspired by a god"
     - ¶2.7: "not only to defend him, but even after Patroclus was already dead"
     - ¶2.2–2.3: Parmenides' "maker of Love" undercuts "no record of his parents"
     - ¶4.1: "under his dominion" points to Asclepius instead of Love
