## 1. Argument summary

### Socrates questions Agathon [7.0]–[7.44]

**Main claim:** Love is not beautiful, and not good. Love always wants something it lacks, and Agathon himself said that what love wants is beauty.

- Love is relational, like "father" or "brother": it is always love *of* something [7.0]–[7.9].
- Love desires what it loves. Whoever desires something lacks it, "necessarily" and not just "probably" [7.10]–[7.19].
  - *The extra clause "he who desires nothing lacks nothing" [7.14] overstates the case, and the argument doesn't need it.*
- Socrates heads off an objection. A healthy man who "wants to be healthy" really wants to stay healthy in the future, and he does not have that future yet. So desire is still for what one lacks [7.20]–[7.25].
- Agathon said love is love of beauty. So Love lacks beauty; what lacks beauty isn't beautiful; so Love isn't beautiful [7.26]–[7.39].
  - *This does not quite follow. By the previous step, a beautiful Love could want to go on being beautiful, and the text never rules that out.*
  - *The step also slides from love the desire to Love the god (see obstacle 4).*
- Good things are beautiful, so Love lacks good things too [7.40]–[7.42].
  - *The inference is valid, but the premise is simply asserted.*
- Agathon gives in. Socrates replies that it is the truth, not Socrates, that can't be refuted [7.43]–[7.44].

*Agathon's part:* he only agrees, and then admits "I fear that I did not understand what I was saying" [7.39].

### Diotima, part 1: what Love is [7.45]–[7.49] (as Socrates reports it)

**Main claim:** Love is an in-between being. He is neither god nor mortal, neither beautiful nor ugly, neither wise nor ignorant. He is a "spirit" and a lover of wisdom.

- Socrates once held Agathon's view, and Diotima refuted him in the same way [7.45].
- "Not beautiful" does not mean "ugly." There are in-betweens. For example, right opinion (being right without being able to say why) sits between knowledge and ignorance [7.45]–[7.46].
- Gods are happy, which means they possess good and beautiful things. Love lacks these things, so Love is not a god. Socrates' own premises deny that he is one [7.46].
  - *Small wobble: he has "no share in what is good," yet a few lines earlier he is "in between them."*
- Love is a "great spirit," midway between divine and mortal. He is the go-between who carries prayers up to the gods and commands down to men. Prophecy, priesthood, rites and dreams all work through him [7.47].
  - *Wobble: "all communication ... is carried on through Love," yet Love is only "one of" many spirits.*
- Birth story. The gods hold a feast on Aphrodite's birthday, and Poverty conceives Love by Plenty/Resource while he lies drunk and asleep [7.47]–[7.49].
  - So Love attends Aphrodite and loves beauty.
  - He is needy, barefoot and homeless like his mother, and bold and scheming like his father.
  - He flourishes, "dies" and comes alive again. He is never rich and never destitute.
- The gods and the wise don't seek wisdom, and the ignorant don't feel their lack. Those who seek it are in between. Wisdom is beautiful and Love loves beauty, so Love is a philosopher [7.49].
- Diagnosis: Socrates' mistake came from confusing the lover with the beloved. The beloved is beautiful; the lover is not [7.49].

### Diotima, part 2: what love does [7.50]–[7.61]

**Main claim:** love is the desire to have the good forever. Mortals can come near "forever" only by producing offspring, so love's work is "birth in beauty." That can mean children of the body or, for those "pregnant in soul," wisdom, virtue, poems and laws.

- To love the beautiful is to want it to be yours. Socrates can't say what having beauty gives you, so Diotima puts "good" in its place. Having the good gives happiness, and happiness is a final answer [7.50].
  - *Break: the swap is never justified, and the beauty question is left hanging until [7.54].*
- Everyone wants the good, yet only some people are called "lovers." That is because one kind takes the name of the whole, just as "poetry" (which literally means "making") is used only for verse. Money-makers, athletes and philosophers love too [7.51]–[7.52].
- Against the "other half" idea: we love only what is good. People amputate their own limbs if those limbs are bad [7.52].
- So love is wanting the good to be one's own *forever* [7.52].
  - *Break: "everlasting" is added by bare agreement. The reason for it is back at [7.20]–[7.22] and is never recalled.*
- Love's activity is "birth in beauty" [7.53]–[7.54].
  - Everyone is "pregnant" in body and in soul.
  - Birth is something immortal in a mortal creature, so it can happen only in beauty, which is in harmony with the divine.
  - Near beauty, the pregnant creature gives birth; near ugliness, it shrinks back.
  - *The core metaphor is never plainly unpacked (see obstacle 1).*
- Reproduction is how a mortal gets a kind of immortality, and wanting the good forever means wanting immortality [7.54]–[7.57]. The evidence offered:
  - animals' mating frenzy and their self-sacrifice for their young;
  - even one person lasts only because body, soul and knowledge are constantly replaced.
- Ambition for undying fame is the same drive: Alcestis, Achilles, Codrus [7.58].
- Those pregnant in body have children with women. Those pregnant in soul produce wisdom and virtue (poets, inventors, lawgivers) [7.59]–[7.61].
  - Such a man seeks a beautiful soul in a beautiful body and educates him.
  - The two raise "children" (works and ideas) that last longer than human children: Homer, Hesiod, Lycurgus, Solon.
  - Temples are raised for children like these, and never for bodily ones.

### Diotima, part 3: the "greater mysteries" [7.62]–[7.66]

**Main claim:** when love is pursued rightly, it climbs from one beautiful body to Beauty itself. Contemplating that Beauty is the best human life and the closest a mortal comes to immortality.

- Everything so far was the "lesser mysteries." The higher rites follow, and Diotima doubts Socrates can reach them [7.62].
- The climb, first version [7.62]–[7.63]:
  1. love one body, and produce beautiful thoughts;
  2. see that all bodily beauty is one;
  3. love all beautiful bodies, less intensely;
  4. value souls above bodies;
  5. institutions and laws;
  6. branches of knowledge;
  7. the "vast sea of beauty";
  8. a single knowledge.

  *Each move is asserted rather than argued: "how foolish he would be not to recognize," "compelled."*
- At the end comes a sudden vision of Beauty itself [7.64]. It:
  - lasts forever;
  - is never beautiful in one respect, time, place or eye and ugly in another;
  - is not found in any face, speech, knowledge, creature or place;
  - stays unchanged while all other beautiful things share in it.
- The climb, recap as "steps" [7.65]: one body → two → all bodies → practices → branches of knowledge → absolute beauty.
  - *This does not match the first version: the souls stage is missing, and "institutions and laws" has become "practices."*
- This is the best life. Next to it, gold, clothes and beautiful boys are nothing [7.65].
- Only there does a man give birth to "realities," meaning true virtue, rather than "images." That makes him dear to the gods and immortal "if any mortal man can" [7.66].
  - *From the text alone I could not reconstruct "images vs realities."*

### Socrates closes [7.67]–[7.68]

- He is persuaded, and he tries to persuade others that human nature has no better helper than Love in reaching this goal.
- So everyone should honor Love, and he devotes himself to Love's ways.
- They may call it a speech in praise of Love "or anything else you please."
  - *Phaedrus, the person he addresses, appears nowhere else in this chapter.*

## 3. Unclear points

Each entry gives the paragraph, the type of problem and its severity, the exact quote, and what I understood.

**[7.0] (hard to follow / misleading, M)**
Quote: "I am not asking whose child Love is, as though he were the love of some father or mother; that would be a ridiculous question."
- I understood only that Socrates is ruling out *some* other meaning of "love of X."
- "The love of some father or mother" sounds like "love for a parent" or "a parent's love," so the distinction doesn't come through in English.
- At [7.47] Socrates asks Diotima exactly this "ridiculous question" ("who was his father, and who was his mother?"), and nothing marks the echo.

**[7.0] (hard to follow, L)** The opening speech has no named speaker until [7.8] ("'And now,' said Socrates,"). By ear, only the chapter title tells you it is Socrates.

**[7.0], [7.45] (opaque, L)**
Quote: "speaking first of the nature of Love and afterwards of his works"
- I understood "works" as deeds or effects. It is guessable, and [7.50] ("what use is he to men?") marks the switch.

**[7.10] (hard to follow, M)**
Quote: "Keep in mind what it is that love loves — hold that answer back for now"
- Agathon hasn't said what love loves, so the listener doesn't know what to keep in mind.
- It pays off only at [7.28] ("Remember, too, what you said in your speech").
- I understood: remember *privately* your claim that love is of beauty, and don't say it yet.

**[7.14] (hard to follow, L)**
Quote: "'No,' replied Socrates"
- Coming right after Agathon's "Probably not," this briefly sounds like disagreement with the "not." It actually means "not *probably*."

**[7.14] (misleading, M)**
Quote: "and that he who desires nothing lacks nothing, is in my judgment, Agathon, absolutely and necessarily true"
- This overstates the case and invites an objection: you can lack what you don't want.
- Diotima later says exactly that about the ignorant: "he has no desire for what he does not feel he lacks" [7.49].
- The argument needs only "whoever desires, lacks."

**[7.20] (tonally odd, L)**
Quotes: "have their advantages at the present moment, whether they like it or not" / "for at this moment, whether you like it or not, you have them"
- It sounds as if someone might dislike being healthy.
- I understood: wanting it makes no difference, because you already have it.

**[7.20] (hard to follow by ear, M)**
Quote: 'So when someone says, "I am well and wish to be well," or "I am rich and wish to be rich, and I desire simply to have what I have," we shall reply to him:'
- Three voices are nested inside one long paragraph. It is manageable on the page and harder aloud.

**[7.28] (opaque: a callback, M) [PK]**
Quote: "You said that the love of the beautiful set the realm of the gods in order, because there is no love of ugly things."
- Nothing in this chapter explains how love "set the realm of the gods in order."
- I recognize it as a callback to Agathon's speech only through prior knowledge.
- Only the second half of the sentence matters for the argument.

**[7.34] / [7.38] (misleading by ear, H) [PK]**
Quotes: "Then Love lacks beauty, and does not have it?" … "Then would you still say that Love is beautiful?"
- Spoken aloud, "Love" (the god) and "love" (the desire) are the same word.
- The argument shows that a *desire* is for what it lacks, then concludes that the *god* is not beautiful.
- Even the printed page wobbles: "is love the love of something" [7.0] vs "Is Love of something" [7.8].
- I can follow the double meaning only because I already know Love here is both a god and a passion.

**[7.36] (archaic word order, L)**
Quote: "And would you call that beautiful which lacks and does not possess beauty?"

**[7.40] (opaque premise, H) [PK]**
Quote: "isn't what is good also beautiful?"
- To a modern ear, "beautiful" means good-looking, so the equation sounds arbitrary.
- Agathon agrees at once, and the premise carries real weight at [7.50].
- I understood it only because I know the Greek word covers "fine" and "noble" as well as "beautiful."

**[7.44] (tonally odd, L)**
Quote: "No, beloved Agathon"
- To a modern ear this sounds gushing or archaic. The rest of the line is excellent (see What works).

**[7.45] (opaque / hard to parse, M) [PK]**
Quote: "Long ago, when the Athenians offered sacrifices before the plague came, she delayed the disease for ten years."
- Which plague? Did the Athenians' sacrifices delay it, or did Diotima? And how?
- I understood: she was a holy woman or seer whose religious advice postponed an epidemic.
- Knowing "the plague" as a particular event is [PK].

**[7.45], [7.55] (tonally ambiguous, L)**
Quotes: "She was my teacher in the art of love" / "a master in the art of love"
- In modern English, "the art of love" suggests seduction technique, and a teenage listener will snicker.
- What is meant is expertise about what love is.

**[7.45] (tonally odd / archaic, L)**
Quote: "'Hush,' she cried."
- Today "hush" means "be quiet." Here it is a rebuke: don't say such a thing.

**[7.46] (misleading, L)**
Quote: "But how can he be a god if he has no share in what is good or beautiful?"
- A few lines earlier Love is "in between them," which implies he has *some* share.

**[7.47] (opaque, L)**
Quote: "He is a great spirit, and like all spirits he is midway between the divine and the mortal."
- "Spirit" here means neither ghost, nor soul, nor mood.
- The gloss ("these spirits, or in-between powers") comes only at the end of the paragraph.

**[7.47] (misleading, M)**

Quote: "but what is immortal is immortal in another way."
- Cryptic. I understood that the truly immortal simply stays the same.

**[7.58] (tonally ambiguous, M) [PK]**
Quote: "And she answered with all the authority of an accomplished sophist, one of those professional teachers of wisdom"
- Is this praise or mockery? The last time "sophist" came up, at [7.48], it stood next to "sorcerer."
- I hear irony only because I know Plato's view of sophists.

**[7.58] (opaque names, M) [PK]**
Quote: "Do you imagine that Alcestis would have died to save Admetus, or Achilles to avenge Patroclus, or your own Codrus, an early king of Athens"
- Codrus is glossed; Alcestis, Admetus and Patroclus are not. The verbs make the point anyway.
- That Alcestis was a wife who died in her husband's place, and who Patroclus was, are [PK]. Earlier chapters may have covered them.

**[7.58] (hard to follow, L)**
Quote: "in the hope of the glorious fame of immortal virtue"
- Too many abstract nouns stacked together.

**[7.59] (opaque: cultural context, M) [PK]**
Quote: "Those who are pregnant in body only turn to women and have children"
- This implies that the soul-pregnant turn to *men*, but the chapter never says so.
- The Greek custom of an older man courting and educating a youth is [PK].

**[7.59] (hard to follow, L)**
Quote: "the ordering of states and families, which is called self-control and justice"
- By ear, "states" could mean "conditions."
- The text doesn't explain why this wisdom is *called* self-control and justice.

**[7.60] (opaque: cultural context, M) [PK]**
Quotes: "he embraces the two, body and soul, in one person." … "and he tries to educate him." … "And so the two of them are married by a far closer bond"
- Who are these two: teacher and student, or lovers?
- "Married" is a metaphor, and a first-time listener can't be sure of that.

**[7.60] (tonally odd, L)**
Quote: "the children they have in common are more beautiful and more immortal"
- "More immortal" is odd, though the meaning comes through.

**[7.60] (hard to follow by ear, L)**
Quote: "to be the saviors not only of Lacedaemon, that is, Sparta, but of Hellas, the whole of Greece, as one might say"
- The glosses pile up by ear; "Sparta" and "Greece" alone would carry it.
- "Hesiod" is identified only as a great poet, which is enough.

**[7.62] (hard to follow, M)**
Quote: "if the one who guides him through these mysteries leads him rightly"
- A new figure, the guide, appears without introduction.
- The pronouns (him, him, he) blur the learner and the guide.

**[7.62] (hard to follow: an asserted step, M)**
Quote: "how foolish he would be not to recognize that the beauty in all bodies is one and the same!"
- The move from "akin" to "one and the same" is announced with an exclamation, not argued.

**[7.62] (tonally misleading, L)**
Quote: "and he will become a lover of all beautiful bodies."
- To a modern ear this can sound promiscuous. The point is a calmer, general appreciation of beauty.

**[7.62] / [7.63] (hard to follow, L)**
Quotes: "the beauty of the mind" vs "someone with a virtuous soul"
- The text switches between "mind" and "soul" for the same stage.

**[7.63] (hard to follow by ear, M)**
Quote: "So even if someone with a virtuous soul has only a little physical beauty, he will be content to love him and care for him"
- Spoken aloud, "he" could refer to the "someone."

**[7.63] (hard to follow: an asserted step, M)**
Quote: "until he is compelled to contemplate and see the beauty of institutions and laws"
- Compelled by what? Nothing motivates the jump from one soul to laws.
- [7.65] then renames this stage "beautiful practices."

**[7.63] (hard to follow, L)**
Quote: "He will not be like a servant in love with the beauty of one young man, or one man, or one institution, himself a slave, petty and narrow-minded."
- A compressed metaphor that could be heard as a literal servant.

**[7.63] (hard to follow, L)**
Quote: "growing and gaining strength on that shore"
- The metaphor slides from sea to shore.

**[7.63] (misleading, M)**
Quote: "he is at last granted the vision of a single kind of knowledge, the knowledge of beauty everywhere."
- "Beauty everywhere" suggests knowing all the beautiful things in the world.
- The very next paragraph says the goal is a beauty that does not "exist in any other being ... or any other place."

**[7.64] (opaque, L)**
Quote: "not beautiful at one time, or in one relation, or in one place"
- "In one relation" is opaque.

**[7.64] (misleading, L)**
Quote: "nor does it take the form of any speech or knowledge"
- This is puzzling right after the listener was promised "a single kind of knowledge."

**[7.64] (abstract / opaque, H) [PK]**
Quote: "It is beauty absolute, separate, simple and everlasting, which, without growing less or greater, or changing in any way, is imparted to the ever-growing and perishing beauties of all other things."
- This is the climax, and it is delivered in philosophers' vocabulary. Separate from what? Simple how? What does "imparted" mean?
- I recognize it as Plato's Form of Beauty only through prior knowledge.
- From the text alone I would get, barely, "a perfect, unchanging Beauty that all beautiful things borrow from."

**[7.64] (tonally misleading, M)**
Quote: "under the influence of true love"
- To a modern ear, "true love" is the romantic cliché of soulmates. That is the opposite of what is meant here: love that is rightly directed.

**[7.65] (hard to follow by ear, L)**
Quote: "And the true order of going, or of being led by another, to the things of love is this"
- Awkward phrasing when spoken.

**[7.65] (misleading: inconsistent with the first version, M)**
Quote: "from one beautiful body to two, and from two to all beautiful bodies; from beautiful bodies to beautiful practices"
- The recap skips the souls stage of [7.62]–[7.63] and renames "institutions and laws" as "practices."
- A listener holding the first ladder in mind can't match the second one to it.

**[7.65] (tonally jarring / opaque context, M) [PK]**
Quote: "it is not to be measured against gold, and clothes, and beautiful boys and young men, whose presence now entrances you."
- The chapter never explains the Athenian custom, so Socrates being "entranced" by "beautiful boys" will startle or mislead a modern listener.
- "Clothes" also sounds bathetic; the sense seems to be "fine clothes."

**[7.65] (archaic, L)**
Quote: "all the colors and vanities of human life"
- "Vanities" is used in its old sense of empty trifles; a modern ear hears "conceit."

**[7.66] (opaque, H) [PK]**
Quote: "will he be able to give birth not to images of beauty but to realities, because what he has hold of is not an image but a reality."
- What is an "image of beauty," and how does one give birth to it?
- The next sentence says what is born is "true virtue," so is it beauty or virtue?
- I follow the image/reality contrast only from prior knowledge of Plato.

**[7.66] (tonally odd, L)**
Quote: "Would that be a life to despise?"
- The understatement works, but only on a second hearing.

**[7.67] (opaque name, M) [PK]**
Quote: "Such, Phaedrus — and I speak not only to you, but to all of you — were the words of Diotima"
- Phaedrus appears nowhere else in this chapter. Why address him and not Agathon?
- I know he proposed the round of speeches only through prior knowledge; earlier chapters may have established it.

**[7.67] (misleading, L)**
Quote: "the power and spirit of Love"
- Since [7.47], "spirit" has been a technical term for an in-between being. Here it means vigor or essence.

## 4. What works

**Socrates and Agathon**
- **[7.0]–[7.9]:** the father / mother / brother analogy is concrete, short and repeated. It is an ideal way to open an argument for listeners.
- **[7.14]:** "whether 'necessarily,' and not 'probably,' is the right word" is a crisp turning point that is easy to hear.
- **[7.20]–[7.22]:** "I am rich and wish to be rich" → "you want to have in the future what you have now." A relatable everyday example makes an abstract point.
- **[7.26]:** "let us sum up the argument" is good signposting.
- **[7.39]:** "I fear that I did not understand what I was saying." A clear dramatic turn, and funny.
- **[7.44]:** "it is the truth that you cannot refute, for Socrates is easily refuted." Witty and memorable.

**Diotima on what Love is**
- **[7.45]:** "I shall take both parts myself, hers and mine" cleanly sets up a story inside a speech. The parallel (Socrates once said what Agathon said) makes Socrates sympathetic.
- **[7.46]:** the gloss of right opinion ("being right without being able to give a reason for it"), and the teasing "You and I are two of them."
- **[7.47]:** Love as a go-between who "carries the prayers and sacrifices of men across to the gods, and brings the commands and replies of the gods to men." An easy picture to hold.
- **[7.47]–[7.48]:** the birth story works as a story.
  - The beggar at the door and the drunk god asleep in Zeus's garden are easy to picture.
  - "His fortunes match his parentage" sets up the portrait of a barefoot, homeless Love sleeping in doorways.
  - The pun "when he has plenty, he is alive and flourishing" lands if the listener kept the name "Plenty."
- **[7.49]:** "whatever flows in to him is always flowing out again"; "A child could answer that question"; the argument that Love is a philosopher; and above all "confusing love with the beloved — what loves with what is loved."

**Diotima on what love does**
- **[7.51]:** "Our word for poetry literally means 'making.'" The part-for-whole analogy is one of the clearest arguments in the chapter.
- **[7.52]:** money-making, athletics and philosophy as other "paths" of love are concrete examples.
- **[7.53]:** "It would take a prophet to know what you mean" lets the listener share Socrates' confusion. The gloss between the dashes ("they carry something within them that is waiting to be born") half-rescues the idea.
- **[7.54]:** the bodily imagery is vivid and deliberately physical ("relaxes and opens" / "frowns and contracts … shrivels up"; "flutter and ecstasy"). "Reproduction is a kind of eternity and immortality" is the clearest turning point in Diotima's speech.
- **[7.55]:** "catch the infection of love" and "the weakest are ready to fight the strongest" are strong, concrete evidence.
- **[7.56]:** "his hair, flesh, bones, blood and whole body are always changing" feels modern and true, and is excellent by ear.
- **[7.58]:** "leaving behind them a name that will be eternal." Codrus is glossed in passing ("an early king of Athens").
- **[7.60]–[7.61]:** Homer's and Hesiod's poems as "children," and the memorable punchline "no temple was ever raised in anyone's honor for the sake of his mortal children." The glosses for Lycurgus, Solon and "barbarians — Greeks and non-Greeks alike" also help.

**The ascent**
- **[7.62]:** "the lesser mysteries of love, the first rites of initiation, into which even you, Socrates, may enter." Glossed, and full of character.
- **[7.63]:** "the vast sea of beauty." And "I will now go on to this; please give me your very best attention:" is a perfect signpost for a listener.
- **[7.64]:** the rhythm of the list of what Beauty is not ("not beautiful at one time … and ugly at another") carries the listener even where the words are abstract.
- **[7.65]:** "using these only as steps," followed by the one-line ladder, is the single clearest statement of the climb. "Content to live just seeing them … without food or drink" is humanizing and funny.
- **[7.66]:** "be immortal, if any mortal man can" neatly ties off the immortality thread.
- **[7.68]:** "you may call a speech in praise of Love, or anything else you please" is a wry, clear ending.

**Throughout**
- Speaker tags are frequent ("I said," "she replied"), so the nested dialogue can be tracked by ear.
- Names get short in-line glosses: Poros, Penia, Metis, sophist, Codrus, Lycurgus, Sparta, Hellas, Solon, barbarians.
- The plan "his nature first, then his works" is stated at [7.0] and [7.45], and the switch is marked at [7.50]. That gives the listener a map of the chapter.

## 5. Rating: 3 / 5

A first-time listener can retell the backbone of the chapter:
- love wants what it lacks, so Love isn't beautiful;
- Love is an in-between spirit, son of Poverty and Plenty, and a philosopher;
- love wants the good forever, which means wanting immortality, which means producing offspring of the body or the soul;
- love then climbs to Beauty itself.

The first third, where Socrates questions Agathon, would rate 4 on its own. Three things limit the chapter as a whole:

- **Its two central ideas are the least explained:** "birth in beauty" [7.53]–[7.54], and the vision of absolute Beauty with its "images vs realities" [7.63]–[7.66].
- **The steps that connect the sections are asserted, not shown:** "good is beautiful," the swap of good for beautiful, and "everlasting."
- **The densest stretch, the climb, comes last,** when a listener's attention is lowest.

---

## 6. The five biggest obstacles

1. **"Birth in beauty" is never plainly unpacked.** It is introduced at [7.53]–[7.54] and carried into [7.59]–[7.60] and [7.66].
   - The text never says that "in beauty" means "in the presence of a beautiful person or thing."
   - Pregnancy comes before union, the reverse of the familiar order.
   - "The goddess of destiny and of childbirth" adds a further riddle.
2. **The climax depends on philosophical vocabulary and prior knowledge** [7.63]–[7.66]. [PK]
   - "The knowledge of beauty everywhere" is contradicted by the next paragraph.
   - "Beauty absolute, separate, simple," "imparted to" and "the essence of beauty" are abstract.
   - "Images of beauty" are set against "realities," and then what is born turns out to be "true virtue."
3. **Premises that carry the argument are asserted, not argued.**
   - "Isn't what is good also beautiful?" [7.40] leads to "let me put the word 'good' in place of the beautiful" [7.50], which drops the question about beauty.
   - "The everlasting possession of the good" [7.52]: the reason for "everlasting" ([7.20]–[7.22]) is never recalled, yet the whole immortality argument rests on it.
4. **The refutation of Agathon rests on slides that a listener can't check by ear** [7.0]–[7.44].
   - "Love" (the god) and "love" (the desire) sound identical, and the capitals wobble even in print.
   - By Socrates' own reasoning at [7.20]–[7.22], a beautiful Love could want to stay beautiful, and this is never ruled out.
   - "He who desires nothing lacks nothing" [7.14] overstates the case, and a sharp listener will reject it.
5. **The chapter leans on background it never explains.** [PK]
   - The older-man/youth relationship behind "educate him" and "married by a far closer bond" [7.60], and behind "beautiful boys and young men, whose presence now entrances you" [7.65].
   - Phaedrus [7.67].
   - Agathon's earlier claims [7.28], [7.48].
   - "The plague" [7.45].
   - Aphrodite, Zeus and nectar [7.47]–[7.48].
   - Alcestis, Admetus and Patroclus [7.58].

**Runner-up: the two versions of the climb don't match** ([7.62]–[7.63] vs [7.65]). The souls stage is missing from the recap, and "institutions and laws" becomes "practices." An unintroduced "guide" and tangled pronouns make the first version harder still.

## 7. Everything I understood only through prior knowledge [PK]

- Love (Eros) is both a god and a passion. That is what makes [7.8]–[7.44] hang together.
- The content of Agathon's earlier speech: love of beauty putting the gods in order [7.28]; Love as "tender" [7.48].
- The Greek word for "beautiful" also covers "fine," "noble" and "good" [7.40], [7.50].
- "The plague" is the Athenian plague, and Diotima is a seer [7.45].
- "Mysteries" means initiation rites [7.47]. [7.62] glosses it later.
- Aphrodite (goddess of love and beauty), Zeus, and nectar [7.47]–[7.48].
- The Cupid-as-Venus's-son myth is prior knowledge that could *mislead* at [7.47]–[7.48].
- The portrait of Love (barefoot, poor, a philosopher) resembles Socrates [7.48].
- Plato uses "sophist" with suspicion, so [7.58] reads as irony.
- "Other half" is a rebuttal of Aristophanes' speech [7.52].
- The Greek birth goddesses behind "goddess of destiny and of childbirth" [7.54].
- Alcestis and Admetus, Achilles and Patroclus [7.58]. Homer [7.60]; Hesiod I know only as a "great poet."
- The Athenian custom of an older man courting and educating a youth [7.59]–[7.60], [7.65].
- Plato's theory of Forms: absolute Beauty, other things sharing in it, images vs realities [7.64]–[7.66].
- Phaedrus is the one who proposed the round of speeches [7.67]–[7.68].
- **[PK-risk]:** Plato's doctrine of "recollection" could lead a listener to misread [7.56].
