# Symposium: blind comprehension review, reader BL2 (chapters 5–8)

**Packet:** `blind-BL2.md`, paragraphs 5.0–8.46.
**Method:** I read it straight through once as a first-time reader or listener. Then I checked every quote below against the packet.

**Blindness.** I read only the brief and this packet, and I looked nothing up. But I already know this dialogue and can't un-know it. Where I understood something *only* because of that background, I mark it **[PK]**. Everything else is judged by what this text makes clear to a curious sixteen-year-old, or to an adult hearing it read aloud.

**Tags:**
- [ear]: hard to follow by ear
- [opaque]: a name, myth, custom or word the text doesn't unpack
- [misleading]: seems to say something the speaker surely doesn't mean
- [archaic]
- [tone]: tonally odd
- [gap]: a step is missing or merely asserted, or I can't rebuild it
- [PK]: understood only through prior knowledge

### The five biggest obstacles

1. **Chapter 7's logical hinges are its least clear sentences.** The steps can be rebuilt, but the sentences that carry them are the hardest to hear:
   - 7.0 rules out “a father or of a mother, as if I were asking whose child he is”, then uses father and mother as the example anyway.
   - 7.10 is a tongue-twister: “Keep in mind what love is love of”.
   - 7.22 concludes the chapter's best example in its stiffest Victorian syntax.
   - 7.32–7.34 slide from “something which a man lacks” to “Love lacks beauty”, and aloud "Love" and "love" sound the same.
   - 7.51 “do we not say that all men love, but only some of them?” misparses by ear.
   - 7.54 “But why of reproduction?” has no clear speaker.
   - The unidiomatic "love is of …" formula runs through the whole chapter.
2. **The pregnancy / "birth in beauty" metaphor (7.53–7.54).** “all human beings are pregnant” includes men and comes *before* any union. Add “Beauty … is the Fate and the goddess of childbirth”, and a first-time listener is lost for about six paragraphs. The fog lifts only at 7.59–7.60: “Those who are pregnant in body only turn to women”.
3. **Two speeds of English.** The speeches are modern, but much of the linking dialogue is still nineteenth-century English, and it falls on short lines a listener hears once. Examples: “did I not know … I should be really afraid”, “your nerves could be fluttered”, “you have not spoken but have well drunken”, “What are you about?”, “I do beseech you”, “Hurrah! … that I may be praised”. Worst clusters: 5.8–5.17, 6.11–6.12, 8.6–8.21, 8.25, 8.40–8.45.
4. **Word choices that contradict each other or blur key terms.**
   - Love is “always in need” (7.48), then “never in need” (7.49).
   - 7.63 promises “the knowledge of beauty everywhere”, but 7.64 says beauty itself exists in no “other place”.
   - The ladder recap (7.65) renames and drops rungs. Souls vanish, laws become “practices”, knowledge becomes “notions”, and Beauty itself becomes a mere “notion”.
   - “Poros, or Plenty … Metis, or Discretion — that is, cunning intelligence” (7.47) piles up names. “Discretion” misleads, and “Plenty” doesn't explain why Love is a resourceful schemer.
   - “yesterday” (8.0) vs “the day before yesterday” (8.5).
5. **Missing who's-who cues, and traps for the ear.**
   - The reporting frame “Aristodemus said” reappears unexplained (6.7; 6.11, where “he added” is ambiguous; 8.46).
   - Alcibiades is never introduced.
   - The packet never states outright that Agathon is a prize-winning tragic poet or that Eryximachus is a doctor. Yet 5.11, 8.0, 8.5, 8.8–8.12 and the 8.46 punchline depend on both.
   - Dining couches are never explained.
   - Words misheard aloud: “daimon” sounds like "demon", “Olympus” like the mountain, “every body” like "everybody". Singular “God” sounds Christian.

## Cross-chapter findings

**A. Two registers.** Most of the long speeches are fluent modern English. Much of the connecting dialogue still reads like a Victorian translation. [PK] I recognise Jowett-style phrasing. The switch is audible, and it lands on short lines heard only once.

Paragraphs affected:
- Chapter 5: 5.8, 5.10, 5.11, 5.12, 5.15, part of 5.16, 5.17.
- Chapter 6: 6.6 (beyond its intended ornament), 6.11, 6.12.
- Chapter 7: 7.4, 7.13, 7.17, 7.18, 7.22, 7.32.
- Chapter 8: 8.0 (“Hail, friends”), 8.6, 8.8, 8.11–8.13, 8.14, 8.15, 8.16, 8.19, 8.21, 8.25, 8.30 (“sweet friend”), 8.32, 8.40, 8.42, 8.44.

Exact quotes are listed under each chapter.

**B. Who is speaking?**
- The reported-speech frame surfaces three times (6.7 “Aristodemus said that everyone cheered”; 6.11; 8.46), with no reminder of who Aristodemus is. In my packet, the first sign that he is even at the party is Alcibiades' roll-call in 8.28. This may be set up in ch. 1.
- Ch. 7 nests speech three deep: Socrates recounts his dialogue with Diotima, and her lines contain further quotes (7.51 “'making'”). Most turns are tagged (“I said”, “she replied”), which works.
- Several runs are untagged: 7.45, 7.46, 7.47, 7.52, 7.54. Worst is 7.54, where “But why of reproduction?” has no speaker and sits between two “she replied”s.

**C. "Love" and "love" sound the same.** A capital marks the god; lower case marks the feeling. The ch. 7 refutation slides between the two (step 8 in the ch. 7 table), and a listener hears only "love". Even on the page the capitals are inconsistent:
- 7.34 “Then Love lacks beauty”, but 7.38 “would you still say that love is beautiful?”
- 6.4 “the Love of the beautiful”.

**D. The built-in glosses are the edition's best tool.** Good examples:
- “Hephaestus, the blacksmith god”
- “a last, the foot-shaped block he works on”
- “Ate, the goddess of ruin”
- “Codrus, the early king of Athens”
- “Silenus, the pot-bellied old satyr”
- “Corybantian revelers — those worshipers who dance in a religious frenzy”
- “Potidaea, the northern city that Athens was besieging”
- “He starts out as their lover, the older man courting them, and ends up with them courting him instead”

Where they fail:
- Without “that is”, an apposition sounds like a list. Compare “the Lacedaemonians, the Spartans” (5.5) with the clearer “Lacedaemon, that is, Sparta” (7.60).
- Stacked names overload the ear: “Poros, or Plenty, who is the son of Metis, or Discretion — that is, cunning intelligence” (7.47).
- Some glosses come at the second mention, not the first. Pericles is bare in 8.24 and only becomes “the statesman Pericles” in 8.37.
- One word gets two glosses: “sophist” is “clever professional arguers” in 7.48 but “professional teachers of wisdom” in 7.58.

**E. A Christian ring.** In a world of many gods, these phrases pull the text toward church English:
- singular capital “God” (7.47, twice; 7.66)
- “hold communion with” (7.65)
- “I walk in his ways” (7.67)
- “peace on earth” (6.5)
- “beloved Agathon” (7.44)

**F. Probably set up in chs. 1–4 (I can't check this).** If chs. 1–4 don't make these explicit, they are real gaps here:
- Who Phaedrus, Pausanias, Eryximachus and Aristodemus are.
- The speech game and its left-to-right order.
- That Phaedrus called Love the oldest god (6.0).
- That Eryximachus is a doctor (needed for 6.3 and 8.8–8.12).
- That Agathon is a tragic poet who has just won a prize (needed for 5.11, 8.0, 8.5 and the 8.46 punchline).
- That Eryximachus likes to tease (5.6–5.7).

## Chapter 5: Aristophanes's Speech

### Argument summary

**Aristophanes (5.0–5.7).** Claim: humanity has never grasped Love's power. Of all the gods, Love is our best friend and heals our deepest wound, so he deserves temples and sacrifices.
- The first humans were round and doubled: four hands, four feet, two faces. They came in three sexes (male, female and man-woman), children of the sun, the earth and the moon.
- Strong and proud, they attacked the gods. Destroying them would have ended the sacrifices, so Zeus cut each one in two, leaving them weaker and more numerous as worshippers. Apollo turned their faces toward the cut and tied the skin off at the navel as a reminder.
- Each half longed for its partner, clung to it, and starved. Zeus moved their genitals to the front so that life could go on: man-woman unions produce children, and man-man unions bring satisfaction.
- So love is an ancient drive to reunite. Each of us is half a "token", and our original kind decides whom we desire. Halves of the man-woman desire the other sex; female halves desire women; male halves desire males.
- He defends male-male lovers as the manliest, with the "strong proof" that only they become statesmen. **[gap/tone]** As an argument this doesn't follow, and a listener can't tell if it's a joke.
- True partners want something they can't name. Hephaestus' offer to weld them into one shows what it is: “the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love.”
- We were split for our wickedness, and impiety risks a second split. So revere the gods and let Love lead us to our own halves. For now, the next best thing is a love “whose nature is truly akin to our own”, and Love holds out hope of being restored and made happy.
- As a story the myth hangs together. **[gap]** It never says why the genitals were not already at the front (5.3). The link between piety and finding your half is asserted, not shown.

**Interlude (5.8–5.17)**
- Eryximachus enjoyed the speech and fears that Agathon and Socrates have nothing left to say.
- Socrates says he will be at a loss after Agathon. He starts quizzing Agathon: would he feel shame before a few wise judges but not before the crowd?
- Phaedrus cuts in to keep the speeches going. **[gap]** Socrates' trap is never finished, which is fine, but a first-time listener can't tell where it was heading.

### Unclear points

- **5.1** [tone/ear] “There were three sexes, of the kinds I have described, because the sun, the moon, and the earth are three.” “because … are three” sounds like a non-sequitur until the next sentence explains who the parents are. Understood: three kinds because there are three heavenly parents.
- **5.1** [opaque] “the man-woman the child of the moon, which is made up of sun and earth”. Why would the moon be made of sun and earth? I took it as mythic logic: the moon shares in both. "Shares in both" would say that plainly.
- **5.1** [ear] “the story of Otys and Ephialtes, the giant brothers” … “as they had once done to the giants”. Are the round people the giants, or a different group? Minor. [PK] “Otys” is an unusual spelling; the usual form is Otus.
- **5.2** [opaque] “or the way you might slice an egg with a hair”. An odd picture. Understood: a clean, fine cut. The sorb-apple gloss works.
- **5.2** [ear] “a man or a woman, as we call them now, though each was really only half of a whole man or a whole woman”. An aside inside an aside, hard to hold by ear. Understood.
- **5.3** [gap] “He moved their genitals round to the front, where they had not always been.” The text never said where they were. I know only from [PK] that they stayed on the old outer side, which became the back when Apollo turned the faces. Half a clause would close the gap.
- **5.3** [tone] “most adulterers come from this stock, and so do adulterous women who lust after men”. Heard straight, it's a sour moral claim. I took it as comic exaggeration only because I know Aristophanes wrote comedies [PK]. Nothing in ch. 5 signals the joke. The first hint in the packet is 8.3 (“a joker or lover of jokes, like Aristophanes”).
- **5.3** [tone] “and they are the best of boys and young men, because they have the most manly nature”. A modern teenager may bristle at "boys" in an erotic context. The gloss in 5.4 (“they court younger men”) helps. The same issue recurs in 7.65 and ch. 8, so a one-line note on the Athenian custom may be needed.
- **5.4** [tone/gap] “And when they grow up, these are the men who become our statesmen, and they are the only ones who do, which is strong proof that what I am saying is true.” Taken at face value, this is a bizarre "proof". I read it as a comic dig at politicians only through [PK].
- **5.4** [misleading, PK] “If they do so at all, it is only because the law requires it”. I suspect "custom" is meant; I don't believe a law compelled marriage. A first-time reader will take "law" literally.
- **5.4** [archaic, mild] “something it senses only darkly and doubtfully”. Poetic. Understood: a half-glimpsed longing.
- **5.5** [ear/opaque] “just as the Lacedaemonians, the Spartans, broke up the city of the Arcadians and dispersed its people into villages”. Aloud, "the Lacedaemonians, the Spartans" sounds like two peoples, and "the city of the Arcadians" is an event I can't place. The gist, a city broken into villages, comes through. “The Spartans” alone would do.
- **5.6** [ear, mild] “we will be like tallies, those tokens split in two whose halves match only each other”. "Tallies" is old but glossed. It nearly repeats 5.3 (“like one half of a token split in two that matches nothing but its partner”), so aloud it sounds like a slip rather than a new point.
- **5.6** [tone, PK] “any allusion to Pausanias and Agathon, who, I suspect, are both of the manly kind and belong to the class I have been describing”. A tease about the two as a couple, possibly with an ironic "manly". Without [PK], the listener hears a neutral remark and misses the joke.
- **5.7** [ear, mild] “each, or rather either, since Agathon and Socrates are the only ones left”. A pedantic self-correction. Fine on the page; sounds like a stumble aloud.
- **5.8** [archaic] “and did I not know that Agathon and Socrates are masters in the art of love, I should be really afraid that they would have nothing to say, after the world of things which have been said already. But, for all that, I am not without hopes.” The first abrupt drop into Victorian English. Understood: "If I didn't know they were experts, I'd fear nothing was left to say. Still, I'm hopeful."
- **5.10** [archaic/ear] “in the hope that I may be disconcerted at the expectation raised among the audience that I shall speak well”. Three stacked clauses. Understood: "You're psyching me out by building up expectations."
- **5.11** [archaic/ear/opaque] “'I should be strangely forgetful, Agathon,' replied Socrates, 'of the courage and magnanimity which you showed when your own compositions were about to be exhibited, and you came upon the stage with the actors and faced the vast theater altogether undismayed, if I thought that your nerves could be fluttered at a small party of friends.'”
  - The hardest sentence in the chapter by ear: the main clause is split around a very long middle.
  - “magnanimity”, “exhibited”, “altogether undismayed” and “nerves could be fluttered” are all dated.
  - That Agathon is a playwright can be inferred. That he has just won a prize before thousands is [PK].
- **5.12** [archaic] “that my head is so full of the theater as not to know how much more formidable to a man of sense a few good judges are than many fools”. Understood, but stiff.
- **5.15** [ear/gap] “'But before the many you would not be ashamed, if you thought that you were doing something disgraceful in their presence?'” Hard to parse aloud, and its point is left hanging when Phaedrus interrupts. I read it as the first half of a Socratic trap only through [PK].
- **5.16** [archaic, mild] “but just at present I must not forget the speech in praise of Love which I ought to receive from him and from everyone”. "Receive" is odd unless you already know Phaedrus is collecting the speeches.
- **5.17** [archaic] “I see no reason why I should not proceed with my speech, as I shall have many other opportunities of conversing with Socrates. Let me say first how I ought to speak, and then speak:—” Stiff, and “:—” is old typography.

### What works
- **5.0–5.1** The round, cartwheeling people are instantly easy to picture: “like acrobats turning cartwheels, going over and over with their legs in the air”.
- **5.1** The gods' dilemma, that killing humans would end the sacrifices, is clear and funny. It makes “more profitable to us” land.
- **5.2** Apollo's surgery is superb aloud: “gathering it like a drawstring purse”, “tied it off in a knot, the one we call the navel”, “a shoemaker smooths leather over a last, the foot-shaped block he works on”.
- **5.2** The halves starving in each other's arms is moving and simple.
- **5.4–5.5** First the lovers who “could not explain what they want from each other”, then Hephaestus arriving with his tools. This is the emotional peak, and it's completely clear: “the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love.”
- **5.6** “go about like the flat figures carved in profile on monuments, with only half a nose”. Vivid and funny.
- **5.13** Socrates' plain modern dialogue (“wouldn't you?”) shows the target register working. It also makes 5.8–5.12 sound all the more out of place.

### Rating: 4 / 5
For a first-time listener the myth is close to a 5. Two things pull the chapter down: the Victorian interlude that depends on context (5.8–5.17), and the listener's inability to tell which claims are jokes (5.3, 5.4, 5.6).

## Chapter 6: Agathon's Speech

### Argument summary

**Agathon (6.0–6.6).** Claim: Love is the happiest of the gods, because he is the most beautiful and the best.
- *Method.* Earlier speakers praised Love's benefits to humans. The right order is his nature first, then his gifts.
- *Beautiful because youngest.* He flees Old Age, and “like is drawn to like”. Against Phaedrus, he is not older than the Titans. The old violence among the gods was Necessity's work; Love's reign brought peace.
- *Beautiful because tender.* Homer's Ate walks on men's heads. Love walks on the softest thing of all, souls, and leaves hard ones, so he must be the softest. **[gap]** Living among soft things doesn't make you soft. I think the listener is meant to notice this, and can.
- *Supple, graceful, well-coloured.* He winds in and out of souls, is at war with gracelessness, and lives among flowers.
- *Good, through the four virtues.*
  - Just: he uses no force, and everything is done by consent.
  - Self-controlled: he masters pleasures.
  - Brave: Ares loves Aphrodite, so Love masters the war god.
  - Wise: he is a poet and makes poets, he creates animals, and he taught the gods their arts.
  - **[gap]** Each step is wordplay, but the chain is easy to follow and its flimsiness is visible.
- *Conclusion.* Love set heaven in order through love of beauty. He is the most beautiful and the best, and the cause of beauty and goodness everywhere. A hymn closes the speech.
- **Why this matters for ch. 7:** Socrates turns this line against him: “Love set the realm of the gods in order — the love of beauty, clearly” (6.4). 7.28 quotes it closely enough to recognise.

**Socrates' response (6.7–6.12)**
- The speech was dazzling, and he is now at a loss, as he predicted in 5.9.
- He had thought praise must be true: pick the finest truths and arrange them well.
- The others instead heaped every glory on Love “whether it really belonged to him or not”. That is appearing to praise, not praising.
- So he withdraws from that contest and offers the truth in plain, unplanned words. First he will question Agathon and build on whatever Agathon agrees to.

### Unclear points
- **6.0** [format, by eye] Agathon's speech has no quotation marks, though Aristophanes' speech (ch. 5) and Socrates' (ch. 7) do. So the single-quoted Homer line in 6.1 looks like a new speaker. This doesn't matter aloud.
- **6.0** [opaque] “those deeds of divine violence that Hesiod and Parmenides told of, were, if the stories are true, the work of Necessity and not of Love”. Two unexplained names, and "Necessity" is a ruling power I can't place. It returns in 6.4: “ruled by Necessity”. Understood: the old myths of divine violence had some cause other than Love. “The poet Hesiod”, “the philosopher Parmenides” and a word on Necessity would fix it.
- **6.0** [opaque, PK] “there would have been no chaining or mutilating of gods”. Enough as "violent myths". The specific stories are [PK] and not needed.
- **6.0** [ear, mild] “the way Homer says of Ate, the goddess of ruin, that she is a goddess and tender”. “that she is a goddess and tender” is clumsy.
- **6.3** [opaque, mild] “as the laws that are the lords of the city declare”. Sounds like a quotation. Understood: "as the law says".
- **6.3** [opaque, mild] “his love for Aphrodite, masters him, as the story goes”. Aphrodite is never glossed. Most readers know her, but she matters in ch. 7, where Love is born on her birthday and serves her. One gloss would help.
- **6.3** [context] “and here, like Eryximachus, I am praising my own art”. This needs the reader to know that Eryximachus is a doctor who praised medicine, and that Agathon is a poet. Presumably covered in earlier chapters.
- **6.4** [misleading, mild] “So too the melody of the Muses, the metalwork of Hephaestus, the weaving of Athene, and the rule of Zeus over gods and men are all owed to Love, who invented them.” The previous sentence said Apollo *discovered* his arts under Love's guidance. Now Love *invented* everything. Who invented what? Understood: the gods learned their arts from Love.
- **6.4** [ear, mild] “And so Love set the realm of the gods in order — the love of beauty, clearly, for Love has nothing to do with ugliness.” Elliptical: "[it was] the love of beauty". Socrates reuses this premise in 7.28, so it's worth making crisp.
- **6.5** [tone] “'Gives peace on earth and calms the stormy deep, who stills the winds and bids the sufferer sleep.'” “peace on earth” has a Christmas ring, and “bids” is archaic. Understood.
- **6.6** [ear; probably intended] The finale is a torrent of jingles. Socrates mocks it next, so I think that's deliberate. A listener catches the music, not the sense.
  - Phrases I couldn't unpack: “desired by those who have no part in him, and precious to those who have the better part in him”; “in every word, work, wish, fear — savior, pilot, comrade, helper”.
  - Archaic beyond the intended ornament: “This is he who … makes them to meet together”, “regardful of the good, regardless of the evil”, “that sweet strain”.
- **6.7** [context] “Aristodemus said that everyone cheered”. Who is Aristodemus? The reporting frame surfaces with no reminder (see B).
- **6.7** [opaque, inferable] “'Tell me, son of Acumenus, was I not right to be afraid?'” Inferable, since Socrates is “looking at Eryximachus”, but still a stumble.
- **6.9** [opaque/ear] “For the speech reminded me of Gorgias, the famous orator; and at its end I imagined Agathon shaking at me the Gorginian — or should I say Gorgonian — head of that great master of rhetoric.” The text never says what in the speech was like Gorgias. Only through [PK] do I know that Gorgias was famous for exactly these jingling antitheses. Aloud, “Gorginian” and “Gorgonian” sound nearly identical, so the pun vanishes. The next sentence, about the Gorgon's head turning people to stone, rescues the sense.
- **6.10** [opaque, mild] “as Euripides would say, was a promise of the lips and not of the mind”. The name is unglossed ("the tragic poet" would do), but the meaning is clear.
- **6.11** [archaic/ear] “Aristodemus said that Phaedrus and the company bid him speak in any manner which he thought best. 'Then,' he added, 'let me have your permission first to ask Agathon a few more questions, in order that I may take his admissions as the premises of my discourse.'” Three problems:
  - “he added” sounds like it means Aristodemus.
  - The register.
  - “admissions as the premises of my discourse” is the key signpost for ch. 7 ("I'll build my argument on what Agathon agrees to"), and it's in the hardest words in the chapter.
- **6.12** [archaic, mild] “'I grant the permission,' said Phaedrus. 'Put your questions.' Socrates then proceeded as follows:” Stiff, but clear.

### What works
- **6.0** “he flees from Old Age — and Old Age is swift enough, swifter, truly, than most of us would like”. The wit lands aloud.
- **6.2** The proof of Love's tenderness, including “the skulls of men, which are not so very soft”, is funny. The listener can hear how thin the reasoning is, which sets up Socrates.
- **6.3** The four virtues come in order, each announced as it arrives. Easy to track.
- **6.9** Socrates' account of true praise is clear: “whatever is said in praise should be true … choose the finest of these truths and set them out in the best way”. The irony lands: “not that each of you should really praise Love, but only that you should appear to praise him”.
- **6.10** “a promise of the lips and not of the mind”. Clear and memorable.

### Rating: 4 / 5
The speech's structure is clear, and so is the send-up. Three things limit it: the overloaded finale (6.6); names left unexplained (Hesiod, Parmenides, Necessity, Gorgias); and the stiff hand-off (6.11–6.12), which buries the one instruction a listener needs for chapter 7.

## Chapter 7: Socrates & Diotima

### Argument summary

**Socrates questions Agathon (7.0–7.44).** Claim: Love is neither beautiful nor good.
- "Love" is a relational word. Love is always love *of* something, as a father is the father of a child and a brother the brother of a sibling.
- Love desires what it is love of. Whoever desires something necessarily lacks it.
- Objection: a healthy man "wants" health. Answer: he wants to keep it in the future, which he doesn't yet have, so it is still desire for something he lacks.
- Agathon said Love is love of beauty, not ugliness. So Love lacks beauty, and what lacks beauty isn't beautiful. Agathon: “I fear that I did not understand what I was saying.”
- The good is beautiful, so Love also lacks the good. **[gap]** This premise is asserted, not argued.

**Diotima, part 1: what Love is (7.45–7.49)**
- Socrates once made Agathon's mistake. Diotima taught him that "not beautiful" doesn't mean "ugly": there are in-betweens. Right opinion, which is true but can't give a reason, lies between knowledge and ignorance.
- The gods are happy, meaning they possess good and beautiful things. Love lacks those, so Love is not a god.
- Nor is he mortal. He is a great "spirit", a go-between who carries prayers up and commands down and binds the two worlds together.
- His parents are Plenty and Poverty, who conceived him at Aphrodite's birthday feast.
  - Like his mother, he is poor, barefoot and homeless.
  - Like his father, he is scheming, bold and hungry for wisdom.
  - He flourishes, dies away, and revives.
- Love is a philosopher. Gods and the wise don't seek wisdom, and the ignorant are content with themselves. Only those in between seek it. Wisdom is beautiful, and Love loves beauty.
- The error, both Socrates' and Agathon's, was confusing the lover with the beloved. The beloved is beautiful; the lover is needy.

**Diotima, part 2: what love aims at (7.50–7.61)**
- To love the beautiful is to want it to be yours. Put "good" in place of "beautiful": to want the good is to want happiness, and happiness needs no further reason.
- Everyone wants the good, so everyone "loves" in the broad sense. We keep the word "love" for one kind only, just as "poetry" (literally "making") has been narrowed to verse.
- Lovers don't seek their "other half" unless it is good; people will cut off their own diseased limbs. So love is wanting the good to be one's own *forever*.
- Love's activity is "birth in beauty". Everyone is "pregnant" in body and soul and can give birth only where beauty is present.
- Since love wants the good forever, it wants immortality. Mortals get that only by reproduction, which leaves the new in place of the old. Even one person's body, character and knowledge are constantly replaced.
- The evidence:
  - Animals die for their young, and people risk everything for undying fame (Alcestis, Achilles, Codrus).
  - Those pregnant in body have children.
  - Those pregnant in soul bring forth wisdom and virtue, such as Homer's poems and the laws of Lycurgus and Solon: finer, longer-lasting children.

**Diotima, part 3: the ascent (7.62–7.66)**
- The climb: love one beautiful body, then see that the beauty in all bodies is one. Then value beautiful souls, then beautiful practices and laws, then kinds of knowledge, then the “vast sea of beauty”.
- At the end comes a sudden vision of Beauty itself. It is everlasting and unchanging, beautiful in every respect and to everyone, and located in no body or place. All beautiful things share in it.
- The best life is contemplating it, above gold, clothes and beautiful boys. Only there does a person give birth to true virtue rather than images. That person becomes dear to the gods, and immortal if anyone can be.

**Socrates closes (7.67–7.68).** He is persuaded. So he honours Love as humanity's best helper toward this goal, and calls his words a speech in praise of Love “or anything else you please”.

### Can the argument be followed step by step?
**Mostly, yes.** A patient reader can rebuild every step, and the edition gives good recaps and signposts: 7.24, 7.26, 7.45 (“I shall take both parts myself”), 7.55 (“All this she taught me at various times”) and 7.63 (“please give me your very best attention”).

### Unclear points
- **7.0** [ear/misleading/PK] “I do not want you to say that Love is the love of a father or of a mother, as if I were asking whose child he is — that would be ridiculous. Rather, I want you to answer as you would if I asked whether a father is a father of something.”
  - In English, "love of a father" never means "whose child Love is". So the disclaimer answers a question nobody asked.
  - The next sentence then uses father and mother as the example. A listener hears "not father or mother … father".
  - I grasped the point only through [PK] of an ambiguity in the Greek.
  - A possible fix: "I don't mean who Love's parents are. I mean: is love always love *for* something, the way a father is always the father *of* someone?"
- **7.0** [ear, mild] “is love the love of something, or of nothing?” “love the love” trips the tongue. The question also sounds too obvious to ask; why it matters only shows at 7.12.
- **7.4** [archaic] “is not a brother to be regarded essentially as a brother of something?”
- **7.10** [ear/gap] “'Keep in mind what love is love of, and tell me what I want to know: does Love desire the very thing he is love of?'” “love is love of” is hard to say and to hear. We haven't yet been told what love is love of, so what are we meant to keep in mind? Understood only via [PK]: Socrates is holding "beauty" back for 7.28.
- **7.13** [archaic, mild] “'Probably not, I should say.'” Also 7.12's “that which he loves and desires”.
- **7.14** [archaic, mild] “he who desires something lacks something, and that he who desires nothing lacks nothing”. The “he who” construction. Followable.
- **7.17** [archaic] “'That would be inconsistent with our previous admissions.'” “admissions” may be heard as confessions or tickets. Understood: "That contradicts what we've agreed."
- **7.18** [archaic/ear] “'True. For he who is anything cannot want to be that which he is?'”
- **7.22** [archaic/ear; a hinge] “'Then,' said Socrates, 'he desires that what he has at present may be preserved to him in the future, which is equivalent to saying that he desires something which is non-existent to him, and which as yet he has not got.'”
  - This concludes the chapter's best example (7.20) in its stiffest syntax.
  - Understood: "So what he wants is to keep it in the future, and he doesn't have the future yet."
  - A listener who loses this loses the "desire means lack" rule that everything else rests on.
- **7.30** [ear, mild] “the remark was a just one”. "Just" can be heard as "merely". It means "fair" or "correct".
- **7.32** [archaic/gap] “'And the admission has been already made that Love is of something which a man lacks and does not have?'” Besides the syntax, the subject switches to “a man” (as in 7.26, “that a man lacks”). Then 7.34 concludes “Love lacks beauty”. The step from "the lover lacks what he loves" to "Love the god lacks beauty" is the biggest jump in the refutation. It isn't repaired until 7.49.
- **7.34 / 7.38** [by eye] “'Then Love lacks beauty, and does not have it?'” / “'Then would you still say that love is beautiful?'” Inconsistent capitals for the same subject.
- **7.40** [gap/PK] “isn't the good also the beautiful?” Asserted and accepted without a reason, and "the good is beautiful" sounds odd in everyday English. From [PK], I know the Greek word also covers "fine" or "noble", which makes it natural. A light touch ("fine and beautiful") would help.
- **7.44** [tone] “'No, beloved Agathon — it is the truth that you cannot refute, for Socrates is easily refuted.” “beloved” sounds romantic or churchy today. Socrates naming himself in the third person briefly muddles who is speaking.
- **7.45** [opaque] “Long ago, when the Athenians offered sacrifices before the plague came, she delayed the disease for ten years.” Which plague, and how did she delay it? Understood: she was a holy woman whose guidance held a plague off.
- **7.45** [ear, mild] “"Certainly," I said. "And is whatever is not wise ignorant? Don't you see that there is something in between wisdom and ignorance?"” Diotima's line has no tag. I inferred the speaker because the turns alternate.
- **7.46** [ear/gap] “"By those who know, or by those who do not know?" "By everyone."” Untagged, and her distinction is dropped at once. I couldn't see why she asked.
- **7.47** [ear] “"Is he mortal?" "No." "What then?" "As in the earlier case, he is neither mortal nor immortal, but in between the two."” Rapid untagged turns, which I could follow only by counting.
- **7.47** [misleading by ear] “He is a great spirit (daimon)”. Read aloud, "daimon" sounds like "demon", which suggests evil: the opposite of the meaning. Drop the Greek, or add "not a 'demon' in our sense".
- **7.47** [opaque] “with their sacrifices and mysteries and charms, and all prophecy and incantation”. "Mysteries" (secret initiation rites) is unexplained here, yet it matters in 7.62 and 8.28. A listener may hear "puzzles".
- **7.47** [misleading/tone] “For God does not mix with man; all communication and conversation between God and man, whether man is awake or asleep, is carried on through Love.” A singular capital "God" sounds monotheistic in a world of many gods. "The gods do not mix with humans" would fit. The same applies to 7.66.
- **7.47** [ear/misleading] “the god Poros, or Plenty, who is the son of Metis, or Discretion — that is, cunning intelligence”.
  - Five names for two figures in one breath.
  - "Discretion" misleads in modern English (tact, keeping secrets), and the gloss has to undo it.
  - "Plenty" doesn't explain why the son is later “always scheming … full of resources”: that trait comes from Metis.
  - [PK] "Poros" is closer to "Resource" or "Means"; rendering it that way would make 7.48 hang together.
- **7.48 → 7.49** [misleading; a contradiction] “and like his mother he is always in need” … “and so he is never in need and never rich”. Two paragraphs apart, and flatly contradictory. I understood the second as "never utterly without resources, never rich" only through [PK]. Something like "never quite destitute, never rich" would keep both statements true.
- **7.48** [ear, mild] “at another he is dead; and then he comes alive again”. Startling, but understood as dying away and reviving.
- **7.50** [ear] “But someone may ask: what is it about the beautiful that love wants, Socrates and Diotima?” The vocative confuses: who is asking whom? Understood: an imagined questioner addressing them both.
- **7.50** [archaic, mild] “"To what you have asked," I replied, "I have no answer ready."”
- **7.50** [ear] “if the one who loves loves the good, what is it that he loves?” “loves loves” trips the ear. It also switches from "what does he desire?", the question just asked about beauty, to "what does he love?". That breaks the parallel the listener is tracking.
- **7.51** [ear; a hinge] “"Why, then, Socrates," she asked, "do we not say that all men love, but only some of them? Yet you say that all men are always loving the same things."” Aloud this misparses as "why don't we say that all men love but only some of them [do something]?" Intended: "Then why don't we say everyone is in love? Why only some people?" This question sets up the whole poetry analogy.
- **7.51** [ear, mild] “Our word for poetry literally means 'making'” and “every passage from not-being into being”. "Our" means the Greek word, though the English etymology happens to work, so it half-lands. The second phrase is abstract.
- **7.52** [gap, mild] “Only those whose love takes one particular form are given the name that belongs to the whole”. The particular form (romantic or sexual love) is never named, so the listener has to supply it.
- **7.52** [misleading by ear] “And men will cut off their own hands and feet and throw them away, if they are bad.” "They" can be heard as the men. The meaning is: if the limbs are diseased.
- **7.52** [ear] “For people do not love what is their own just because it is their own — unless perhaps someone calls whatever belongs to him good, and whatever belongs to another bad.” The "unless" clause is hard to hold. Understood: we love our own only when we count it as good.
- **7.52** [gap] “"And not only the possession, but the everlasting possession of the good?" "That must be added too."” "Everlasting" is added with no reason. It can be rebuilt from the healthy-man example (7.20–7.22: we want to keep good things in the future), but nothing points back to it. Everything about immortality hangs on this one word.
- **7.53** [opaque; partly intended] “The goal they have in view is birth in beauty, whether of body or of soul.” Socrates says he doesn't understand either, which is good. But in his reply, “The oracle needs explaining”, "oracle" is unglossed. I read it as "that's a riddle".
- **7.53** [misleading/opaque; major] “I mean that all human beings are pregnant, in body and in soul.”
  - To a modern listener this is biologically backwards: everyone, men included, is pregnant *before* any union.
  - The next sentence then says “This procreation is the union of man and woman”.
  - It only makes sense at 7.59 (“Those who are pregnant in body only turn to women”), where "pregnant" turns out to mean full of seed or creative potential. That is about six paragraphs of fog.
  - A cue would carry the listener, e.g. "pregnant, so to speak: full of something waiting to be born".
- **7.53** [ear] “procreation must take place in beauty, not in ugliness”. "In beauty" means "in the presence of something beautiful", which only 7.60 shows.
- **7.54** [opaque] “Beauty, then, is the Fate and the goddess of childbirth who presides over birth.” "The Fate" is an unexplained figure from mythology. Understood: Beauty acts as the birth-goddess or midwife.
- **7.54** [ear; who is speaking?] “"Yes," I said. "Yes, indeed," she replied. "But why of reproduction?" "Because for a mortal creature, reproduction is a kind of eternity and immortality," she replied.”
  - Who asks “But why of reproduction?” It comes after one “she replied” and before another.
  - "Why of reproduction" is elliptical: why is love *of* reproduction?
  - The "Yes" / "Yes, indeed" exchange sounds empty.
- **7.54 / 7.55** [ear] “So love is also of immortality.” / “If you believe that love is of the immortal”. The formula "love is of X" is unidiomatic aloud; it can sound like "made of" or "belonging to". Yet it is the chapter's backbone phrase. Once past the father and brother section, "love is for …" or "love aims at …" would be kinder to listeners.
- **7.56** [ear] “not only do whole branches of knowledge spring up and decay in us, so that we are never the same with respect to them, but each one of them, individually, undergoes the same change”. The referent of “each one of them, individually” is unclear: each branch, or each fact?
- **7.56** [misleading/PK] “For what does the word 'recollection' imply, if not that knowledge departs?” In plain English it half-works: you only need to recollect what has left you. But from [PK], "recollection" is a loaded Platonic term for something else, and the idea here is closer to study or revision. "Study" or "review" would serve both kinds of reader.
- **7.56** [opaque] “but the immortal shares in it in another way”. How does the immortal "share in" immortality? It simply is immortal. I couldn't unpack this.
- **7.58** [tone] “And she answered with all the authority of an accomplished sophist, one of those professional teachers of wisdom”. Praise, or a dig? The gloss differs from the one in 7.48. I suspect gentle mockery only through [PK].
- **7.58** [ear] “I am convinced that all men do all things, and the better they are the more they do them, in the hope of the glorious fame of immortal virtue”. “do all things … do them” tangles aloud.
- **7.59** [ear, mild] “conceive what it is proper for the soul to conceive or contain”. “or contain” puzzles.
- **7.59** [archaic, mild] “he wants to beget offspring and bring them to birth”. "Beget" plus "bring to birth" mixes father and mother roles. That fits the metaphor, but it's dizzying.
- **7.60** [misleading, mild] “And so the two of them are married by a far closer bond”. "Married" is figurative; a young listener may take it literally.
- **7.62** [opaque] “First, if his guide leads him rightly”. Who is "his guide"? Never introduced. The idea comes back in 7.65: “or of being led by another”.
- **7.62** [misleading by ear] “how foolish he would be not to recognize that the beauty in every body is one and the same!” Aloud, "every body" is "everybody". A point about bodies becomes a claim that all people are equally beautiful. "In all bodies" avoids the trap.
- **7.62** [tone/misleading] “he will relax his intense passion for the one, looking down on it and thinking it a small thing, and he will become a lover of all beautiful bodies”. A modern teenager may hear advice to be promiscuous. Nothing signals that this is about appreciation.
- **7.63** [gap] “until he is compelled to contemplate and see the beauty of institutions and laws”. Compelled by whom, or by what? The step from souls to laws is the least motivated rung on the ladder.
- **7.63 vs 7.64** [misleading, mild] “the knowledge of beauty everywhere” vs “nor does it exist in any other being, such as an animal, or the heavens, or the earth, or any other place”. For an attentive listener, "everywhere" and "in no place" collide.
- **7.64** [ear, mild] “not beautiful at one time, or in one relation, or in one place”. "In one relation" is abstract.
- **7.64** [tone, mild] “When someone rises from these under the influence of true love”. "True love" has a fairy-tale or romance ring.
- **7.65** [ear] “And the true order of going, or of being led by another, to the things of love is this”. Awkward to hear.
- **7.65** [misleading; the recap doesn't match] “from beautiful bodies to beautiful practices, and from beautiful practices to beautiful notions; until from beautiful notions he arrives at the notion of absolute beauty”.
  - The recap renames and drops rungs. Souls (7.62–7.63) vanish, “institutions and laws” become “practices”, and “branches of knowledge” become “notions”.
  - A listener comparing the two tellings can't be sure it's the same ladder.
  - "Notion of absolute beauty" makes Beauty sound like a mere idea, just before 7.66 insists it is “not an image but a reality”.
- **7.65** [tone] “beautiful boys and young men, whose presence now entrances you”. The same issue with "boys" as in 5.3. It does set up Alcibiades' remark in 8.26 nicely.
- **7.65** [archaic/tone] “not clogged with the pollutions of mortality and all the colors and vanities of human life” and “hold communion with that true beauty, simple and divine”. Ornate and churchy.
- **7.66** [tone/misleading] “he can become the friend of God and be immortal”. Singular "God" again.
- **7.66** [ear, mild] “give birth not to images of beauty but to realities”. The listener must work out that "realities" means the "true virtue" of the next sentence.
- **7.67** [tone, mild] “I walk in his ways”. A biblical ring.

### What works
- **7.14** “whether "necessarily," and not "probably," is the right word”. Natural, modern and exact.
- **7.20** The imagined talk with the rich or healthy man is the clearest step in the refutation: “don't you really mean that you want to have in the future what you have now?”
- **7.24, 7.26** Summaries exactly where a listener needs them.
- **7.28** Agathon's own words come back to him, close enough to 6.4 to recognise, so the trap is visible.
- **7.39** “I fear that I did not understand what I was saying.” A satisfying payoff.
- **7.45** “I shall take both parts myself, hers and mine”, together with Socrates admitting he once stood where Agathon stands. Warm and clearly signposted.
- **7.46** The definition of right opinion is excellent: “being right without being able to give a reason for it”. The smiling proof that Love is no god is a clean syllogism.
- **7.47** “He carries the prayers and sacrifices of men across to the gods, and brings the commands and replies of the gods to men.” And “these spirits, or in-between powers” is a clear gloss.
- **7.48** Love as barefoot, homeless and scheming (“far from being tender and beautiful, as most people imagine him”) is vivid, and it overturns Agathon point by point. It also sets up Socrates barefoot on the ice in 8.32, an echo a first-time reader can catch.
- **7.49** “A child could answer that question” and “confusing love with the beloved — what loves with what is loved”. The key clarification, clearly put.
- **7.50** “there is no need to ask why a man desires happiness; that answer is already final.”
- **7.51** The analogy of poetry as "making" works without a word of Greek.
- **7.52** “you hear people say that lovers are seeking their other half”. A recognisable nod to Aristophanes, confirmed at 8.0.
- **7.54** The body relaxing near beauty and shrinking from ugliness is vivid.
- **7.55** Animals starving and fighting for their young: concrete and persuasive.
- **7.56** Hair, flesh, bones, blood, moods and opinions, all constantly renewed. “the old and worn-out mortal thing leaving behind another that is new and similar” is one of the clearest lines in the packet.
- **7.58** Alcestis, Achilles and Codrus need no background in context.
- **7.60–7.61** Poems and laws as children. “no temple was ever raised in anyone's honor for the sake of his mortal children” is memorable, and “barbarians — Greeks and non-Greeks alike” is a good gloss.
- **7.63** “please give me your very best attention”. A perfect cue for listeners before the climax.
- **7.64** The description of Beauty itself is rhythmic and clear aloud.
- **7.65** “using these only as steps” gives a clear ladder. “content to live just seeing them and talking with them, without food or drink” is human and funny.

### Rating: 3 / 5
A patient reader can follow the argument, and the recaps help. By ear, though:
- The logic breaks at its hinges (7.0, 7.10, 7.22, 7.32–7.34, 7.51, 7.54).
- The pregnancy metaphor (7.53–7.54) strands a first-time listener for several paragraphs.
- Word choices create contradictions (7.48 vs 7.49; 7.63 vs 7.64; the 7.65 recap).
- Untagged turns in the nested Diotima dialogue make it hard to keep track of who is speaking.

## Chapter 8: Alcibiades

### Argument summary

**Frame (8.0–8.22).** Alcibiades bursts in drunk to crown Agathon and finds Socrates next to him. After some mock-jealous banter, he crowns Socrates too and makes everyone drink. Asked for a speech, he refuses to praise anyone but Socrates.

**Alcibiades' praise of Socrates (8.23–8.38).** Claim: Socrates is like a Silenus statue, comic and ugly outside and full of divine images inside. He is also like the satyr Marsyas, except that he enchants with words alone.
- His words overwhelm people. Alcibiades weeps, feels ashamed of his life in politics, and flees Socrates as sailors flee the Sirens.
- Socrates is the only person who makes him ashamed. He sometimes wishes Socrates dead, but knows he would grieve if he died.
- Outside, Socrates plays the lovesick know-nothing. Inside, he has perfect self-control and contempt for beauty, wealth and honour.
- Proof 1, the seduction. Alcibiades offered himself in exchange for wisdom. Socrates refused to trade “gold armor … for bronze” and slept beside him as chastely as a father would.
- Proof 2, the war.
  - He endured hunger and cold, barefoot on the ice, and never got drunk.
  - He stood thinking from one dawn to the next.
  - He saved the wounded Alcibiades, then made sure Alcibiades got the prize for valour.
  - He stayed calm in the retreat from Delium.
- Socrates is unlike anyone who has ever lived; only Silenus and the satyrs compare. His talk is Silenus-like too: donkeys and cobblers outside, the most divine meaning inside.
- Blame: he plays the lover to young men, then makes them court him. Agathon, beware.
- The logic holds. The argument works through images and anecdotes, and the Silenus image is set up (8.23) and explicitly paid off (8.26, 8.37).

**Socrates' reply and the ending (8.39–8.46)**
- Socrates says the whole speech is a disguised ploy to split him from Agathon: a "satyr play", now exposed.
- Seating comedy: Agathon moves next to Socrates so as to be praised by him, and Alcibiades protests.
- Revellers crash in. At dawn Socrates is arguing that the same person can write both comedy and tragedy. The poets fall asleep, and Socrates leaves and spends his day as usual.

### Unclear points
- **8.0** [opaque] “the sound of a flute-girl could be heard”. Her role, a musician hired for the party, isn't explained [PK].
- **8.0** [opaque; major] “A little while later they heard the voice of Alcibiades ringing through the courtyard.” Alcibiades gets no introduction.
  - A first-time listener has to piece him together: handsome (8.26), wealthy with attendants, involved in politics (8.24 “busying myself with the affairs of the Athenians”), a cavalryman (8.36).
  - Who he is, and what he later became, is [PK]. That background gives an edge to 8.24 and to the "uninitiated" joke in 8.28.
  - A one-line tag introducing him would help.
- **8.0** [archaic, mild] “'Hail, friends,' he said”.
- **8.0** [opaque/PK] “Or shall I just crown Agathon, which is what I came to do” and “I couldn't come yesterday”. Why crown Agathon? Nothing in my packet says he has just won a prize for a tragedy. I know it only through [PK], though "yesterday" hints at a celebration.
- **8.0** [ear, mild] “will you accept the terms I mentioned”. Which terms? Understood: taking him in drunk.
- **8.2** [opaque] “'Take off his sandals,' said Agathon, 'and let him make a third on the same couch.'” The custom of reclining two or three to a couch is never explained. A modern teenager may find men lying together on couches odd, and the custom is needed for the seating comedy in 8.40–8.45.
- **8.5** [misleading, mild] “and not just once, as you were the day before yesterday, but always”. This clashes with 8.0's “I couldn't come yesterday”. When was the victory? "The other day" would remove the clash.
- **8.5** [ear, mild] “this universal despot”. Odd, but explained by the next clause.
- **8.6** [archaic] “which is a thing not to be endured” / “until you are well drunk” / “and bade the attendant fill it again for Socrates” / “and not be at all nearer being drunk”.
- **8.6** [misleading, mild] “bring me that wine-cooler” / “a vessel holding more than two quarts”. "Wine-cooler" now also names a sweet bottled drink, and "quarts" means little to many listeners.
- **8.8** [archaic/opaque] “'Hail, worthy son of a most wise and worthy sire!'” "Sire" means father. The joke, that his father was also a doctor, is [PK].
- **8.11–8.12** [ear/opaque/archaic] “'"The wise physician skilled our wounds to heal"'” / “'shall prescribe, and we will obey. Understood: "You're the doctor: prescribe."

The “spoken / well drunken” jingle is archaic, and the directions (“left to right”, “right hand neighbor”) are hard to picture by ear.

- **8.21** [archaic] “'I not only permit, but exhort you to speak the truth.'”
- **8.23** [tone] “'And now, my boys,”. Sounds fatherly or schoolmasterly when addressed to grown men.
- **8.23** [misleading by ear] “For the melodies of Olympus, Marsyas's pupil, come from Marsyas”. "Olympus" is heard as the gods' mountain before the gloss arrives. Putting the role first ("the musician Olympus") would fix it.

- **8.24** [opaque, mild] “I have heard Pericles and other great orators”. Pericles is only glossed later, in 8.37.

The most heartfelt paragraph of the speech is the one left in the old register.
- **8.26** [misleading, mild/PK] “he spends his whole life mocking and scorning them”. This sounds cruel. I take it as his famous irony, pretending not to know, only through [PK].
- **8.28** [tone] “as the proverb says, "In vino veritas": in wine there is truth”. A Latin proverb from a fifth-century Athenian is jarring, though the gloss keeps the sense.

### What works
- **8.0–8.3** The entrance is great theatre: roaring for Agathon, the ivy and violets, ribbons over his eyes so he doesn't see Socrates, and the leap at “By Heracles”.
- **8.4** Socrates' mock plea for protection is funny and clear.
- **8.22** “don't be surprised if I jump around … not an easy task for a man in my condition”. Natural and modern.
- **8.23** The Silenus bust (hollow, opening down the middle, gods inside) is explained well enough to carry the whole speech.
- **8.24** “Corybantian revelers — those worshipers who dance in a religious frenzy” is a model gloss. “I would grow old sitting at his feet” and “neglecting the needs of my own soul and busying myself with the affairs of the Athenians” are powerful and plain.
- **8.26** “His outer mask is the carved head of the Silenus; … when he is opened up, what self-control you find inside!” This ties the image explicitly to the man.
- **8.27** The escalating seduction attempts (dismissing the attendant, the wrestling, the dinner) are comic, clear and well paced. “as if our roles were reversed and he were the handsome youth and I the older lover with designs on him” explains the Athenian custom in passing. Excellent.
- **8.28** “bitten by something worse than a viper … the pang of philosophy”.
- **8.29–8.30** The gold-for-bronze bargain is self-contained, and “as if I had been sleeping beside my father or an elder brother” is a clean punchline.
- **8.31–8.36** The war stories are concrete and cinematic: barefoot on the ice; standing in thought from dawn to dawn while soldiers drag out mats to watch; the rescue and the prize; the calm retreat from Delium, “stalking like a pelican”.
- **8.37** “he talks about pack-donkeys and blacksmiths and cobblers and leather-workers” makes the outside/inside contrast concrete.
- **8.38** “He starts out as their lover, the older man courting them, and ends up with them courting him instead.” A perfect gloss.
- **8.39** The satyr-play gloss: “like the bawdy comic pieces staged after the tragedies”.
- **8.46** The ending is modern, clear and charming. It pays off 8.31: “no human being had ever seen Socrates drunk … his powers will be tested before long”.

### Rating: 4 / 5
Alcibiades' speech is the most vivid and easily followed part of the packet. What holds the chapter back:
- Alcibiades is never introduced.
- The archaic banter clusters (8.6–8.21, 8.40–8.45) and 8.25.
- The doctor joke (8.8–8.12) depends on context the packet doesn't give.
- The couch-seating comedy can't be pictured by ear.
- Words that trip the ear: "Olympus", "In vino veritas".

## Points I understood only through prior knowledge [PK]

My understanding of each of these came from background knowledge, not from the text:
1. Aristodemus is the source of the whole reported account (6.7, 6.11, 8.46). This may be set up in ch. 1.
2. Agathon is a tragic poet who has just won a prize (5.11, 8.0, 8.5). This is what makes the 8.46 punchline work.
3. Eryximachus is a doctor, the son of the doctor Acumenus (6.3, 6.7, 8.8–8.12).
4. Aristophanes' lines about statesmen, adulterers and a "manly" Agathon are comic jabs, and Pausanias and Agathon are a couple (5.3, 5.4, 5.6).
5. Where the genitals were before Zeus moved them (5.3).
6. Hesiod is a poet, Parmenides a philosopher, and Necessity a primordial power (6.0, 6.4).
7. Agathon's finale imitates Gorgias' jingling style (6.9).
8. The ambiguity in the Greek "love of a father" that lies behind the disclaimer in 7.0.
9. The Greek word behind "beautiful" also means "fine" or "noble", which makes "the good is beautiful" (7.40) natural.
10. "Daimon" does not mean "demon" (7.47).
11. "Poros" is closer to "Resource" than "Plenty". This reconciles "full of resources" (7.48) with "never in need" (7.49).
12. The "mysteries" are religious initiations taken in stages (7.47, 7.62, 8.28).
13. "Recollection" in 7.56 means study or revision, not Plato's famous doctrine of that name.
14. Who Alcibiades is, and why his lines about politics and the "uninitiated" carry an edge (8.0, 8.24, 8.28).
15. The custom of dining on couches, and what "below me" means (8.2, 8.41–8.43).
16. "The enduring man" is Odysseus (8.34).
17. Socrates' irony, as distinct from mockery (8.26).
18. The Lyceum was a gymnasium (8.46).
19. The passages that haven't been modernized read like Jowett's Victorian translation (cross-chapter finding A).
20. Older spellings: Otys for Otus, Diomede for Diomedes, Athene for Athena. Harmless for a listener.
