## Findings

**No blocking defects found in this packet.**

- **Paquette's monologue** ([24.13]): one of the longest single-paragraph
  speeches in the book, detailing seduction, disease, employer abuse,
  wrongful imprisonment, coerced remarriage-by-blackmail, and prostitution.
  Checked clause by clause — every specific event (the Grey Friar
  confessor, the surgeon's jealous wife, the poisoned "remedy," the judge's
  condition for release, being "supplanted by a rival") survives in the
  same sequence with no compression or softening. This is exactly the kind
  of passage where a rewrite could quietly sand down the account into
  something gentler; it does not happen here — the final line ("you would
  conclude that I am one of the most unhappy creatures in the world")
  lands with the same weight as the source.
- **Pococurante's literary criticism** ([25.9]–[25.26]): the specific
  objections to Homer (repetitive battles, Helen barely appearing, Troy
  never falling), Virgil (naming Cloanthus, Achates, Ascanius, Latinus,
  Amata, Lavinia as flat characters), Horace (the Brundisium trip, the
  Rupilius/vinegar-tongued quarrel, the Maecenas flattery), Cicero
  (Rabirius, Cluentius), and Milton (the compasses, Sin and Death, the
  cannonading devils) are all reproduced with every named reference intact
  — none dropped, none substituted, none "corrected." This is a
  proper-noun-dense passage that would be easy to quietly thin out; it
  wasn't.
- **The six dethroned kings** (chapter 26): each king's specific
  biographical claim (Achmet III's nephew/vizier detail, Ivan's cradle
  dethronement, Charles Edward's "eight hundred adherents...hanged, drawn,
  and quartered," the two rival Polish kings, Theodore of Corsica's coined-
  money-to-farthing reversal) is preserved with exact figures and no
  actor confusion — this is a section with six near-identical speech
  patterns in a row where copy-paste drift or attribution swaps would be
  easy to introduce; checked each king's lines against the correct
  speaker and found no cross-attribution errors.
- **Pangloss's resurrection story** ([28.3]): the anatomically specific
  and macabre account (the crucial incision from navel to clavicle, the
  sub-deacon executioner, the wet rope, the surgeon fainting, the wife's
  fear of a "heretic" possessed by the devil) is reproduced in full, with
  the same black comic momentum, not summarized.
- **The Baron's continued opposition to the marriage** ([29.3]–[29.6],
  [30.0]): the causal chain — Candide only proceeds with the marriage
  because the Baron's "impertinence" provokes him, not from his own
  desire — is preserved exactly, including the explicit statement at [30.0]
  that "At the bottom of his heart Candide had no wish to marry Cunegonde."
  This is a load-bearing causality/motivation detail (it recontextualizes
  the whole "happy ending") and it survives intact.
- **The dethroning-of-kings catalogue** ([30.25]): all eighteen-plus named
  historical/biblical figures (Eglon, Ehud, Absalom, Nadab, Baasha, Elah,
  Zimri, Ahaziah, Jehu, Athaliah, Jehoiada, Jehoiakim, Jeconiah, Zedekiah,
  Croesus, Astyages, Darius, Dionysius of Syracuse, Pyrrhus, Perseus,
  Hannibal, Jugurtha, Ariovistus, Caesar, Pompey, Nero, Otho, Vitellius,
  Domitian, Richard II, Edward II, Henry VI, Richard III, Mary Stuart,
  Charles I, the three Henrys of France, Henry IV) are all present in the
  candidate in the same order, with the sentence left unfinished by
  Candide's interruption exactly as in the source ("You know—"). No names
  dropped, none substituted.
- **The famous closing lines** ([30.26], [30.31], [30.32]) — "we must
  cultivate our garden" / "let us cultivate our garden" — are rendered
  consistently across their three occurrences and match the source's
  repetition pattern exactly. This is the single most load-bearing phrase
  in the book; verified it is not paraphrased differently in different
  places.

## Satire/tone check (Candide-specific)

The ending is the book's ultimate test of whether the modernization
preserves irony over sincerity: Candide's "happy ending" is materially
diminished (an ugly, bitter wife; a shrinking fortune; bored philosophers;
a farm), and Pangloss's closing speech ([30.31]) still asserts the "best of
all possible worlds" chain of causation completely unrevised in his
convictions, immediately undercut by Candide's flat, non-triumphant "let us
cultivate our garden." The candidate does not add narratorial commentary
explaining that this is ironic, does not soften Cunegonde's described
ugliness, and does not give Pangloss a more chastened or self-aware tone
than the source allows him. The deadpan is intact through the final line.

**ACCEPT AS-IS** for chapters 24–30. No fixes required.
