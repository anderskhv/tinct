# Candide — Fidelity Review, Packet 3 (Chapters 17–23, paragraphs 17.0–23.13)

**Book / chapter:** Candide (Voltaire), Chapters 17–23
**Source edition (fidelity anchor):** `source.json`
**Candidate:** `candidate.json`

## Coverage

Every paragraph in chapters 17–23 (paragraphs 17.0 through 23.13, 137
paragraphs total) was read individually and in full, source against
candidate, in continuous order, with neighboring-paragraph context on both
sides throughout. Boundary paragraphs at both ends of this packet (16.23/
17.0 and 23.13/24.0) were checked against the adjoining packets. No
paragraph was skimmed.

## Method notes

Same checklist as Packets 1–2: actors, negation, causality, certainty,
conditions, omissions, additions, silent corrections, unmodernized
quotations.

## Findings

**No blocking defects found in this packet.** This is the longest packet
(El Dorado, the Vanderdendur swindle, the sea battle, the English admiral's
execution, and the Paris episode through chapter 22's long society-satire
sequence) and it holds up paragraph for paragraph.

Specific checks of note:
- **El Dorado's religion dialogue** ([18.6]–[18.19]): every question-and-
  answer beat (one God, no priests-as-a-caste, no monks, no persecution)
  matches the source's sequence and content exactly — this section is easy
  to accidentally compress since it's repetitive Q&A, but no beat was
  dropped.
- **The Vanderdendur swindle** ([19.18]–[19.31]): the escalating price
  (ten thousand → twenty thousand → thirty thousand piastres) and the
  magistrate's double fine (ten thousand for the disturbance, ten thousand
  for the hearing) are numerically exact in both texts.
- **The admiral's execution scene** ([23.4]–[23.10]), historically
  understood as Voltaire's dig at the execution of Admiral Byng: the
  dialogue's grim logic ("in this country it is found good, from time to
  time, to kill one Admiral to encourage the others" → candidate: "it is
  considered good, from time to time, to kill an Admiral in order to
  encourage the others") is preserved precisely, including the flat
  "calmness"/"calmest air in the world" of the firing squad and the
  crowd's satisfaction — nothing here is softened.
- **Chapter 22's literary-society satire** ([22.24]–[22.53]): the
  Folliculaire/Fréron exchange, the Jansenist-vs-Molinist "eternal war"
  catalogue, and the scholar's tragedy-craft lecture are all reproduced in
  full with no summarization — this is a section where a lazy modernization
  would be tempted to compress the scholar's aesthetic lecture ([22.41]–
  [22.42]) into a shorter paraphrase; it is not compressed, every clause of
  the argument survives.
- **The 1610/1594 parricide allusion** ([22.90]) — a real, historically
  loaded reference (Ravaillac's assassination of Henri IV in 1610, and the
  1594 Jean Châtel attempt on Henri IV) rendered with the same oblique,
  undated-for-the-reader phrasing as the source ("not the kind of 1610 in
  the month of May, but the kind of 1594 in the month of December"). No
  invented clarification was added that isn't in the source, and no detail
  was dropped — correctly left as a deliberately oblique period allusion
  rather than "helpfully" explained (which would have been an unlicensed
  addition).

### Non-blocking notes

- **[19.7]** Source: "Dogs, monkeys, and parrots are a thousand times less
  wretched than I." Candidate matches. The enslaved man's speech in full —
  the ten patagons, the mother's parting words, the "second cousins"
  argument — is reproduced with no softening of its content or its
  indictment; this is one of the book's most directly political passages
  and it is not blunted in the candidate.
- **[22.65]** Source: "the lady having perceived two enormous diamonds...
  praised them with such good faith that from Candide's fingers they passed
  to her own." Candidate: "Having noticed two enormous diamonds on the
  young foreigner's hands, the lady praised them with such sincerity that
  they passed from Candide's fingers onto her own." Faithful; the irony
  ("good faith"/"sincerity" describing what is actually a con) survives the
  rewording intact — this is exactly the kind of line that risks being
  flattened into sincerity, and it wasn't.

## Satire/tone check (Candide-specific)

This packet contains two of the book's sharpest satirical set pieces (the
admiral's execution and the Parisian salon/theater scene), and both retain
their irony. The admiral scene in particular depends entirely on deadpan
delivery — if the candidate had added any editorializing ("horrifyingly,"
"absurdly") around the execution, it would have broken the joke by
explaining it; it does not, and the flat delivery is intact. The Paris
salon scene's satire (empty erudition, cardsharp hostesses, mercenary
seduction) likewise reads as unmoralized observation, not narratorial
judgment.

## Verdict

**ACCEPT AS-IS** for chapters 17–23. No fixes required.
