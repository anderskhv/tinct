# Candide — Fidelity Review, Packet 2 (Chapters 9–16, paragraphs 9.0–16.23)

**Book / chapter:** Candide (Voltaire), Chapters 9–16
**Source edition (fidelity anchor):** `source.json`
**Candidate:** `candidate.json`

## Coverage

Every paragraph in chapters 9–16 (paragraphs 9.0 through 16.23, 121
paragraphs total) was read individually and in full, source against
candidate, in continuous order, giving each paragraph neighboring context
on both sides. The last paragraph of Packet 1 (8.9) and first of this
packet (9.0) were re-checked together for boundary continuity, as were
16.23 and the first paragraphs of Packet 3. No paragraph was skimmed.

## Method notes

Same checklist as Packet 1: actors, negation, causality, certainty,
conditions, omissions, additions, silent corrections, unmodernized
quotations.

## Findings

**No blocking defects found in this packet.**

The killing of the Jew and the Inquisitor ([9.1]–[9.7]), Candide's
self-justifying interior reasoning ([9.7]), the Old Woman's two-chapter
autobiography (all of chapter 11–12), and the Jesuit-Paraguay episode
(chapter 14) all preserve their actors, sequence of events, and specific
numeric/geographic detail (thirty miles, the Sierra Morena, "seventy-two
quarterings," the Emperor Muley-Ismael's fifty sons, "three hundred
leagues," etc.) exactly.

### Non-blocking notes

- **[11.1]** Source: "begging of the corsair an absolution _in articulo
  mortis_." Candidate: "begging the pirate for absolution in articulo
  mortis." The Latin tag is carried over untranslated in both source and
  candidate. Per the modernization mandate, quoted/embedded formulas should
  generally be modernized unless the source's own point depends on the
  exact foreign wording. Here nothing in the surrounding text depends on
  the Latin specifically (unlike the Italian exclamations below) — this is
  a legitimate **candidate for a light fix**: either translate to "absolution
  at the point of death" or add a brief in-line gloss. Not blocking (the
  scene's meaning — soldiers begging for a deathbed pardon before being
  killed anyway — is still recoverable from context), but worth a fix pass.
- **[11.6] and [12.6]** Source keeps the eunuch's exclamation in Italian
  ("_O che sciagura d'essere senza coglioni!_" / "_Ma che sciagura d'essere
  senza coglioni!_"); candidate does too, unchanged. **This is correct, not
  a defect** — the very next paragraph [12.0] hinges on the Old Woman
  recognizing it as "my native language," so translating the line into
  English would break the plot logic of the recognition scene. Confirmed
  this is a deliberate, fidelity-correct choice, distinct from the Latin
  tag above.
- **[12.7], [12.10]–[12.16]** Ottoman/Russian-campaign proper nouns and
  titles (Dey, Aga of the Janissaries, Palus Meotides → "Sea of Azov" in
  candidate, Boyar) are preserved with one legitimate substitution: source
  uses the archaic Latin geographic name "Palus Meotides," candidate uses
  the modern equivalent name "Sea of Azov." Checked this is the same body
  of water (the source text itself glosses it as being besieged by
  Russians defending "Azof," i.e. Azov) — this is a correct, meaning-
  preserving modernization of a place name, not a silent correction of a
  fact the source got wrong. No defect.
- **[16.19]** Cacambo's speech persuading the Oreillons not to eat them is
  a dense, high-stakes rhetorical set piece; verified paragraph-by-paragraph
  that every clause of the argument (kill-your-neighbor is natural law,
  they have better food so don't eat people themselves, better to eat
  enemies than waste them, but this "Jesuit" is actually their defender,
  proof is available at the border) survives in the same order with no
  omissions. No defect.

## Satire/tone check (Candide-specific)

The Old Woman's litany of disasters (chapters 11–12) is one of the book's
most extreme black-comedy set pieces — mass rape, cannibalism-by-consent
under religious sanction (the Imam's sermon, [12.12]–[12.14]), being sold
repeatedly as property — all delivered in the same brisk, unslowed,
undramatized narration as the source. The candidate does not pause to
moralize or soften any of it (e.g., [12.9]'s catalogue of what a
fifteen-year-old endured "in less than three months" keeps its flat,
list-like delivery rather than being rewritten as sympathetic reflection).
The Imam's sermon on eating a "buttock" instead of the whole person
([12.13]) keeps its absurdist practicality intact — this is a prime spot
where "smoothing into sincerity" could have crept in, and it did not.
