# Candide — Accessibility Review (Reviewer A, blind pass)

**Book:** Candide (Voltaire) — modern-en candidate, all 30 chapters
**Reviewer stance:** Fresh read, blind to source text and drafter's notes, per
`books/prompts/accessibility-review-prompt.md`.

## Coverage statement

I read `candidate.json` in full, start to finish: all 30 chapters, all 709
paragraphs, every paragraph read individually and in full (none skimmed,
none sampled). I did not open `source.json` or any other file while writing
this review.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

This is a strong, lively modern rendering. Voltaire's deadpan comic rhythm
comes through clearly — the Pangloss/Candide "cause and effect" exchanges,
the escalating catalogue of disasters, and the dialogue-driven pacing all
read naturally as contemporary prose. Sentence construction is generally
smooth; I found very little of the "mechanically chopped" problem the
protocol warns about. The defects that exist are concentrated in one
category: **unglossed period/institutional vocabulary that recurs across
many chapters without ever being explained**, plus a handful of isolated
foreign-language or elliptical phrases. None of this breaks comprehension
of the plot — a reader will follow the story fine — but several terms will
cause real stumbles or force a reader to guess at meaning from context
alone, repeatedly, across the whole book.

## What's already working well

- The opening Pangloss "sufficient reason" monologues (1.3–1.4) and the
  Bulgarian recruitment scene (2.1–2.17) read with excellent comic timing —
  short exchanges, natural contemporary dialogue rhythm, nothing archaic
  left showing.
- Cunegonde's chapter-8 monologue and the Old Woman's two-chapter life
  story (11–12) are long single-speaker passages that could easily have
  become a wall of text; instead they're broken into readable sentences
  with clear cause-and-effect chaining.
- The Pococurante literary-criticism scene (25.9–25.26) is dense with
  proper nouns by necessity (it's a satire of erudite name-dropping) but
  the sentences themselves are clean and each judgment is easy to parse
  even if the reader doesn't know every author cited.
- The dethroned-kings dinner (26.20–26.31) and final "cultivate our garden"
  sequence (30) land cleanly; the famous closing line lands with full
  force because nothing around it is over-explained or softened.

## Paragraph-level issues

### Recurring unglossed terms (flagged once each at first occurrence, but they recur — see notes)

- **[1.0]** "he could prove only seventy-one quarterings of nobility" —
  "quarterings" (a heraldic count of noble ancestral lines) is never
  explained anywhere it appears. It recurs at **[10.14]** ("born a baroness
  of seventy-two quarterings"), **[13.6]**, and **[29.3]** ("enter the
  chapters of the nobility"/quarterings again implied) — each time doing
  real comic/plot work (it's the running joke about aristocratic snobbery),
  so a reader who doesn't know what a "quartering" is will miss why it's
  funny four separate times.
- **[6.0]** "Eight days later they were dressed in sanbenitos and their
  heads adorned with paper miters." — "sanbenitos" (the penitential
  garment worn by those condemned by the Inquisition) is unglossed. The
  surrounding sentence describes what it looks like (painted with flames
  and devils) so a reader can infer *something* but not what the garment
  actually is or why it's being worn.
- **[6.0]** / Chapter 6 title / **[6.1]** / **[8.7]** / **[9.5]** / **[13.6]**
  / **[30.2]** — "auto-da-fé" is used repeatedly (it's literally the
  chapter 6 title) but never once explained. Context makes clear it
  involves burning people, but the term itself — a formal Inquisition
  ceremony of public penance/execution — stays opaque throughout the book.
  This is a term worth a one-clause gloss on first use, since it recurs as
  a load-bearing plot term through half the book.
- **[5.14]** "A little man dressed in black, a Familiar of the Inquisition"
  — "Familiar" as an Inquisition office title (an lay officer/informant) is
  unglossed and easy to misread as "a familiar [acquaintance]" rather than
  a title.
- **[14.9]** "a spontoon in his hand" — an obscure military weapon term
  (a type of half-pike), unglossed, with no contextual cue to its
  appearance or function.
- **[12.7], [12.10], [28.1], [28.3], [30.1], [30.18]** — a cluster of
  Ottoman/administrative titles (Dey, Aga of the Janissaries, cadi,
  Ichoglan, Effendis, Pashas) recur across the back third of the book,
  none glossed. Most are recoverable from context (a "cadi" is clearly
  some kind of judge who metes out punishment; an "Ichoglan" is clearly a
  young man), but a reader unfamiliar with Ottoman court vocabulary is
  inferring blind through several chapters in a row.

### Isolated hard passages

- **[3.10]** "The orator's wife... emptied over him a full…. Oh, heavens!"
  — the ellipsis leaves what was "emptied" implicit. This mirrors a
  deliberately coy rhetorical device, but a new reader may genuinely not
  follow what physically happened here (a chamber pot being dumped on
  Candide) rather than register it as authorial delicacy.
- **[4.15]** "we would have neither chocolate nor cochineal" — "cochineal"
  (a red dye derived from insects, a real New World trade good) is
  unglossed and has no contextual support; a reader who doesn't already
  know the word gets nothing from the sentence except "some second item."
- **[8.6]** "they could not agree whether the night from Saturday to
  Sunday belonged to the old law or to the new" — this refers to Jewish
  vs. Christian sabbath observance, but "the old law"/"the new [law]" is
  an oblique way to say that, and a reader without background may not
  parse what's being contested.
- **[11.1]** "begging the pirate for absolution in articulo mortis" — a
  Latin legal/religious phrase left untranslated inside English narration
  (not inside quoted dialogue), with no gloss. "at the point of death" is
  the sense, but it's not recoverable from the sentence alone.
- **[11.6]** and **[12.6]** — "O che sciagura d'essere senza coglioni!" /
  "Ma che sciagura d'essere senza coglioni!" — Italian left untranslated
  both times it appears. The eunuch's later English dialogue (12.1, 12.6)
  makes the general subject (castration) recoverable, so the plot point
  lands, but the actual line itself stays opaque to a reader with no
  Italian.
- **[22.27]** "It is... a hack pamphleteer—a Fréron." — the Abbé names a
  real historical critic (Élie Fréron, a contemporary target of Voltaire's)
  as if the reader will recognize it; a general contemporary reader will
  not, and nothing in the sentence explains why the name itself is the
  punchline.
- **[30.25]** Pangloss's list of assassinated kings ("Eglon, King of Moab...
  Croesus, Astyages, Darius...") is a genuinely dense, unglossed wall of
  historical/biblical proper nouns. This reads as *intentionally*
  overwhelming — Candide cuts him off before he finishes — so I'm noting
  it rather than flagging it as a defect: the tedium is the joke, and a
  reader doesn't need to recognize any individual name for the scene to
  land.

## Flow / sentence construction

I did not find meaningful "choppy" prose — the specific failure mode of
short sentences that don't connect naturally. The closest candidates are
long single-paragraph monologues (Cunegonde's chapter 8, the Old Woman's
chapters 11–12, Pangloss's genealogy speech at 4.13), but in each case the
sentences are variably sized and connect with clear causal/temporal
markers ("After three months...", "At last...", "Scarcely had..."), so
they read as fluent spoken narration rather than a stitched list. No
paragraph needs restructuring on flow grounds alone.

## Summary

The book is accessible at the level of story, character, and comic timing.
The fix list is narrow and mechanical: gloss "auto-da-fé," "quarterings,"
"sanbenito," and "Familiar [of the Inquisition]" briefly on first use (they
each recur and carry real plot/joke weight); consider a light touch on the
Ottoman-title cluster in chapters 12/27–30; and decide a policy for the two
untranslated Italian exclamations and the "in articulo mortis" Latin tag
consistent with the book's modernization mandate (the protocol requires
modernizing embedded quotations — these read as candidates for that same
treatment, or at minimum a brief bracketed sense-gloss). None of this
requires touching plot, character, or the book's tone.
