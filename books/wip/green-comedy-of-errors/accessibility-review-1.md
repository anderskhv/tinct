# Accessibility Review — The Comedy of Errors (`comedy-of-errors`, modern-en)

Reviewer A pass per `books/prompts/accessibility-review-prompt.md`. Blind to
source text; candidate.json only.

**Coverage:** All 11 chapters (Act 1 Scene 1 through Act 5 Scene 1), all 690
paragraphs, read in full, start to finish, then re-scanned paragraph by
paragraph. No sampling.

## Flagged passages

**Ch1 p6** (Egeon's shipwreck narrative — one long unbroken paragraph). The
pronoun/referent tracking across "two goodly sons," "the poor woman's male
twins," "the other twins," and "the other pair" is hard to hold onto in a
single dense paragraph: *"My wife, more concerned for the younger-born, had
tied him to a small spare mast... One of the other twins was bound to him,
while I had done the same with the other pair."* A first-time reader can
lose track of which pair of boys (the merchant's sons vs. the purchased
servant twins) is meant at each clause. Not a comprehension-blocker by the
end of the paragraph, but a genuine stumble point on a first read.

**Ch2 p13**: *"Here comes the almanac that tells my exact age."* A
compressed metaphor (Dromio-as-living-clock) that lands a beat late — a
reader has to reread once the joke resolves a line later.

**Ch2 p30**: *"…and many other such permissive sins."* Grammatically odd —
"permissive" doesn't cleanly modify "sins" in a way a reader can parse on
first pass (permissive by whom? of what?). Reads as slightly garbled next to
the otherwise crisp list of con-artist types around it.

**Ch4 p13**: *"…let foolish gnats sport about…"* — "sport" as an
intransitive verb ("play/frolic") is a a period usage that a general
contemporary reader may misread as the noun. Minor, resolves in context.

**Ch6 p50**: *"…casting their rich light down toward the hot breath of
Spain, who sent whole armadas of cargo ships to be ballasted at her
nose."* The Spanish Armada allusion underneath the joke (ruby-red nose =
treasure ships drawn to Spain's "hot breath") isn't accessible without
period/historical context; the joke still reads as "extravagant, glowing
nose," so comprehension survives, but the specific wit is likely lost on a
general reader.

**Ch6 p52**: *"…she would have transformed me into a stub-tailed dog and
made me run in the kitchen wheel."* "Kitchen wheel" (a turnspit dog
treadmill) is an obscure historical image with no gloss; a modern reader
gets "some kind of servitude/torment" but not the specific picture.

**Ch8 p22**: Dromio's list describing the sergeant/officer — *"a
back-stabbing friend, a shoulder-tapper, one who blocks the passages of
alleys, creeks, and narrow lanes; a hound that runs the wrong way and yet
hunts well by scent…"* — several compressed period idioms in a row. The
comic tone ("sinister figure in disguise") comes through even if individual
images (esp. "runs the wrong way and yet hunts well by scent") don't fully
resolve. Borderline; flagging because it's the densest patch of unglossed
period-specific imagery in the play.

**Ch11 p146** (Abbess's closing speech): *"Thirty-three years have I been in
labour with you, my sons — and till this hour my heavy burden never
delivered."* The extended childbirth metaphor (33 years of "labour" =
grief since the twins were separated) needs a beat to click; a reader's
first instinct on "labour" is toil/work, not childbirth, until "heavy
burden never delivered" resolves it two clauses later.

## What's working well

The dialogue throughout is unusually clean for a modernized Shakespeare
comedy: the Dromio/Antipholus wordplay exchanges (the "why/wherefore"
routine in Ch4, the door-knocking cross-talk in Ch5, the geography-body
joke in Ch6, the "crow/crowbar" pun in Ch5) all land with natural
contemporary rhythm and no stitched-together or mechanically chopped
sentences. Character voices stay distinguishable — Adriana's aggrieved,
rhetorical registers vs. Luciana's measured caution vs. the Dromios'
rapid-fire punning vs. the Duke's formal authority vs. Egeon's lament —
none of them collapse into a generic "modern" voice. Long verse speeches
(Adriana's Ch4 p59, Luciana's Ch6 p2, the Abbess's diagnosis in Ch11 p36,
Antipholus of Ephesus's recap in Ch11 p78) are genuinely difficult material
in the source and are rendered as long-but-followable single arguments
rather than choppy fragments — a real achievement given their length.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** No chapter needs a structural rewrite or
broader pass. The flagged items above are targeted, mostly single-clause
spots (one dense narrative paragraph in Ch1, a handful of period-specific
images/idioms scattered through the comic prose scenes) that a light touch
could clarify but that do not block comprehension of plot or character on
their own. A general adult reader would follow this play's plot, jokes, and
emotional beats without difficulty.
