# Batch A Independent Review — Brothers Karamazov modern-en (chapters 1-12, Book I & Book II)

**Reviewer:** independent adversarial pass, not the drafting agent.

**Verdict: ACCEPT AS-IS.**

This is a strong batch and clears the bar the project exists to enforce (the
prior modern-en pass judged 77% mechanical/light). I read every paragraph of
all 12 chapters against the Garnett source. Structural integrity is exact. I
found no dropped content, no invented content, no meaning inversions, no
sanitizing of Fyodor Pavlovitch's crudeness or the family's dysfunction, and
no character-name drift. The modernization is genuine sentence-level
rewriting — real clause restructuring and contraction use, not a mechanical
word-swap pass — and it holds up across the very different registers this
batch requires (Fyodor's buffoonery, Zossima's homiletic cadence, Ivan's
argumentative prose, Miüsov's brittle liberal irony, the peasant women's
dialect-inflected grief, Rakitin's cynicism). This is the reader's first
encounter with the book and it reads well in contemporary English.

## 2. Fidelity — full read, all 12 chapters, all 420 paragraphs

I read every paragraph pair, not a sample. Areas given special scrutiny:

- **Fyodor Pavlovitch's buffoonery and crudeness (ch. 1-2, 7)** — the
  "tickled her" anecdote, the Napravnik/Ispravnik pun story, the Diderot
  fabrication, the "hooks in hell" riff, the "vale of roses"/monastery
  mockery, the parricide accusation scene. All preserved at full crudeness;
  nothing softened. The buffoon's self-interrupting, performative cadence
  ("H'm!... So that's where you want to be, my gentle boy?") survives as a
  distinct voice from every other character.
- **Family dysfunction (ch. 1-3)** — Adelaïda Ivanovna's contempt, Fyodor
  beating vs. being beaten by his first wife, the "harem" and drunken orgies,
  Sofya Ivanovna's suicide attempt in the loft, the abandonment of all three
  sons to Grigory. Nothing sanitized — "he did not beat his wife but was
  beaten by her," "he gathered loose women into his house, and carried on
  drunken orgies right in front of his wife" both land with full force.
- **The monastery scene (ch. 6-7)** — Fyodor's mockery of monastic celibacy
  ("not one woman goes in at this gate"), the Diderot/Metropolitan Platon
  anecdote and its walk-back, the escalating humiliation of Miüsov. Register
  differentiation between Fyodor (clowning), Miüsov (brittle irony curdling
  into anger), and the monks (grave, unmoved) is consistently maintained.
- **Ivan's Church/State argument (ch. 10)** — the year's densest
  argumentative prose (source P18 and P27 run 375 and 982 words). Checked
  clause-by-clause: every logical step of Ivan's position, Father Païssy's
  rebuttal, and Zossima's synthesis survives intact, including the "So be
  it, so be it!" refrain and the "third temptation of the devil" line.
- **The parricide scene (ch. 11)** — Dmitri's "Why is such a man alive?",
  Fyodor's "if you were not my son I would challenge you to a duel," the
  Grushenka/"creature" exchange, Zossima's prostration at Dmitri's feet. Full
  emotional and physical violence of the scene preserved, including Fyodor's
  "worse than a tom-cat in love" line about himself (rendered accurately by
  Rakitin later, not softened).
- **Rakitin's monologue (ch. 12)** — the long cynical breakdown of Ivan's
  and Dmitri's motives re: Katerina Ivanovna and Grushenka, the "sensualist
  from your father, a crazy saint from your mother" line about Alyosha, the
  "common harlot" remark about Grushenka. Kept at full cynical, gossipy
  register, distinct from Ivan's or Zossima's prose.
- **Peasant women / grief scenes (ch. 8)** — Nastasya's dead-child
  monologue, the "possessed" women, the widow's confession of wishing her
  abusive husband dead. Full emotional weight preserved, including the sung,
  repetitive "from afar off, Father, from afar off" cadence.
- **Character-name spelling** — consistent throughout: Fyodor Pavlovitch,
  Adelaïda Ivanovna, Miüsov, Zossima, Païssy, Smerdyakov, Grushenka, Katerina
  Ivanovna, Kalganov, Rakitin. No drift within the batch.

I found no meaning-inverting errors, no dropped clauses, and no factual
distortions anywhere in the 12 chapters.

## 3. Genuine-modernization quality

This is a real present-day rendering, not a light cleanup:

- Consistent, natural contraction use throughout dialogue and free-indirect
  narration ("he'd," "wasn't," "I'm," "couldn't've") where Garnett is
  contraction-free.
- Real clause-level rebuilding, e.g. ch. 4 P0: Garnett's "was simply an
  early lover of humanity, and that he adopted the monastic life was simply
  because at that time it struck him, so to say, as the ideal escape for his
  soul struggling from the darkness of worldly wickedness to the light of
  love" becomes "He was simply an early lover of humanity, and the reason he
  took up monastic life was simply that, at the time, it struck him as the
  ideal escape for a soul struggling out of the darkness of worldly
  wickedness into the light of love" — restructured, not just re-lexed.
- Register is genuinely differentiated by character, which is the harder and
  more meaningful modernization test than sentence-level naturalness alone:
  Fyodor's manic run-ons stay manic; Zossima's speech stays measured and
  homiletic ("Fear nothing, and never be afraid; and don't fret..."); Ivan's
  argument stays formal and structured; Rakitin is fast, sneering, and
  colloquial ("Bah, and there goes Miüsov driving away in his carriage").
- Idiom modernization is judicious: "confound this dinner," "to hell with,"
  "the devil take the lot of them" read as natural contemporary register
  shifts without distorting the speaker's established voice.
- French phrases and Biblical/liturgical quotations are handled thoughtfully
  (see notes-claim check below) rather than mechanically preserved or
  mechanically translated across the board.

I did not find passages that still read as period translation-ese with only
cosmetic word-swaps. This clears the modernization bar decisively.

## 4. Notes.md claims — spot-checked, with findings

- **Paragraph counts** — verified independently (§1) — accurate, matches
  exactly.
- **Name-spelling convention ("Zossima"/"Païssy")** — verified — accurate,
  no drift.
- **"No paragraph fell below 75% of source word count"** — verified
  independently with my own word-ratio check (§1) — accurate.
- **French phrases left untranslated** — **partially inaccurate.** The
  notes list five items as left in French: `un chevalier parfait`, `Il
  faudrait les inventer`, `arrière-pensée`, the Frenchman's hell quip
  (`J'ai vu l'ombre d'un cocher...`), and `coup d'état`. I checked all five.
  Four were in fact left in French exactly as claimed. But
  **`arrière-pensée` (ch. 10, source paragraph 11) was translated into
  English** as "ulterior motive," not left in French:
  - SRC (ch. 10, P11): "...with evident goodwill and apparently without the
    slightest _arrière-pensée_."
  - MOD: "...evidently with goodwill, and, it seemed, without the slightest
    ulterior motive."
  This is not a fidelity problem (the meaning is preserved, and arguably
  "ulterior motive" is the more reader-friendly choice for this less
  well-known phrase, vs. the more recognizable `coup d'état` or `arrière`
  cousin phrases), but the notes' claim that this specific phrase was "left
  untranslated" does not match the actual text. Should be corrected in the
  notes or the phrase reconsidered for consistency with the other four.
- **Versts-to-miles conversion** — the notes claim four converted distances
  ("four miles," "two hundred miles," "five miles," "three hundred miles").
  On checking the source directly: **only one of these four was actually a
  verst conversion.** Garnett's own translation already renders "two hundred
  miles," "five miles," and "three hundred miles" directly in miles in the
  source text (not versts) — these paragraphs were correctly carried over
  unchanged, not converted by the drafter. The only genuine verst→miles
  conversion in the batch is ch. 8 P6, where source "six versts" becomes
  modern-en "four miles" (correct math: 6 versts ≈ 3.98 miles). The output
  text itself is accurate in every instance; the notes simply overstate how
  much conversion work happened. Not a content or fidelity issue, just an
  inaccurate characterization in the notes.

## 5. Additional findings not flagged by the drafter

- **Copeck/kopeck spelling inconsistency.** Source uses "copeck" spelling
  consistently (2 instances: "ten-copeck piece," "sixty copecks"). The
  modern-en output is inconsistent: ch. 6 P1 keeps "ten-copeck" (matches
  source spelling), but ch. 8 P49 renders "sixty copecks" as "sixty
  kopecks" (spelling changed). A third, unrelated instance in ch. 7 P2
  changes source "farthings" (sold "for a few farthings at all the fairs")
  to "kopecks" — a defensible modernization, since farthings are a British
  coin with no place in a Russian setting and this looks like a slight
  Garnett translation artifact, but it introduces a third currency-word
  spelling variant into the batch. Recommend standardizing all three
  instances on one spelling (source's own "copeck," to keep continuity with
  the rest of Batch A/B/etc. if the source consistently uses that form).
- **"Seven righteous men" generalized to "a handful of righteous men"**
  (ch. 10, the elder's long speech, source paragraph 27): "the Christian
  society now is not ready and is only resting on some seven righteous men"
  becomes "Christian society today isn't ready for this, and rests on only
  a handful of righteous men." This drops a specific number for a vague
  quantifier. It's a minor loss of textual specificity (not a meaning
  inversion — the sense of "very few, but sufficient" survives), but "seven"
  is a real detail from Zossima's theology-adjacent speech and could be
  restored with no loss of readability ("resting on a mere seven righteous
  men" or similar).
- **Minor Biblical-quote wording drift**: ch. 7 P22, Fyodor's declamation
  "Blessed be the womb that bare thee" (source) becomes "Blessed is the
  womb that bore thee" (modern-en) — a small deviation from the source's own
  quoted wording (which itself is a slight variant on the real KJV, "Blessed
  is the womb that bare thee"). Cosmetic; doesn't change meaning or
  misrepresent the quotation's content, but worth noting since the notes
  claim scripture quotations were kept "in their traditional
  King-James-adjacent English" verbatim, and this one was lightly edited.

None of the items in §5 are fidelity violations — they're small, easily
fixed nits (spelling standardization, one restored numeral, one quote-word
choice) that a future polish pass could pick up in minutes. They do not
block downstream use.

## Recommendation

Accept Batch A as-is for downstream use (modern-da translation, audio, QA
sign-off gate). The optional nits above (copeck/kopeck spelling, "seven
righteous men," the arrière-pensée notes correction) are worth a quick fix
if a polish pass happens anyway, but none of them rise to the level of
blocking acceptance — this batch is the strongest kind of evidence that the
genuine-modernization approach is working, and it needed to be strong, since
this is the book's opening.
