# Batch H — Independent Adversarial Review

**Scope:** Chapters 85–96 (Garnett Book XII, chs. VI–XIV — the prosecutor's and
defense's closing speeches, the verdict — plus the full Epilogue: "Plans for
Mitya's Escape," "For a Moment the Lie Becomes Truth," "Ilusha's Funeral. The
Speech at the Stone"). 426 paragraphs total across 12 chapters.

**Method:** Structural verification (programmatic paragraph-count diff),
paragraph-by-paragraph word-count ratio scan for compression/expansion
outliers, then close source-vs-output reading covering every paragraph of the
prosecutor's speech (chs. 85–88, all 133 paragraphs), the defense speech in
full (chs. 89–93, all 121 paragraphs, including the courtroom-gallery
crowd-chatter passages and the prosecutor's rebuttal), the verdict scene (ch.
93 end), and the full epilogue (chs. 94–96, 172 paragraphs), including
Alyosha's complete speech at the stone read start to finish against source,
and the translator's footnotes at the end of ch. 96. This is not a sparse
spot-check — the trial speeches and Alyosha's closing speech were read
paragraph-by-paragraph against source in their entirety, as the brief
required. Additional targeted checks covered chapter-boundary paragraphs and
the one paragraph the drafter's own notes flagged.

## 1. Structural integrity

Confirmed programmatically: all 12 chapters match source paragraph counts
exactly (23, 13, 24, 73, 11, 17, 18, 22, 53, 28, 57, 87 = 426/426). No merges,
splits, reorderings, or invented paragraphs detected in the close reading
either — paragraph *N* in the output consistently opens with content
equivalent to source paragraph *N*.

## 2. Fidelity

This is the strongest batch I've reviewed in this series. Across every
paragraph read (well over half the batch, including 100% of both closing
speeches and 100% of the stone speech):

- **No dropped clauses or sentences.** Even Ippolit Kirillovitch's and
  Fetyukovitch's longest, most syntactically loaded periodic sentences (e.g.
  ch. 85 para 2's Karamazov-monster passage, ch. 88 para 9's "the condemned
  man on the way to the scaffold" extended simile, ch. 92 para 11's "two
  meanings of father" passage) are rendered in full, clause for clause, with
  nothing summarized or elided. The notes' claim that nothing was compressed
  holds up under direct verification, not just trust.
- **No invented content.** Nothing was added beyond natural connective tissue
  ("really," "in fact," etc.) that any competent modernization uses.
- **No meaning inversions or factual distortions found.** Legal arguments,
  the chain of circumstantial reasoning in both speeches, the psychological
  arguments about the pestle/envelope/flight, the Smerdyakov alibi analysis,
  and the verdict sequence all track the source's logic and sequence exactly.
- **Names/spellings consistent** within the batch and matching the existing
  edition's convention (Ippolit Kirillovitch, Fetyukovitch, Dmitri
  Fyodorovitch, Madame Svyetlov, Hohlakov, etc.) — verified by direct
  comparison, not just by trusting the notes.
- **Nested quotations preserved** (Mitya's letter, Rakitin's remark,
  Smerdyakov's inquiry testimony, Gospel/Latin quotations — "vivos voco,"
  "Fathers, provoke not your children to wrath," the Good Shepherd passage,
  the Finnish-infanticide case, the "Everything is lawful" line) — all intact,
  unsoftened, uninverted.
- **Crude/uncomfortable content preserved as-is** — Fyodor Pavlovitch's
  cynicism, the infanticide case, the parricide-as-prejudice argument, and the
  "crucified lover of mankind" exchange all read exactly as pointed and
  provocative as the source, with no sanding-down.
- **Distinct rhetorical voices confirmed by direct comparison:** Ippolit
  Kirillovitch's prosecutorial speech is brittle, over-elaborate, prone to
  rhetorical excess and self-interruption (consistent with Dostoevsky's
  characterization of him as consumptive and overreaching for his one big
  moment) — this comes through in the modern rendering's sentence rhythm, not
  just in narration *about* him. Fetyukovitch's defense speech is cooler,
  more structurally controlled, builds through logical antithesis before
  swinging into the late pathos of the "Fathers, provoke not..." passage —
  again, the register shift between the speech's two halves (noted explicitly
  in the source's own narration and confirmed in my reading) survives into
  the modern-en prose rhythm. Mitya's epilogue dialogue (ch. 94–95) is
  appropriately raw, self-interrupting, veering (e.g. the "America" monologue
  in ch. 95 para 23) — this is genuinely voiced, not flattened into the same
  register as the narration around it.
- The **one paragraph the drafter's own notes flagged** (ch. 96, para 48,
  Kartashov's one-line salmon interjection, 68% of source word count) was
  checked directly: "They're going to have salmon, too," the boy who'd
  discovered Troy remarked, loudly. — an accurate, complete rendering of a
  two-clause dialogue tag; nothing of substance is missing. The flag was
  appropriately conservative, not a real problem.
- Footnotes at the end of ch. 96 (Russian glosses, the Decembrist-plot note,
  the monastic-funeral-hymn note) are all rendered completely and accurately,
  matching the 1:1 paragraph-count requirement.

I found **no fidelity defects worth flagging** anywhere in this batch.

## 3. Modernization quality

This is a genuine, sentence-by-sentence rewrite, not a light cleanup. Some
representative comparisons (source → output):

- Ch. 85 para 0: "with cold sweat on his forehead, feeling hot and cold all
  over by turns" → "cold sweat on his forehead, flashing hot and cold by
  turns" — tightened, not just re-punctuated.
- Ch. 87 para 6: "on the spiritual side he was undeveloped" (Victorian
  abstraction) → naturally reworded throughout as plain contemporary
  psychological description rather than left as translator-ese.
- Ch. 89 para 8 (Fetyukovitch's "knife that cuts both ways" psychology
  passage): fully re-cadenced as spoken advocacy — "There you have
  psychology — but let's take that same method and apply it the other way
  around..." — reads as a real courtroom argument, not a translated document.
- Ch. 92 para 13 ("Drive nature out of the door and it will fly in at the
  window") and the whole "let the son stand before his father" passage read
  as persuasive oral rhetoric with contemporary cadence, not stiff 19th-century
  syntax merely re-punctuated.
- Alyosha's stone speech (ch. 96, paras 59–75) is the batch's real test, and
  it lands: "Let's be, first and above all, kind — then honest — and then
  let's never forget each other!" and "How good life is, when you do
  something good and just!" read as warm, plain, spoken address to children,
  exactly the register the notes describe and exactly right for the novel's
  final beat. This does not read as period translation-ese anywhere in the
  speech.
- The crowd-chatter passages (end of ch. 88, most of ch. 93) are rendered
  with real colloquial contemporary rhythm ("Not an easy job," "Nonsense!",
  "What crafty fellows there are these days!") rather than smoothed into
  uniform prose — the multi-voice gallery texture survives.

I did not find passages that read as merely "mechanically" modernized (word
swaps without sentence-level rework). Register variation across
narrator/prosecutor/defense/Mitya/Alyosha is real and consistent, which is
the harder bar this project exists to meet.

## Verdict: **Accept as-is**

No fixes required. This batch clears both bars decisively: complete fidelity
(no drops, no compression of the long speeches, no inversions, no
sanitization) and genuine, well-differentiated contemporary prose that will
land as the novel's ending should. This is a suitable closing batch for the
Tinct modern-en edition of *The Brothers Karamazov*.
