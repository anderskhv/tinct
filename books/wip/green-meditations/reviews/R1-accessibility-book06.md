# Accessibility review — Book VI (6.0–6.58)
Reviewer: candidate-only accessibility reviewer (source not seen). Coverage: 6.0, 6.1, 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 6.5, 6.6, 6.7, 6.8, 6.9, 6.10, 6.11, 6.12, 6.13, 6.14, 6.15, 6.16, 6.17, 6.18, 6.19, 6.20, 6.21, 6.22, 6.23, 6.24, 6.25, 6.26, 6.27, 6.28, 6.29, 6.30, 6.31, 6.32, 6.33, 6.34, 6.35, 6.36, 6.37, 6.38, 6.39, 6.40, 6.41, 6.42, 6.43, 6.44, 6.45, 6.46, 6.47, 6.48, 6.49, 6.50, 6.51, 6.52, 6.53, 6.54, 6.55, 6.56, 6.57, 6.58 (all 59).

Proposed rewrites are based on the candidate text only. Where the rewrite depends on how the source reads, this is marked "(check source)".

## Findings

### 6.0 — MINOR
"The reason that governs it has no cause in itself for doing evil" — "has no cause in itself for" sounds translated. A listener has to stop and work out that it means "nothing in it would make it do evil."
Proposed: "The reason that governs it has nothing in it that would lead it to do evil, because it has no malice: it does evil to nothing, and nothing is harmed by it."

### 6.3 — MINOR
"Either they will be turned into vapor, if indeed all substance is one, or they will be scattered." — The condition sits in the middle of the either/or, so a listener loses the pairing. The listener also cannot tell that "if all substance is one" explains only the vapor option.
Proposed: "Either—if indeed all substance is one single thing—they will be turned into vapor, or else they will be scattered."

### 6.4 — BLOCKING
"The governing reason knows what its own disposition is, what it does, and what material it works on." — Four sections earlier (6.0), "the reason that governs it" meant the reason that governs the universe. On one hearing, "the governing reason" here sounds like the same cosmic reason. It seems to mean the reader's own mind instead (check source). Three sections later, 6.7 calls what looks like the same thing "the ruling faculty," which adds to the confusion. "Disposition" is also a formal word a newcomer may not connect to "its own state or character."
Proposed (if the individual mind is meant): "Your own governing reason—the ruling part of your mind—knows what state it is in, what it does, and what material it works on."

### 6.7 — MINOR
"The ruling faculty is what wakes itself up and turns itself around." — This is the first use of "ruling faculty," with no gloss, and it is not linked to "governing reason" in 6.4. "Turns itself around" can also be heard as "reverses itself."
Proposed: "The ruling faculty—the part of you that thinks and decides—is what wakes itself up and turns itself in whatever direction it chooses." Pick one term for this idea and use it in both 6.4 and 6.7.

### 6.8 — MINOR
"whether a nature that surrounds this one from outside, or a nature contained inside it, or a nature outside it and independent of it" — Heard once, "surrounds from outside" and "outside it and independent of it" sound the same. "This one" and "it" have to be traced back to "the nature of the universe."
Proposed: "Certainly nothing is brought about in keeping with some other nature—whether one that surrounds the universe's nature from outside, one that is contained inside it, or one that exists apart from it and does not depend on it."

### 6.9 — MINOR
"then I revere, I stand firm, and I trust in the one who governs." — "Revere" has no object, so a listener waits for one that never arrives.
Proposed: "But if the second is true, then I feel reverence, I stand firm, and I trust in the one who governs it."

### 6.10 — MINOR
"When circumstances have forced you to be thrown off balance" — "forced you to be thrown" doubles the passive and reads as translated.
Proposed: "When circumstances have thrown you off balance in some way, come back to yourself quickly, and do not stay out of tune any longer than you are forced to."

### 6.12 — MINOR
"this Falernian wine is only a little grape juice" — The point is that something prized is only grape juice. A listener who does not know the name "Falernian" misses that it was prized, which removes the contrast. (The closing "what Crates says about Xenocrates himself" cannot be followed either. It is an allusion that the text never explains. It is not fixable without adding content, so it is only noted here.)
Proposed: "And again, that this fine Falernian wine is only a little grape juice, and this purple robe some sheep's wool dyed with the blood of a shellfish."

### 6.13 — BLOCKING
"People who are more educated still admire things held together by a rational soul—not a soul that is rational in the universal sense, but one that is rational only in being skilled in some craft or clever in some other way, or simply in owning a large number of slaves." — This is the key step of the ladder, and a listener cannot follow it. "Things held together by a rational soul" is never tied to people. The long "not… but… or" qualification loses the thread. "Rational only in… owning a large number of slaves" sounds as if owning slaves were a form of being rational. Earlier in the section, "held together simply by cohesion" and "held together by a principle of life" are also technical, but the examples that follow (stones, flocks) rescue them.
Proposed: "People who are more educated still admire things held together by a rational soul, that is, human beings. But they do not admire the soul for reason in its universal sense. They admire it only for being skilled in some craft or clever in some other way, or they simply admire owning a large number of such beings as slaves."

### 6.14 — MINOR
"like the vapor given off by the blood, and the drawing in of air. For breathing in the air once and giving it back, as we do every moment, is just the same as giving back the whole power of breathing—which you received at your birth, yesterday or the day before—to the element from which you first drew it." — "Vapor given off by the blood" is an obscure image to a modern ear. The last sentence is long, with a dash-insert in the middle. A listener loses the equation (one breath out = the final breath out).
Proposed (last sentence): "We breathe in the air once and give it back at every moment. Giving back the whole power of breathing to the element from which you first drew it—a power you received at your birth, yesterday or the day before—is just the same thing."

### 6.15 — MINOR
Three spots.
(a) "to move yourself and to hold yourself back in keeping with your own proper constitution" — A modern listener hears "constitution" as physical health or a political document, not "the way you are made."
(b) "The upbringing and teaching of the young aim at something too. Here, then, is where the value of upbringing and teaching lies." — "Something" leaves the listener unsure what they aim at. The next sentence then speaks as if it had been named.
(c) "who can take those things away… what you value… any of these things" — "those" and "these" both point back to "many other things," but they sound like two different groups.
Proposed: (a) "to move yourself and to hold yourself back in keeping with your own proper constitution, the way you are made." (b) "The upbringing and teaching of the young aim at this same kind of end too." (check source) (c) "For you are bound to be envious, jealous and suspicious of those who can take those things away, and to plot against those who have the things you value. Anyone who needs any of those things…"

### 6.19 — MINOR
"given you a wound by crashing into your head" — It is unclear what crashed into your head. Heard once, it sounds like he ran headlong into your head, which is an odd picture.
Proposed: "suppose someone has clawed you with his nails and given you a wound by butting you with his head." (check source)

### 6.21 — MINOR
"For they are either lifeless, or without reason, or they have wandered off and do not know the way." — "They" means "other things." A listener does not realize that the third group is people who have gone astray, so "wandered off" sounds odd when applied to "things."
Proposed: "For those other things are either lifeless, or without reason, or else they are beings who have wandered off and do not know the way."

### 6.24 — MINOR
"in that one and all which we call the Cosmos" — "One and all" is normally an idiom meaning "everybody," so a listener may misread it.
Proposed: "exist at the same time in that single whole, which we call the Cosmos."

### 6.25 — MINOR
"Would you not calmly go on and count out each letter in turn?" — "Count out" is an unexpected verb for spelling, and it clashes with "shout out each letter" just before.
Proposed: "Would you not calmly go on and spell out each letter in turn?"

### 6.26 — MINOR
"But that is not really so. Then teach them and show them, without being angry." — It is unclear what "that" refers to: that they suppose it, or that the things are good for them. "Then" sounds like time ("next") rather than "in that case."
Proposed: "But those things are not really suited to their nature or good for them. In that case, teach them and show them, without being angry."

### 6.29 — MINOR
"Take care that you are not turned into a Caesar" and "or a sophist" — "Sophist" is an obscure term, and a newcomer will not know it means someone who uses clever argument for show. "Turned into a Caesar" is reachable only because "dyed in that dye" follows it. (Also note that "Antoninus" here is a person Marcus learns from, while in 6.43 "as far as I am Antoninus" refers to Marcus himself. A first-time listener will find this confusing. It is flagged for awareness, with no rewrite proposed.)
Proposed: "He was not given to criticizing people, and he was not timid, or suspicious, or a clever talker for show like a sophist."

### 6.31 — MINOR
"But to the understanding…" and "only the ones that concern the present count" — "The understanding" is a new name for the mind that the listener has not heard yet. The listener also cannot tell what "count" means (count as what?).
Proposed: "But to the understanding, the mind, only those things are indifferent that are not its own activities… And even of these, only the ones that concern the present matter; for the mind's future and past activities are, for now, indifferent too."

### 6.32 — MINOR
"as long as it does a human being's work" — "It" refers to "labor" while "him" refers to the person, so the pronouns tangle.
Proposed: "In the same way, a human being's labor is not against nature for him as a human being, as long as he is doing a human being's work."

### 6.38 — MINOR
"the things among which your lot has been cast" — This is an archaic idiom.
Proposed: "Fit yourself to the circumstances you have been given; and love the people among whom you have been given your place—but love them truly, sincerely."

### 6.39 — MINOR
"Every instrument, tool or vessel is doing well if it does what it was made for, even though the one who made it is not there." — The contrast (maker outside it vs. maker inside it) is what matters. But "even though" makes it sound as if the maker's absence were a problem the tool overcomes, so the next sentence's "But…" does not land.
Proposed: "With every instrument, tool or vessel, the one who made it is outside it and no longer there; it is doing well if it does what it was made for. But in the things that nature holds together, the power that made them is inside them and stays there."

### 6.40 — MINOR
"Whatever things outside your power you suppose to be good or bad for you, it is inevitable that when such a bad thing happens to you…" — The sentence starts one way and finishes another (broken syntax), which reads as translated.
Proposed: "If you suppose that any of the things outside your power are good or bad for you, then inevitably, when such a bad thing happens to you, or you lose such a good thing, you will blame the gods."

### 6.41 — MINOR
"will take you in as some part of the fellow workers" — The phrasing is awkward. (The Chrysippus line in the play is an unexplained allusion. It is noted only.)
Proposed: "will take you in as one part among the fellow workers whose labors lead to one end."

### 6.43 — MINOR
"What would they gain from it, or the whole, which is the special object of their providence?" — The listener has to supply "what would the whole gain" from the word "or" alone. "Providence" has not been explained anywhere in this packet: it appears only once before, as a bare word, in 6.9. "So only the things that are useful to these cities" — "these cities" has to be traced back to Rome and the world.
Proposed: "What would they gain from it, or what would the whole gain—the whole that their providence, their care for the world, is chiefly concerned with?" … "So only the things that are useful to these two cities, Rome and the world, are useful to me."

### 6.46 — MINOR
"To that place, then, we must go" — It is unclear what "that place" is (where the dead are). The listener has to infer it from context several clauses back.
Proposed: "To the same place they have gone, then, we must go too, where there are so many great orators and so many noble philosophers…"

### 6.47 — MINOR
"and appear there in abundance, as far as possible" — The tacked-on "as far as possible" is awkward, and it is unclear what it qualifies.
Proposed: "when they show themselves in the characters of those who live with us, and appear there together in as great abundance as possible."

### 6.49 — BLOCKING
"Let us try to persuade them." / "your attempt was made with a reservation" / "What, then, did you want? Some such attempt as this." — Three problems.
(a) The section opens with "them," and nothing tells the listener who is meant.
(b) A modern listener hears "reservation" as doubt or a booking, not as "a built-in condition."
(c) "Some such attempt as this" does not make the idea audible. A first-time listener will not get the key point: what you wanted was only to make the attempt, so you succeed even if the outcome fails.
Proposed: "Let us try to persuade the people concerned. … And remember that you made your attempt with a condition attached: you never wanted to do the impossible. What, then, did you want? Simply to make this kind of attempt. And that aim you achieve, even if the things you set out to do are not accomplished."

### 6.54 — MINOR
"would they listen to anyone else? Or how could the helmsman…" — "Anyone else" sounds as if it means "someone other than the helmsman," which reverses the point. "Or" at the start of the second question is odd.
Proposed: "If sailors insulted their helmsman, or sick people their doctor, whom else would they listen to? And how could the helmsman keep the people on board safe, or the doctor bring health to the people he looks after?"

Recurring issue: the reader's own mind goes by three names ("governing reason" 6.4, "ruling faculty" 6.7, "understanding" 6.31), and none of them is glossed on first use. Choosing one term and glossing it once would fix several findings.
