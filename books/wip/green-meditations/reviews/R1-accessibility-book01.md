# Accessibility review — Book I (1.0–1.16)
Reviewer: candidate-only accessibility reviewer (source not seen). Coverage: 1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 1.7, 1.8, 1.9, 1.10, 1.11, 1.12, 1.13, 1.14, 1.15, 1.16.

## Findings

### 1.3 — MINOR
Problem words: "not to have gone to the public schools".
Why: Many English readers (especially British ones) take "public schools" to mean elite private boarding schools, which reverses the sense. A listener also has to hold "on things like this" across the sentence to see that it means education.
Proposed rewrite: "From my great-grandfather: not to have been sent to the public schools open to everyone, but to have had good teachers at home, and to know that on an education like this one should spend generously."

### 1.4 — MINOR
Problem words: "nor a fan of either the Parmularius or the Scutarius at the gladiator fights".
Why: The two Latin labels are opaque. A listener cannot tell that they are two kinds of gladiator, each with its own fans, which is the point that parallels the Green/Blue racing factions.
Proposed rewrite: "…nor a fan of either the Parmularius or the Scutarius, the light-shield and heavy-shield fighters, at the gladiator fights."

### 1.5 — MINOR
Problem words: "to have become at home with philosophy, and to have heard the lectures … to have written dialogues when I was young; and to have wanted a plank bed …"
Why: The list moves from lessons ("not to busy myself…", "to put up with…") to things Marcus did ("to have heard", "to have written", "to have wanted") without signalling the change. This perfect infinitive reads like translation, and a listener loses track of what "From Diognetus" governs by the end of the long sentence.
Proposed rewrite: split after "speaking their minds freely": "… to put up with people speaking their minds freely. It was through him, too, that I became at home with philosophy and heard the lectures first of Bacchius, then of Tandasis and Marcianus; that I wrote dialogues when I was young; and that I wanted a plank bed and an animal skin to sleep on, and whatever else of that kind belongs to the Greek philosophical training."

### 1.6 — MINOR
Problem words: "ambition to shine as a sophist".
Why: "Sophist" appears here for the first time with no help. A newcomer does not know it means a paid, showy public speaker, so the rest of the list (abstract theories, sermons, fancy writing) loses its common thread. The sentence is also very long for one hearing.
Proposed rewrite: "From him I learned not to be drawn into ambition to shine as a sophist, a showman of clever speeches, nor into writing about abstract theories, …" (and optionally end the sentence after "for show" and begin "I learned to keep away from rhetoric…").

### 1.7 — MINOR
Problem words: (a) "to look to nothing but reason, not even for a moment"; (b) "without either being lowered by them or letting them pass unacknowledged".
Why: (a) "not even for a moment" hangs off a positive phrase, so the negation is hard to parse by ear. (b) "Being lowered by" favours is unclear: does it mean humiliated, or put in someone's debt?
Proposed rewrites: (a) "never, even for a moment, to look to anything but reason"; (b) "how to accept what are counted as favors from friends, without either being made smaller or beholden by them or letting them pass unacknowledged."

### 1.8 — MINOR
Problem words: "the idea of living in keeping with nature"; "He never showed anger or any other passion; he was entirely free from passion, and yet full of affection."
Why: This is the first time "living in keeping with nature" appears, and a newcomer may hear it as a back-to-the-land lifestyle. It recurs in 1.16, so it matters. In modern English "passion" usually means enthusiasm or romance, so "free from passion, yet full of affection" sounds almost self-contradictory. The intended sense is disturbing, overpowering emotion.
Proposed rewrites: "the idea of living in keeping with nature, that is, as nature intends a human being to live"; "He never showed anger or any other passion; he was entirely free from such disturbing emotions, and yet full of affection."

### 1.10 — MINOR
Problem words: "those among us who are called Patricians are rather lacking in fatherly affection".
Why: A newcomer may not know that Patricians are the old aristocratic families, and "fatherly affection" is likely to be heard as "they are bad fathers". The broader point, that they lack warm natural feeling, is missed, and the link between the name and "fatherly" is lost.
Proposed rewrite: "and that, on the whole, those among us who are called Patricians, our old aristocratic families, are rather lacking in fatherly, natural affection."

### 1.11 — MINOR
Problem words: "not to say to anyone often, or without real need, or to write in a letter, that I have no time".
Why: The main verb and its object ("say … that I have no time") are split by three inserted qualifiers, and the listener has to rebuild the sentence.
Proposed rewrite: "not to say often to anyone, or write in a letter, without real need, that I have no time; …"

### 1.13 — MINOR
Problem words: "to keep up good hopes".
Why: This reads like translation, and the plural "hopes" is unidiomatic.
Proposed rewrite: "to stay hopeful".

### 1.14 — MINOR
Problem words: "He seemed like a man who could not be turned from what was right, rather than one who had needed correcting."
Why: The contrast is compressed. A listener has to work out that the point is that his goodness looked natural to him and not like the result of being reformed.
Proposed rewrite: "He seemed like a man who was naturally upright and could not be turned from what was right, rather than one who had needed correcting to become so."

### 1.15 — BLOCKING
Problem words: "that he could both do without and enjoy the things that most people are too weak to do without and cannot enjoy without excess. To be strong enough both to hold out in the one case and to stay sober in the other…"
Why: The first sentence stacks two parallel pairs ("do without / enjoy", "too weak to do without / cannot enjoy without excess") around one object. The second sentence then points back with "the one case … the other". A listener hearing it once cannot map "hold out" to going without and "stay sober" to having plenty, so the thought is lost.
Proposed rewrite: "What is recorded of Socrates could be said of him: he could do without the things that most people are too weak to do without, and he could enjoy the things that most people cannot enjoy without excess. To be strong enough both to hold out when one lacks such things and to stay sober when one has them is the mark of a man with a perfect and unconquerable soul, such as he showed during the illness of Maximus."

### 1.15 — MINOR (further items in the same section)
- "those who had been kept from going with him by some urgent business always found him the same". "Found him the same" is vague. Rewrite: "…always found him no different toward them afterward."
- "a flippant house-born slave". The insult is obscure: it points to the cheeky manner of a slave born and pampered in the household. Rewrite: "a flippant, pert house-born slave".
- "He always acted according to the institutions of his country". "Institutions" is heard as organisations. Rewrite: "according to the ancestral customs of his country".
- "His clothes came from Lorium, his villa on the coast, and mostly from Lanuvium." "Came from X … and mostly from Y" is illogical as heard. Rewrite: "His clothes came from Lorium, his villa on the coast, but mostly from Lanuvium."
- "nothing, as they say, pushed to the point of a sweat". This idiom is opaque in modern English. Rewrite: "nothing, as they say, pushed to the point of a sweat, that is, nothing forced or overdone."

### 1.16 — MINOR
- "I was never rushed into offending any of them". "Them" could point to the gods or to the relatives and friends just listed. Rewrite: "I was never rushed into offending any of the gods".
- "I thank the gods for giving me such a brother, … ; that my children … ; that I did not get further … ; …" This single sentence runs through about eighteen "that" clauses, and it starts with "for giving", which does not fit the "that" clauses. Heard aloud, the governing verb is lost. Rewrite the opening as "I thank the gods that I had such a brother, whose character …". Optionally, repeat "I thank the gods, too," about halfway through (for example, before "that my body has held out so long").
- "without being on that account any meaner in thought or any slacker in action". "Meaner" is used in the old sense of lower or humbler, and a modern reader hears "more unkind". Rewrite: "without being on that account any lower in spirit or any slacker in action".
- "instead of putting them off with the hope that I would do it later, since they were still young". The reason clause is loosely attached. Rewrite: "instead of putting them off with the hope that I would do it later, on the grounds that they were still young."
- "so obedient, so affectionate, so unaffected". "Unaffected" is easily heard as "unmoved". Rewrite: "so obedient, so affectionate, so natural and unpretentious".
- "remedies have been shown to me in dreams, for other things and against spitting blood and dizziness...". "For other things and against" is awkward. Rewrite: "remedies have been shown to me in dreams, both for other ailments and against spitting blood and dizziness...".
- "working out syllogisms, or on studying the appearances in the sky". "Syllogisms" is technical, and "the appearances in the sky" is obscure. Rewrite: "working out syllogisms in logic, or on studying the phenomena of the sky."
