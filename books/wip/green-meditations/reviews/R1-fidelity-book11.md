# Fidelity review — Book XI (11.0–11.38)
Reviewer: independent fidelity reviewer. Coverage: 11.0–11.38, all 39 paragraphs checked sentence by sentence against Long (1862) and against STYLE-AND-TERMINOLOGY.md.

## Findings

### 11.19 — BLOCKING
- Source: "And yet no force is imposed on it, but only those things which are conformable to its nature: still it does not submit, but is carried in the opposite direction."
- Candidate: "And yet nothing is forced on it except what is in keeping with its nature."
- Problem: Meaning change. Long says *no force at all* is imposed on the intelligent part; only what accords with its nature is required of it. The candidate's "nothing is forced on it except…" says that the things in keeping with its nature *are* forced on it. That breaks the contrast the section is built on: the elements are held in place "perforce", while the mind is under no compulsion and still rebels.
- Proposed: "And yet nothing is imposed on it by force; only what is in keeping with its nature is imposed on it."

### 11.0 — MINOR
- Source: "it makes what has been set before it full and complete"
- Candidate: "it makes what it set out to do full and complete"
- Problem: Long's "what has been set before it" means a task or purpose placed before the soul. "What it set out to do" turns this into the soul's own undertaking, which changes who sets the task.
- Proposed: "In every part, and wherever it is stopped, it makes what was set before it full and complete, so that it can say: I have what is my own."

### 11.7 — MINOR
- Source: "Finally, the branch, which from the first grew together with the tree…"
- Candidate: "In short, the branch that grew together with the tree from the start…"
- Problem: "Finally" adds a last point. "In short" presents the sentence as a summary of what came before, which it is not. The grafting comparison is a new observation.
- Proposed: "Finally, the branch that grew together with the tree from the start, and has always shared one life with it, is not like a branch that was cut off and then grafted back on."

### 11.14 — MINOR
- Source: "so that the bystander as soon as he comes near him must smell whether he choose or not."
- Candidate: "so that anyone who comes near him must notice it at once, whether he wants to or not."
- Problem: The image is flattened. Marcus's point about smell (you cannot help smelling it) becomes a generic "notice".
- Proposed: "so that anyone who comes near him must smell it at once, whether he wants to or not."

### 11.14 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "The voice ought to be plainly written on the forehead."
- Candidate: "Your very voice ought to declare it, as plainly as if it were written on your forehead."
- Note: Long's sentence is compressed and odd. The Greek juxtaposes "it ought to be written on the forehead" with "at once the voice rings so". The candidate chooses one reading, in which the voice declares the character as plainly as forehead-writing would. Another reading is that the declaration ("I will deal fairly with you") should be visible on the forehead, with no need to say it aloud. The candidate's reading is defensible, but it adds "declare" and the "as if" simile. A closer alternative: "It ought to be plainly written on your forehead; your very voice ought to sound like it." Log in AMBIGUITIES.md.

### 11.15 — MINOR
- Source: "this power is in the soul, if it be indifferent to things which are indifferent."
- Candidate: "if it is indifferent to things that are indifferent—that is, to things that are neither good nor bad."
- Problem: This is a terminology and gloss consistency issue, not a meaning error. The standard allows "indifferent" once, with its explanation, and says glosses are given once, at first use in reading order. The same gloss ("indifferent—neither good nor bad") already appears in earlier books of candidate.json, so this repeats it. Keeping "indifferent to things that are indifferent" is right, because the argument depends on the repeated word.
- Proposed: "The power to live in the best way lies in the soul, if it is indifferent to things that are indifferent."

### 11.17 — MINOR
- Source: "Fifth, consider that thou dost not even understand whether men are doing wrong or not"
- Candidate: "Fifth, consider that you do not even know for certain whether they are doing wrong"
- Problem: "for certain" is added. It weakens Long's claim: not even understanding becomes merely lacking certainty. The "or not" alternative also goes.
- Proposed: "Fifth, consider that you do not even understand whether they are doing wrong or not, for many things are done with some circumstance in view."

### 11.24 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "saying, It is because I would not perish by the worst of all ends; that is, I would not receive a favor and then be unable to return it."
- Candidate: closes the quotation after "ends" and makes "that is, to receive a favor…" Marcus's explanation.
- Note: In Long it is unclear whether the explanation belongs to Socrates or to Marcus. Long's "I would not receive" keeps the first person, which suggests Socrates is still speaking. The candidate's choice is acceptable, but the alternative is to keep it inside the quotation: "…the worst of all ends: that is, I do not want to receive a favor and then be unable to return it." Log in AMBIGUITIES.md.

### 11.25 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "In the writings of the [Ephesians] there was this precept"
- Candidate: "In the writings of the Ephesians there was this rule"
- Note: Long's brackets here do not mark a supplement for sense. They mark a doubtful reading of the text, and editors have proposed "Epicureans" instead. The candidate absorbs the bracket silently and so presents a conjecture as certain. Following Long's reading is correct under the style guide, but the doubt should be logged in AMBIGUITIES.md.

### 11.32, 11.34, 11.35 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: the attribution to Epictetus in these sections appears only in Long's parenthetical apparatus: "(Epictetus, iii. 24, 87)", "(Epictetus, iii. 24)" and "(Epictetus, iii. 22, 105)".
- Candidate: adds "So Epictetus says." (11.32, 11.34) and "as Epictetus says" (11.35) to the text.
- Note: Marcus's text does not attribute these sayings. Only 11.33 and 11.36 name Epictetus in the text itself. The style guide's rule on keeping attributions in words supports the choice, and the attribution is factually correct, so this is not an error. It is still an editorial addition taken from translator apparatus, so the approach should be applied consistently and logged. If the edition wants to stay strictly with Marcus's words, drop the three added phrases.

## Summary
- BLOCKING: 1 (11.19)
- MINOR: 5 (11.0, 11.7, 11.14, 11.15, 11.17)
- AMBIGUITY: 4 entries (11.14, 11.24, 11.25, and 11.32/11.34/11.35 as one entry)

Verdict: **FAIL**. There is one blocking finding: in 11.19, "nothing is forced on it except…" reverses Long's "no force is imposed on it". Otherwise the candidate is highly faithful. The long catalogue in 11.17 keeps all ten rules, the lacuna and the bracketed supplements. Terminology follows the standard: ruling faculty, impulse, neither good nor bad, "set us against others" for "unsocial", noble/shameful. The self-admonishing voice is kept.
