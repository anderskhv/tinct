# Fidelity review — Book II (2.0–2.16)
Reviewer: independent fidelity reviewer. Coverage: 2.0–2.16, all 17 paragraphs checked sentence by sentence against George Long (1862) and against STYLE-AND-TERMINOLOGY.md. Section numbers 1–17 are all present and each paragraph opens with content equivalent to its source's first sentence; no content bleeds between sections.

## Findings

### 2.0 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "not [only] of the same blood or seed, but that it participates in [the same] intelligence and [the same] portion of the divinity"
- Candidate: "he is my kin—not because we share the same blood or seed, but because we share the same intelligence and the same portion of the divine."
- Issue: Long supplies "[only]", so his text reads "not only of the same blood or seed, but also sharing the same intelligence". The candidate absorbed "[the same]" but dropped "[only]". That turns Long's "not only" into a flat "not because" and denies blood as a basis of kinship. The Greek (οὐχὶ αἵματος ἢ σπέρματος … ἀλλὰ νοῦ) supports the candidate's plain "not … but", so both readings are defensible. Because Long is the fidelity anchor and the style guide says his bracketed supplements are absorbed, I recommend following Long.
- Proposed: "he is my kin—not only because we share the same blood or seed, but because we share the same intelligence and the same portion of the divine."

### 2.2 — MINOR
- Source: "let them always be fixed opinions."
- Candidate: "hold them always as settled convictions."
- Issue: This is a terminology drift. The standard renders opinion/δόγμα as "opinion / judgment", and "convictions" brings in a new term. Book II uses "opinion" as a key word only a few sections later (2.14 "everything is opinion").
- Proposed: "Let these principles be enough for you; hold them always as settled judgments."

### 2.2 — MINOR
- Source: "That which is from fortune is not separated from nature …"
- Candidate: "Even what comes from chance is not separate from nature …"
- Issue: The same word is rendered two ways. Long's "fortune" (τύχη) becomes "chance" here but stays "fortune" in 2.16 ("fortune is hard to predict"). The standard keeps necessity, fate and destiny distinct, so this family of terms should be rendered consistently. "Even" is a small emphasis the source lacks, but it is harmless.
- Proposed: "Even what comes from fortune is not separate from nature; it is woven and bound together with the things that providence directs."

### 2.4 — MINOR
- Source: "Thou seest how few the things are, the which if a man lays hold of, he is able to live a life which flows in quiet, and is like the existence of the gods"
- Candidate: "You see how few things there are that a person must hold on to in order to live a life that flows quietly, like the life of the gods."
- Issue: Long states a sufficient condition: if a person holds these few things, that person *is able* to live such a life. "Must … in order to" turns it into a necessary condition, which loses the reassurance that these few things are enough.
- Proposed: "You see how few things there are: whoever holds on to them is able to live a life that flows quietly, like the life of the gods."

### 2.5 — MINOR
- Source: "but thou wilt no longer have the opportunity of honoring thyself."
- Candidate: "But you will not have the chance to honor yourself much longer."
- Issue: The modality is softened. Long (οὐκέτι καιρὸν ἕξεις) says flatly that the chance will be gone. "Not … much longer" turns this into "soon gone", which weakens the self-rebuke.
- Proposed: "But you will no longer have the chance to honor yourself."

### 2.5 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "Every man's life is sufficient."
- Candidate: "Each person has one life, and it is enough;"
- Issue: The Greek here is corrupt. Editors read it as "life is short for each", "each has only one life" or "each life suffices". Long chose the last. The candidate's "has one life" adds a second reading on top of Long's, so the sentence carries two readings where Long gives one.
- Proposed (following Long): "Each person's life is enough; but yours is nearly over, and still your soul does not respect itself, but makes your happiness depend on the souls of others."

### 2.11 — MINOR
- Source: "To observe too how man comes near to the Deity, and by what part of him, and when this part of man is so disposed."
- Candidate: "Observe, too, how a human being comes near to God, and through what part of himself, and in what condition that part is when it does."
- Issue: The subject shifts. In Long, every "To observe" continues the list of tasks that belong to "the intellectual faculty". The candidate keeps this correctly for the middle items ("It should observe, too, … And it should observe what death is"). For the last item, though, it switches to a bare imperative addressed to the reader, which breaks the parallel.
- Proposed: "And it should observe how a human being comes near to God, and through what part of himself, and in what condition that part is when it does."

### 2.14 — MINOR
- Source: "For what was said by the Cynic Monimus is manifest: and manifest too is the use of what was said"
- Candidate: "What the Cynic Monimus said makes this plain; and the use of his saying is plain too"
- Issue: Long says Monimus's saying *is* plain (obvious in its point), and the parallel "manifest too" repeats that predicate. The candidate turns it into "makes this plain", so the saying becomes proof of "everything is opinion". The logic is altered and the "manifest … manifest too" repetition is lost. There is also an underlying textual ambiguity: the Greek (τὰ πρὸς τὸν κυνικὸν Μόνιμον λεγόμενα) can mean words said *to* or *against* Monimus rather than *by* him. Long chose "by", and following Long is correct.
- Proposed: "For what the Cynic Monimus said is plain; and the use of his saying is plain too, if you take from it what it has to offer, as far as it is true."

### 2.15 — MINOR
- Source: "a separation of ourselves from nature, in some part of which the natures of all other things are contained."
- Candidate: "to break away from nature, and some part of nature holds the natures of all other things."
- Issue: The relative clause, which explains *why* resenting an event separates us from nature, became a loose "and". The candidate also reads as though one single part of nature holds every other thing's nature. The Greek (ἧς ἐν μέρει αἱ ἑκάστου τῶν λοιπῶν φύσεις περιέχονται) means that the natures of all other things are each contained within nature as parts of it. Long's own wording allows the same misreading, but the modern edition should make the argument step clear.
- Proposed: "For to resent anything that happens is to break away from nature, which holds within its parts the natures of all other things."

## Paragraphs with no findings

Notes:

- 2.16 "Written at Carnuntum" is the standard sense of Long's "This in Carnuntum", so it is not flagged.

## Summary
- BLOCKING: 0
- MINOR: 7 (2.2 ×2, 2.4, 2.5, 2.11, 2.14, 2.15)
- AMBIGUITY: 2 (2.0, 2.5)

Verdict: **PASS** (no blocking findings). The Stoic terms follow the standard throughout: ruling faculty, divine spirit within, providence, the nature of the universe, impulse, noble/shameful, and good/bad in the paired argument. The second-person self-admonishing voice and meaningful repetitions (2.5 "wrong yourself, wrong yourself"; 2.0 "like feet, like hands …") are preserved. I recommend applying the MINOR fixes before the Book II text is locked, as these patterns will recur in later books. Most important are the sufficiency/necessity shift in 2.4 and the modality softening in 2.5.
