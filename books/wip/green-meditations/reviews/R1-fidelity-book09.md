# Fidelity review — Book IX (9.0–9.41)
Reviewer: independent fidelity reviewer. Coverage: 9.0–9.41, all 42 paragraphs checked sentence by sentence against Long (1862) and against STYLE-AND-TERMINOLOGY.md.

## Findings

### 9.0 — MINOR
- Source: "alleging that it assigns things to the bad and the good contrary to their deserts"
- Candidate: "He will complain that it hands out things to bad people and good people without regard to what they deserve"
- Problem: Long's charge is stronger. The complaint is that nature distributes *against* desert (the bad get pleasure, the good get pain, as the next clause says). "Without regard to" weakens this to indifference or randomness. The earlier sentence ("to help one another as each deserves") keeps the desert idea, so this sentence should keep the opposition too.
- Proposed: "He will complain that it hands out things to bad people and good people contrary to what they deserve, because bad people often enjoy pleasure and own the things that bring it, while good people get pain as their share, and the things that cause pain."

### 9.1 — MINOR
- Source: "For the destruction of the understanding is a pestilence, much more, indeed, than any such corruption and change of this atmosphere which surrounds us. For this corruption is a pestilence of animals so far as they are animals; but the other is a pestilence of men so far as they are men."
- Candidate: "For the corruption of the mind is a plague—far more so than any such foulness and change in the air that surrounds us. That corruption is a plague to animals insofar as they are animals; but this one is a plague to human beings insofar as they are human."
- Problem: the candidate uses the word "corruption" for the *mind* and "foulness" for the *air*. The next sentence then says "That corruption is a plague to animals", which a listener will most readily take to mean the mind's corruption, the only thing the candidate has called "corruption". That reverses Long's pairing, where the air's corruption harms animals as animals and the mind's destruction harms humans as humans. The referents need to be explicit.
- Proposed: "For the corruption of the mind is a plague—far more so than any such foulness and change in the air that surrounds us. The foulness of the air is a plague to animals insofar as they are animals; the corruption of the mind is a plague to human beings insofar as they are human."

### 9.10 — MINOR
- Source: "remember that indulgence is given to thee for this purpose. And the gods, too, are indulgent to such persons"
- Candidate: "remember that patience was given to you for this very purpose. The gods too are patient with such people"
- Problem: Long's "indulgence" (the Greek word means kindness or goodwill) is an active good disposition toward wrongdoers. The sentence then continues "so kind they are" and describes the gods actively helping such people. "Patience" narrows this to putting up with them, which loses the benevolence the sentence goes on to illustrate.
- Proposed: "If you cannot, remember that kindness was given to you for this very purpose. The gods too are kind to such people; for some purposes they even help them to health, wealth and reputation—that is how kind they are." (If the triple "kind" is felt to be too heavy, "forbearance … forbearing" is an acceptable alternative.)

### 9.25 — MINOR
- Source: "Thou hast endured infinite troubles through not being contented with thy ruling faculty when it does the things which it is constituted by nature to do."
- Candidate: "You have put up with endless troubles because you were not content to let your ruling faculty do what nature made it to do."
- Problem: in Long, the ruling faculty *does* its natural work, and the fault is that you were not satisfied with that and wanted more. The candidate's "not content to let it do" is most naturally heard as "unwilling to allow it", meaning you stopped it from doing its work. That shifts who does what.
- Proposed: "You have put up with endless troubles because you were not content with your ruling faculty doing what nature made it to do."

### 9.36 — MINOR
- Source: "Towards the gods then, now become at last more simple and better."
- Candidate: "So toward the gods, too, become simpler and better now, at last."
- Problem: "too" is added. It implies a second object ("toward others, and toward the gods too") that neither Long nor the Greek has.
- Proposed: "So toward the gods, now at last, become simpler and better."

### 9.23 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "and so what is exhibited in the representation of the mansions of the dead strikes our eyes more clearly."
- Candidate: "And so the picture of the dwellings of the dead strikes our eyes more clearly."
- Note: the passage alludes to the Nekyia, the visit to the dead in *Odyssey* XI. Long's "representation" could mean the poetic account or a staged or painted depiction. The candidate's "picture" leans toward a visual image. Both readings are defensible, and the candidate stays close to Long's wording. Log this in AMBIGUITIES.md. A neutral option is "the scene of the dwellings of the dead". No change is required.

### 9.40 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "…is a principle of all schools of philosophy; but to be intent only on that which thou art now doing and on the instrument by which thou doest it."
- Candidate: "For it is a principle of every school of philosophy never to abandon philosophy … and never to chatter idly …, but to be intent only on what you are doing now, and on the instrument you are doing it with."
- Note: Long's semicolon leaves open whether "to be intent only on…" belongs to the principle shared by every school, or is a separate injunction from Marcus to himself. The candidate folds it into the shared principle. That is a legitimate reading and the usual one for the Greek, but it should be logged. The alternative is: "…is a principle of every school of philosophy. But be intent only on what you are doing now, and on the instrument you are doing it with."
