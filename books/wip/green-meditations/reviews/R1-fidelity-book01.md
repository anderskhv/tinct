# Fidelity review — Book I (1.0–1.16)
Reviewer: independent fidelity reviewer. Coverage: 1.0–1.16, all 17 paragraphs checked sentence by sentence against Long (1862) and against `STYLE-AND-TERMINOLOGY.md`.

## Findings

### 1.10 — BLOCKING
- Source: "From Fronto I learned to observe what envy and duplicity and hypocrisy are in a tyrant"
- Candidate: "From Fronto I learned to notice how much envy, deceit and hypocrisy there is in a tyrant"
- Problem: meaning change. Long (and the Greek *oia*, "of what kind") is about the *nature* of these vices in a tyrant: Marcus learned what they look like. "How much … there is" turns this into a claim about quantity, that tyrants have a lot of them. That is a different observation.
- Proposed: "11. From Fronto I learned to notice what envy, deceit and hypocrisy are like in a tyrant; and that, on the whole, those among us who are called Patricians are rather lacking in fatherly affection."

### 1.5 — MINOR
- Source: "about incantations and the driving away of daemons and such things; and not to breed quails [for fighting]"
- Candidate: "about spells and driving out evil spirits and things of that kind; not to keep quails for fighting"
- Problem: (a) "evil" is added. Long says only "daemons", and the style guide bans "daemon" without setting a word for this exorcism sense. Plain "spirits" is enough. (b) "keep" is weaker than "breed" (raise). Both are small.
- Proposed: "not to believe what miracle-workers and conjurers say about spells and driving out spirits and things of that kind; not to breed quails for fighting, or get carried away by such pastimes;"

### 1.6 — MINOR (terminology)
- Source: "From Rusticus I received the impression that my character required improvement and discipline"
- Candidate: "From Rusticus I got the idea that my character needed correcting and training."
- Problem: Long's "impression" renders *phantasia*, and the standard sets **impression** as the stable rendering. The candidate uses "impressions" in 1.16 for the same word ("clear and frequent impressions"), so this paragraph is inconsistent with that one. The meaning is intact.
- Proposed: "7. From Rusticus I got the impression that my character needed correcting and training."

### 1.9 — MINOR
- Source: "or joining in an inquiry about the thing itself, not about the word"
- Candidate: "or while discussing the matter itself rather than the word"
- Problem: "joining in" drops out. Alexander's tact lies in taking part *with* the speaker, and "discussing" loses that shared act.
- Proposed: "…in the course of an answer, or while agreeing with them, or while joining them in looking into the matter itself rather than the word, or by some other fitting hint."

### 1.12 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "not to be indifferent when a friend finds fault … and to be ready to speak well of teachers, as it is reported of Domitius and Athenodotus"
- Candidate: "not to shrug it off when a friend finds fault with me … to be ready to speak well of my teachers, as Domitius and Athenodotus are said to have done"
- Notes: (a) "with me" is not in Long. The friend's fault-finding may be aimed at Marcus or at something else. The candidate's reading is the usual one and is defensible. Plain "finds fault" keeps it open. (b) "as it is reported of Domitius and Athenodotus" can be read two ways. Domitius and Athenodotus may themselves have spoken well of their teachers, which is the candidate's reading. Or the reference may be to what is recorded about them as teachers, for instance Catulus's own praise of them. Long leaves this open, and the candidate has chosen the first reading. "my teachers" also narrows Long's "teachers". Neutral option: "to be ready to speak well of teachers, as is reported of Domitius and Athenodotus".

### 1.15 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "or to attend him of necessity when he went abroad"
- Candidate: "or to go with him when he went out"
- Notes: Long's "abroad" can mean "out of doors" or "away from home, on a journey". The Greek (*synapodēmein*) points to travelling with him. "Went out" picks the weaker sense. It also drops "of necessity", although "any obligation" earlier in the sentence covers it. Option: "to dine with him or to be obliged to travel with him when he went away".

### 1.15 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "He was most ready to give without envy to those who possessed any particular faculty"
- Candidate: "He was very ready, and without envy, to give way to people who had some special ability"
- Notes: Long's "give" has no object. The candidate reads it as "give way" (yield precedence), which matches the Greek *parachōrein* and the clause that follows, "he gave them his help". The other reading is "give (credit, rewards)". The candidate's choice is sound. Flagged only because Long is elliptical here.

### 1.15 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "His dress came from Lorium, his villa on the coast, and from Lanuvium generally."
- Candidate: "His clothes came from Lorium, his villa on the coast, and mostly from Lanuvium."
- Notes: this passage is corrupt in the Greek and obscure in Long. "Mostly from Lanuvium" makes Lanuvium the main source and Lorium secondary. The sentence can also be read as saying his dress came from Lorium, while his things in general came from Lanuvium. Neutral option: "His clothes came from Lorium, his villa on the coast, and generally from Lanuvium."

### 1.15 — MINOR
- Source: "and never any mean thoughts or action"
- Candidate: "never anything mean in thought or action"
- Problem: Long's "mean" means base, low or petty. A modern reader will take it as "unkind" or "stingy". The same issue comes up in 1.16.
- Proposed: "he showed moderation and steadiness in everything, never anything petty or base in thought or action, and no craving for novelty."

### 1.16 — MINOR
- Source: "without being for this reason either meaner in thought, or more remiss in action, with respect to the things which must be done for the public interest in a manner that befits a ruler"
- Candidate: "without being on that account any meaner in thought or any slacker in action when it comes to the things a ruler must do for the public good"
- Problem: (a) "meaner" means lower or less dignified in Long, not unkinder, so modern readers will misread it. (b) Long's "in a manner that befits a ruler" describes *how* the public business is done. The candidate folds this into "things a ruler must do", which is close but loses the manner.
- Proposed: "…without being on that account any less dignified in thought or any slacker in action when it comes to doing what must be done for the public good in the way that befits a ruler."

### 1.16 — MINOR
- Source: "Among the Quadi at the Granua."
- Candidate: "Written among the Quadi, on the Granua."
- Problem: "Written" is not in Long. It is the standard interpretation, but it is still an added gloss.
- Proposed: "Among the Quadi, on the Granua."
