# Fidelity review — Book VIII (8.0–8.60)
Reviewer: independent fidelity reviewer. Coverage: 8.0–8.60, all 61 paragraphs checked sentence by sentence against Long (1862) and the style standard.

## Findings

### 8.0 — MINOR
- Source: "If he has principles from which come his affects and his acts."
- Candidate: "By having principles from which his feelings and his actions come."
- Problem: Long's "affects" here renders ὁρμαί (impulses: the Greek is ἀφ' ὧν αἱ ὁρμαὶ καὶ αἱ πράξεις). The style standard fixes ὁρμή as **impulse**. "Feelings" brings in emotion, which the text does not say, and makes the pair ("impulse / action") harder to recognise when it comes back later (8.15, 8.27).
- Proposed: "By having principles from which his impulses and his actions come."

### 8.2 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "and the ruling principles of these men were the same [or conformable to their pursuits]."
- Candidate: "and their ruling faculties were in keeping with what they pursued."
- Note: Long gives two readings. The main text says "the same", which could mean "their ruling faculties were their own" or "self-consistent". His bracket gives "conformable to their pursuits". The candidate follows the bracket. That is defensible, but it should be logged in `AMBIGUITIES.md`. Another option: "and their ruling faculties were their own [or: in keeping with what they pursued]". No change is required if the log entry is made.

### 8.7 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "Thou hast not leisure [or ability] to read. But thou hast leisure [or ability] to check arrogance …"
- Candidate: "You are not free to read. But you are free to curb arrogance …"
- Note: Long leaves it open whether the problem is time ("leisure") or capacity ("ability"). The candidate's "free" makes both open, but a newcomer may hear "free" as "permitted". Keeping the word "free" throughout the repeated list keeps Marcus's deliberate repetition, which is good. If the permission reading seems a risk, an alternative is "You have no time to read. But you have time to curb arrogance; you have time to rise above …". That keeps the repetition but loses "ability". Log the choice either way.

### 8.16 — AMBIGUITY
- Source: "For if thou canst, correct [that which is the cause]; but if thou canst not do this, correct at least the thing itself"
- Candidate: "If you can, correct the cause; if you cannot do that, at least correct the thing itself"
- Note: The candidate correctly follows Long's supplement. The Greek (διόρθωσον, with the person as the implied object) is often read as "correct the person responsible", and several modern translations have that. Following Long is correct under the style standard. Record this in `AMBIGUITIES.md`. A small point: the source's opening "For" is dropped. The logic still reads correctly, so no change is needed.

### 8.34 — MINOR
- Source: "For as the universal nature converts and fixes in its predestined place everything which stands in the way and opposes it, and makes such things a part of itself"
- Candidate: "For just as the nature of the universe turns to its own ends everything that stands in its way and opposes it, fixes it in its destined place, and makes it part of itself"
- Problem: "turns to its own ends" adds a purpose ("its own ends") that Long's "converts" does not state. Long means it turns the obstacle around or transforms it. A second, smaller issue is that Long joins "converts and fixes" under one object, and the candidate splits them into a list of three. The meaning survives.
- Proposed: "For just as the nature of the universe turns round everything that stands in its way and opposes it, fixes it in its destined place, and makes it part of itself, so a rational being …"

### 8.36 — AMBIGUITY (editor's query: "Fergamus" vs "Pergamus")
- Source: "Does Panthea or Fergamus now sit by the tomb of Verus?"
- Candidate: "Do Panthea or Pergamus still sit by the tomb of Verus now?"
- Judgment on the name: **accept "Pergamus".** The Greek has Πέργαμος (Pergamus), and the standard translations (Farquharson, Haines, Hard, Hays) give Pergamus. "Fergamus" is a misprint of P as F in the Long text as transmitted (Gutenberg). It is not a separate reading. It is not a variant spelling of the kind the proper-names rule protects ("kept as Long spells them"), because that rule preserves Long's transliterations and does not keep typesetting errors. The live modern-en edition already reads "Pergamus". Required action: log this in `AMBIGUITIES.md` (8.37: Long/Gutenberg "Fergamus" is a misprint for Πέργαμος; modern-en uses "Pergamus"). Leave `original-en` untouched.
- Separate minor point in the same sentence: "still … now" says the same thing twice, and "still" is not in the source ("now sit"). Proposed: "Do Panthea or Pergamus sit by the tomb of Verus now?"
- The candidate turns "What then would those do after these were dead?" into "And once these mourners were dead, what would the dead do then?" That resolves the antecedent correctly: "these persons" are the mourners and "those" are Verus and Hadrianus. No finding.

### 8.43 — MINOR
- Source: "See that thou secure this present time to thyself: for those who rather pursue posthumous fame do not consider …"
- Candidate: "Make sure you take hold of this present time for yourself. Those who would rather chase fame after death do not stop to think …"
- Problem: The causal "for" is dropped. The second sentence is Marcus's reason for the first, and without the link it reads as a new remark.
- Proposed: "Make sure you take hold of this present time for yourself. For those who would rather chase fame after death do not stop to think that …"

### 8.46 — AMBIGUITY (with a minor wording point)
- Source: "Take thy departure then from life contentedly, just as he dies who is in full activity, and well pleased too with the things which are obstacles."
- Candidate: "Then leave life contentedly, just as someone dies who is still in the midst of his work, and at peace, too, with the things that stood in his way."
- Note: In Long's English, "well pleased too with the things which are obstacles" could describe either you, the one departing, or "he who dies". In the Greek, ἵλεως ("gracious, well disposed") agrees with the subject of "depart", so it describes you. The candidate attaches it to the dying man ("his way"). Also, "at peace with" is weaker than Long's "well pleased". The Greek implies a kindly, favourable attitude toward the obstacles, not just a calm one.
- Proposed: "Then leave life contentedly, just as someone dies who is still in the midst of his work—and leave it well pleased, too, with the things that stand in your way."

### 8.47 — MINOR
- Source: "Therefore the mind which is free from passions is a citadel, for man has nothing more secure to which he can fly for refuge and for the future be inexpugnable."
- Candidate: "That is why a mind free from passions is a fortress. A human being has nothing more secure to run to for refuge, where from then on he cannot be taken."
- Problem: The causal "for" is dropped. The second sentence gives the reason the mind is a citadel.
- Proposed: "That is why a mind free from passions is a fortress: a human being has nothing more secure to run to for refuge, where from then on he cannot be taken."
