# Book IV draft notes

## Glosses added
- IV.14: "generative principle" (Long "seminal principle", λόγος σπερματικός), glossed once as "the seed of reason from which things grow". IV.21 then says "generative intelligence of the universe" with no gloss.
- No other glosses. The Book II terms (ruling faculty, providence, opinion, the city) appear without their glosses. "Clotho, one of the Fates" (IV.34) is Long's own bracketed supplement, absorbed into the sentence.

## Source ambiguities (Long's reading followed)
- IV.1: "under certain conditions, however". This is the Stoic "reservation" (ὑπεξαίρεσις). Rendered "though only on certain conditions". Another option: "always with a reservation".
- IV.9: "The nature of that which is [universally] useful has been compelled to do this." The sentence is cryptic and has no antecedent. Rendered literally: "The nature of what is useful to all has been compelled to do this." I did not add "this" = whatever has happened.
- IV.19: Long ends on "clinging to something else...." because the text is corrupt. I kept his ellipsis. I rendered "unseasonably" as "at the wrong time".
- IV.21: The last sentence is Long's elliptical question and answer ("The division into that which is material and that which is the cause of form"). I made it a full question and answer and absorbed "[the formal]".
- IV.22: "maintain the faculty of comprehension [or understanding]" (the Stoic "grasping" impression). Rendered "keep hold of your power to grasp and understand it". I did not add a gloss.
- IV.27: "a chaos huddled together, but still a universe". The Greek is corrupt here. I followed Long: "a chaos thrown together, but even so, a universe".
- IV.30: The speaker of the second clause ("and I do not get the means of living out of my learning, and I abide [by my reason]") is unclear. I kept Long's punctuation: the first clause is in quotation marks as "he says", then a dash, then Marcus's "I" outside the quotation marks. Another reading makes both clauses the half-naked man's words.
- IV.45: "a mere enumeration of disjointed things, which has only a necessary sequence". Rendered "a mere list of unconnected things, joined only because one must follow another".

## Kept close to the baseline, and why
- IV.7, 15, 17 and 41 were already plain modern English and faithful, so I changed them only lightly or not at all. IV.43 got only a light touch-up.
- IV.20 keeps "beautiful" rather than "noble". The argument runs across physical objects (an emerald, gold, a lyre) and moral ones (law, truth, kindness) in a single sequence, so splitting the term would break it.
- IV.28 keeps Long's list of eleven items in order. I modernised only the words: "black" became "dark", "animal" became "brutish", "counterfeit" became "fake", "scurrilous" became "coarse and jeering" and "fraudulent" became "cheating". "Womanish" is kept, as in the Book II exemplar (II.10).
- IV.33 keeps Long's spelling "Hadrianus", per the proper-names rule.

## Hard passages
- IV.3: long, with four "But perhaps…" objections, each answered and closed with "be quiet at last". I kept all four refrains, including those Long put in brackets. The alternative "providence or atoms" is kept as an alternative.
- IV.4: a chain of "if this is so" steps. Every link is kept.
- IV.23: "Dear city of Cecrops" (Athens) has no gloss. The contrast with "Dear city of Zeus" works without one. A gloss could be added if the reviewer thinks a newcomer will stumble.
- IV.49: Long's rhetorical questions on "misfortune" and "a deviation from man's nature" are recast as "a departure from human nature" and "what human nature wills". Unhappy/happy and misfortune/good fortune are kept as Long distinguishes them.
