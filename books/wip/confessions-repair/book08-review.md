## 2. Packet-by-packet findings

### Packet A — paras 1–3 (opening, Simplicianus, Victorinus introduced)

**Para 1 — MINOR — technical term inconsistency.**
- Source: "there was an incorruptible **substance**, whence was all other **substance**".
- Candidate: "there was an incorruptible **reality**, the source of all other **reality**".
- "Substance" is the established term across the accepted books (book04 ×7, book05 ×11, book06, book03) and the candidate itself uses "substances" correctly in para 25 — where it carries the *same* metaphysical weight, since para 25's rebuttal of "two contrary substances" depends on the reader having met "substance" as the word for a kind of being. Switching to "reality" in para 1 breaks the link.
- Proposed: "there was an incorruptible substance, the source of all other substance".

**Para 1 — no other issues.** The key polarity ("no longer wanted to be more certain of you, but more steady in you") is correct: certainty is already possessed, steadiness is what is wanted. This matters for finding M3 below.

**Para 2 — no issues.** The concession structure is intact: the Apostle permits marriage, advises better; Augustine "chose the easier path" out of weakness. Nothing flipped. "Vain/vanity" rendered "foolish/foolishness" consistently within the paragraph.

**Para 3 — MINOR — supplied mechanism.**
- Source: "Simplicianus, the father of Ambrose (a Bishop now) **in receiving Thy grace**".
- Candidate: "Simplicianus, **the man through whose hand** Ambrose (now a bishop) had received your grace".
- Historically correct, but the source says only that Simplicianus was Ambrose's spiritual father in the receiving of grace; "through whose hand" supplies a specific sacramental mechanism the source leaves unstated. Low-harm, but it is an added interpretive connective (risk pattern #2).
- Proposed: "Simplicianus, who was father to Ambrose (now a bishop) in his receiving of your grace".
- Polarity of the Platonists-vs-other-philosophers comparison is correct (Platonists relatively good, others full of falsehood).

## 3. Dedicated assessment: the "serenity" → "certainty" choice (para 30)

**Source:** "for instantly at the end of this sentence, **by a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart**, all the darkness of doubt vanished away."

**Candidate:** "for instantly, at the end of that sentence, **it was as though a light of certainty had been poured into my heart**, and every shadow of doubt melted away."

**Verdict: reject the choice. Restore an affective word. This should be corrected before acceptance.**

Reasoning, in order of weight:

1. **It contradicts the book's own repeatedly stated position.** The drafter's defence is that the phrase "functions as the resolution of doubt... the paragraph's central tension since para 1." That is precisely backwards about para 1. Para 1 says: "Of your eternal life I was now **certain**... I had stopped doubting that there was an incorruptible substance... **I no longer wanted to be more certain of you, but more steady in you**." Augustine states at the outset that certainty is the thing he already has. Para 13 repeats it: "I no longer even had my old excuse — that I still hesitated... because the truth was not yet fully certain to me — **for now it was certain**." Para 19 repeats it a third time, in his conscience's own voice: "You used to say that you didn't want to throw off the baggage of vanity for the sake of an uncertain truth — **well, now it is certain**, and that burden still weighs you down." Book 8's entire thesis is that the problem is *not* an epistemic deficit. Making the climactic gift "certainty" hands Augustine, at the decisive moment, the one thing he has told us three times he already had — and quietly converts a book about the divided will into a book about resolved doubt.

2. **The word in play is not epistemic.** Pusey's "serenity" renders *securitas* — freedom from care, untroubledness, assurance in the sense of being no longer anxious. It belongs to the same affective register as the paragraph it ends (a man who has just stopped sobbing under a fig tree) and answers the *agitation* of paras 22–29, not a syllogism.

3. **The paired clause does not need the help.** "All the darkness of doubt vanished away" already does the doubt-removal work, and the candidate preserves it ("every shadow of doubt melted away"). The light and the doubt-clause are *different* contributions: the light supplies peace, the clause reports that doubt went with it. Making the light itself "certainty" collapses two clauses into one and makes the sentence partly tautological.

4. **"Modern readers might miss it" is not a translation warrant.** The stated reason for the change is a worry about reader inference, which is the definition of an added interpretive gloss (risk pattern #2). Nothing in "serenity" is archaic or obscure.

**Proposed correction:** "for instantly, at the end of that sentence, it was as though a light of peace had been poured into my heart, and every shadow of doubt melted away."

"Serenity" itself is perfectly readable modern English and would also be acceptable. "Peace", "calm assurance" or "settled calm" all preserve the affective sense. "Certainty" is the one option that should not ship.

## 5. Whole-chapter read

Read straight through, 1–31, for voice and intensity.

This reads as the climactic book it is. The arc is legible without a commentary: the strategic opening ("certain of you, but not steady"), Victorinus's public shame converted into public boldness, the long meditation on why recovered things are loved more, the forged chain of habit built link by link, Ponticianus walking in and unknowingly detonating the whole thing, the officials at Trier, the turn to self-confrontation, the philosophical crisis in the middle, and then the fall into the garden. The middle stretch (paras 20–29) accelerates properly: the paragraphs get more fragmented, the questions get more insistent, and the candidate lets them. It does not smooth Augustine's repetitions, which is the correct instinct — para 22's pounded "Where does this monstrous condition come from? And to what end?" is hammered three times, para 29's "How long, how long", para 26's "I very nearly did it, and did not do it", para 27's three whispering questions from the mistresses.

The emotional register is not softened anywhere. The garden weeping (29) is physical and violent as it must be; "a huge storm rose up in me, bringing with it a huge downpour of tears" and "I threw myself down, somehow, under a certain fig tree" both land. The chains-and-flood imagery of the two-wills struggle survives intact across 12, 22, 26. Continence's "persuasive mockery" in 28 is genuinely taunting rather than reassuring. The tolle-lege scene in 30 has the right stillness after the storm — apart from the "certainty" substitution, which is the one place where the candidate speaks in a register Augustine does not.

Voice is consistent with the accepted Books 3–6: declarative, unfussy, willing to keep a long sentence long where the argument is a chain. The prose is noticeably better than average in paras 13, 16, 20 and 29 — four of the hardest — and does not falter in the philosophically dense 21–25, where the temptation to paraphrase is highest and the candidate correctly stays close.

Three blemishes are visible on a straight read rather than only on collation: the doubled "all the more... all the more abundantly" in para 11 reads as an editing artifact; the para 18 parenthetical "— not even the finding of it —" trips the reader mid-sentence; and "into that man's chest" in para 6 is the one phrase in the book that sounds clinical where it should sound devotional.

The two major findings (22 and 31) are both single-clause fixes and neither requires rewriting a paragraph.
