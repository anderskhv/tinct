## Verdict: ACCEPT WITH CHANGES

Read cold, the edition works. I could follow the frame, every speech and the ending. The prose is clear contemporary English, and the eight main voices sound distinct. The text is frank rather than prudish, and it does not moralize. It is not yet a clean accept because a first-time reader will trip on a set of local problems:

1. **Who is speaking.** In the tightly packed Diotima dialogue and in Alcibiades's bedroom scene, an untagged line that follows "'…,' I said." reads as the same speaker continuing when it is actually the other one.
2. **Quotation marks around verse lines.** In chapter 1 and at 8.10–8.12, a verse line set as its own paragraph closes the speaker's quotation. This breaks the edition's own rule for speeches that continue across paragraphs.
3. **Opaque passages.** Five phrases, references or pronouns block understanding: 7.0 father/mother, 3.8 'uses base', 4.1 "his rule", 8.23 Olympus and 8.28 "with boys or without them".
4. **The ascent.** Its stages are named differently in the description (7.63) and the recap (7.65).
5. **The opening.** The frame's first paragraph has no speaker label, although 1.8–1.11 use labels.
6. **Punctuation.** There are two errors.

All of these are fixes at the level of a phrase. None needs restructuring. Count: 15 SHOULD-FIX, 53 OPTIONAL.

## 1. What I understood

**The frame (ch. 1).** Apollodorus, a follower of Socrates for not quite three years, is answering an unnamed listener (labelled COMPANION and once addressed as "my friends", so perhaps a small group) who has asked about the speeches on love at Agathon's dinner. He is well rehearsed because two days earlier, walking into Athens from Phalerum, he told the same story to Glaucon, who had heard a vague version via Phoenix and wrongly thought the party was recent. The dinner took place long ago, when they were boys, on the day after Agathon's first tragic victory; Apollodorus was not there but has the story from Aristodemus, a little barefoot devotee of Socrates, and has checked parts of it with Socrates himself. After banter about his nickname "the madman", the retelling begins: Aristodemus meets a freshly bathed Socrates in sandals, who brings him along uninvited (punning on Agathon's name, "good") but then falls behind, lost in thought on a neighbor's porch, so Aristodemus arrives alone and Socrates turns up halfway through dinner to recline beside Agathon. Because most guests are hung over, Pausanias and the physician Eryximachus propose drinking only moderately. Eryximachus sends the flute-girl away and, taking up Phaedrus's complaint that no poet has properly praised Love, proposes that each guest in turn, left to right starting with Phaedrus, give a speech in Love's praise; Socrates agrees. I hesitated over whether COMPANION is Glaucon (I decided not, from "as I told you at the start" in 1.7) and over why the format switches to speaker labels at 1.8.

**Phaedrus (ch. 2).** Love is a great and wonderful god, and the oldest, since he has no parents (Hesiod, Parmenides and Acusilaus are cited). He is also the source of our greatest goods: the bond between an older lover and a younger beloved instills shame at dishonor and love of honor better than family, office or wealth, because neither wants to be seen by the other acting like a coward, so an army of lovers would be almost invincible. Only lovers will die for someone: Alcestis died for her husband when his own parents would not, and the gods let her return, while Orpheus, "only a harp-player", tried to enter Hades alive, got only a phantom of his wife, and was later killed by women. Achilles, Patroclus's younger, beardless beloved (Aeschylus is wrong to reverse the roles), avenged his lover and followed him in death, and the gods honored him even above Alcestis and sent him to the Islands of the Blessed, because they especially admire a beloved's devotion to his lover. So Love is the oldest, most honored and most powerful god, the source of virtue in life and happiness after death. I hesitated where the speech slides from reported speech into Phaedrus's own "I" with no marker (2.0→2.4), and I had to work out why "the lover is more divine" means the beloved's devotion is rewarded more (2.7).

**Pausanias (ch. 3).** Phaedrus praised Love too indiscriminately: there are two Aphrodites (the older, motherless "Heavenly" daughter of Uranus and the younger "Common" daughter of Zeus and Dione) and so two Loves. Acts are neither good nor bad in themselves, only in how they are done; Common Love is the undiscriminating love of baser men, aimed at women and boys alike, at bodies rather than souls, and only at the act. Heavenly Love is aimed only at males, and at youths whose minds are maturing (around the first beard) rather than boys, with lifelong faithfulness in view; love of young boys ought to be banned by law. Customs differ: in Elis and Boeotia such relationships are simply allowed; in Ionia, under foreign rule, they are condemned because tyrants fear strong bonds (the Athenian tyrant-slayers Aristogeiton and Harmodius are the example); and Athenian custom is complex, giving lovers extraordinary licence while fathers shield their sons. His resolution: it is dishonorable for a youth to "yield" quickly, to a bad lover, or for money or power, but honorable to yield to a good lover for the sake of becoming wise and good, and then even being deceived is noble. That is Heavenly Love, valuable to individuals and cities; every other love belongs to the Common goddess. I hesitated over "'uses base'" (3.8), the long sentence about the "two laws" meeting in one (3.8), and why poor speaking explains the permissiveness of Elis and Boeotia (3.3).

**Eryximachus (ch. 4).** Eryximachus accepts Pausanias's two loves but, arguing from his medical art, extends them to bodies, plants and the whole cosmos. Medicine is knowledge of the body's loves: it indulges the healthy desires, discourages the diseased ones, and reconciles opposites such as hot and cold, as his ancestor Asclepius first did. Music does the same with high and low notes and long and short rhythms (what Heraclitus should have said), and in practice, in composing and in what "is called education", one must favor the noble, heavenly love of the Muse Urania and control the common love of Polyhymnia, as a doctor moderates a gourmet's appetite. In the seasons the balanced love brings health and good harvests and the unruly love brings plague, frost and blight (the study of this is astronomy), while divination and sacrifice exist to keep the good love healthy between gods and humans. Love as a whole is all-powerful, but the love aimed at the good, with self-control and justice, is greatest and makes us friends with each other and with the gods. I could not tell whether "his rule" in 4.1 means Love's or Asclepius's, why performing existing melodies is "called education", or how "those who are not yet self-controlled only so that they may become so" applies to music.

**Aristophanes (ch. 5).** Humans have never grasped Love's power, so Aristophanes explains our original nature: there were three sexes (male, female and androgynous), each a round creature with four arms, four legs, two faces and two sets of genitals, that could cartwheel along and was the child of the sun, the earth or the moon. In their pride they attacked the gods, so Zeus cut them in half (threatening to halve them again) and Apollo turned their faces toward the cut and tied off the skin at the navel. The halves clung to each other and died of neglect until Zeus moved their genitals to the front, so that man and woman could reproduce and male pairs could at least find satisfaction and get back to their lives. Love is therefore the drive to restore our original unity: halves of the androgynous kind love the opposite sex (adulterers come from them), halves of the female love women, and halves of the male love males; these are the manliest boys, who become statesmen. When true halves meet they want something they cannot put into words, and if Hephaestus offered to weld them into one person in life and death, every pair would accept, because "the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love". We must be pious so that we are not split again, and should praise Love as our greatest benefactor, who promises to restore us. I hesitated over why anyone would think Aristophanes was "hinting at Pausanias and Agathon" (5.6), since the text never says they are a couple, and over "sorb-apple".

**Agathon (ch. 6).** The earlier speakers praised Love's gifts rather than the god himself, so Agathon starts with Love's nature. Love is the happiest and most beautiful god because he is the youngest (he runs from Old Age, and the old violence among the gods came from Necessity, not Love), tender (he walks and lives only in soft souls), supple and graceful, and lives among flowers. He is also supremely virtuous: just (everyone serves him willingly), self-controlled (he "masters" every pleasure), brave (he conquers even the God of War) and wise (a poet who makes others poets, brings all creatures to birth, and taught the gods their arts). Since Love was born, love of beauty has been the source of every good thing among gods and humans. Agathon ends with a lyrical, jingling list of Love's blessings and calls the speech "partly playful and partly, in a modest way, serious". It was easy to follow; the arguments are visibly word-play, which I took to be deliberate.

**Socrates questions Agathon (7.0–7.44).** Socrates ironically admits he had assumed praise must be true, and asks to question Agathon before speaking. He gets Agathon to agree that love, like "father" or "brother", is always love of something, and that it desires what it loves. One desires only what one lacks: even a healthy man who wants to be healthy really wants to keep his health in the future, which he does not yet have. Agathon had said Love is love of beauty, not ugliness, so Love lacks beauty and cannot be beautiful, and since good things are beautiful, Love lacks goodness too. Agathon admits he did not understand what he had been saying, and Socrates answers that it is the truth that cannot be refuted, since "Socrates is easily refuted". The argument was easy to follow except for the opening example in 7.0, "whether love is the love of a father or the love of a mother", which I could not make sense of.

**Diotima's teaching (7.45–7.68).** Socrates says he once believed what Agathon believed, until Diotima, a wise woman from Mantinea, showed him that what is not beautiful need not be ugly, just as correct opinion lies between knowledge and ignorance. Love is neither a god (gods possess beauty and goodness) nor a mortal but a "daimon", a spirit who carries messages between gods and humans; as the son of Plenty (Poros) and Poverty (Penia), conceived at the party for Aphrodite's birth, he is poor, barefoot and homeless but resourceful and scheming, a lifelong lover of wisdom between wisdom and ignorance, and Socrates's mistake was to confuse the lover with the beloved. Love aims to possess the good forever (the word "love" has been narrowed to one kind of desire, as "poetry" has been narrowed from all "making", and we love our "other half" only if it is good), and its activity is "giving birth in beauty", in body or in soul. Procreation is how mortal things share in immortality, as body, character and even knowledge are constantly renewed; that is why animals fight for their young and people crave undying fame (Alcestis, Achilles, Codrus), and why those pregnant in soul beget wisdom and virtue, such as the poems of Homer and Hesiod and the laws of Lycurgus and Solon, "children" that outlast human ones. In the greater mysteries, the ascent, a rightly guided young man (1) loves one beautiful body and gives birth to beautiful thoughts with it, (2) sees that the beauty of all bodies is one and loves them all, (3) values beauty of mind or soul above beauty of body, (4) sees the beauty of institutions (or practices) and laws, (5) then of the branches of knowledge, and (6) turns to "the vast sea of beauty", giving birth to many ideas in a boundless love of wisdom. At last (7) he suddenly sees beauty itself: everlasting, unchanging, beautiful in every respect and to everyone, not located in any body, speech or thing, and shared in by all other beautiful things; contemplating it, he brings forth true virtue rather than images of it and becomes dear to the gods and as immortal as a human can be. Socrates says he is persuaded and urges everyone to honor Love. I hesitated because the stage names differ between 7.63 and 7.65 (institutions vs practices; branches of knowledge vs "beautiful ideas"), and because in several exchanges I first gave Diotima's lines to Socrates (see section 2).

**Alcibiades's arrival and speech, and the ending (ch. 8).** As Aristophanes starts to answer Socrates's allusion to his speech, a very drunk Alcibiades bursts in with revelers and a flute-girl, crowned with ivy, violets and ribbons, to crown Agathon; he sits between Agathon and Socrates before noticing Socrates, and the two trade mock-jealous complaints. He makes himself master of the drinking, drains a huge wine-cooler and, asked for a speech, praises Socrates through images "for the sake of the truth": Socrates is like the Silenus figures that open to reveal gods inside, and like the satyr Marsyas, except that he enchants with words, which make Alcibiades's heart pound, shame him for neglecting his soul for politics, and make him run away. Believing Socrates desired him, Alcibiades tried to seduce him (sending his attendant away, wrestling with him, inviting him to dinner, finally lying all night under his cloak), but Socrates ironically declined the trade, saying Alcibiades would be exchanging bronze for gold, and nothing happened, leaving Alcibiades humiliated, admiring and enslaved. War stories follow: at Potidaea Socrates endured hunger, walked barefoot on ice, stood thinking for a day and a night, and saved Alcibiades's life, then gave the prize for valor to him; in the retreat from Delium he stayed calm. Socrates is unlike any other man, and his words are laughable on the outside but divine within; Alcibiades warns Agathon against him, and Socrates retorts that the whole speech was a ploy to separate him from Agathon. A comic reshuffling of places follows until a second band of revelers bursts in and everyone drinks heavily. Aristodemus falls asleep and at dawn finds Socrates making Agathon and Aristophanes admit that the same man should be able to write both comedy and tragedy, until both fall asleep, after which Socrates goes to the Lyceum, bathes, spends the day as usual and goes home in the evening. I hesitated over "the melodies of Olympus" (8.23), "wine — 'with boys or without them' — tells the truth" (8.28), who asks "Do you know what I have been thinking?" (8.29), and the seating logic in 8.43.

## 3. Consistency

- **Frame format.** Speaker labels appear only in 1.8–1.11 (row 1).
- **Quotation marks.** Otherwise the stated convention is applied reliably. The scripted pass found the marks in every paragraph balanced, and every speech that runs across paragraphs reopens correctly (5.1→5.2, 6.9→6.10, 7.47→7.48, 7.53→7.54, 7.55→7.57, 7.58→7.66, 8.0→8.1, 8.29→8.30). The exceptions:
  - Verse lines set as separate paragraphs close the speaker's quotation (1.15–1.22, 1.44–1.46, 8.10–8.12; rows 5 and 58).
  - Verse quoted inside a quoted speech is in single marks in chapter 1 but in double marks inside single at 8.11.
  - 7.0 is unquoted while 7.2–7.44 are quoted (row 43).
  - At 8.38 Alcibiades's own advice is in quotation marks inside his unquoted speech.
  - The innermost quotation in 7.20, "I desire what I have and nothing else", is unmarked. This is readable, but italics would help.
- **Speaker tags.** The pattern "'X,' I said. 'Y.'" is used both when the same person keeps speaking (7.45 "'Hush!' she said. 'Must whatever…'"; 7.50 "'Happiness,' I replied. 'That question is easier to answer.'") and when the speaker changes (rows 44, 45, 47, 63). One rule should apply: tag every change of speaker that follows a tagged line.
- **Two speakers in one paragraph of narration.** This happens at 1.7 (Apollodorus and Glaucon) and 3.11 (Eryximachus and Aristophanes). It is readable, but elsewhere each new speaker gets a new paragraph.
- **Capitalization of Love.** Love/love switches within a single argument: 7.34 "Then Love lacks beauty", then 7.38 "Then would you still say that love is beautiful?" and 7.42 "Then if love lacks what is beautiful, it lacks…". Other examples are 6.4 "whom love inspires … anyone Love has not touched" and 7.52 "power of love. But those who turn to him". The pronoun switches between he and it along with the capital.
- **Capitalization of Heavenly and Common.** 3.0 has "is called the heavenly Aphrodite" but 3.1 has "the Heavenly Aphrodite". 3.6 has "The bad lover is the common lover" and 4.3 "the common love, which belongs to the Muse Polyhymnia".
- **Terminology.** One concept often has several names:
  - Pausanias's key word "yield" becomes "gratify" when Eryximachus recaps him (4.0 "it is honorable to gratify good men and dishonorable to gratify bad ones"), "favor" at 8.29 and "the acceptance of another" at 3.9.
  - "beloved" vs "loved one" (3.8).
  - "barbarians" (3.3) vs "among Greeks and non-Greeks alike" (7.61).
  - "gymnastics" (3.3) vs "physical training and farming" (4.1) vs "money-making or athletics or philosophy" (7.52).
  - The Silenus figures are "the busts of Silenus" (8.23), "the statues of Silenus that open up" (8.37) and "the carved head of the Silenus" (8.26).
  - The stages of the ascent (row 53), and "mind" (7.62) vs "soul" (7.63).
  - The word "everlasting" is used both for a good lover's attachment (3.6) and for beauty itself (7.64).
  - The opposite of ignorance is "wisdom" in 7.46 ("something in between ignorance and wisdom") but "knowledge" in 7.49 ("in between ignorance and knowledge"). This is tolerable.
- **Name forms.**
  - "Iapetus and Kronos" uses a Greek K beside the Latin forms Uranus, Asclepius and Dionysus.
  - "Athene", in "the weaving of Athene", is an older form where American readers expect Athena.
  - "Otys" is usually spelled Otus.
  - Ares appears only as "the God of War", capitalized, while the other gods are named.
- **Headings.** "Socrates & Diotima" is the only chapter title with an ampersand.
- **Voices.** The voices are distinct:
  - Phaedrus is earnest and argues from examples, though the reported-speech opening flattens him slightly.
  - Pausanias is legalistic, with his vocabulary of custom, law and honorable/dishonorable and his survey of city customs.
  - Eryximachus is pedantic: "my own art", his correction of Heraclitus, his prescription for hiccups.
  - Aristophanes is comic in the myth (cartwheels, the sorb-apple, hopping on one leg, "slices", the jab at statesmen) and earnest at the end, which seems intended.
  - Agathon is florid, especially in the jingling list of 6.6.
  - Socrates is ironic (6.9–6.10, "Socrates is easily refuted").
  - Diotima is oracular: "Hush!", "Even a child could tell you", the lesser and greater mysteries.
  - Alcibiades is drunk and confessional, with run-on asides such as 8.30 "listen, judges; for you shall be the judges of Socrates's haughty virtue — nothing more happened", plus oaths and self-mockery.

  The voices do not blur, beyond the honor/shame vocabulary Phaedrus and Pausanias share, which is natural since Pausanias is answering Phaedrus.
- **Author's verdict.** None found. Diotima's teaching sounds the most authoritative, but it stays inside Socrates's report ("I am convinced that it is true") and Alcibiades interrupts it at once. No speech is framed by editorial comment.

## 4. Tone and treatment

- **Frank, not prudish, overall.** "two sets of genitals" (5.0), "the desire for sex" (5.4), sex between men (5.3), Pausanias on boys (3.2), "beautiful boys and youths" (7.65) and Alcibiades's night with Socrates (8.29–8.30) are all stated plainly.
- **Places that soften the source:**
  - Slaves appear only as "servants" or "attendants", so the reader never learns they are slaves. Agathon also addresses one as "boy" ("'Go and look for him, boy,'" 1.29), which reads as dated and possibly offensive to modern American readers.
  - Pausanias's vocabulary ("yield", "these connections", "the acceptance of another") can be decoded but is coy, and "'uses base'" is opaque.
  - At 5.3, "they seek out men's company and embrace them" and the circular sentence about women (row 37) are noticeably more coy than the rest of the speech.
  - At 7.54, "it grows gentle, opens out in kindness" smooths over a physical image.
  - At 7.64, "Whoever rises from these things under the influence of true love" reads like a greeting-card phrase. From general knowledge I believe this is the well-known phrase about loving boys rightly, so it should be checked against the source.
  - At 8.26, "I thought this gave me a wonderful opportunity to hear from him everything he knew" leaves the bargain Alcibiades had in mind (his favors in exchange for Socrates's knowledge) unstated until 8.29.
- **No preachiness.** I found no modern editorial judgment, moralizing or added commentary on pederasty, slavery or the treatment of women ("at the hands of women", "the women in the house"). That is as it should be.
- **Anachronistic or jarring phrases.**
  - Worth changing:
    - "'Gives peace on earth and calms the stormy deep" (6.5) echoes the Christmas phrase.
    - "the best and brightest of leaders" (6.6) is a modern cliché with ironic overtones.
    - "rolling his eyes" (8.36) now signals exasperation.
  - Small modernisms that can stay: "regulate a gourmet's appetites" (4.3), "held more than two quarts" (8.6), "rich men and businessmen like you" (1.7), "reclining alone at the end of the table" (1.33, although the diners lie on couches) and "gymnastics" (3.3).
- **Colloquialisms.** "take your shot" (4.9), "Ever since I fell for him" (8.4), "rattle your nerves" (5.11), "Let me fix this oversight." (3.0), "a lame ending" (4.0) and "Now get on with it." (3.11) are lively and in character. I would keep them.
- **Archaic leftovers.** "'uses base'" (3.8), "hymn that praiseworthy one" (3.0), "some other particular of virtue" (3.8), "play the fool with them" (3.2), "is owing to the mental laziness" (3.4), "drowned in drink" (1.37), "most worthy of remembrance" (1.48), "'I am not equal to it,'" (1.39), "'For shame,'" (8.15), "'Hurrah!'" (8.44) and ":—" (6.12). The register swings between Victorian and contemporary; a light pass on these would even it out.

## 5. Errors

- **None mechanical.** I found no typos, doubled words, stray spaces or unbalanced quotation marks, both on reading and in the scripted pass over all 226 paragraphs.
- **Punctuation.** 6.1 ends a verse with a colon although a new sentence follows (row 39). 6.12 uses ":—" (row 41).
- **Breaks in the quotation-mark rule.** 1.15–1.22, 1.44–1.46 and 8.10–8.12 (rows 5 and 58).
- **Missing speaker tags that give lines to the wrong speaker.** 7.45, 7.46, 7.50, 7.51–7.52, 7.52 and 8.29 (rows 44, 45, 47 and 63).
- **Grammar and usage.**
  - 1.9: "the reason … is precisely because" (row 4).
  - 1.36: misplaced "badly" (row 10).
  - 1.43–1.44: double speech tag (row 11).
  - 7.59: misplaced "only" (row 51).
- **Redundancy and logic slips.**
  - 3.7: "money, wealth" (row 24).
  - 5.3: the sentence that goes in a circle (row 37).
  - 8.25: "He is the only person who has ever made me feel ashamed" … "and no one else does". This is tolerable in a drunk man's speech.
  - 8.27: "pretended that it was late" after "far into the night" (row 61).
  - 4.2: "What is in discord cannot be harmonized" (row 31).
- **Capitalization.** 3.0 has "the heavenly Aphrodite", but "Heavenly" is capitalized elsewhere.
