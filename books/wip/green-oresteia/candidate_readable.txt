=== CH1: Agamemnon — Prologue ===
[0] A WATCHMAN. I pray the gods to release me from this labor, to end the watch I have kept this whole long year. Lying like a watchdog with no real rest, propped on one arm upon the palace roof of the house of Atreus, too long and too well I have come to know the starry council of the midnight sky — too well, the splendors of the firmament, those royal lords of light whose movements, as they rise and set in turn, mark out the seasons of the year, bringing frost or fire.

[1] And now, as always, I am stationed here to watch for the moment when the signal-flame will blaze up — the beacon-fire that will tell its Trojan tale: Troy is taken. So she hopes, the one with a woman's body and a man's heart beating in her breast.

[2] And so I lie here on my restless couch, soaked with the dews of night, untouched by dreams — for instead of sleep, Fear stands beside me as my companion and drives off any soft repose that might close my eyelids. And whenever I try to medicine my soul with a snatch of melody or song to make up for the lost balm of sleep, soon enough I find myself in tears, lamenting the sorrow that hangs over this house — no longer governed by honor as it once was.

[3] But now at last may a happy hour come that will set me free — whenever the thick night blazes up with the beacon-fire of a hope deferred no longer. All hail!

[4] [A beacon-light is seen reddening the distant sky]

[5] Fire of the night that brings my spirit daylight, shedding on Argos light, and dance, and song — I greet you and welcome our good fortune!

[6] Let my loud cry ring out within the ears of Agamemnon's queen, so she may rise at once from her couch and lift a piercing shout of welcome to the beacon-blaze, for Ilion has fallen — that is the fiery message blazing from this high flame. And I, before all the rest, will dance the opening steps of our joy. For I can say, my master's dice have fallen well — see, the triple six, the lucky throw! Now may it be my lot to clasp in loyal love the hand of the one restored to us, who rules our home. Home — but I say no more: an ox stands heavy on my tongue, as the saying goes. If the house itself had a voice, it could best tell its own tale. As for me, I speak words the wise will understand, but to the rest, I remember nothing and recognize nothing.

[7] [Exit. The chorus of old men of Mycenae enter, each leaning on a staff. During their song Clytemnestra appears in the background, kindling the altars]

=== CH2: Agamemnon — Parodos ===
[0] CHORUS. Ten long years have rolled away since the twin lords of sceptered power, the mighty chieftains of the house of Atreus, endowed by Zeus with their high office, sailed forth long ago to plead with Priam face to face before the judgment-seat of War.

[1] A thousand ships from Argive land set out to bear that warlike host, who with stern and unyielding spirit went forth to right the kingdom's wrong. As they went, their battle-cry pealed out, wild as the cry of vultures when, soaring high above their eyrie in bereaved agony, they wheel round and round in airy rings, beating the air with the oarage of their wings — only to find the nestlings gone that once called them home. But let Apollo from on high, or Pan, or Zeus only hear the cry, the exile-cry, the forlorn wailing of birds whose home has been torn from them — and on those who did the savage robbery, Heaven sends down the avenging Furies of hell.

[2] Even so does Zeus, the jealous Lord and guardian of hearth and table, send the sons of Atreus in vengeful fury against Paris — sends them out, fired with rage, to win her back through war and bloodshed, the woman one man married but many men courted. And many, by his decree, will feel the last embrace of foes; many a knee will be bowed in the dust, and shattered spears ring loud against shields, both Trojan and Greek, before that iron wedding-feast is over. But all is ordered as he wills it, and the woes that heaven appoints must fall — the wrath of god, unsoothed by tears or wine poured out too late, glares vengeance on the flameless shrine.

[3] And we, in dishonored gray old age, too feeble of frame to be counted fit to join the warrior ranks that marched out to battle then, stay here at home, content to support our feeble steps each on his staff. So strength wanes and turns again to childishness. For while the sap of youth is still green, still unripe and leaping within, the young are weak as the old, and neither one fit to hold the post of war. And ah! when flower and fruit are gone, and the leaves on life's tree are withered, old age makes its dreary journey leaning on a prop — as feeble as a child, as light and fleeting as a dream of the night, lost in the glare of day.

[4] But you, O child of Tyndareus, Queen Clytemnestra — speak! Tell us what messenger of joy has reached your ears today, what welcome news has moved you to bid altar-fires rise in sacrifice all the way out to the city's borders. Every god who guards our city and keeps watch over Argos from heaven above and earth below — the mighty lords who rule the skies, the lesser deities of the marketplace — for each and all of them the altars are blazing, piled high for the sacrifice. Here and there, near and far, many a beacon-star streams up toward heaven, conjured and charmed and kindled well by the soft, guileless spell of pure oil, hidden no longer in the palace's secret store.

[5] O queen, we beg you — whatever you know, if it can rightly be revealed, share it with us. Set our anxious hearts at ease. They waver now between despair and a fair-rising hope. Hope dawns from the altar — to drive the vulture Care from our torn hearts.

[6] Listen — for the power is mine to chant on high the kings' great undertaking, and the strength that the omens gave them. Listen — even now a melody breathes upon my soul, sent from realms of ageless powers, mighty to save.

[7] How the brother kings, twin lords of one command, led forth the youth of Hellas in their flower, urged on their way with vengeful spear and brand by warrior-birds that watched the hour of their parting.

[8] Go forth to Troy! the eagles seemed to cry — and the sea-kings obeyed the sky-kings' word, when on the right they soared across the sky, and one was black, one bore a white tail barred.

[9] High over the palace they were seen to soar, then settled in full sight of all and tore apart, far from the fields she would roam no more, a mother-hare heavy with her unborn young.

[10] And one man saw — the soldier-prophet, true-eyed — and in those two-colored eagles he at once recognized the two chieftains, unlike in soul and will. He spoke the omen aloud, for good and ill.

[11] (Ah, sorrow and sorrow! but may the outcome be fair!)

[12] Go forth, he cried, and Priam's town shall fall. Yet long the time will be; and the flock and herd, the people's wealth that roam outside the walls, must be hewn down by force when Fate gives the word.

[13] But beware! lest wrath remain in heaven, to dim the glowing forge of battle once more, and mar the mighty curb that should bridle Trojan pride — the steel of vengeance, welded as for war.

[14] For the virgin Artemis bears jealous hatred against the royal house, the eagle-pair, who tear apart the unborn brood, never satisfied — yes, she loathes their feast on the quivering hare.

[15] (Ah, sorrow and sorrow! but may the outcome be fair!)

[16] For well she loves — the kindly, gentle goddess — the tender new-born cubs of bold lions, too weak to range; and well she loves the suckling young of every beast that wanders wood and field.

[17] So to the Lord of Heaven she still prays: "No — if it must be, let the omen prove true. Yet the visioned eagles foretell ill: may the end be well, but it is crossed with evil too!"

[18] Healer Apollo! hold her wrath in check. Do not weave the long delay of contrary winds, to thwart the Greeks and hold their sails back from the open sea.

[19] For she craves — alas — to see a second life shed forth, an accursed and unhallowed sacrifice — a thing that breeds strife between wedded souls, a hate that knows no fear, a deadly device.

[20] For at home, like a lurking snake, there waits a wrath unreconciled, biding its time — a cunning watcher, passionate to slake in blood its vengeance for a murdered child.

[21] Such was the mighty warning that pealed forth long ago — amid good news, that word of fear — when the fateful eagles hovered above the kings, and Calchas read the omen plain.

[22] (In strains like his, once more, sing sorrow and sorrow! but may the outcome be fair!)

[23] Zeus — if to the Unknown that name of many names seems fitting — Zeus, upon you I call. I have travelled every road of the mind, and all are vain, except the road that names you Zeus, the Highest One — if only it might be mine to throw off this burden, this weary burden that weighs my spirit down.

[24] He who was Lord of old, in the full-blown pride of place and valor bold, has fallen and is gone, like an old tale told. And he who next held the throne, overthrown by a stronger grip, has passed away. And whoever now bids the song of triumph rise to Zeus, and Zeus alone, he shall be found truly wise. It is Zeus alone who shows the perfect path of knowledge — Zeus has ordained that mortals shall learn wisdom only through suffering.

[25] In visions of the night, like falling rain, the many memories of pain descend upon the spirit's sight. Through tears and grief, wisdom steals over the unwilling soul — a gift, I think, of all that is divine, sitting on its sacred throne in strength above the sky.

[26] And then the elder chieftain, at whose command the Greek fleet was manned, cast no word of hatred at the seer, but bowed before the sudden breath of Fate.

[27] Ah, weary time! For before they could put out to sea, every store ran low and every diminished vessel failed, while all the Achaean host lay anchored at Aulis, looking across to Chalcis and the coast where the back-flowing waters surge and rock. And rife with cruel delay, the contrary blast blew from northern Strymon — that mother of cruel famine which keeps men wandering still, far from the harbor where they long to be — and pitilessly it wasted each ship and cable rotting at sea, and as each weary hour was doubled by delay, withered the warlike flower of the Achaeans with hope deferred.

[28] But when, as a deadlier relief from the bitter storm — a remedy heavier with ill to either chief — the seer revealed the wrath of Artemis, the two sons of Atreus struck their scepters on the ground, and try as they might, could not hold back their tears. And then the elder king cried out: "It would be a hard lot to disobey! And hard, to strike my own child, my household's love and pride — to stain a father's hands with virgin blood and kill my daughter at the altar! I am caught between woes — I dare not flee like a coward and abandon the league of ships, failing every true ally. For rightly they crave, with eager and fiery hearts, the virgin's blood poured out to calm the contrary wind. God grant the deed be well!"

[29] So he took upon his neck the hard compelling yoke of Fate. Then, in the contrary gale of a will turned dreadful and accursed, his shifting spirit veered toward recklessness. Alas! that Frenzy, first of ills and the worst, has always stirred men's souls with cunning to evil deeds.

[30] And so he steeled his heart — ah, woe — to slay his own child to aid a war fought for one false woman's sake, and with her spilled blood make an offering to speed the ships upon their way.

[31] Lusting for war, the bloody overseers closed heart and ears, and would not hear or heed the girl's pleading voice — "Pity me, Father!" — nor her prayers, nor her tender, virgin years.

[32] So, when the chant of sacrifice was done, her father ordered the young attendants of the priesthood to lift her — like some poor kid — above the altar-stone, where amid her robes she lay sunk in a swoon away. He bid them, as with the bit that mutely tames a horse, to silence her fair lips' speech, lest she should curse the house of Atreus and its line.

[33] So, trailing along the ground her saffron-dyed robe, with one last piteous arrow shot from her pleading eye, she struck those who were about to strike her down — beautiful and silent, like a figure in a painting, yet longing to plead: "Has it all been forgotten? How often the halls of old, where my father held high feast, rang with the soft virginal song when I, a stainless child, sang from pure and undefiled lips — sang of my father and all his honored life, and how on him should fall heaven's highest gift and prize!" And then — but I did not see, and cannot tell what further fate befell. But this is certain: Calchas's prophetic word can never come to nothing. This is the wage that sufferers earn from the hand of Justice — to discern the future. And yet — farewell, O secret of Tomorrow! Foreknowledge is foresorrow. Clear with the clear beams of tomorrow's sun, the future presses on. Now, however dark the house's tale may be, may it yet find a fair outcome! So prays the loyal, solitary band that guards the land of Apia.

=== CH3: Agamemnon — First Episode ===
[0] [They turn to Clytemnestra, who leaves the altars and comes forward]

[1] CHORUS. O queen, I come in reverence of your power — for while the king's high seat is empty, the loyal heart bows before his consort. Now — whether it be sure and certain news of good, or only the fair tidings of a hopeful fancy, that has moved you to spread this light from shrine to shrine, I am eager to hear, yet I will not begrudge you if you keep silent.

[2] CLYTEMNESTRA. As the saying goes: From the womb of Night comes forth, full of fair promise, the young child Light. Yes — my news is even fairer than all hope: by Greek hands the city of Priam has been taken!

[3] CHORUS. What are you saying? A doubting heart makes a treacherous ear.

[4] CLYTEMNESTRA. Hear it again, and plainly — Troy is ours!

[5] CHORUS. Such joy thrills through my heart that it brings tears.

[6] CLYTEMNESTRA. Yes — through those tears your eyes show your loyalty.

[7] CHORUS. But have you proof, to make our certainty sure?

[8] CLYTEMNESTRA. I have, indeed — unless the god has lied.

[9] CHORUS. Has some vision in the night won you to belief?

[10] CLYTEMNESTRA. Away with all such omens of a slumbering soul!

[11] CHORUS. But were you cheered by Rumor's wingless word?

[12] CLYTEMNESTRA. Enough — you scold me as if I were a credulous girl.

[13] CHORUS. Tell me then — how long ago did the city fall?

[14] CLYTEMNESTRA. Even in this very night that is now bringing forth the dawn.

[15] CHORUS. Yet who could be swift enough to bring the message here?

[16] CLYTEMNESTRA. From the top of Ida, Hephaestus, lord of fire, sent forth his signal — and on and on, beacon to beacon, sped the courier-flame. From Ida it leapt to the crag of Lemnos, the one that Hermes loves; from there to the high summit of Athos, throne of Zeus, the broad blaze flared. From there, lifted up to shoot across the sea, the moving light rejoiced in its strength and sped from a pyre of pine, urging its way in golden glory like some strange new sun, onward, until it reached the watching heights of Macistus. There, with no sluggish delay or careless sleep, the watcher sped the tidings on in turn, until the guard on Messapius's peak saw the distant flame gleam over Euripus's tide, and from a high-piled heap of withered furze lit the new signal and sent the message on. Then the strong light, far-flown and still undimmed, shot through the sky above Asopus's plain, bright as the moon, and on Cithaeron's crag roused another watch of flying fire. And there the sentinels in no way disowned the duty, but sent on doubled the command of the flame — swift shot the light over Gorgopis's bay to Aegiplanctus's mountain, and bade that peak not fail the onward ordinance of fire. And like a long beard streaming in the wind, full-fed with fuel, the blaze roared and rose, and flaring onward gleamed above the cape beneath which the Saronic bay shimmers, and from there the light leapt to Arachne's peak, the mountain watch that looks upon our town. From there it came to the roof of Atreus's sons — a bright descendant, in fair lineage, of Ida's fire. So it sped from stage to stage, each in its turn fulfilled, flame after flame along the appointed course, and behold! the last to speed upon its way is the first to sight the end and glow unto the goal. And Troy is taken, and by this sign my lord tells me the tale, and now you have heard my word.

[17] CHORUS. To heaven, O queen, I will lift up a new song. But if you would speak once more, I would gladly hear from beginning to end the marvel of the tale.

[18] CLYTEMNESTRA. Imagine — this very morning — the Greeks inside Troy, and the loud sound of utter lamentation within. Pour vinegar and oil into one cup, and see — they remain unmixed, unreconciled, at war with each other. So in the twofold outcome of the struggle, the shout of the victor and the moan of the captives are mingled. For all the conquered whom the sword has spared cling weeping — some to a slain brother, some like children to the body of a fostering father, and they wail the loved and lost, while already their necks bow beneath the captive's chain. And meanwhile the victors, now that the fighting is done, goaded by restless hunger, range disordered through every part of the town, snatching such food and rest as chance may give them in the captured halls that were once Troy — joyful to be rid of the frost and dew under which they bivouacked on the plain of old, joyful to sleep through the kindly night all the way, with no sentinel's call to summon them. And yet — let them duly revere the city's gods, the lords of fallen Troy, and her shrines; so shall the spoilers not in turn be spoiled. Yes, let no craving for forbidden gain make our conquerors give way before the arrows of greed. For we still need, before the race is won, to round the course once more — homeward, unharmed. For if the host should grow reckless before it returns home, then, even though the sudden blow of fate may be spared, still in the eyes of the gods

[19] the great wrong of the slain shall rise once more to claim revenge. Now, having heard from this woman's mouth the tale and the warning that goes with it, pray with me: may Luck tip the scale with no uncertain weight. For my fair hopes have already been changed to fairer joys.

[20] CHORUS. A gracious word, my queen — your woman's lips have spoken what is worthy of a wise man's utterance. Now with clear trust in your convincing tale, I set myself to salute the gods with song — the gods who bring us joy to counterbalance our pain.

=== CH4: Agamemnon — First Stasimon ===
[0] [Exit Clytemnestra]

[1] CHORUS. Zeus, Lord of heaven! And welcome, night of victory, that has crowned our strength with all glory. Over the towers of Ilion, free no longer, you have flung the mighty net of war and drawn it tight around them, until neither warrior may escape nor stripling lightly leap over the meshes as they close and close, until our enemies are bound in the grip of doom in the coil of slavery.

[2] Zeus, Lord of hospitality — in grateful awe I bow to you. It is you who struck the blow! Long ago we saw you bend your vengeful bow at Alexander, but you held back the eager arrow long and warily — for if it had been loosed too soon or aimed too high and let fly without restraint, it would have wandered through the sky without drawing blood.

[3] Zeus, the high God! — whatever else may be hidden in doubt, this much our thought can trace: the blow that strikes down the sinner comes from God, and as he wills, the rod

[4] of vengeance falls heavy. Someone in the old days said: "The gods do not concern themselves to settle accounts with the man whose feet trample on the grace of holiness" — an impious word! For whenever a father has breathed forth rebellious fire — when his household has overflowed the proper measure of bliss and health and treasure — his children's children read the bill at last in tears and pain. May my fortune bring no woe with it, and may I be content with that. Whoever spurns the shrine of Right shall find no wealth or power high enough to be his tower of safety from the gulf — there is his lot, where all things are forgotten. Lust drives him on — desperate, reckless lust, the sin-contriving child of Fate — and there is no cure. Beyond all hiding, the deadly glare of his sin breaks out clearly. Just as a worn coin, beneath the rubbing touch, betrays its false metal by stain and smudge, so is the sinner. Before him on light wings flits fair Pleasure, luring him on like a child, while home and kin groan beneath the grinding burden of his crime. And in the end of time, cast down by heaven, he pours out fruitless prayer to powers that will not hear.

[5] Such was the man Paris, who came to the home of Atreus's son and there, paying back his welcome with sin and shame, ravished away the wife. And she, leaving behind for her country and her kindred the clash of shields and spears and arming ships, and bringing destruction to Troy as her dowry — overbold in her sin — she went swiftly through the gates at the midnight hour. Often from the prophets' lips moaned out the warning and the wail: Woe! Woe for the home, the home! And woe for the chieftains! Woe for the bridal bed, still warm with the impress of the lovely body of her who loved her lord a little while ago! And woe for him who stands shamed, silent, uttering no reproach, stretching out his hands that find her not — and seeing, will not see, that she is far away! And his sad fancy, yearning out across the sea, shall summon and call back her phantom to queen it once more in his hall. And sad with many memories, the fair cold beauty of each sculpted face — all their grace turned to hatefulness, seen blankly by forlorn and hungering eyes! And when the night runs deep, come visions sweet and sad, bearing the pain of vain hopes — empty, empty and vain. For scarcely has the sleeping sight seen its old delight when, escaping from the embraces of love that try to hold it, it vanishes away on silent wings that wander down the paths of sleep.

[6] Such are the sights, the dreadful sorrows about our hearth — and worse, things I may not tell. But all through the wide town, every home that sent its master far away from the shores of Hellas feels today the keen thrill of heart, the pang of loss. For to tell the truth, the touch of bitter death takes many forms. Each face that went to the war was familiar, and dear as life. But to the place from which the warrior set out, nothing returns — nothing but a spear and sword, and ashes in an urn. For Ares, lord of strife, who holds the swaying scales of battle, the money-changer of war, giving dust for gold, sends back to hearts that held them dear scant ash of warriors, wept over with many a tear — light to the hand, but heavy to the soul. Yes, he fills the light urn full with what survived the flame — death's dusty measure of a hero's frame.

[7] "Alas!" one cries, "and yet alas again! Our chief is gone, that hero of the spear — and he has left no equal!" "Ah, woe!" another moans — "my husband is slain, an honorable death, rolled in dust and blood, slain for a woman's sin, the shame of a false wife!" Such mutterings of bitter mood rise against those who went out to reclaim her — yes, jealous wrath creeps on against the name of Atreus's sons.

[8] And others, far beneath the wall of Ilion, sleep their last sleep — the goodly chiefs and tall, lying in the enemy's land, where they gave up their breath and now, lords of Troy in their Trojan graves, each one rests.

[9] Therefore for each and all of them the city's breast is heavy with a wrath suppressed, as deep and deadly as a curse cried out by the common crowd. And brooding deeply, my soul awaits tidings of the coming fate, still buried in the womb of darkness. For the doom of the high gods is not forgetful against the sons of slaughter. Too long does the unjust man seem to prosper and grow strong, until the dark Furies come and with stern reversal smite all his house down into dim ruin — and he is gone, and among the lost there is no help and no hope.

[10] Over the man who boasts an excessive fame, a fitting woe hangs ready: the vengeful thunderbolt blazes upon his eyes, sped from the hand of god. This blessing be mine, ungrudged by God, to know — to trample no city into the dust, nor see my own life thrust down to a slave's estate beneath another man's heel.

[11] See how through the wide city the swift feet of Rumor have run, roused by the joyful flame. But is the news they scatter true? Or do they perhaps offer for truth some deception that heaven has framed? A child he would be, and all unwise, who let his heart be stirred with joy at the rising of beacon-fires, only to sicken with deferred hope when some contrary word came. The edge of a woman's insight all too easily cuts good news off from true. Light rumors leap quickly within the bounds that fence in female credence — but the tale that springs from her guesswork, as lightly as it is born, dies just as lightly.

[12] Soon we shall know what the beacon-fires tell — those signal-flames kindled with transmitted fire — whether, as I am inclined to think, their tale is true, or whether the welcome blaze came like some delusive dream to fool our souls. For look! I see a herald drawing near from the shore, his head shadowed with the olive-wreath — and the thirsty dust, twin-brother of the clay, speaks plainly of long travel and of truthful news. No silent guesswork, no tongue of flame in smoke fitfully kindled from a mountain pyre — but his voice will say more plainly: All is well — or — but away with you, contrary forebodings, now,

[13] and may fair fulfillment come upon fair promise! And whoever prays otherwise for the state — let him reap the harvest of his own evil desire!

=== CH5: Agamemnon — Second Episode ===
[0] [Enter HERALD]

[1] HERALD. O land of Argos, fatherland of mine! At last, beneath the sun of the tenth year, my feet return to you. The ship of my undertaking, though one by one its anchors of hope broke away, has held by the last and now rides safely home. Long, long my soul despaired of ever winning, in death, its longed-for rest within our Argive land. And now all hail, O earth, and hail to you, newly risen sun! And hail our country's God, high-ruling Zeus — and you, the Pythian lord, whose arrows once smote us — smite us no more! Was not your wrath wreaked fully upon our heads, O King Apollo, beside the Scamander? Turn now, be turned, be savior, healer! And hail, all you gods who rule the streets and marketplaces — and Hermes, hail! my patron and my pride, herald of heaven, lord of heralds here. And you, Heroes who sped us on our way — to one and all I cry: Receive again with grace such Argives as the spear has spared.

[2] Ah, home of royalty, beloved halls, and solemn shrines, and gods that face the morning! As benignly as before, with sun-flushed faces, greet your king on his return after so many days. For just as out of night the beams of day flash forth, so out of darkness a light, a king, dawns now upon you, upon Argos — Agamemnon comes. Then hail and greet him well! Such honor befits the man whose right hand hewed down the towers of Troy with the great axe of Zeus, righter of wrongs — and beat down the plain, beat each altar and every shrine to nothing. Far and wide all the land's fair offspring is dying.

[3] Such a mighty yoke of fate did he set on Troy — our lord and monarch, the elder son of Atreus — and now at last he comes home in blessed honor, the highest of all who walk on earth today. Neither Paris nor the city itself, which paid the price of sin together with him, can boast: "Whatever happens, the prize we have won outweighs it all." No — at the judgment-seat of Fate the robber stands condemned for his rape, and his prey is torn out of his hands. By his own deed a bloody harvest is reaped: his home and land have gone down to death, and for his guilt and lust his father's race pays double in the dust.

[4] CHORUS. Hail, herald of the Greeks, newly come from war.

[5] HERALD. All hail! Not death itself can frighten me now.

[6] CHORUS. Was your heart wrung with longing for your homeland?

[7] HERALD. So that even now this joy fills my eyes with tears.

[8] CHORUS. Then this same sweet distress fell on you too —

[9] HERALD. How do you mean? Make me master of your meaning.

[10] CHORUS. You longed for us who pined for you again.

[11] HERALD. Did the land long for us as we longed for it — love returning love?

[12] CHORUS. Yes — until my brooding heart moaned out with pain.

[13] HERALD. Where did such despair come from, that mars the army's joy?

[14] CHORUS. "The only cure for wrong is silence" — so the saying goes.

[15] HERALD. With your kings far away, were there other men you feared?

[16] CHORUS. Death would have been sweet — as you yourself just said.

[17] HERALD. It is true: Fate smiles at last. Throughout our hardship, all these years, some chances turned out fair, and some, I admit, were chequered with a curse. But who on earth has won the bliss of heaven, an unbroken prosperity through the whole length of his time? I could unfold a tale of toiling oars, of poor rest, of scant landings on rock-strewn shores — every kind of pain, every kind of sorrow, was our daily lot. And on land our woes were worse and more hateful, for where we slept, close beneath the enemy's wall, the river-plain was always damp with dews dropping from the sky and oozing from the earth — a curse that clung to our soaked clothing and to our hair, as bristling as a wild beast's hide. Why tell the woes of winter, when the birds lay stark and stiff, so cruel was the snow on Ida? Or summer's scorch, when the still wave sank to its sleep beneath the noonday sun? Why mourn old griefs? Their pain has passed; and for those who fell, all care has passed away forever — they will not rise again.

[18] Why count up our deaths, and pay back the gift of life by moaning over hostile fate? Farewell — a long farewell to all our woes! For us, the remnant of the Greek host, the good outweighs every counterweight of woe. So we may rightly boast to that bright sun, like him sweeping far over sea and land: "The Argive host prevailed and conquered Troy, and in the temples of the gods of Greece they hung up these spoils, a shining sign for Time." Let those who hear this tale rightly bless the city and its chieftains, and pay the meed of gratitude to Zeus, who willed and brought about the deed. So stands the story fulfilled.

[19] CHORUS. Your words overcome my doubt — for good news, an old man's ear has youth enough. But those within and Clytemnestra herself would gladly hear all of it. Gladden their ears and mine.

[20] [Re-enter CLYTEMNESTRA]

[21] CLYTEMNESTRA. Last night, when first the fiery messenger came as a sign that Troy is taken and razed to the earth, so wild a cry of joy broke from my lips that I was chided — "Has the beacon-watch made the sack of Troy a certainty in your soul? You are quite the woman, whose heart leaps lightly at wandering rumors!" — and with words like these they showed me how I had strayed, misled by hope. And yet I set the sacrifice on every shrine, and in that very style they call feminine, heralds went through the city to and fro proclaiming joy with loud voices. And in every temple they kindled and quenched with wine the spicy incense fading in the flame. All is fulfilled. I spare you the longer tale — the king himself shall soon tell me everything.

[22] What remains is to think how best to honor and welcome my lord, the majesty of Argos, home again. What day shines fairer on a woman's eyes than this, on which she flings open the gate to greet her lord, shielded by heaven, returning from the war? Tell this to my husband — let him not delay, but come, and turn the city's longing into joy. Yes, let him come, and let him find when he comes a wife no different from the one he left: true and faithful as a watchdog to his home, foe to his foes, loyal in all her duties, trustworthy to keep for ten long years unbroken the store on which he set his master's seal. Steel must run deep with blood before you look to see ill joy or ill fame from any other man — least of all in me!

[23] HERALD. Fairly said. Such words befit a noble lady — and they speak no falsehood, when truth is in the boast.

[24] [Exit Clytemnestra]

[25] CHORUS. So she has spoken — and it is for you to learn, by clear interpreters, the meaning of her plausible word. Turn to me, herald — tell me, will the second well-loved lord of Argos soon arrive? Has Menelaus come safely home with you?

[26] HERALD. Alas — it would be a brief favor to my friends to flatter them, for truth, with fair lies.

[27] CHORUS. Speak joy, if truth be joy — but the truth, even at its worst. Too plainly, truth and joy are here divorced.

[28] HERALD. The hero and his ship were torn away, far from the Grecian fleet — that is the truth I tell.

[29] CHORUS. Was it in full sight of all, carried off from Ilion, or torn from the fleet by stress of weather?

[30] HERALD. Full on the mark your shaft of speech has fallen — one short word has told the long woes aright.

[31] CHORUS. But say — what does each comrade now say of him? What do they fear about his life or death?

[32] HERALD. Ask no more: the truth is known to no one, except the earth-fostering, all-surveying Sun.

[33] CHORUS. Say — by what doom was the Greek fleet driven? How did the storm, the wrath of heaven, rise and fall?

[34] HERALD. No — it would be wrong to mar with a tale of sorrow this day of blessed news. The gods demand thanksgiving kept apart from anxious cares. If a herald came with rueful face to say, "The curse has fallen and the army has gone down to death; one wide wound has reached the city's heart, and from many homes many men have been cast out and consecrated to death, under the double scourge that Ares loves — the bloody pair, the fire and sword of doom" — if such heavy burden weighed on my tongue, then it would be fitting to speak words that gladden fiends. But coming, as I come, with news of safe return from toil and a fair issue, to men who rejoice in a restored prosperity — dare I dash good words with ill, and tell how the gods' anger smote the Greeks in the storm? For fire and sea, who once held a bitter feud, swore conspiracy now and pledged their faith to lay waste the Argives, worn out with toil and war. Night and the great horror of the rising waves came over us, and the blasts that blow from Thrace clashed ship with ship; and some, with plunging prows, vanished through scudding drifts of spray and raving storm, like strays driven by some bad shepherd. And when at last the sun rose bright, we saw the field of the Aegaean sea flecked with flowers of death — corpses of Greek men and shattered hulls. For us, indeed, some god, as I am sure — no human power — laid hand upon our helm. He snatched us, or pleaded for us, from the powers of the air, and brought our ship through it all unharmed in the hull. Saving Fortune herself sat at the helm and steered us fair, so that no surge should engulf us in the brine, and no rocky shore should grind our keel.

[35] So we escaped the death that lurks beneath the sea, but in the white light of day, distrustful of every smile of fortune, we sat and brooded deeply, like shepherds forlorn, our thoughts wandering wildly over this new woe. For our host had been struck down and lost like ashes scattered from the pyre. If any of them still draws breath, you may be sure he counts us as dead, just as we forebode no other fate for him. But heaven save them all! If Menelaus is alive, he will not delay — he will surely come. Therefore, if anywhere the high sun's ray sees him on earth, preserved by Zeus, who does not yet will to wipe out his race, there is still hope that he may make his way home. Enough — you have the truth, to the end.

=== CH6: Agamemnon — Second Stasimon ===
[0] CHORUS. Tell me, from whose lips fell the prophecy? Who read the future all too well, and named her at her birth-hour Helen — the bride with war for her dowry? It was one of the Invisible ones, guiding his tongue with prescient power. Upon fleet and host and citadel, war sprung from her, and death loured down, when from the bridal bed's fine-spun veil she spread her sail to the West Wind.

[1] Strong blew the breeze, and the surge closed over the cloven track of keel and oar; but while she fled, fast in her wake there drove along a mighty throng — thirsty for blood, thirsty for war. Forward in fell pursuit they sprang, and leapt ashore on the bank of Simois among the leafy thickets — no rangers of wood and field were they, but huntsmen of the sword and shield.

[2] Heaven's jealousy, working its will, sped thus its destined ill upon Troy — well named, at once, the Bride and the Bane. Loud rang out the bridal song; but those for whom that song was sung soon turned to tears again. Zeus delays, but he avenges still the husband's wrong, the household's stain. He, the lord of the hearth, will not endure to see its hospitality outraged.

[3] Even now, in a far different tone, Troy chants her dirge of mighty mourning: "Woe upon Paris, woe and hate — who wooed his country's doom for a mate!" This is the burden of the groaning lament with which she disconsolately wails the blood that so many of her own have poured out in vain to ward off her fate. Troy! you have fed and let roam free a lion-cub within your home!

[4] A suckling creature, newly taken from its mother's teat, still wholly eager for nursing care; often caressed, held in arms and to the breast, lying like an infant. Or fawning and licking, when hunger pressed it, the hand that would soothe its pain — in life's young dawn a well-loved guest, a darling for the children's play, a joy to the old and gray.

[5] But growing time and maturity betray the blood-thirst of the lion-race; and as repayment for the household's fostering care, all unbidden it revels there and pays back its bloody recompense. Its claws tear rent flesh; a mighty beast, that slays and slays, and mars with blood the fair household — a God-sent plague invincible, a minister of fate and hell.

[6] Even so came stealing into the city of Ilion a spirit as of windless seas and skies — a gentle phantom-form of joy and wealth, love's soft arrows speeding from its eyes — love's rose, whose thorn pierces the soul in subtle ways.

[7] Ah, woe! the bitter bridal-bed, when the fair mischief lay at Paris's side! What curse on palace and on people sped along with her — the Fury sent on Priam's pride by angry Zeus! What tears of many a widowed bride!

[8] Long, long ago this was told to mortals: that sweet security and blessed state have curses for their offspring — so men say — and for the man of all-too-prosperous fortune, from a bitter seed there springs some unappeasable woe.

[9] I, alone among them, judge it far otherwise. It is not happiness or wealth from which that aftergrowth of evil rises, but the impious deed. Woe springs from wrong, and the plant is like the seed — while Right, in the house of honor, breeds its own likeness.

[10] Some past impiety, some gray old crime, breeds the young curse that runs riot in our suffering — early or late, when the appointed time comes — and still brings forth from light the power of darkness, a master-fiend, a foe unseen and invincible:

[11] an accursed pride that broods upon the race and home where dark Ate holds her sway — child of Sin and of Woe, wearing the face of its parents — while Right shines clear as day in smoky cribs, and adorns with prosperity the life of the man who walks the righteous way.

[12] From gilded halls that polluted hands have raised, Right turns away her proud averted eyes, and from the wealth that men miss-stamp with praise, heedless, she retires to poorer, holier shrines, and guides all things to Fate's goal in its appointed way.

[13] Hail to you, chief of Atreus's line, returning proud from conquered Troy! How shall I greet your victorious face? How shall I neither press my praise too far, nor stint the meed of gratitude? For mortal men, when they fall into ill, pay little heed to open truth and seek only its semblance. The show of weeping and of pity will all men pay to the forlorn — but of the grief their eyes display, nothing pierces through to the heart. And with the joyful they twist their lips into a feigned smile, forcing a joy they do not feel; but the man who, like a wise shepherd, truly knows his flock can never misread truth in the falsehood of those eyes that hide, beneath a kindly guise, a lukewarm love. And as for you, our leader — when long ago you bade Greece march to war for Helen's sake — I dare to say that I did not hold you then as I do now; in my eyes, you were tinged with the hues of low esteem. I held you for a poor pilot, reckless of your own will, endowing others doomed to die with empty, forced audacity. But now, from my heart, ungrudgingly, to those who did the deed, let this word be said —

[14] [Well done — the labor that you have sped —]

[15] Let time and inquiry declare, O king, what men within your city's bounds were loyal in the kingdom's care, and who were found unfaithful.

=== CH7: Agamemnon — Third Episode ===
[0] [Enter Agamemnon in a chariot, accompanied by Cassandra. He speaks without descending]

[1] AGAMEMNON. First, as is fitting, let a king's all-hail be spoken to Argos and to the gods who guard the land — gods who, with me, helped speed us home, and with me wrung from Priam's town the due of justice. In the court of heaven the gods sat in conclave and judged the case, not from a pleader's tongue, and at the end, unanimous, they cast this sentence into the urn of doom: "Death upon Ilion and her men." And to the urn of pardon, where Hope drew near, no hand was raised to cast a vote. And still the smoke of fallen Ilion rises in the sight of all men, and the flame of Ate's hecatomb still lives. Where the towers sink down in dusty ashes, the rich fumes of consumed pomp and wealth rise up. For all this men must pay the gods the meed of mindful hearts and gratitude. For by our hands the meshes of revenge closed on the prey, and for one woman's sake Troy lies trampled by the Argive monster — the foal, the shielded band that leapt the wall, when with autumn the Pleiades sank low. Yes, over the ringing wall a lion sprang, ravening, and lapped its fill of the blood of kings.

[2] Such is the prelude I have spoken in full to the gods. Now I turn to you, and to that hidden matter you spoke of just now. In that thought I am as you are, and what you say, I say. For few are those who possess such inborn grace as to look up with love and not with envy when another stands on the height of prosperity. Deep in the heart of the man whom jealousy has seized, her poison lurks and increases his burden — for he chafes now under his own woes, and on top of that sighs at the sight of another's good fortune. I do not speak idly, but from sure knowledge — there are men who boast utter loyalty that is no more than the ghost of a dead friendship, a shadow in a mirror, of a faith long gone. One man only — he who went reluctantly across the seas with me — Odysseus — was loyal to me with strength and will, a trusty trace-horse harnessed to my chariot. So — whether he is still beneath the light of day, or dead, as I much fear — I speak his praise.

[3] Last of all, whatever is owed to men or to gods, we will decide by joint debate held in public council, and warily contrive that all which now is well may stay so; and for what may need the healer's art, we will medicine it, judging well whether cautery or knife best fits the time.

[4] Now, into my palace and to the shrines of home I will pass; and first I greet you fairly, you gods, who bid me forth and home again — and long may Victory tarry in my company!

[5] [Enter Clytemnestra, followed by maidens bearing purple robes]

[6] CLYTEMNESTRA. Old men of Argos, lieges of our realm — shame shall not make me shrink from showing before your eyes the love I bear my lord. Such blushing fear dies away at last from human hearts. From my own soul, and from no alien lips, I know and will reveal the life I bore — reluctant — through the lingering, livelong years while my lord beleaguered the wall of Ilion.

[7] First, that a wife sat sundered from her husband in widowed solitude was utter woe — and woe, to hear how rumor's many tongues all foretold evil — woe, when one came and another followed, each speaking of fresh misfortune, wailing through hall and chamber: "Lost, lost, all lost!" Had this husband of mine taken as many wounds as rumor's thousand channels reported, he would have been more full of holes than any net. Had he been slain as often as the tidings of his death came in, he might well boast himself now a second Geryon of triple frame, with a triple covering of earth laid above him — for that below, no matter — three times dead, dead by one death for each form he wore. And so, distraught by news of wrath and woe, often I had slung the noose for self-slaughter, but others tore it from my neck. This is why Orestes — yours and mine, the pledge and symbol of our wedded faith — does not stand here beside us as he should. Do not marvel at this: he lives with one who guards him loyally — Strophius, king of Phocis, who warned me long ago: "Bethink yourself, queen, what doubtful sorrows may yet fall! Your lord daily in danger at Troy, and here at home an unbridled populace may cry, 'Down with the council, down!' Bethink yourself too — it is the way of the world to set a heavier heel on fallen power." Such is my excuse for our child's absence — no cunning afterthought. As for me, the gushing fount of tears was wept dry long ago; not a drop is left to shed. Dim are the eyes that watched until dawn, weeping over your beacon-fires, piled night after night for your return and never kindled. If I slept, every sound — the tiniest humming of a gnat — woke me again and again from fitful dreams in which I felt you struck and saw you slain, three deaths for every moment of my hour of sleep.

[8] All this I bore, and now, released from woe, I hail my lord as the watchdog of a sheepfold, as the saving stay-rope of a storm-tossed ship, as the stout column that holds the roof aloft, as the only child of a bereaved father, as land glimpsed past all hope by forlorn crews, as the bright sunshine when the storm's wrath is over, as a gushing spring to a thirsty wayfarer. So sweet it is to escape the press of pain. With such a salute I bid my husband hail! Heaven not be angered with it — for long and hard I bore that ancient anger. Sweet lord, step forth, step from your chariot, I pray you — but, O king, do not plant on the bare earth the proud foot that trod down Troy! Women! Why do you delay, whose task it is to spread your monarch's path with tapestry? Swift, swift, with purple strew his passage fair, so that Justice may lead him at last to a home he scarcely looked to see. For all that remains, zeal unsubdued by sleep shall nerve my hand to work as Right and as the gods command.

[9] AGAMEMNON. Daughter of Leda, watcher over my home, your greeting befits my long absence well — for late and hardly has it come to its end. But know that the praise which honor bids us crave must come from the lips of others, not our own. And see that you do not, in womanish fashion, make a warrior's pathway delicate. Do not bow yourself to the earth before me as before some Eastern lord and offer loud homage. Do not strew this purple that will make every step an arrogance; such pomp befits the gods, not me. A mortal man to set his foot on these rich dyes? I hold such pride in fear, and I bid you honor me as a man, not as a god.

[10] Fear not — without these footcloths and all such ornaments, the trumpet of Fame blows my name loud and clear. The best gift of heaven, in the hour of glory, is to think on it with soberness. And as for you — bethink yourself of the saying: "Call no man blessed until peaceful death has crowned a life of prosperity." That is said — and I would gladly fare untroubled by fear.

[11] CLYTEMNESTRA. No — unsay it. Do not thwart my will!

[12] AGAMEMNON. Know that I have said it, and I will not mar my word.

[13] CLYTEMNESTRA. Was it fear that made you so meek before the gods?

[14] AGAMEMNON. If a cause is a cause, that is mine for this resolve.

[15] CLYTEMNESTRA. What do you think Priam in your place would have done?

[16] AGAMEMNON. He surely would have walked on broidered robes.

[17] CLYTEMNESTRA. Then do not fear the voice of human blame.

[18] AGAMEMNON. And yet the murmur of a crowd is mighty.

[19] CLYTEMNESTRA. Do not shrink from envy, the appanage of bliss.

[20] AGAMEMNON. War is not a woman's part — nor a war of words.

[21] CLYTEMNESTRA. Yet happy victors may well yield in such a contest.

[22] AGAMEMNON. Do you crave a triumph in this petty strife?

[23] CLYTEMNESTRA. Yield — of your grace, permit me to prevail!

[24] AGAMEMNON. Then, if you will, let someone stoop to loose these sandals quickly — slaves at my feet — and stepping thus upon the sea's rich dye, I pray: "Let none among the gods look down with a jealous eye on me" — reluctant indeed to trample and mar a thing of such price, wasting the wealth of garments worth their weight in silver. Enough of this. As for the stranger maid, lead her inside, but gently: God on high looks graciously on the man whose hour of triumph has not made him pitiless. No one willingly bears the slave's yoke — and she, the prize and flower of all we won, comes here in my train, the army's gift to its chief and lord. — Now, since in this matter my will bows down to yours, I will pass in on purples to my home.

[25] CLYTEMNESTRA. There is a Sea — and who shall stay its springs? And deep within its breast lies a mighty store, precious as silver, of the purple dye by which the dipped robe renews its hue. There lies enough of it within your halls, O king — a store that cannot fail. But I — I would gladly have vowed to the gods the cost of a thousand garments trodden in this way (had any oracle ever asked such a gift) to contrive a ransom for the saving of your life. For while the stem is firm, the foliage climbs and spreads a shade in the season when the dog-star glows; and you, returning to your hearth and home, are like a kindly warmth in winter hours, or like a coolness when the lord of heaven mellows the juice within the bitter grape. Such blessings, and more, the present footstep of its proper lord brings into a home. Zeus, Zeus, lord of fulfillment! Fulfill my vows, and whatever it may be, work out your will!

=== CH8: Agamemnon — Third Stasimon ===
[0] [Exeunt all but Cassandra and the Chorus]

[1] CHORUS. Why does a dreary vision forever hover before my boding heart on the wings of fear? An unbidden, unwelcome strain thrills in my ear, oracular of pain. No longer, as of old, does Confidence sit on the throne of my breast and spurn such fears, like dreams we cannot interpret. Old, old and gray long ago has grown the time when these eyes saw the linked cables mooring the fleet on its first arrival at Ilion's sandy shore. And now my own eyes, and no other's, have seen its safe return.

[2] Yet none the less, within me the inner spirit sings a song of foreboding — self-prompted, it sings the Furies' strain — and seeks, seeks in vain, to hope and to be strong.

[3] Ah! to some unseen, unguessed end of Fate, these wild throbbings of my heart and breast must tend. Yes — they tell of some doom: every pulse a knell. Gladly, gladly would I see all this fall away into the hidden realm of unfulfillment.

[4] Too far, too far our mortal spirits strive, grasping at utter prosperity, never satisfied — until the fierce curse that dwells close beside thrusts down the wall between. Too fair, too fair are the breezes that waft our bark on Fortune's tide! Swift we sail — only the sooner to drive upon the hidden rock, the reef of woe.

[5] Then if the hand of caution warily flings out into the sea part of the cargo, lest all should sink, that throw saves the ship from utter destruction. Even so, doom-laden though it may be, the household of him who is timely wise may rise once more.

[6] How often the famine-stricken field is saved by God's great gift, the new year's yield! But the blood of a man once spilled, once shed at his feet to darken the plain — no chant or charm can call it back again.

[7] So Zeus has willed it. Otherwise he would have spared even Asclepius, the healer skilled to bring men back from the dead: the divine hand struck him down with death — a warning and a sign.

[8] Ah me! if Fate, ordained of old, did not hold the will of the gods constrained and controlled, helpless toward us, and apart — swifter than speech my heart would have poured out its presage! Now, fretting and chafing in the dark of doubt, it is hopeless to unfold truth from the tangled skein of fear; and yearning to proclaim its thought, my soul is prophecy and flame.

[9] [Re-enter CLYTEMNESTRA]

[10] CLYTEMNESTRA. Get inside, you too, Cassandra — go! For Zeus in gracious mercy grants you to share the sprinklings of the lustral bowl, beside the altar of his guardianship, a slave among many slaves. What, still proud? Step down from the chariot. Alcmena's son, they say, was sold once and bore the yoke. Yes, it is hard; but if such fate befalls you, it is a fair chance to serve within a house of ancient wealth and power. An upstart lord, to whom wealth's harvest came beyond his hope, is like a lion to his slaves — fierce in everything, immoderate in his rule. Go in. You have heard what our ways will be.

[11] CHORUS. Her command to you is plain, O maiden — but you are caught within the toils of Fate. If your will allows, I urge you to obey; yet I fear you neither hear nor heed.

[12] CLYTEMNESTRA. I think — unless, like a swallow, she speaks some strange barbarian tongue from over the sea — my words must persuade her soul.

[13] CHORUS. Obey: there is no gentler way than this. Step down from the high seat of the chariot and follow her.

[14] CLYTEMNESTRA. Enough of this useless waiting outside! I will not stay. Beside the central shrine the victims stand, ready for the knife and the fire — offerings from hearts that have been gladdened beyond all hope. You — if you give any heed to my command, it would be well done quickly. But if your understanding is shut against my words, let your barbarian hand fulfill by gesture the lack of speech.

[15] CHORUS. She is no native of this country, that she should read your words unaided. Look — like some wild thing of the wood newly trapped, she shrinks back and glares at you.

[16] CLYTEMNESTRA. It is madness, the rule of a distraught mind. Since she watched her city sink in fire, she comes here and will not bear the bit, until in foam and blood her wrath has been champed away. See to her, you. It is unqueenly of me to cast away my words and have them unheeded.

=== CH9: Agamemnon — Fourth Episode ===
[0] [Exit Clytemnestra]

[1] CHORUS. But in me pity sits where anger should. Poor maiden, come down from the chariot. There is no other way — take up your servitude.

[2] CASSANDRA. Woe, woe, alas! Earth, Mother Earth! And you, Apollo, Apollo!

[3] CHORUS. Peace! Do not shriek so to the bright prophetic god — he will not endure the suppliance of grief.

[4] CASSANDRA. Woe, woe, alas! Earth, Mother Earth! And you, Apollo, Apollo!

[5] CHORUS. Listen — with a wild curse she calls again on him who stands far off and loathes the voice of mourning.

[6] CASSANDRA. Apollo, Apollo! God of all ways, but only of Death's to me — once and again, O you who are named Destroyer, you have destroyed me, you, my love of old!

[7] CHORUS. She grows full of presage of her coming woes — slave though she is, alive with prophecy.

[8] CASSANDRA. Apollo, Apollo! God of all ways, but only of Death's to me — O Apollo, you who are named Destroyer! What way have you led me — to what evil home?

[9] CHORUS. Do you not know it? The home of the house of Atreus. Take my words for truth and ask no more.

[10] CASSANDRA. House cursed of God! Bear witness to me, you visions of woe within — the blood-stained hands of those who strike their kin — the strangling noose, and the reeking floor spattered with human blood!

[11] CHORUS. Like a bloodhound questing on the trail, keen-scented after blood and death, she goes!

[12] CASSANDRA. Ah! can the ghostly guidance fail by which my prophet-soul is led on? Look! the spectre-children wail for their flesh, their sodden limbs on which their father fed!

[13] CHORUS. Long ago we heard of your prophetic fame — but for those deeds we need no prophet's tongue.

[14] CASSANDRA. God! it is another crime — worse than the storied woe of olden time, a curseless, abhorrent thing that someone is plotting here — a shameful death, for those who should be dear! Alas, and far away in a foreign land stands the one who should help!

[15] CHORUS. The old tales I knew — the city rings with them. But your speech now is dark, beyond my understanding.

[16] CASSANDRA. O wretched woman, O fierce design! For your wedded lord you have poured out the cleansing wave — a treacherous welcome! How shall I tell what comes next? Too soon it will come — too soon — for now, even now, she strikes him, blow on blow!

[17] CHORUS. Riddles beyond my reading. I peer in vain through the dim films that screen the prophecy.

[18] CASSANDRA. God! a new sight! a net, a snare from hell, set by her hand — and she herself the deadlier snare! A wedded wife slays her lord, helped by another hand! You powers, whose hatred for the house of Atreus no blood can satiate, raise the wild cry above the abhorred sacrifice!

[19] CHORUS. Why do you call upon some fiend — I do not know whom — to shriek over the house? Yours is no cheering word. Back to my heart in frozen fear I feel my waning life-blood run — the blood that ebbs slowly around the wounding steel, as life's parting sun sinks down. Swift, swift and sure, some woe comes pressing on!

[20] CASSANDRA. Away, away — keep him away — the monarch of the herd, the pasture's pride, far from his mate! In treacherous wrath, muffling his swarthy horns with secret violence, she gores his unguarded side! Hark! in the brimming bath, the heavy splash — the dying cry — hark — in the laver — hark, he falls by treachery!

[21] CHORUS. I read amiss such dark sayings as yours, yet something warns me they tell of evil. O dark prophetic speech, you teach ill tidings always to mortals here below! Always some tale of awe and woe through all your manifold windings do we untangle and unfold!

[22] CASSANDRA. Ah, woe! the cup of agony of which I sing now foams with a draught for me. Ah lord, ah leader, you have led me here — was it only that I should die with you, whose doom is near?

[23] CHORUS. You are distraught, divinely stirred, and wail a tuneless dirge for yourself, as piteous as the ceaseless tale with which the brown melodious bird forever cries Itys! Itys! deep-bowered in sorrow all the days of her little life.

[24] CASSANDRA. Ah, for your fate, O shrill-voiced nightingale! Some solace for your woes did Heaven afford — clothed you with soft brown plumes, and a life set apart from wailing — but for my death is sharpened the double-biting sword!

[25] CHORUS. What pangs are these, what fruitless pain, sent on you from on high? You chant terror's frantic strain — yet in shrill measured melody. How can you sweep along, unerring, the prophet's path of boding song?

[26] CASSANDRA. Woe, Paris, woe upon you! Your bridal joy was death and fire upon your race and Troy! And woe for you, river Scamander! On your banks, O fair stream, I grew up under tender nursing care from childhood into maidenhood. Now not by yours, but by Cocytus's stream and on the banks of Acheron my boding scream shall ring.

[27] CHORUS. Too plain is all of it, too plain! A child could read your fateful song aright. Deep in my heart Terror and Sorrow set their piercing fang while I heard that piteous, low, tender word — yet to my ear and heart a crushing pang.

[28] CASSANDRA. Woe for my city, woe for the fall of Ilion! Father, how often the blood of cattle, slain that heaven might guard our wall, streamed in sanguine stain on your altar-stone! But all was poured out in vain. Low lie the shattered towers where they fell — and I — ah burning heart! — I shall soon lie low as well.

This is the sum and final issue of an old strife, deeply pondered by me and at length fulfilled. All is avowed; and as I struck I stand, with my foot set firm on a finished thing! I do not turn to denial: thus I worked, so that he could neither flee nor ward off his doom — even as the trammel hems in the scaly shoal, I trapped him in inextricable toils, the evil abundance of a baffling robe. Then I struck him, once, again — and at each wound he cried out, then as in death his every limb relaxed and he sank to earth. And as he lay, I struck him once more, with the third and last blow, sacred to Hades, savior of the dead. And so he fell; and as he passed away, his spirit chafed against his body. Each dying breath flung from his breast swift bubbling jets of gore, and the dark sprinklings of the rain of blood fell upon me — and I was glad to feel that dew. === CH11: The Libation Bearers — Prologue ===
[0] ORESTES. Lord of the shades and patron of the realm where my father once held sway, hear my prayer now, Hermes, and save me with your aiding arm — me who, returning from banishment, stand here on my own country's soil. See, my foot is set on this grave-mound, and like a herald — like you — once and again I bid my father hear me. And these twin locks, shorn from my head, I bring as offerings: one I dedicate to Inachus the river-god, the nurturer of my young life; the other, in sign of mourning unfulfilled, I lay — though late — on this my father's grave. For O my father, I did not stand beside your body to wail your death, nor was my hand stretched out to carry you to burial.

[1] What sight is that? What is this throng of women coming this way, made plain as mourners by their sable garb? What has happened? Has some new sorrow come upon the house? Or am I right to think they are drawing near bearing libations, such as soothe the wrath of angered dead men, to my father's grave? Yes — that is what they are; for I can make out Electra, my own sister, walking this way, conspicuous in her moody grief. Grant me, O Zeus, grant me to avenge my father's murder — be my willing champion! Pylades — let us step aside, until I can rightly discern why these women are gathered here in supplication.

[2] [Exeunt Pylades and Orestes; enter the Chorus bearing vessels for libation; Electra follows them; they pace slowly towards the tomb of Agamemnon]

=== CH12: The Libation Bearers — Parodos ===
[0] CHORUS. Forth from the royal halls by high command I bear libations for the dead. My striking hand rings on my stricken breast, and all my cheek is rent and red, freshly furrowed by my nails — and all my soul, for many a day, has fed on cries of grief. And trailing tatters of my robe hang in looped and tattered ruin, forlorn, rent around my breast — just as I, saddened and torn by the sternest blows of Fate.

[1] Oracular through visions ghastly clear, bringing a blast of wrath from the realms below, stiffening every rising hair with dread, Fear came out of dreamland; and, loud and awful, it bade the shriek ring out at midnight's witching hour, and brooded, stern with woe, above the inner house — the women's chambers. And inspired seers read the dream on oath, chanting aloud: "In the realms below, the dead are wroth; against their slayers their anger still glows."

[2] Therefore to bear this gift of graceless worth — O Earth, my nursing mother! — the god-accursed woman sends me forth, lest one crime bring on another. The very word is ill to speak — for nothing can ransom or atone for blood once shed, darkening the plain. O hearth of woe and ruin! O state that lies low! Sunless, accursed of men, the shadows brood above the home of murdered majesty.

[3] Rumor of might, unquestioned and unsubdued, pervading the ears and souls of lesser men, is silent now and dead. Yet a viler dread now rules; for prosperity and power, however won, dazzle our mortal sight as gods, and more than gods.

[4] Justice keeps watch with scales that swiftly sway: some who still walk in light, others in the interspace of day and night, until Fate rouses them, she stays; and some are lapped in night, where all things are undone.

[5] On the life-giving lap of Earth blood has flowed forth; and now, the seed of vengeance, it clots the plain — the stain unmelting, uneffaced. And Ate tarries long, but at the last the sinner's heart is cast into pervading, growing pangs of pain.

[6] See, when man's force opens the virgin gates, there is no cure or hope for what is lost. So too, I think, though all the streams of Earth ran in one channel, washing the hand defiled by the stain of murder, it would be in vain.

[7] And upon me — ah me! — the gods have laid the woe that wrapped round Troy, when they led me down from my home and kin into a slave's employ — the doom to bow my head and watch my master's will work out deeds of good and ill, to see the headlong sway of force and sin, and to hold back the bitter hatred of my spirit, wailing the monarch's fruitless fate, hiding my face within my robe, and aching for tears, chilled with the frost of hidden pain.

=== CH13: The Libation Bearers — First Episode ===
[0] ELECTRA. Handmaidens, orderers of the palace halls — since you come at my side, a suppliant train, companions of this offering — counsel me as best befits the time. For I, who pour upon the grave these funereal streams, with what fair word can I invoke my father? Shall I declare, "Behold, I bring these gifts from a well-loved wife unto her well-loved lord," when it is from her, my mother, that they come? I dare not say it. Of all words I lack the one with which to consecrate to my father these sacrificial honors on his grave. Or shall I speak this word, as mortals are wont to do — "Give back to those who send these wreaths a full recompense — ill for ill deeds"? Or shall I pour this draught for Earth to drink without word or reverence, as my father was slain, and pass homeward with eyes that do not turn back, casting the bowl away as one who flings the household sweepings into the common road? Be a partner with me, my friends, in this my doubt — even as you are sharers in that one common hatred which is our companion in life. Have no fear of any man's wrath, and do not hide your word within your breast: the day of death and doom awaits the freeman and the slave alike. Speak, then, if you know anything that may help us further.

[1] CHORUS. You bid me — I will speak my soul's thought out, revering as a shrine your father's grave.

[2] ELECTRA. Speak then your say, as you revere his tomb.

[3] CHORUS. Speak solemn words to those who love him, and pour.

[4] ELECTRA. And of his kin, whom shall I dare to name as kind?

[13] CHORUS. This: "Upon them may some god or mortal come" —

[14] ELECTRA. As judge, or as avenger? Speak your thought.

[15] CHORUS. Pray in set terms: "Who shall the slayer slay."

[16] ELECTRA. Is it seemly to ask such a boon of heaven?

[17] CHORUS. How not? — to wreak a wrong upon a foe.

Homeless we are, and sold; and she who sold us is the very one who bore us; and the price she took is the man who joined with her to work your death — Aegisthus, her new lord. Behold me here, brought down to a slave's estate; and far away wanders Orestes, banished from the wealth that once was yours, the profit of your care — on which they revel now in shameful joy. Father, my prayer is said; it is yours to hear it. Grant that some fair fate bring Orestes home, and grant me these things: a purer soul than my mother's, a hand more stainless than hers."

[20] These be my prayers for us; and for you, O father, I cry that someone may come to smite your foes — that the slayers may in their turn be slain. Cursed is their prayer, and thus I bar its path, praying my own, a counter-curse against them. And you — send up to us the righteous gift for which we pray: let heaven and earth be your aids, and let justice guide the right to victory.

[21] [To the Chorus]

Let the teardrop fall, splashing on the ground where our lord lies low; let it fall and cleanse away the cursed libation's stain shed on this grave-mound, where together, fenced within, are gathered the gifts of good or evil from the dead. Lord of Argos, hear us! Though around you darken the mist of death and hell, arise and hear! Hearken and awaken to our cry of woe! Who with might of spear shall deliver our home? Who, like Ares, shall bend the northern bow until it quivers? Who with hand on hilt shall thrust with the glaive — thrust and slay and save?

[25] ELECTRA. See — the earth drinks the libations; they pass to my father. Learn with me of this strange new thing.

=== CH14: The Libation Bearers — Kommos ===
[0] ELECTRA. Yes, and my heart overflows with the gall of grief; I am pierced as with a cleaving dart. Like the first drops of rain after drought, my tears fall freely, a bitter bursting tide, as I gaze upon this lock. I cannot believe that any Argive but Orestes himself was ever lord of it. Nor — well I know — has she, the murderess, shorn and laid this lock in mourning for the man she slew — my mother she, with no mother's heart, but a spirit of loathing toward her own race, and itself loathed by heaven. Yet to affirm with utter certainty that this offering comes from the hand of my Orestes, brother of my soul — that I cannot venture, though hope flatters fair. Ah, woe — that this dumb hair had a voice to gladden my ears like a messenger, bidding me sway no longer between fear and hope, plainly commanding: "Cast me away from here — I was clipped from a head you do not love"; or, "I am kin to you, and like you I have come here to weep and adorn our father's grave." Aid me, you gods! For well indeed you know how, in the gale and counter-gale of doubt, like a sailor's bark, we whirl and stray. But if God wills our life, how strong shall the new tree of our home spring up from a seed how small! See — a second sign! These footsteps — look — like my own, a corresponding print; and look, another footmark — this his own, and that the foot of one who walked beside him. Mark how heel and tendon-prints together combine, exact in measure, coincident with mine! Alas! For doubt and anguish rack my mind.

[1] ORESTES (approaching suddenly). Pray, in gratitude for prayers fulfilled: "Fair fall the rest of what I ask of heaven."

[2] ELECTRA. Why? What do I win from the gods by prayer?

[3] ORESTES. This: that your eyes behold your heart's desire.

[4] ELECTRA. On whom of mortals do you know that I call?

[5] ORESTES. I know your deep yearning for Orestes.

[6] ELECTRA. Tell me then — wherein has the event crowned my prayer?

[7] ORESTES. I — I am he. Seek no one more akin.

[8] ELECTRA. Some fraud, O stranger, you are weaving for me?

[9] ORESTES. Against myself I weave it, if I weave one.

[10] ELECTRA. Ah — you have a mind to mock me in my woe!

[11] ORESTES. It is at my own woe, then, that I mock — when I mock yours.

[12] ELECTRA. Do I speak to you, then, as to Orestes himself?

[13] ORESTES. My very face you see, and you do not know me. And yet just now, when you saw the lock shorn for my father's grave, and when your search was eager upon the footprints I had made, the very sight of them — me, your brother, shaped and sized as you are — fluttered your spirit. Lay now this ringlet beside the place from which it was cut, and judge; and look upon this robe, the work of your own hands — the shuttle-prints, the creature wrought into it — restrain yourself; do not lose prudence in your joy, for well I know our kin are less than kind.

[14] ELECTRA. O you who are to our father's house love, grief and hope — for you the tears ran down — for you, the son, the savior who should be! Trust your own arm and win back your father's halls! O aspect sweet of a fourfold love to me — for the heart's compulsion bids me call you father, and the claim of love from me toward my mother turns to you, for she is very hatred; to you too turns whatever of my heart went out to her who died a ruthless death upon the altar-stone; and as for myself, I love you — you who were a loyal brother, my sole stay of love. Now by your side be strength and right, and may Zeus the savior almighty stand to aid us both!

[15] ORESTES. Zeus, Zeus! look down on our estate — on us, the orphaned brood of him, our eagle-sire, whom a fearful serpent brought to his death, enwinding him in coils. And we, bereft and without food, sink with famine, all too weak to bear back to the eyrie, as he bore, the kind of quarry that he slew. Lo! I and she, Electra, stand before you, fatherless — each alike cast out and made homeless.

[16] ELECTRA. And if you leave to death the brood of him whose altar blazed for you, whose reverence was yours — all yours — from where, in years to come, shall any hand like his adorn your shrine with sacrifice of flesh? With the eaglets slain, you would have no messenger to bear your omens, once so clear, to mortal men; so, if this kingly stock is withered all, none on high festivals will tend your shrine. Stoop and raise us up! Strong the race shall show, though puny now it seems, and fallen low.

[17] CHORUS. O children, saviors of your father's home, beware your words, lest someone hear them and bear them — for the tongue has a lust to tell — to our masters; whom God grant me to see in the pitchy reek of funeral flame!

[18] ORESTES. No — Apollo's oracle is mighty, and shall not fail me, who was bidden by it to pass through all this peril. Clear the voice rang out with many warnings, sternly threatening my hot heart with the wintry chill of pain, unless I pressed for vengeance on the slayers of my father. This was the god's command: that I, in fury for the home and wealth despoiled, should with a craft like theirs slay the slayers; or else, with my very life I should atone for this deed undone, in many a ghastly form. For he declared in the ears of men that offerings poured out to the angry powers of death exude again, if their will is not done, as grim disease upon those who poured them — leprous ulcers mounting on the flesh, with fierce fangs corroding what of old wore its natural form. And on the brow arise white poisoned hairs, the crown of this disease. He spoke too of assailing fiends empowered to exact from me my father's blood, wreaking their wrath upon me, in the night, when beneath shut eyelids the spirit's eye sees clear. The dart that flies in darkness, sped from hell by spirits of the murdered dead who call upon their kin for vengeance — formless fear, the night-tide's visitant, the curse of madness — these should drive and rack me; and my tortured frame should be chased forth from man's society, as with the brazen scorpions of the scourge. For me and such as me, no lustral bowl should stand, no spilth of wine be poured to God for me, and the unseen wrath of my dead father should drive me from every shrine; no man should dare to take me to his hearth or dwell with me. Slow, friendless, cursed of all should be my end, and pitiless horror should wind me for the grave. This the god spoke. Dare I disobey it? Yes — even if I dared, the deed must still be done. For diverse desires combine to that one end: the god's command, deep grief for him who died, and last, the grievous emptiness of wealth despoiled — all these weigh on me, urging that Argive men, the minions of valor, who with souls of fire reduced fenced Troy to a ruinous heap, should not be left as slaves to two — and each of those a woman! For he, the man, wears a woman's heart; or if not, soon shall he know it, confronted by a man.

=== CH15: The Libation Bearers — Second Episode ===
[0] [Orestes, Electra, and the Chorus gather round the tomb of Agamemnon for the invocation which follows]

[1] CHORUS. Mighty Fates, on you we call! Bid the will of Zeus ordain power to those to whom Justice once again turns with hand and aid! Grievous was the prayer one made — grievous let the answer fall! Where the mighty doom is set, Justice claims aloud her debt: who has dipped the steel in blood, deep in blood his measure shall feel. Hear the immemorial word: "Whoever takes up the sword shall perish by the sword."

[2] ORESTES. Father, unblest in death, O father mine! What breath of word or deed can I waft to you from this far place, down to your lowly bed — to waft upon you, lying in the midst of darkness, hope's counter-gleam of fire? Yet the loud dirge of praise brings undying grace to every parted father.

[3] CHORUS. O child, the spirit of the dead — although the grim teeth of the flame have fed upon his flesh — is not quelled. [4] ELECTRA. Hear me too, even me, O father, hear! These groans, these tears upon your sepulchre are shed not by one child alone. [5] CHORUS. No — if so the god wills, he can still turn our tears to joy. He can bid a song of triumph drown the dirge beside this urn. He can welcome to kingly halls the child restored, the homeward-guided feet.

[7] CHORUS. For mark — that serpent sprang from the same womb as I; in swaddling-bands it was wrapped by the same hands; it lipped the same breast, and sucking forth the same sweet mother's milk infused a clot of blood; and in alarm she cried out the cry of pain over her wound. The reading is clear: the thing of dread she nursed, the death of blood she dies; and it is I — I — in the semblance of a serpent, who must slay her. You are my seer, and thus I read the dream.

[60] CHORUS. So do — yet before you act, speak to us; some by acting and some by not acting may aid.

[61] ORESTES. My command is brief: I bid my sister pass in silence into the house, and I bid you all conceal this design of mine with wariness — so that those who by craft slew a chieftain may by like craft and in like noose be taken, dying the death that Loxias foretold — Apollo, king and prophet undisproved. I, with this warrior Pylades, will come in the likeness of a stranger, fully equipped as travellers come, and at the palace gates will stand, as a stranger yet allied to this house in friendship's bond. And each of us will speak the tongue that sounds round Parnassus, feigning such speech as Phocian voices use. And if none of those who tend the gates welcomes us with gladness, since the house is haunted with ills divine — if that should be — we will wait at the gate until some townsman, passing by, makes his conjecture and proclaims: "How? Is Aegisthus here, and knowingly keeps suppliants aloof, by bolt and bar?" Then I will win my way in; and if I cross the threshold of the gate, the palace's guard, and find him throned where my father once sat — or if he should come and, face to face confronting, drop his eyes from mine — I swear he shall not utter, "Who are you, and where from?" before my steel leaps, and compassed round with death he shall lie low; and thus, full-fed with doom, the Fury of the house shall drain once more a deep third draught of rich unmingled blood. But you, O sister, look that all within be well prepared to bring these things to pass. And you — I say it would be well to bear a tongue full of fair silence and of fitting speech, each as the time requires. And last, do you, Hermes the warder-god, keep watch and ward, and guide to victory my striving sword.

=== CH16: The Libation Bearers — Second Stasimon ===
[0] CHORUS. Many and marvellous are the things of fear that Earth's breast bears; and the sea's lap teems with many monsters; and windy thunderbolts and meteor gleams breed many deadly things — unknown and flying shapes, with fear upon their wings, and in their tread death; and rushing whirlwinds, whose blasting breath man's tongue can tell. But who can rightly tell of that fiercer thing, the aweless soul that dwells within man's breast? Who can tell how, passion-fraught and love-distraught, the eager craving thought of woman weds mankind to woe and to fierce ruin? Yes, how the loveless love that possesses woman, even as it possesses the lioness, rends and wrests apart, with eager strife, the link of wedded life?

[1] Let him be witness whose thought is not borne on light wings through the air, but abides with knowledge, of what thing was wrought by Althea's despair. For she marred the life-grace of her son, with ill counsel rekindling the flame that was quenched as it glowed on the brand — what time from her womb he came, with the cry of a new-born child; and from the burning she won the brand, for the Fates had foretold it coeval, in life and death, with her son.

[2] Yes — and man's hatred tells of another: even Scylla of murderous guile, who slew her own father for an enemy's sake, won over by the wile and the gifts of Cretan Minos, the trinkets of high-wrought gold. For she clipped from her father's head the lock that should never grow old, as he breathed in the silence of sleep and knew not her craft and her crime. But Hermes, the guard of the dead, lays hold of her in the fullness of time.

[3] And since I tell of the crimes of the cruel, let my song record the bitter, loveless wedlock — the curse poured out upon these halls — the crafty device of a woman by which a chieftain fell, a warrior stern in his wrath, the fear of all his enemies — a song of dishonor, untimely! And cold is the hearth that once was warm, ruled now by the cowardly spear, the unwomanly arm of a woman.

[4] But the summit and crown of all crimes is that which befell in Lemnos: a woe and a mourning it is, a shame and a thing of spitting to speak of; and he who in after time speaks of his deadliest thought says: "It is like the deed wrought of old in Lemnos." And loathed of men were the doers, and perished — they and their seed — for the gods brought hatred upon them; no one loves the impious deed.

[5] It is well to tell of these tales — for the sword in the grasp of Right with a cleaving, piercing blow strikes to the innermost heart, and the deed unlawfully done is neither trodden down nor forgotten, when the sinner oversteps the law and heeds not the high God. But Justice has planted the anvil, and Destiny forges the sword that shall smite in her chosen time; by her the child is restored; and, darkly devising, the Fiend of the house, world-cursed, shall repay the price of the blood that was shed in the day gone by.

=== CH19: The Eumenides — Prologue ===
[0] THE PYTHIAN PRIESTESS. First, in this prayer, of all the gods I name the prophet-mother Earth; and Themis next, who second sat — for so it is truthfully said — on this her mother's oracular shrine. Then by her grace, who unconstrained allowed it, another child of Earth sat there — Titanian Phoebe. She, in after time, gave over the throne, as a birth-gift to a god, to Phoebus, who in his own name bears Phoebe's. He from the lake and ridge of Delos's isle steered to the port of Pallas's Attic shores, the home of ships; and from there he passed and came to this land and to Parnassus's shrine. And at his side, revering him with awe, went the children of Hephaestus's seed, the hewers of the sacred way, who tame the stubborn tract that once was wilderness. And all this folk, and Delphos, chieftain-king of this their land, with honor gave him home; and in his breast Zeus set a prophet's soul, and gave him this throne on which he sits, fourth prophet of the shrine; and called Loxias, he gives voice to what Zeus his sire decrees.

[1] Such gods I name in my preluding prayer; and after them, I call with honor due on Pallas, wardress of the temple, and on the Nymphs who dwell about the Corycian rock, where in the hollow cave, the wild birds' haunt, wander the feet of lesser gods. And there, right well I know it, Bromian Bacchus dwells — since in his godhead he led his Maenad host, devising death for Pentheus, whom they tore piecemeal, like a hare among the hounds. And last, I call on Pleistus's springs, on Poseidon's might, and on Zeus most high, the great Accomplisher. Then, as a seeress, I pass to the sacred chair and sit; and may the divine powers make this entrance of mine fruitful in response beyond every former coming — three times blessed. And if there stand outside, bound from Hellas, men seeking oracles, let each pass in by the order of the lot, as custom allows; for the god guides whatever my tongue proclaims.

[2] [She goes into the interior of the temple; after a short interval, she returns in great fear]

[3] Things fell to speak of, fell for eyes to see, have sped me forth again from Loxias's shrine, with strength unstrung, moving erect no more, but aiding with my hands my failing feet, unnerved by fear. An old woman's force is nothing — is as a child's, when age and fear combine. For as I paced toward the inmost fane, bay-filleted by many a suppliant's hand, see — at the central altar I espied one crouching as for refuge — a man, indeed, abhorred of heaven; and from his hands, in which he held a sword newly drawn, blood reeked and fell. A wand he bears, the olive's topmost bough, twined as if of purpose with a deep close tuft of whitest wool. This — what I plainly saw — I plainly tell. But look, in front of him, crouched on the altar-steps, a grisly band of women slumbers — not like women they, but rather like Gorgons; no — that word is weak, nor can I match the shape of the Gorgons with theirs. Such have I seen before in painted likeness — winged Harpies, snatching food from Phineus's board — but these are wingless, black, and all their shape an abomination for the eye to behold. Fell is the breath — let none draw near it — with which they snort in slumber; from their eyes ooze the damnèd drops of poisonous wrath. And such their garb as no one should dare to bring before statues of the gods or homes of men. I know nothing of the tribe from which can come so fell a legion, nor in what land Earth could rear, unharmed, such creatures, nor avow that she had travailed and brought forth such death. But for the rest, let all these things be a care to the mighty Loxias, the lord of this our shrine: healer and prophet he is, discerner of portents, and cleanser of other men's homes — behold, his own to cleanse!

[4] [Exit]

[5] [The scene opens, disclosing the interior of the temple: Orestes clings to the central altar; the Furies lie slumbering at a little distance; Apollo and Hermes appear from the innermost shrine]

[6] APOLLO. Look — I never desert you. To the end, hard at your side as now, or sundered far, I am your guard; and against your enemies I implacably oppose myself. Look on them — these greedy fiends, subdued beneath my craft! See, they are fallen asleep — these aged hags, to whose grim and withered maidenhood neither god nor man nor beast can ever come near. Evil were they born, for evil's doom; evil the dark abyss of Tartarus where they dwell, and they themselves the hatred of men on earth and of Olympian gods. But you, flee far and with unfaltering speed. For they shall hunt you through the wide mainland, wherever throughout the tract of travelled earth your foot may roam, and over and over the seas and island homes of men. Faint not, nor fail; do not too soon and timidly shepherd in your breast forlorn thoughts about this toil. But go to Pallas's city, and there crouch at her shrine, and fold in your arms her ancient image. There we shall surely find meet judges for this cause, and persuasive pleas, skilled to contrive deliverance for you from all this woe. Be such my pledge to you — for by my command you slew your mother.

[7] ORESTES. O King Apollo — since you know all too well what justice bids, have heed and fulfill it. Your strength is all-sufficient to achieve it.

[8] APOLLO. Have heed yourself — and do not let your fear prevail above your will. And do you guard him, Hermes, whose blood is brother to mine, whose sire is the same high God. Men call you guide and guard; therefore guide and guard my suppliant — for Zeus himself reveres the outlaw's right, the gift of fair escort conferred upon man.

=== CH20: The Eumenides — Parodos ===
[0] [Exeunt Apollo, Hermes, and Orestes. The Ghost of Clytemnestra near]

[1] GHOST OF CLYTEMNESTRA. Sleep on! Awake! === CH21: The Eumenides — First Episode ===
[0] ORESTES. Look on me, queen Athena! I come at Loxias's command. Of your grace, receive me — driven by avenging powers — not now a red-handed slayer unannealed, but with the guilt fading, half-effaced, worn down on the many homes and paths of mortal men. For to the limit of every land and every sea I have roamed, obedient to Apollo's bidding, and at last I come, O Goddess, to your fane, and clinging to your image, I await my doom.

[1] [Enter the Chorus of Furies, questing like hounds]

[2] CHORUS. Ho! clear is here the trace of him we seek. Follow the track of blood, the silent sign! Like a hound that hunts a wounded fawn, we snuff along the scent of dripping gore; and inwardly we pant, for many a day toiling in a chase that shall undo the man. For over and over the wide land I have ranged, and over the wide sea, flying without wings, swift as a sail I pressed upon his track. Now hard by, I think well, he is crouching — for the scent of mortal blood draws me here. Follow, seek him — round and round scent and snuff and scan the ground, lest unharmed he slip away — he who slew his mother! Hist — there he is! See him entwine his arms around the image of the maiden divine — thus aided, he wills to be brought to judgment for the deed he wrought.

[3] It may not be! A mother's blood, poured out upon the stainèd earth, no one gathers up: it lies there — bear witness, Hell! — indelible forever! And you who shed it shall give your own blood to atone for that shedding. Yes, from your living limbs I will suck it out, red, clotted, gout by gout — a draught abhorred of men and gods; but I will drain it, suck you dry. Yes, I will waste you living, nerve and vein; for your slain mother, I will drag you down, where you shall endure the weird of agony! And you, and whoever among men has sinned — has wronged a god, or a friend, or a parent — learn how to all and each the arm of doom can reach! Sternly does the judgment-seat of Death requite, in the world below; yes, Death, beholding every man's deed, records it forever.

[4] ORESTES. I, schooled in many miseries, have learned how many refuges of cleansing shrines there are. I know when law allows speech and when it imposes silence. Look — I stand fixed now to speak, for he whose word is wise commands me to. See how the stain of blood is dull upon my hand and wastes away; and washed and lost along with it is the deep curse of matricide. For while the guilt was fresh, it was banished from me at Apollo's hearth, atoned and purified by the death of swine. Long would my speech be, if I should sum the tale of how often, since then, I stood among my fellow-men and brought no curse on them. Time cleanses all — Time, coeval of all that is. Now from pure lips, in words of fair omen, I call upon Athena, lady of this land, to come as my champion. So, in time to come, she shall not fail of love and loyal service — won not by war — from me and my land, and all the people of Argos, vowed to her. Now — whether she be far away in Libyan land, where the wave of her birth flows from Triton's lake, standing there with planted feet, or in some hour of rest concealing them, champion of her friends wherever she may be — or whether she looks forth, like a bold warrior, over the Phlegraean plain — I cry to her to come, wherever she may be (and she, as a goddess, from afar can hear), and aid and free me, set as I am among my foes.

[5] CHORUS. Neither Apollo's nor Athena's strength can save you from perishing, a castaway among the Lost, where no delight shall meet your soul — a bloodless prey of the nether powers, a shadow among shadows. Do you answer nothing? Do you cast away my words with scorn, you, prey prepared and consecrated to me? Not as a victim slain upon the shrine, but living shall you see your flesh become my food. Hear now the binding chant that makes you mine.

[6] Weave the weird dance — behold, the hour to utter forth the chant of hell, our sway among mankind to tell, the guidance of our power. Of Justice are we ministers; and whoever of men may stand lifting a pure, unsullied hand, that man incurs no doom of ours, and walks through all his mortal path untouched by woe, unharmed by wrath. But if, like that man yonder, he has blood on hands that he strives to hide, we stand avengers at his side, decreeing: "You have wronged the dead — we are doom's witnesses against you." The price of the blood his hands have shed we wring from him; in life, in death, hard at his side are we!

[7] Night, Mother Night, who brought me forth, a torment to living men and dead — hear me, O hear! By Leto's young son I am dishonored: he has taken from me the man who cowers in refuge, the one consecrated to me — a rightful victim, the man who slew his mother — given over to me and to fate.

[8] Hear the hymn of hell sounding over the victim — chant of frenzy, chant of ill, sense and will confounding! Round the soul entwining without lute or lyre — soul in madness pining, wasting as with fire!

[9] Fate, all-pervading Fate, has spun me this service, commanding me to abide in it: whoever of mortals, made perverse and lawless, is stained with the blood of kin, by his side we are, and hunt him ever onward, until he comes to the Silent Land, the realm of death; nor there in freedom shall he stand.

[10] Hear the hymn of hell sounding over the victim — chant of frenzy, chant of ill, sense and will confounding! Round the soul entwining without lute or lyre — soul in madness pining, wasting as with fire!

[11] When from the womb of Night we sprang, this labor was laid upon us, and shall abide. Immortal gods are you, yet beware that you touch not that which is our pride! None may come near us gathered around the blood-feast. For us no white garments gleam on a festal day; for us a darker fate is, another darker rite. That is my hour when an ancient line falls — when in the heart of the household the god of blood slays by kindred hands. Then do we bear our part: on him who slays we sweep with chasing cry. Though he be three times strong, we wear and waste him; blood atones for blood, new pain for ancient wrong.

[12] I hold this task — it is mine, and not another's. Even the gods on high, though they can silence and annul the prayers of those who cry to us, they may not strive with us, who stand apart — a race abhorred by Zeus, blood-boltered, held unworthy of the council and converse of Heaven's lord. Therefore the more I leap upon my prey; upon their heads I bound. My foot is hard; like one that trips a runner, I cast them to the ground — yes, to the depth of intolerable doom. And they who once were great, and on earth held high their pride and glory, are brought to low estate. In the underworld they waste and are diminished, while around them flit the dark wavings of my robes, and the subtly woven paces of my feet.

[13] Whoever falls infatuate does not see, nor does he know, that we stand at his side — so closely round about him, darkly flitting, glides the cloud of guilt. Heavily it is told, how around his hearth-stone the murk of hell rises. Stern and fixed is the law: we have hands to achieve it, cunning to devise. Queens are we, and mindful of our solemn vengeance. Not by tear or prayer shall any man avert it. In unhonored darkness, far from the gods, we fare, lit on our task by torches from sunless regions; and along a deadly way — deadly to the living as to those who see not the life and light of day — we hunt and press onward. Who of mortals, hearing all that Fate, through the hand of God, has given us for ordinance and law, does not quake in awe? Yes — this right was assigned to us in the dark abyss far back in ages: none shall wrong my office, though in nether regions and sunless dark I dwell.

=== CH22: The Eumenides — Second Episode ===
[0] [Enter Athena from above]

[1] ATHENA. Far off I heard the clamor of your cry, as I set my foot beside Scamander, asserting right upon the land given over to me by those who held sway and leadership over the Achaean host. No scant share of all they won by spear and sword was given to me — the land and all that grew upon it, as a chosen heirloom for my Theseus's clan. Summoned thence, I sped with tireless foot — and instead of wings, the fold of this my aegis, propelled by my feet, hummed on the wind, as a chariot speeds when linked to mettled horses. And now, beholding here Earth's nether brood, I do not fear at all — yet are my eyes amazed with wonder. Who are you? Of all I ask, and of this stranger clinging to my statue. But you — your shape is like no human form, like no goddess whom the gods behold, like no shape that mortal women wear. Yet to stand by and chide a monstrous form is altogether unjust — from such words Right revolts.

[2] CHORUS. O child of Zeus, one word shall tell you all. We are the children of eternal Night, and in the underworld we are called Furies.

[3] ATHENA. I know your lineage now, and your name as well.

[4] CHORUS. Yes — and presently you shall know my rights too.

[5] ATHENA. Gladly would I learn them; speak them clearly forth.

[6] CHORUS. We chase from home the murderers of men.

[7] ATHENA. And where at last can he that slew make pause?

[8] CHORUS. Where this is law: "All joy abandon here."

[9] ATHENA. Say — do you bay this man toward such a flight?

[10] CHORUS. Yes — for of his own choice he slew his mother.

[11] ATHENA. Urged by no fear of other wrath and doom?

[12] CHORUS. What spur can rightly goad to matricide?

[13] ATHENA. Two stand to plead — only one have I heard.

[14] CHORUS. He will not swear, nor challenge us to oath.

[15] ATHENA. You will the form of justice, not its deed.

[16] CHORUS. Prove that word; you are not scant of skill.

[17] ATHENA. I say that oaths shall not enforce the wrong.

[18] CHORUS. Then test the cause; judge and award the right.

[19] ATHENA. Will you trust this decision to me, then?

[20] CHORUS. Yes — revering a true child of a worthy sire.

[21] ATHENA (to Orestes). O man unknown, make your plea in turn. Speak forth your land, your lineage, and your woes; then, if you can, avert this bitter charge — if, as I think, in confidence of right you sit hard beside my holy place, clasping this statue, as Ixion sat, a sacred suppliant for Zeus to cleanse. To all this answer me in plain words.

[22] ORESTES. O Queen Athena, first I will remove a great anxiety from your last words. I am no man bloodguilty; no foul stain clings to your image from my clinging hand. One potent proof of this I have to tell. Look — the law stands: "The slayer shall not plead until, by the hand of him who cleanses blood, a suckling creature's blood besprinkle him." Long since have I done this expiation; in many a home, slain beasts and running streams have cleansed me. Thus I speak that fear away. Next, of my lineage you shall quickly learn: I am an Argive, and right well you know my father — that Agamemnon who arrayed the fleet and those who went in it to war — that chief with whom your hand joined to crush to an uncitied heap what once was Troy. That Agamemnon, when he came home, was brought to no honorable death — slain by the dark-souled wife who bore me to him — entangled and slain in wily nets, blazoned with the blood that ran in the laver. And I, returning from an exiled youth, slew her, my mother — there, it stands avowed! — with blood for blood avenging my loved father. And in this deed Loxias bears a part, decreeing agonies to goad my will, unless by me the guilty found their doom. Decide whether right or wrong was done — your judgment, whatever it be, contents me.

[23] ATHENA. Too mighty is this matter, whatever mortal claims rightly to judge of it. Yes — even me, eternal Right forbids to judge the issues of bloodguilt and the wrath that follows swift behind. This too gives me pause: that you come, as one with all due rites performed, unsinning, pure, to my shrine. Whatever you are, in the name of this my city, as uncondemned I take you to my side. Yet these foes of yours have such dues by fate that I may not banish them; and if they fail, overthrown in judgment of the cause, forthwith the poison of their anger shall infect the land — a dropping plague-spot of eternal ill. Thus we stand with woe on either hand: whether they stay, or go forth at my command, perplexity or pain must follow. Yet, since Fate has imposed the cause on me, I will choose judges who shall be an ordinance forever, set to rule on the dues of bloodguilt, upon declared oath. But you — call forth your witness and your proof, words strong for justice, fortified by oath. And I, whoever are truest in my town, them will I choose and bring, and straitly charge: "Look on this cause, discriminating well, and pledge your oath to utter nothing wrong."

=== CH23: The Eumenides — First Stasimon ===
[0] CHORUS. Now are they all undone, the ancient laws, if here the slayer's cause prevail. New wrong for ancient right shall be, if matricide go free. Henceforth a deed like his shall stand, all too ready to every hand. Too often shall parents in the after-time rue and lament this crime, taught — not in false imagining — to feel their children's thrusting steel. No more the wrath that once on murder fell from us, the queens of Hell, shall fall; no more shall our watching gaze impend — Death shall smite unrestrained.

[1] Henceforth shall one cry to another: "Look — they are stricken, look, they fall and die around me!" And that other shall answer him: "O you who look that your woes should cease, behold — with dark increase they throng and press upon you; yes, dim is every cure, and every comfort vain!"

[2] Let no one henceforth cry out, when the blow of sudden-smiting woe falls, with the sad reiterated strain:

[3] O Justice, aid! aid, O you thrones of Hell!

[4] So though a father or a mother wail, newly smitten by a son, it shall no more avail — since, overthrown by wrong, the shrine of Justice has fallen.

[5] Know that there is a throne which cannot pass away, and one who sits upon it — even Fear, searching with steadfast eyes man's inner soul. Wisdom is the child of pain, and born with many a tear. But who henceforth — what man of mortal men, what nation on earth, that holds nothing in awe nor in the light of inner reverence — shall worship Right as in the older day?

[6] Do not praise, O man, the life beyond control, nor that which bows under a tyrant's sway. Know that the middle way is dearest to God, and those who tread on it shall reach the goal. But those who wander to left or right are sinners in his sight. Take this one truthful word to your heart: of wantonness, impiety is the father; only from calm control and unstirred sanity comes true prosperity, the goal of every man's desire.

[7] Yes, whatever befalls, hold to this word of mine: "Bow down at Justice's shrine; turn your eyes away from earthly lure, and do not with godless foot spurn that altar." For as you do, so shall Fate do to you in return; and the great doom is certain. Therefore let each man adore the trust of a parent, and each with loyalty revere the guest who rests within his halls. For whoever, uncompelled, follows what is just, he shall never go unblest — never shall that man come to the gulf of doom. But he whose will is set against the gods, who treads beyond the law with foot impure,

[8] till over the wreck of Right confusion broods — know that for him, though now he sails secure, the day of storm shall come. Then shall he strive and fail to furl the sail down from the shivered yard; he shall call on Powers, who heed him nothing, to save him, and wrestle vainly with the whirling wave. Hot was his heart with pride — "I shall not fall," he cried. But the god beholds him with watching scorn, forlorn, tangled in toils of Fate beyond escape, hopeless of any safe haven beyond the cape — until all his wealth and the bliss of his bygone days are dashed upon the reef of Rightful Doom, and he is rapt away, unwept, forever, to the dead forgotten world.

=== CH24: The Eumenides — Third Episode ===
[0] [Re-enter Athena, with twelve Athenian citizens]

[1] ATHENA. O herald, make proclamation — bid all men come. Then let the shrill blast of the Tyrrhenian trumpet, filled with mortal breath, peal a loud summons through the wide air, bidding all men attend. For until my judges fill this judgment-seat, silence is fitting, so that this whole city may learn what for all time my ordinance commands, and these men may judge the cause aright.

[2] [Apollo approaches]

[3] CHORUS. O King Apollo — rule what is your own; but in this matter, what share belongs to you?

[4] APOLLO. First, I come as a witness — for this man is my suppliant by sacred right, the guest of my holy hearth, and cleansed by me of bloodguilt. Then, to set myself at his side and bear part in his cause, as I bore part in the deed by which his mother fell. Let whoever knows the case now announce it.

[5] ATHENA (to the Chorus). It is I who announce the cause — but the first speech is yours; for rightfully those whose plaint is being tried should tell the tale first and set the matter clear.

[6] CHORUS. Though we are many, brief shall be our tale. (To Orestes) Answer, setting word to match with word; and first avow — did you slay your mother?

[7] ORESTES. I slew her. I deny no word of this.

[8] CHORUS. Three falls decide the wrestle — this is one.

[9] ORESTES. You vaunt yourself — but over no final fall.

[10] CHORUS. Yet you must tell the manner of your deed.

[11] ORESTES. With drawn sword in hand I gashed her neck. It is told.

[12] CHORUS. But by whose word, by whose craft, were you impelled?

[13] ORESTES. By the oracles of him who here attests for me.

[14] CHORUS. The prophet-god bade you slay your mother?

[15] ORESTES. Yes — and through him I have fared the less ill, until now.

[16] CHORUS. If the vote grips you, you shall change that word.

[17] ORESTES. Strong is my hope; my buried sire shall aid me.

[18] CHORUS. Go to, now — trust the dead, a matricide!

[19] ORESTES. Yes — for in her two stains of sin combined.

[20] CHORUS. How? Speak this clearly to the judges' minds.

[21] ORESTES. Slaying her husband, she did slay my sire.

[22] CHORUS. Therefore you live; death assoils her deed.

[23] ORESTES. Then while she lived, why did you not hunt her?

[24] CHORUS. She was not kin by blood to him she slew.

[25] ORESTES. And I — am I by blood my mother's kin?

[26] CHORUS. O cursed with murder's guilt, how else were you the burden of her womb? Do you forswear your mother's kinship, the closest bond of love?

[27] ORESTES. It is your hour, Apollo — speak the law, declaring whether this deed was justly done. For done it is, and clear and undenied. But if to you the cause of this murder seems right or wrongful, speak — that I may tell these judges.

[28] APOLLO. To you, Athena's mighty council-court, justly for justice will I plead — even I, the prophet-god — and not cheat you by one word. For never did I speak from my prophet-seat one word, of man, of woman, or of state, except what the Father of Olympian gods commanded me. I tell you, then: bethink yourselves of my plea, how strong it stands, and follow the decree of Zeus our sire — for oaths do not prevail over the command of Zeus.

[29] CHORUS. Go to — you say that from Zeus came the oracle that bade this Orestes avenge the slaying of his sire, and to hold his mother's right of kin as nothing!

[30] APOLLO. Yes — for it does not stand with a common death, that he should die — a chieftain and a king, decked with the sceptre that high heaven confers — die, and by the hands of a woman, not smitten down by some far-shooting bow, held stalwartly by some strong Amazon. Another doom was his. O Pallas, hear, and you who sit in judgment, to discern this thing aright! With a specious voice of true welcome she hailed him, returning home triumphant from that mighty market where war for life gives fame; then over the laver, as he bathed himself, she spread from head to foot a covering net, and entangled and trapped her lord in the endless mesh of cunning robes, and struck him down. Lo, you have heard what doom this chieftain met — the majesty of Greece, the high lord of the fleet. Such as I tell it, let it gall your ears, you who stand as judges to decide this cause.

[31] CHORUS. Zeus, as you say, holds a father's death as the first of crimes — yet he himself, by his own act, cast his father old Cronos into chains. How does that deed suit with this you now tell? O you who judge, I bid you mark my words!

[32] APOLLO. O monsters loathed of all, O scorn of gods — he who has bound may loose: there is a cure for that, yes, many a plan that can unbind the chain. But when the thirsty dust has sucked up a man's blood once shed in death, he shall arise no more. No chant or charm has my Sire wrought for this. All else there is, he molds and shifts at will, not scant of strength or breath, whatever he do.

[33] CHORUS. Think yet for what acquittal you plead: he who has shed a mother's kindred blood — shall he dwell in Argos, where his sire dwelt? How shall he stand before the city's shrines, and how shall he share the clansmen's holy lustral bowl?

[34] APOLLO. This too I answer; mark a truthful word. The mother is not the true parent of the child she bears: she does but nurse the seed newly sown. The male is parent; she, as a stranger for a stranger, hoards the germ of life — unless the god blights its promise. And proof of this I will set before you. Birth may come from fathers without mothers: see at your side a witness of the same — Athena, daughter of Olympian Zeus, never within the darkness of any womb fostered or fashioned, but a brighter bud than any goddess might bear in her breast. And I, O Pallas, however I may, will henceforth glorify your town and your clan; and to this end have I sent my suppliant here to your shrine — that he from this time forth be loyal to you for evermore, O goddess-queen, and that you may win him to your side and hold him faithful, he and his line — and that for ever this pledge and troth may remain to children's children of Athenian seed.

[35] ATHENA. Enough is said. I bid the judges now with pure intent deliver just award.

[36] CHORUS. We too have shot every shaft of speech we have, and now we wait to hear the doom of law.

[37] ATHENA (to Apollo and Orestes). Say — by what ordering shall I escape your blame?

[38] APOLLO. I have spoken, you have heard; enough. O stranger men, heed your oath well as you decide the cause.

[39] ATHENA. O men of Athens, you who first do judge the law of bloodshed — hear me now ordain. Here for all time, for the Attic host of Aegeus, this council-court of judges sworn shall stand — here the tribunal, set on the Hill of Ares, where of old the tented Amazons encamped, when in hatred of Theseus they assailed Athens, and against her citadel set up a counterwork of new sky-pointing towers, and there held their sacrifice to Ares — where the rock now bears the name, even Ares' Hill. And from here shall Reverence and her kinsman Fear pass into every free man's heart, by day and night enjoining: "You shall do no unjust thing," so long as the law stands as it stood of old, unmarred by civic change. Look — the spring is pure; but foul it once with vile inflow and muddy clay, and none can drink of it. Therefore, O citizens, I bid you bow in awe to this command: "Let no man live uncurbed by law, nor curbed by tyranny"; and do not banish the monarchy of Awe beyond the walls. Untouched by fear divine, no man does justice in the world of men. Therefore in purity and holy dread stand and revere; so shall you have and hold a saving bulwark of the state and land, such as no man has ever elsewhere known — neither in far Scythia, nor in Pelops's realm. Thus I ordain it now: a council-court pure and unsullied by the lust of gain, sacred and swift to vengeance, wakeful ever to champion men who sleep, the country's guard. Thus have I spoken — thus I commend it to my own clan for ever. You who judge, arise, take each his vote, mete out the right, revering your oath. My word is said.

[40] [The twelve judges come forward, one by one, to the urns of decision; the first votes; as each of the others follows, the Chorus and Apollo speak alternately]

[41] CHORUS. I tell you well — beware! Do not in any way put to shame this grievous company of hell.

[42] APOLLO. I too would warn you: fear my oracles — from Zeus they are — and do not make them void of fruit.

[43] CHORUS. Presumptuous is your claim, to judge bloodguilt; and false henceforth shall your oracles be.

[44] APOLLO. Did then my Sire's counsels fail when Ixion, first of slayers, turned to his side?

[45] CHORUS. These are but words; but I, if justice fail me, will haunt this land with grim and deadly deed.

[46] APOLLO. You are scorned of the younger and the elder gods alike — it is I who shall prevail before long.

[47] CHORUS. Thus did you too of old in the halls of Pheres, overreaching Fate to make a mortal deathless.

[48] APOLLO. Was it not right to aid my worshipper, especially when his need was hardest?

[49] CHORUS. I say you annulled the lots of life, cheating with wine the deities of old.

[50] APOLLO. I say you shall presently, when your pleadings are foiled, spit venom vainly on your enemies.

[51] CHORUS. Since this young god overrides my ancient right, I tarry only to hear your law, not knowing whether my wrath shall blast your state, or spare it.

[52] ATHENA. Mine is the right to add the final vote, and I award it to Orestes's cause. For no mother bore me within her womb; and, save for wedlock — which I have ever eschewed — with all my soul I vouch myself the champion of the man, not of the woman: in heart, as in birth, a father's child alone. Thus I will not too heinously regard the death of a woman who slew her husband, the guardian of her home. And if the votes fall equal, Orestes shall prevail. You of the judges who are named to do so, swiftly shake forth the lots from either urn.

[53] [Two judges come forward, one to each urn]

[54] ORESTES. O bright Apollo, what shall be the end?

[55] CHORUS. O Night, my dark mother — do you mark these things?

[56] ORESTES. Now shall my doom be life, or strangling cords.

[57] CHORUS. And mine — either lost honor, or a wider sway.

[58] APOLLO. O stranger judges, sum aright the count of the votes cast forth, and parting them, take heed that you err not in your decision. The default of a single vote brings deep ruin; one cast aright establishes house and home.

[59] ATHENA. Behold — this man is free from the guilt of blood, for half the votes condemn him, and half set him free!

[60] ORESTES. O Pallas, light and safety of my home — you, you have given me back to dwell once more in that fatherland of mine, deprived of which I wandered. Now shall Greek lips say this: "The man is Argive once again, and dwells once more within his father's wealthy hall — saved by Pallas, by Loxias, and by Him, the great third saviour, Zeus omnipotent" — who thus in pity for my father's fate plucks me from my doom, beholding these confederates of my mother. Lo, I pass to my own home — but I proffer this vow to your land and people: "Never, through all the manifold years of Time to be, shall any chieftain of my Argive land bear his spears this way for battle arrayed." For we, though we shall by then be lapped in earth, will work our will by thwart adversities upon those who shall transgress this oath of mine — ordaining for them paths of despair and ill-starred journeys, until they rue their task. But if this oath is rightly kept, to those men we, the dead, shall be full of grace, while with loyal league they honor Pallas's town. And now farewell, you and your city's folk — firm be your arm's grasp closing with your foes, and, strong to save, may victory attend your spear.

[61] [Exit Orestes, with Apollo]

=== CH25: The Eumenides — Second Stasimon ===
[0] CHORUS. Woe upon you, younger gods! The ancient right you have overridden, torn it from my hands.

[1] I am dishonored of you, thrust to scorn! But heavily my wrath shall fling forth on this land the drops that blast and burn — venom of vengeance, that shall work such harm as I have suffered. Where that dew shall fall, leafless blight shall arise, wasting Earth's offspring — Justice, hear my call! — and through all the land in deadly fashion shall scatter venom, to exude again in pestilence on men. What cry avails me now, what deed of blood — what dark despite upon this land? Alas, alas, forlorn we are; a bitter injury have we borne! Alas, O sisters — O dishonored brood of mother Night!

[2] ATHENA. No — bow to my words, chafe not, nor moan. You are not worsted nor disgraced. Behold, with a balanced vote the cause had a fair outcome, nor in the end did anything dishonor you. But thus the will of Zeus shone clearly forth, and his own prophet-god avouched the same: "Orestes slew; his slaying is atoned." Therefore I pray you, do not shoot forth the dart of vengeance upon this land. Be appeased — do not blast the land with blight, nor loose upon it drops of eternal venom, direful darts wasting and marring nature's seed of growth.

[3] For I, the queen of Athens's sacred right, pledge to you a holy sanctuary deep in the heart of this my land — made just by your indwelling presence — while you sit hard by your sacred shrines that gleam with the oil of sacrifice, and adored by this people.

[4] CHORUS. Woe upon you, younger gods! The ancient right you have overridden, torn it from my hands.

[5] I am dishonored of you, thrust to scorn! But heavily my wrath shall fling forth on his land the drops that blast and burn — venom of vengeance, that shall work such harm as I have suffered. Where that dew shall fall, leafless blight shall arise, wasting Earth's offspring — Justice, hear my call! — and through all the land in deadly fashion shall scatter venom, to exude again in pestilence on men. What cry avails me now, what deed of blood — what dark despite upon this land? Alas, alas, forlorn we are; a bitter injury have we borne! Alas, O sisters — O dishonored brood of mother Night!

=== CH26: The Eumenides — Exodos ===
[0] ATHENA. You are not dishonored. Turn not, I pray, the swelling wrath of goddesses upon men, nor make the friendly earth despiteful to them. I too have Zeus for my champion — that is enough — and I alone of all goddesses know how to open the chamber where his thunderbolts lie stored and sealed; but here is no need of such things. No — be appeased, and do not cast the malice of your tongue upon the ground to blast the world. Calm the bitter wrath's black inward surge. For high shall be your honor, set beside me forever in this land, whose fertile lap shall pour out its teeming firstfruits unto you — gifts for fair childbirth and for the crown of wedlock. Thus honored, praise my spoken pledge for ever.

[1] CHORUS. I — I dishonored to dwell on this earth — ancient of days and of wisdom! I breathe forth poison and breath of frenzied rage. O Earth — woe, woe, for you, for me! From side to side, what pains are these that thrill through me? Hearken, O mother Night, to my wrath, my agony! Whom from my ancient rights the gods have thrust, and brought me to the dust — woe, woe is me! — with craft invincible.

[2] ATHENA. Older you are than I, and I will bear with this fury of yours. Know that, although you are wiser in ancient wisdom, yet from Zeus I too have no scant measure of the same. So take heed of this prophecy: if to another land of alien men you go, all too late shall you feel a deep longing for mine. The rolling tides of time bring round a day of brighter glory for this town; and you, enshrined in honor by the halls where Erechtheus dwelt, shall win a worship from men and from the train of women, greater than any other tribe shall pay elsewhere. Cast not, therefore, on this soil of mine whetstones that sharpen souls to bloodshedding — the burning goads of youthful hearts made hot with frenzy of the spirit, not of wine. Nor pluck, as it were, the heart from cocks that strive, to set it in the breasts of my citizens — a war-god's spirit, keen for fight, made stern against their country and their kin. The man who grievously lusts for fame — let him wage full and immitigable war against the stranger; but of kindred birds I hold the challenge hateful. Such is the boon I offer you: within this land of lands, most loved of gods, with me to show and share fair mercy, gratitude, and grace as fair.

[3] CHORUS. I — I dishonored to dwell on this earth — ancient of days and of wisdom! I breathe forth poison and breath of frenzied rage. O Earth — woe, woe for you, for me! From side to side, what pains are these that thrill through me? Hearken, O mother Night, to my wrath, my agony! Whom from my ancient rights the gods have thrust, and brought me to the dust — woe, woe is me! — with craft invincible.

[4] ATHENA. I will not weary of soft words to you, that you may never say: "Behold me spurned, an elder by a younger deity, and from this land rejected and forlorn, unhonored by the men who dwell in it." But, if Persuasion's grace is sacred to you — soft in the soothing accents of my tongue — tarry, I pray you. Yet, if you will go, then it would not be rightful to sway down the scale on this town of mine, that bears wrath and grief or wasting plague upon this folk. It is yours, if so you will, to be the inheritress of this my land, and to win its utmost grace.

[5] CHORUS. O queen, what refuge do you promise me?

[6] ATHENA. A refuge untouched by ill: take my boon.

[7] CHORUS. What — if I take it — shall my honor be?

[8] ATHENA. No house shall prosper without your grace.

[9] CHORUS. Can you achieve and grant me such power?

[10] ATHENA. Yes — for my hand shall bless your worshippers.

[11] CHORUS. And will you pledge me this for time eternal?

[12] ATHENA. Yes — none can bid me pledge beyond my power.

[13] CHORUS. Lo — I desist from wrath, appeased by you.

[14] ATHENA. Then in the heart of the land you shall win friends.

[15] CHORUS. What chant do you bid me raise, to greet the land?

[16] ATHENA. Such as aspires toward a victory unrued by any: chants from the breast of earth, from wave, from sky; and let the breath of the wild winds pass with soft sunlight over the lap of the land — strong wax the fruits of earth, fair teem the kine, unfailing, for my town's prosperity, and constant be the growth of mortal seed. But more and more root out the impious; for as a gardener fosters what he sows, so do I foster this race, whom righteousness defends from sorrow. Such is the proffered boon. But if wars must be, and their loud clash and carnage, for my town, I will never endure that anything but victory shall crown her fame.

[17] CHORUS. Lo, I accept it; at her very side Pallas bids me dwell. I will not wrong the city of her pride, which even Almighty Zeus and Ares hold to be heaven's earthly citadel — beloved home of Greek gods, the young, the old — the divine sanctuary, the shield of every shrine. For Athens I speak forth a gracious prophecy: the glory of sunlight and the skies shall bid arise from earth warm wavelets of new life and glad prosperity.

[18] ATHENA. Behold — with gracious heart well pleased, for my citizens I grant fulfillment of this covenant. And here, their wrath at length appeased, these mighty deities shall stay; for theirs it is by right to sway the lot that rules our mortal day. And he who has not inwardly felt their stern decree — ere long upon him, not knowing why or whence, the grim life-crushing blow is dealt. The father's sin upon the child descends, and sin is silent death, and leads him on the downward path, by stealth beguiled, unto the Furies. Though his estate on earth were high, and loud his boast, victim of silent ire and hate, he dwells among the Lost.

[19] CHORUS. Now give ear to my blessing — let no scorching blight or singèd air ever blast your fair olives! Drought, that wastes bud and plant, keep to your own place. Avaunt, fell Famine — come not hither to waste and wither in stealth! Let the land, in due season, twice renew her waxing fruits; let the kine teem in double measure; let her be rich in new god-given treasure! Here let men adore the powers for sudden gifts unhoped before!

[20] ATHENA. O hearken, warders of the wall that guards my Athens — what a dower is ordained and given to her! For mighty is the Furies' power, and deeply revered in courts of heaven and realms of hell; and to all they clearly weave the doom, O mortal men. And some in joy and peace shall sing; but to others they bring sad life and tear-dimmed eye.

[21] CHORUS. And far away I ban you and remove — the untimely death of youths too soon brought low! And to each maiden, O gods, when the time comes for love, grant a warrior's heart, a wedded life to know. You too, O Fates, children of mother Night, whose children too are we — O goddesses of just award, queens by sacred right of all things in time and in eternity, a present power for righteousness, honored beyond all Gods — hear and grant my cry!

[22] ATHENA. And I too, with joy am eager — hearing your voice ordain this gift to my land. High thanks be yours, Persuasion, who with divine eyes did look strength into my tongue, to bend and to appease at length those who would not be comforted. Zeus, king of parley, prevails; and you and I will strive and not fail, that good may stand in evil's stead, and lasting bliss for bale.

[23] CHORUS. And nevermore within these walls shall the din of fierce sedition echo, unslaked with blood and crime. The thirsty dust shall nevermore suck up the darkly streaming gore of civic broils, shed out in wrath and vengeance, crying death for death. But man with man and state with state shall vow: "The pledge of common hate and common friendship, that has often made blessing out of curse for man, be ours unto all time."

[24] ATHENA. Have they the skill, or not, to find the path of favoring speech and kindly presage? Yes — even from these, who once glared anger upon you, grim and stern, O citizens, you shall now earn a manifold recompense. Deck them aright, extol them high, be loyal to their loyalty, and you shall make your town and land sure, propped on the saving hand of Justice and on Fame's eternity.

[25] CHORUS. Hail, all hail! and yet again, all hail, O Athens, happy in a secured prosperity! O you who sit at Zeus's right hand, nor fail of wisdom set among you and assured, beloved of the well-loved Goddess-Maid! The King of gods reveres you, beneath her guarding wing.

[26] ATHENA. All hail unto each honored guest! Whom to the chambers of your rest it is mine to lead, and to provide the hallowed torch, the guard and guide. Pass down — while these altars glow with sacred fire — to the earth below and your appointed shrine. There dwelling, restrain from the land the force of fate, the breath of bane, but waft on us the gift and gain of Victory divine! And you, the men of Cranaos's seed, I bid you now with reverence lead these alien Powers, who are thus made Athenian evermore. To you may their will henceforth be fair, to do whatever may bless and aid!

[27] CHORUS. Hail to you all! hail yet again, all who love Athens, gods and men, adoring her as Pallas's home! And while you reverence what we grant — my sacred shrine and hidden haunt — blameless and blissful be your doom!

[28] ATHENA. Once more I praise the promise of your vows; and now I bid the golden torches' glow pass down before you to the hidden depth of earth, borne by my own sacred servants, my loyal guards of statue and of shrine. Come forth, O flower of Theseus's Attic land, O glorious band of children and of wives, and you, O train of matrons crowned with age! Deck yourselves with festal robes of scarlet dye in honor of this day. O gleaming torch, lead onward, that these gracious powers of earth henceforth be seen to bless the life of men.

[30] CHANT. With loyalty we lead you; proudly go, childless children of Night, to your home below! (O citizens, awhile from words forbear!)
