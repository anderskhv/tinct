# Accessibility Review 1 — Oedipus Rex (candidate.json, modern-en)

**Reviewer status:** Blind review. I have not been shown source.json or any
drafter's notes. Judgment is based solely on candidate.json as a piece of
English prose a general adult reader would encounter today.

**Coverage:** Read the entire candidate in full, all 11 chapters (Prologue,
Parodos, First Episode, First Stasimon, Second Episode, Second Stasimon,
Third Episode, Third Stasimon, Fourth Episode, Fourth Stasimon, Exodos),
474 paragraphs total, start to finish, then re-read each chapter
paragraph by paragraph.

## Chapter-by-chapter findings

### Ch.1 Prologue
Mostly accessible. Speaker-tag dialogue reads naturally.

- Para 2 (Priest's speech): a single very long paragraph packed with clauses
  and proper nouns — "Pallas," "Ismenus gives his oracles by fire," "the
  house of Cadmus," "the dark kingdom of Pluto." A general reader unfamiliar
  with Greek myth will not know who Pallas or Ismenus are, or why Pluto's
  kingdom is relevant (god of the underworld) — none of this is glossed.
  Still readable as rhetorical build-up, but the density of unglossed names
  in one paragraph asks a lot of a first-time reader.
- Para 2: "that cruel songstress" (referring to the Sphinx, never named
  directly) — a reader who doesn't already know the Oedipus myth has no way
  to resolve this reference until much later. This is presumably intentional
  dramatic economy, but it will read as confusing on a first pass rather
  than as a puzzle to be solved.
- Para 40 (Oedipus's long speech): "successor to his throne, his bed, his
  wife (and had he not been frustrated in the hope of offspring, children
  born of one womb would have forged a closer bond between him and me — but
  Fate swooped down on him)" — a long parenthetical with an inverted,
  counterfactual construction. A reader has to hold several conditions in
  mind at once (what didn't happen, what would have happened if it had) to
  parse this correctly. Likely to require a re-read.

### Ch.2 Parodos (choral ode)
This chapter will be the hardest for a general reader in the whole play.

- Para 1: "gold-paved Pythian shrine," "Healer of Delos" — unglossed epithets
  for Apollo, back to back, with no explanation that this is Apollo being
  addressed by different names.
- Para 2: a cascade of proper nouns in six lines — Athena, Artemis
  ("Lady of Thebes"), an unnamed "Lord of the death-winged arrow" (Apollo
  again, but not named this time), all invoked without any signal to a new
  reader that these are three different gods being called on in turn.
- Para 5: "Amphitrite's bed" — completely unglossed; a general reader has no
  way to know this refers to the sea (Amphitrite is a sea goddess).
- Para 6: "Lycean King," "Bacchus," "Maenads," "Evoe" in one paragraph — four
  unglossed mythological terms in five sentences. "Evoe" in particular is a
  transliterated ritual cry, not an English word, and nothing marks it as
  such for a reader encountering it cold.
- Overall this ode reads as a wall of invocation. The syntax itself is
  clear sentence by sentence, but the sheer density of unglossed proper
  nouns and epithets (roughly one every line) means most readers will lose
  track of who is being addressed and why.

### Ch.3 First Episode
Generally strong — the Teiresias/Oedipus exchange is short, punchy
stichomythia and reads very naturally.

- Para 2 (Oedipus's proclamation): one very long paragraph with a chain of
  conditional and legal-formula clauses — "if he shrinks from it, let him
  consider this... but if any of you knows the murderer to be a
  foreigner... but if you still keep silent..." The repeated "if... but
  if... but if" structure is logically clear but visually and rhythmically
  monotonous; a reader can lose the thread of which condition is currently
  active partway through.
- Para 116 (Oedipus's tirade against Teiresias): "this mountebank, this
  juggling charlatan, this tricksy beggar-priest" — "mountebank" and
  "tricksy" are words most contemporary readers will not use in daily life
  and may need to infer from context (both land fine as insults, but they
  are genuinely uncommon vocabulary).
- Para 118 (Teiresias's curse): "with what a hymeneal thou wast borne" area
  is now modernized to "on what wedding night you were brought home," which
  reads clearly — flagging as a place where the modernization already works
  well.
- Para 132 (Teiresias's exit speech): long, single-paragraph prophecy with
  many stacked clauses ("he will be shown to be both brother and father —
  of the woman who bore him, son and husband both, bedfellow, and killer of
  his own father"). The content is inherently riddle-like by design, but the
  list of kinship terms piled up without pause will require most readers to
  slow down and re-read to keep track of which relationship attaches to
  whom.

### Ch.4 First Stasimon (choral ode)
- Para 1: "sleuth-hounds" — an archaic-flavored compound; likely
  interpretable in context (the Fates hunting the murderer) but not a word
  in everyday use.
- Para 1: "Parnassus' snowy peak" — unglossed place name; contextually clear
  it's associated with Apollo/prophecy but a reader unfamiliar with Greek
  geography won't know why.
- Overall shorter and more manageable than the Parodos; the reasoning ("I
  know of no quarrel... There is no proof: how then can I challenge our
  king's good name?") is followed easily.

### Ch.5 Second Episode
Very accessible — long argumentative prose (Creon's defense speech) and
fast stichomythia both read naturally, with clear cause-and-effect logic.
No significant stumbling blocks found.

### Ch.6 Second Stasimon
Short and clear throughout.

### Ch.7 Third Episode
- Jocasta's exposition speech (the oracle/crossroads story) is long but
  reads as clean narrative prose — good example of the modernization
  working well: clear sequence of events, accessible vocabulary.
- Oedipus's long autobiographical speech (the "my father was Polybus of
  Corinth" paragraph) is the single longest paragraph in the play. It moves
  through several distinct episodes (the banquet insult, the trip to
  Delphi, the crossroads killing) inside one block of prose with no
  internal paragraph breaks. Individual sentences are readable, but the
  sheer length means a reader can lose track of chronology (is this
  before or after Delphi?) without re-reading. This is a pacing/structure
  issue more than a vocabulary issue — flagging for awareness, though
  restructuring is not this reviewer's call (source paragraphing must be
  preserved per protocol).

### Ch.8 Third Stasimon (choral ode)
- Para 1-4: abstract, aphoristic language about impiety and the gods —
  "Perdition seize his vain imaginings," "lays an impious hand on holiest
  things" — dense figurative language that a general reader will need to
  slow down for, though each sentence individually resolves grammatically.
  "Abae's hallowed cell" is an unglossed place name.
- The rest of the chapter (Jocasta's prayer, the Corinthian messenger's
  arrival) is clear, naturalistic dialogue.

### Ch.9 Fourth Episode
The longest chapter (147 paragraphs) but almost entirely rapid dialogue —
very accessible. The stichomythia between Oedipus and the Messenger about
his parentage reads like a real, tense conversation; no notable stumbling
blocks.

### Ch.10 Fourth Stasimon (choral ode)
- Para 2: "the vulture-maid" (the Sphinx) — unglossed epithet; a reader who
  hasn't inferred by now that this refers to the Sphinx from earlier
  references may be lost here specifically because the word "Sphinx" itself
  is never used in this passage.
- Otherwise the ode's central image (Oedipus's rise and fall) comes through
  clearly even without full grasp of every epithet.

### Ch.11 Exodos
- The Second Messenger's long speech describing Jocasta's suicide and
  Oedipus's self-blinding is graphic and dense but reads as clear, urgent
  narrative — a strong point in the modernization; nothing here trips a
  reader up structurally.
- Oedipus's long lament (the "O Polybus, O Corinth, O my home" paragraph)
  piles up apostrophes to places and things (Cithaeron, the crossroads, the
  marriage bed) in quick succession. Each is addressed directly ("do you
  happen to recall those deeds of mine which you witnessed") which is a
  classical rhetorical device (apostrophe) that a modern general reader may
  find disorienting on first encounter — who is he talking to, since none
  of these things can answer? Worth flagging as a place where a reader
  might need a beat to realize this is address-to-inanimate-objects, not
  dialogue.
- The final exchange with Creon and the closing choral maxim ("wait to see
  the ending of a life before you call any mortal blessed") land clearly
  and are genuinely well modernized — a strong, accessible close to the
  play.

## Where the modernization works especially well

- All of the stichomythia (rapid-fire one-line exchanges, especially in
  Ch.3, Ch.5, Ch.9) reads naturally and builds real tension without any
  archaic residue.
- Jocasta's crossroads narrative (Ch.7) and the Second Messenger's report
  of the deaths (Ch.11) are both clean, vivid, well-paced narrative prose —
  genuinely accessible high points.
- The final choral lines closing the play are clear and land with real
  force.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes, not a broader pass.** The dialogue scenes — which
make up most of the play's paragraph count (Ch.3, Ch.5, Ch.7's
stichomythia, Ch.9, most of the Exodos) — are genuinely accessible modern
prose with no significant stumbling blocks. The choral odes (Ch.2, Ch.4,
Ch.6 short/clear, Ch.8, Ch.10) are where a general reader will struggle
most: dense, unglossed mythological proper nouns stacked several to a
paragraph, especially in the Parodos (Ch.2). A handful of individual long
sentences in the character speeches (Ch.1 para 40, Ch.3 para 2, Ch.3 para
132, Ch.7's Oedipus autobiography, Ch.11's apostrophe passage) are dense
enough to require a careful reader to slow down or re-read, though none
are ungrammatical or incoherent. None of this rises to "needs a broader
pass" — the underlying prose is sound throughout; the accessibility gap is
concentrated and specific (unglossed classical proper nouns in the odes,
plus a few very long single-paragraph speeches), not systemic.
