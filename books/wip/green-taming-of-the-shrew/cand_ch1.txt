# Act 1, Scene 1 — Padua. A public place

[0] Flourish. Enter Lucentio and Tranio.

[1] LUCENTIO. Tranio, because of my great desire to see fair Padua, the nursery of the arts, I have arrived in fruitful Lombardy, the pleasant garden of great Italy. With my father's love and permission, I'm armed with his good will and your trusted company — you, my reliable servant, proven in everything. Let's catch our breath here and, with luck, begin a course of learning and serious studies. Pisa, famous for its respected citizens, gave me my birth, and my father before me — Vincentio, a great international merchant, descended from the Bentivolii. Vincentio's son, raised in Florence, should live up to every hope placed in him, decorating his family's fortune with virtuous deeds. So, Tranio, while I'm studying, I'll devote myself to virtue and to that branch of philosophy that deals with happiness — happiness achieved specifically through virtue. Tell me what you think. I've left Pisa and come to Padua like someone abandoning a shallow puddle to plunge into the deep, seeking to quench his thirst by drinking his fill.

[2] TRANIO. _Mi perdonato_, my gentle master. I feel exactly as you do — glad you're sticking to your plan to drink in the sweetness of philosophy. Only, good master, while we admire this virtue and moral discipline, let's not become stoics or stones, please. And don't be so devoted to Aristotle's restraints that you completely reject Ovid. Practise logic with friends, and use rhetoric in everyday conversation. Use music and poetry to keep yourself alive. Tackle mathematics and metaphysics whenever you have the stomach for them. No profit comes from anything without pleasure. In short, sir, study what you most enjoy.

[3] LUCENTIO. Thanks, Tranio — that's good advice. If Biondello had come ashore, we could get ourselves settled right away and find lodgings suitable to entertain whatever friends time brings us in Padua. But wait a moment — who are these people?

[4] TRANIO. Master, some sort of show to welcome us to town.

[5] [_Lucentio and Tranio stand aside._]

[6] Enter Baptista, Katherina, Bianca, Gremio and Hortensio.

[7] BAPTISTA. Gentlemen, don't press me any further. You know I'm firmly decided: I will not give my younger daughter away before I've found a husband for the elder. If either of you loves Katherina — and because I know you both well and like you both — you have my permission to court her as you please.

[8] GREMIO. Cart her, more like — she's too rough for me. There, Hortensio, do you want a wife?

[9] KATHERINA.

[10] [_To Baptista_] I beg you, sir, is it your wish

[11] to make a laughingstock of me in front of these men?

[12] HORTENSIO. Men, girl! What do you mean by that? No man for you, unless you were of gentler, milder stuff.

[13] KATHERINA. Honestly, sir, you'll never need to worry. I assure you, marriage isn't halfway to my heart. But if it were, you can be sure my concern would be to comb your skull with a three-legged stool, paint your face, and treat you like a fool.

[14] HORTENSIO. From all such devils, good Lord deliver us!

[15] GREMIO. And me too, good Lord!

[16] TRANIO. Quiet, master! There's some good entertainment coming. That girl is either stark mad or remarkably willful.

[17] LUCENTIO. But in the silence of the other one, I see a girl's mild manners and modesty. Quiet, Tranio!

[18] TRANIO. Well said, master — hush! And look your fill.

[19] BAPTISTA. Gentlemen, so I can quickly make good on what I've said — Bianca, go inside. And don't let this upset you, sweet Bianca. I'll love you no less for it, my girl.

[20] KATHERINA. A precious pet! She should stick her finger in her eye and cry, if she knew why.

[21] BIANCA. Sister, find your contentment in my discontent. Sir, I humbly submit to your wishes. My books and instruments will be my companions — I'll look at them, and practise by myself.

[22] LUCENTIO. Listen, Tranio! You may hear Minerva herself speaking.

[23] HORTENSIO. Signior Baptista, will you really be so distant? I'm sorry our good intentions cause Bianca grief.

[24] GREMIO. Why would you lock her up, Signior Baptista, on account of this hellish fiend, and make her pay the price of her sister's tongue?

[25] BAPTISTA. Gentlemen, settle down — I've made up my mind. Go inside, Bianca.

[26] [_Exit Bianca._]

[27] And since I know she takes the greatest delight in music, instruments, and poetry, I'll keep schoolmasters in my house qualified to instruct her youth. If you, Hortensio, or you, Signior Gremio, know any such men, send them to me. I'll be very kind to skilled men, and generous toward giving my own children a good upbringing. So, farewell. Katherina, you can stay — I have more to discuss with Bianca.

[28] [_Exit._]

[29] KATHERINA. Why, and I trust I'm free to leave too, am I not? What — am I to have my hours assigned, as if, presumably, I didn't know what to do and what not to do? Ha!

[30] [_Exit._]

[31] GREMIO. You can go to the devil's mother — your gifts are so wonderful that nobody here is going to hold you back. Their love isn't so strong, Hortensio, that we can't blow on our fingers together and wait it out. Our cake is dough on both sides. Farewell — though, for the love I have for my sweet Bianca, if I can by any means find a suitable man to teach her what she likes, I'll recommend him to her father.

[32] HORTENSIO. So will I, Signior Gremio. But a word, please. Though our rivalry has never let us talk before, now, after some thought, it concerns us both — that is, so we may again have access to our lovely lady and be happy rivals for Bianca's love — we ought to work together on one specific thing.

[33] GREMIO. What's that, pray?

[34] HORTENSIO. To get a husband for her sister, of course.

[35] GREMIO. A husband! You mean a devil.

[36] HORTENSIO. I said a husband.

[37] GREMIO. I said a devil. Do you really think, Hortensio, that even though her father is rich, any man is fool enough to marry hell?

[38] HORTENSIO. Come on, Gremio! Though it goes beyond your patience and mine to put up with her loud outbursts, there are good fellows in the world, man — and if a man could find them, they would take her with all her faults, provided there's enough money.

[39] GREMIO. I can't say. But I'd just as soon take her dowry on this condition: that I be whipped at the public cross every morning.

[40] HORTENSIO. As you say — there's small choice among rotten apples. But come on. Since this legal obstacle has made us friends, let's keep it friendly to that extent — until, by helping Baptista's eldest daughter to a husband, we set his youngest free to be married — and then we go at it again. Sweet Bianca! May the best man win! The one who runs fastest gets the prize. What do you say, Signior Gremio?

[41] GREMIO. Agreed. I'd give the best horse in Padua to the man who'd court her thoroughly, marry her, bed her, and rid the house of her. Let's go.

[42] [_Exeunt Gremio and Hortensio._]

[43] TRANIO. I beg you, sir, tell me — is it really possible that love should take such a sudden hold?

[44] LUCENTIO. Oh Tranio! Until I felt it myself, I never thought it possible or even likely. But look — while I stood idly watching, I felt the effect of love-in-idleness. And now I admit plainly to you, who are as private and as dear to me as Anna was to the Queen of Carthage — Tranio, I burn, I pine, I am dying, Tranio, if I don't have this young modest girl. Counsel me, Tranio, because I know you can. Help me, Tranio, because I know you will.

[45] TRANIO. Master, it's no time to scold you now. Affection isn't reasoned out of the heart. If love has struck you, nothing remains but this: _Redime te captum quam queas minimo._

[46] LUCENTIO. Thanks, lad — go on. That helps. The rest will comfort me, because your advice is sound.

[47] TRANIO. Master, you looked so long at the girl, you may not have caught the essential point.

[48] LUCENTIO. Oh, yes — I saw sweet beauty in her face, like the beauty of Agenor's daughter, the one that made great Jove kneel and kiss the Cretan shore.

[49] TRANIO. Did you see no more? Did you not notice how her sister began to scold and raise such a storm that mortal ears could barely stand the racket?

[50] LUCENTIO. Tranio, I saw her coral lips moving, and her breath perfumed the air. Everything I saw of her was sacred and sweet.

[51] TRANIO. All right, then — it's time to shake him out of his trance. Please wake up, sir. If you love the girl, bend your thoughts and your wits to winning her. Here's how things stand: her elder sister is so vicious and shrewish that until her father gets her off his hands, master, your love will have to live as a maid at home. He has shut her up tight on purpose, because he doesn't want suitors bothering her.

[52] LUCENTIO. Ah, Tranio, what a cruel father! But haven't you heard he took some trouble to find skilled schoolmasters to instruct her?

[53] TRANIO. Yes, indeed I have, sir — and now the scheme is taking shape.

[54] LUCENTIO. I've got it, Tranio.

[55] TRANIO. Master, I'd bet my hand both our ideas meet and fit as one.

[56] LUCENTIO. Tell me yours first.

[57] TRANIO. You'll become the schoolmaster, and take on the teaching of the girl. That's your plan.

[58] LUCENTIO. It is — but can it be done?

[59] TRANIO. Impossible — because who's going to play your part and be Vincentio's son here in Padua? Who keeps house and studies, welcomes friends, visits countrymen, and entertains them?

[60] LUCENTIO. _Basta_, calm down — I've thought it through. We haven't been seen at any house yet, and no one can tell our faces apart for master and man. So it follows: you'll be master in my place, Tranio. Keep up the house and rank and servants, as I should. I'll be someone else — some Florentine, some Neapolitan, or some lesser man from Pisa. It's hatched, and so it shall be. Tranio, undress at once — take my coloured hat and cloak. When Biondello arrives, he'll wait on you. But I'll first convince him to keep his tongue quiet.

[61] [_They exchange habits._]

[62] TRANIO. You'd better. In short, sir, since this is what you want, and I'm bound to obey — for so your father instructed me at our parting: 'Be helpful to my son,' he said, although I think he meant it in another sense — I'm content to be Lucentio, because I love Lucentio so well.

[63] LUCENTIO. Tranio, be so, because Lucentio loves. And let me be a slave, if it means winning the girl whose sudden sight has captured my wounded eye.

[64] Enter Biondello.

[65] Here comes the rogue. Hey, where have you been?

[66] BIONDELLO. Where have I been? No — how about this: where are you? Master, has my fellow Tranio stolen your clothes? Or have you stolen his? Or both? Tell me, what's the news?

[67] LUCENTIO. Hey, come here. This is no time for jokes — so adjust your manners to the situation. Your fellow Tranio here, to save my life, has put on my clothes and my appearance, and I, to escape, have put on his. In a fight since I came ashore I killed a man, and I'm afraid I was spotted. Wait on him, I order you, as you should — while I get out of here to save my life. Do you understand me?

[68] BIONDELLO. I, sir! Not at all.

[69] LUCENTIO. And not a word of Tranio out of your mouth. Tranio is changed into Lucentio.

[70] BIONDELLO. Lucky him — I wish I could swap too!

[71] TRANIO. So could I, boy — for the next wish in line, that Lucentio really did get Baptista's youngest daughter. But, fellow, not for my sake but your master's, I'm advising you: use your manners carefully in every kind of company. When I'm alone, I'm Tranio; but in all other places, your master Lucentio.

[72] LUCENTIO. Tranio, let's go. One more thing remains for you to do — to join up with these suitors. If you ask me why, it's enough that my reasons are good and weighty.

[73] [_Exeunt._]

[74] [_The Presenters above speak._]

[75] FIRST SERVANT. My lord, you're nodding off — you're not paying attention to the play.

[76] SLY. Yes I am, by Saint Anne. A good story, surely — is there more of it?

[77] PAGE. My lord, it's only just begun.

[78] SLY. It's an excellent piece of work, madam lady — I wish it were over!

[79] [_They sit and watch._]
