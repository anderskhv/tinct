# Act 3, Scene 2 — The same. Before Baptista’s house

[0] Enter Baptista, Gremio, Tranio, Katherina, Bianca, Lucentio and Attendants.

[1] BAPTISTA. [_To Tranio._] Signior Lucentio, this is the appointed day when Katherine and Petruchio are to be married, and yet we hear nothing of our son-in-law. What will people say? What a mockery this will be — to lack a bridegroom while the priest waits to perform the marriage ceremony! What does Lucentio say to this shame of ours?

[2] KATHERINA. No shame but mine. I must, indeed, be forced to give my hand, against my heart's wishes, to a mad-brained ruffian, full of malice — who wooed in a hurry and intends to marry at his leisure. I told you, didn't I — that he was a frantic fool, hiding his bitter jokes in blunt manners. And to be known as a witty man, he'll woo a thousand women, set the wedding day, make friends, invite guests, and proclaim the banns — yet never intends to marry where he has wooed. Now the world will point at poor Katherine and say, 'Look! There is mad Petruchio's wife, if he ever bothers to come and marry her.'

[3] TRANIO. Patience, good Katherine — and Baptista too. On my life, Petruchio means only well, whatever misfortune holds him from his word. Though he's blunt, I know him to be very wise. Though he's merry, he is still honest.

[4] KATHERINA. I wish Katherine had never seen him at all!

[5] [_Exit weeping, followed by Bianca and others._]

[6] BAPTISTA. Go, girl — I can't blame you for weeping now. Such an injury would vex a saint, let alone a shrew of your impatient temper.

[7] Enter Biondello.

[8] Master, master! News! Old news, and news of a kind you've never heard before!

[9] BAPTISTA. Is it new and old at once? How can that be?

[10] BIONDELLO. Why — isn't it news to hear that Petruchio is coming?

[11] BAPTISTA. Has he come?

[12] BIONDELLO. Why no, sir.

[13] BAPTISTA. What then?

[14] BIONDELLO. He is coming.

[15] BAPTISTA. When will he be here?

[16] BIONDELLO. When he stands where I am and sees you there.

[17] TRANIO. But say — what about your old news?

[18] BIONDELLO. Well, Petruchio is coming — in a new hat and an old jerkin; a pair of old breeches turned three times; a pair of boots that used to be candle-cases, one buckled, one laced; an old rusty sword taken from the town armoury, with a broken hilt and no scabbard, and two broken laces. His horse hipped, with an old moth-eaten saddle and stirrups that don't match. Beyond that, riddled with glanders and likely to drip from the spine — troubled with the lampass, infected with the fashions, full of windgalls, ridden with spavins, jaundiced with the yellows, beyond cure of the fives, ruined by staggers, gnawed by botflies, sway-backed and shoulder-shotten, knock-kneed in front, with a half-checked bit and a head-stall of sheep's leather, which, being tightened to keep him from stumbling, has often broken and is now patched with knots. One girth pieced together six times, and a woman's crupper of velvet with two letters of her name set in studs, here and there patched with packing-thread.

[19] BAPTISTA. Who comes with him?

[20] BIONDELLO. Oh, sir! His lackey — for all the world rigged out like the horse: with a linen stocking on one leg and a coarse boot-hose on the other, gartered with a red and blue strip; an old hat, and the trim of forty fancies stuck in for a feather. A monster, a perfect monster of apparel, and not like a Christian footboy or a gentleman's lackey at all.

[21] TRANIO. Some odd whim has driven him to dress this way. Yet often he goes plainly enough dressed.

[22] BAPTISTA. I'm glad he's come, however he comes.

[23] BIONDELLO. Why, sir — he isn't coming.

[24] BAPTISTA. Didn't you just say he's coming?

[25] BIONDELLO. Who? That Petruchio came?

[26] BAPTISTA. Yes — that Petruchio came.

[27] BIONDELLO. No, sir — I'm saying his horse comes, with him on its back.

[28] BAPTISTA. Why, that amounts to the same thing.

[29] BIONDELLO. No, by Saint Jamy — I bet you a penny: a horse and a man are more than one, and yet not many.

[30] Enter Petruchio and Grumio.

[31] PETRUCHIO. Come on, where are these gallants? Who's at home?

[32] BAPTISTA. You're welcome, sir.

[33] PETRUCHIO. And yet I come not well.

[34] BAPTISTA. And yet you do not limp.

[35] TRANIO. Not so well-dressed as I'd wish you to be.

[36] PETRUCHIO. Even if I were better, I'd still rush in like this. But where is Kate? Where is my lovely bride? How is my father? Gentlemen, you seem to frown. Why does this good company stare, as if they saw some wondrous monument, some comet, or unusual prodigy?

[37] BAPTISTA. Why, sir — you know this is your wedding day. First we were sad, fearing you wouldn't come. Now we're sadder, because you come so unprepared. Fie! Take off this outfit — it's a shame to your standing, and an eyesore at our solemn festival.

[38] TRANIO. And tell us what matter of importance has detained you so long from your wife, and sent you here looking so unlike yourself?

[39] PETRUCHIO. It would be tedious to tell and harsh to hear. It's enough that I've come to keep my word, even though forced in some part to digress — which at more leisure I'll explain so well that you'll be satisfied. But where is Kate? I've stayed away too long. The morning is wearing on — it's time we were at church.

[40] TRANIO. Don't see your bride in these disrespectful clothes. Go to my room, put on some of mine.

[41] PETRUCHIO. Not I — believe me. This is how I'll visit her.

[42] BAPTISTA. But, I trust, this is not how you'll marry her.

[43] PETRUCHIO. In good faith, just like this. So have done with words. She's married to me, not to my clothes. If I could mend what she'll wear out in me as easily as I can change these poor accoutrements, it would be well for Kate, and better for me. But what a fool I am to chat with you, when I should be wishing my bride good morning, and sealing the bond with a loving kiss!

[44] [_Exeunt Petruchio, Grumio and Biondello._]

[45] TRANIO. There's some meaning in this mad attire. We'll persuade him, if we can, to put on better clothes before he goes to church.

[46] BAPTISTA. I'll go after him and see how this turns out.

[47] [_Exeunt Baptista, Gremio and Attendants._]

[48] TRANIO. But, sir, regarding love, we still need to add her father's approval — and to bring that about, as I told your worship before, I need to get hold of a man — whoever he may be, it doesn't matter much, we'll suit him to our purpose — and he shall be Vincentio of Pisa, and provide guarantees here in Padua of greater sums than I've promised. So you'll quietly enjoy your hope, and marry sweet Bianca with consent.

[49] LUCENTIO. Were it not that my fellow schoolmaster watches Bianca's every step so closely, I think it would be good to steal our marriage. Once that's done, let all the world say no — I'll keep what's mine in spite of all the world.

[50] TRANIO. We'll look into that step by step, and watch for our advantage in this business. We'll outwit the greybeard, Gremio, the narrow-prying father, Minola, and the dainty musician, amorous Licio — all for my master's sake, Lucentio.

[51] Re-enter Gremio.

[52] Signior Gremio, are you coming from the church?

[53] GREMIO. As gladly as ever I came home from school.

[54] TRANIO. And are the bride and bridegroom coming home?

[55] GREMIO. A bridegroom, you say? He's a groom indeed — a grumbling groom, and that the girl will find out.

[56] TRANIO. Worse than her? Why, that's impossible.

[57] GREMIO. Why, he's a devil, a devil, a very fiend.

[58] TRANIO. Why, she's a devil, a devil, the devil's own mother.

[59] GREMIO. Pah! She's a lamb, a dove, a fool, compared to him. I'll tell you, Sir Lucentio: when the priest was supposed to ask if Katherine should be his wife — 'Yes, by God's wounds,' he said, and swore so loud that the priest, all astonished, dropped the book. And as he stooped down again to pick it up, the mad-brained bridegroom struck him such a cuff that down fell priest and book, and book and priest. 'Now pick them up,' he said, 'if anyone wants to.'

[60] TRANIO. What did the girl say when he stood up again?

[61] GREMIO. She trembled and shook — because he stamped and swore as if the vicar meant to cheat him. But after many ceremonies were done, he called for wine. 'A health!' he said, as if he'd been abroad, carousing with his mates after a storm. He quaffed off the muscadel and threw the sops in the sexton's face — for no other reason than that his beard grew thin and hungry-looking, and seemed to be begging for sops as he was drinking. With that done, he took the bride about the neck and kissed her lips with such a clamorous smack that at the parting all the church echoed. And I, seeing this, came away out of sheer shame. After me, I know, the whole crowd is coming. There was never such a mad marriage. Listen, listen — I hear the minstrels playing.

[62] [_Music plays._]

[63] Enter Petruchio, Katherina, Bianca, Baptista, Hortensio, Grumio and Train.

[64] PETRUCHIO. Gentlemen and friends, I thank you for your trouble. I know you planned to dine with me today, and have prepared a great spread of wedding cheer. But things being as they are, my haste calls me away — so this is where I mean to take my leave.

[65] BAPTISTA. Is it possible you'll be off tonight?

[66] PETRUCHIO. I must be off today, before night comes. Don't be surprised — if you knew my business, you'd urge me to go rather than stay. And, honest company, I thank you all — you who have watched me give myself away to this most patient, sweet, and virtuous wife. Dine with my father, drink a health to me. For I must go — and farewell to you all.

[67] TRANIO. Let us beg you to stay till after dinner.

[68] PETRUCHIO. It can't be done.

[69] GREMIO. Let me beg you.

[70] PETRUCHIO. It cannot be.

[71] KATHERINA. Let me beg you.

[72] PETRUCHIO. I am content.

[73] KATHERINA. Content to stay?

[74] PETRUCHIO. Content that you should beg me to stay — but not to actually stay, no matter how you beg.

[75] KATHERINA. Now, if you love me, stay.

[76] PETRUCHIO. Grumio, my horse!

[77] GRUMIO. Yes, sir, they're ready — the oats have eaten the horses.

[78] KATHERINA. No, then — do what you can, I will not go today. No, nor tomorrow — not until I please myself. The door is open, sir — there's your way. You can be off while your boots are still fresh. As for me, I won't leave until I please myself. It looks like you'll prove a fine surly groom, taking it on yourself like this so soon.

[79] PETRUCHIO. Oh Kate! Settle down — please don't be angry.

[80] KATHERINA. I will be angry — what does it matter to you? Father, be quiet — he shall wait until I'm ready.

[81] GREMIO. Yes, indeed, sir — now it begins to work.

[82] KATHERINA. Gentlemen, let's go forward to the wedding feast. I see a woman can be made a fool, if she doesn't have a spirit to resist.

[83] PETRUCHIO. They shall go forward, Kate, at your command. Obey the bride, you who attend her. Go to the feast — revel and lord it over things. Carouse a full measure to her maidenhood. Be mad and merry, or go hang yourselves. But as for my bonny Kate — she must come with me. No — don't look big, or stamp, or stare, or fret. I will be master of what is my own. She is my goods, my chattel; she is my house, my household stuff, my field, my barn, my horse, my ox, my ass, my anything. And here she stands — touch her whoever dares. I'll bring my action against the proudest man who stops my way in Padua. Grumio — draw your weapon. We're beset by thieves. Rescue your mistress, if you're a man. Don't fear, sweet wench — they shall not touch you, Kate. I'll defend you against a million.

[84] [_Exeunt Petruchio, Katherina and Grumio._]

[85] BAPTISTA. No, let them go — a quiet pair, those two.

[86] GREMIO. If they hadn't gone quickly, I'd have died laughing.

[87] TRANIO. Of all mad matches, there was never the like.

[88] LUCENTIO. Mistress, what's your opinion of your sister?

[89] BIANCA. That, being mad herself, she's madly matched.

[90] GREMIO. I guarantee, Petruchio is well and truly Kated.

[91] BAPTISTA. Neighbours and friends — though we lack a bride and groom to fill the places at the table, you know there's no lack of sweetmeats at the feast. Lucentio, you shall fill the bridegroom's place. And let Bianca take her sister's seat.

[92] TRANIO. Shall sweet Bianca practise how to play the bride?

[93] BAPTISTA. She shall, Lucentio. Come, gentlemen, let's go.

[94] [_Exeunt._]
