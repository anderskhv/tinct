# Act 1, Scene 2 — Padua. Before Hortensio’s house

[0] Enter Petruchio and his servant Grumio.

[1] PETRUCHIO. Verona, I take my leave of you for a while, to visit my friends in Padua — but above all, my best loved and most trusted friend, Hortensio. I believe this is his house. Here, Grumio, knock, I say.

[2] GRUMIO. Knock, sir? Knock whom? Has somebody insulted your worship?

[3] PETRUCHIO. Villain, I said — knock here for me, and do it soundly.

[4] GRUMIO. Knock you here, sir? Why, sir — what am I, sir, that I should knock you here, sir?

[5] PETRUCHIO. Villain, I say — knock me at this gate; and rap me well, or I'll knock your knave's skull.

[6] GRUMIO. My master is in a quarrelsome mood. If I knock you first, then I'll know who's worse off after.

[7] PETRUCHIO. Won't you do it? Honestly, fellow, if you won't knock, I'll ring it — I'll try how you can sol, fa, and sing it.

[8] [_He wrings Grumio by the ears._]

[9] GRUMIO. Help, masters, help! My master is mad.

[10] PETRUCHIO. Now knock when I tell you to, you wretched villain!

[11] Enter Hortensio.

[12] HORTENSIO. Hey now! What's going on? My old friend Grumio! And my good friend Petruchio! How are you all in Verona?

[13] PETRUCHIO. Signior Hortensio, have you come to break up the fight? _Con tutto il cuore ben trovato_, I may say.

[14] HORTENSIO. _Alla nostra casa ben venuto; molto honorato signor mio Petruchio._ Get up, Grumio, get up — we'll settle this quarrel.

[15] GRUMIO. No, sir, it doesn't matter what he claims in Latin. If this isn't a lawful cause for me to leave his service, look here, sir — he ordered me to knock him and rap him soundly, sir. Well, was that fitting for a servant to do to his master? Who, maybe, for all I see, is two-and-thirty and a little off. I wish to God I'd knocked him soundly first — then Grumio wouldn't be the worse off.

[16] PETRUCHIO. A senseless villain! Good Hortensio, I told the rascal to knock on your gate, and I couldn't, for the life of me, get him to do it.

[17] GRUMIO. Knock on the gate! Oh heavens! Didn't you say these very words: 'Fellow, knock me here, rap me here, knock me well, and knock me soundly'? And now you come with 'knocking on the gate'?

[18] PETRUCHIO. Fellow, be gone, or stop talking — I advise you.

[19] HORTENSIO. Petruchio, calm down — I'll vouch for Grumio. Look, this is a heavy quarrel between him and you — your old, trusty, pleasant servant Grumio. And tell me now, sweet friend — what happy wind blows you to Padua from old Verona?

[20] PETRUCHIO. The same wind that scatters young men across the world to seek their fortunes farther from home, where there's little experience to be had. In short, Signior Hortensio, here's where I stand: Antonio, my father, has died, and I've thrust myself into this maze, hoping to marry and prosper as well as I can. I have coins in my purse and property at home, and I've come abroad to see the world.

[21] HORTENSIO. Petruchio, shall I get straight to the point with you, then, and wish you onto a shrewish, ill-favoured wife? You wouldn't thank me much for the advice. And yet I'll guarantee she'll be rich — very rich. But you're too much my friend — I won't wish her on you.

[22] PETRUCHIO. Signior Hortensio, between friends like us, few words are needed. So if you know one rich enough to be Petruchio's wife — since wealth is the burden of my wooing dance — let her be as foul as Florentius's love, as old as Sibyl, and as vicious and shrewish as Socrates's Xanthippe or worse, she won't move me, or at least won't blunt the edge of my desire, were she as rough as the swelling Adriatic seas. I've come to marry wealthily in Padua; if wealthily, then happily in Padua.

[23] GRUMIO. There you go, sir — he's telling you flat out what's on his mind. Give him enough gold and marry him off to a puppet or an aglet-doll, or an old hag without a tooth in her head, though she has as many diseases as fifty-two horses — nothing's wrong, as long as the money comes with it.

[24] HORTENSIO. Petruchio, since we've gone this far, I'll continue what I started as a joke. I can, Petruchio, help you to a wife with wealth enough, and she's young and beautiful, raised as well as a gentlewoman should be. Her only fault — and that's faults enough — is that she's intolerably shrewish, and so unreasonably wilful and stubborn that, were my fortune far worse than it is, I wouldn't marry her for a mine of gold.

[25] PETRUCHIO. Hortensio, hush! You don't know what gold can do. Just tell me her father's name, and that's enough — I'll board her, though she rails as loud as thunder when the autumn clouds split.

[26] HORTENSIO. Her father is Baptista Minola, a friendly and courteous gentleman. Her name is Katherina Minola, famous in Padua for her scolding tongue.

[27] PETRUCHIO. I know her father, though I don't know her. And he knew my late father well. I won't sleep, Hortensio, until I see her. So let me be this blunt with you — I'll leave you on this first encounter, unless you'll come along with me.

[28] GRUMIO. Please, sir, let him go while the mood is on him. On my word, if she knew him as well as I do, she'd realize scolding would do little good on him. She may call him half a dozen knaves or so — but that's nothing. Once he starts, he'll rail with his rope-tricks. I'll tell you what, sir — if she stands up to him even a little, he'll throw such a figure of speech in her face that he'll disfigure her with it, so she won't have any more eyes to see with than a cat. You don't know him, sir.

[29] HORTENSIO. Wait, Petruchio — I must go with you, because in Baptista's keeping is my treasure. He holds the jewel of my life — his youngest daughter, beautiful Bianca — and he keeps her from me and others, suitors to her and rivals in my love. He thinks it impossible, given the defects I just rehearsed, that Katherina will ever be wooed. So Baptista has issued this order: no one shall have access to Bianca until Katherine the shrew has got a husband.

[30] GRUMIO. Katherine the shrew! Of all titles for a girl, the worst.

[31] HORTENSIO. Now my friend Petruchio shall do me a favour, and present me disguised in sober robes to old Baptista as a music master, well versed in music, to instruct Bianca — so that by this scheme I may at least have leave and leisure to make love to her, and court her unsuspected, by herself.

[32] GRUMIO. No knavery here! See how, to fool the old folks, the young folks lay their heads together!

[33] Enter Gremio and Lucentio disguised, with books under his arm.

[34] Master, master, look about you — who goes there, ha?

[35] HORTENSIO. Quiet, Grumio! It's the rival for my love. Petruchio, stand aside a moment.

[36] GRUMIO. A handsome young lad, and an amorous one!

[37] GREMIO. Oh! Very well — I've looked over the list. Listen, sir — I'll have them very handsomely bound. All books of love, mind you — see to it. And don't read any other kinds of lecture to her. You understand me. Beyond and besides Signior Baptista's generosity, I'll add to it with a tip. Take your papers too, and have them very well perfumed — for she they're going to is sweeter than perfume itself. What will you read to her?

[38] LUCENTIO. Whatever I read to her, I'll plead for you, as for my patron, you can rest assured — as firmly as if you yourself were still in my place. Yes, and perhaps with more successful words than you, unless you were a scholar, sir.

[39] GREMIO. Oh! This learning — what a thing it is!

[40] GRUMIO. Oh! This woodcock — what an ass he is!

[41] PETRUCHIO. Quiet, fellow!

[42] HORTENSIO. Grumio, hush! God save you, Signior Gremio!

[43] GREMIO. And you are well met, Signior Hortensio. Can you guess where I'm going? To Baptista Minola. I promised to enquire carefully about a schoolmaster for fair Bianca, and by good fortune I've landed well on this young man — for learning and behaviour fit for her purpose, well read in poetry and other books, good ones, I guarantee.

[44] HORTENSIO. That's good. And I've met a gentleman who has promised to help me to another — a fine musician to instruct our lady. So I won't be a bit behind in duty to fair Bianca, so beloved of me.

[45] GREMIO. Beloved of me — and that my deeds shall prove.

[46] GRUMIO.

[47] [_Aside._] And that his moneybags shall prove.

[48] HORTENSIO. Gremio, this isn't the time to air our love. Listen — if you speak fairly to me, I'll tell you news that's equally good for both of us. Here is a gentleman I met by chance, who, on an agreement to his liking from us, will undertake to woo cursed Katherine — yes, and to marry her, if her dowry pleases him.

[49] GREMIO. Well said — well done is even better. Hortensio, have you told him all her faults?

[50] PETRUCHIO. I know she's an irritating brawling scold. If that's all, gentlemen, I see no harm.

[51] GREMIO. No? You say that, friend? Where are you from?

[52] PETRUCHIO. Born in Verona, son of old Antonio. My father is dead, my fortune lives for me — and I hope to see good days and many of them.

[53] GREMIO. Oh sir, such a life with such a wife would be remarkable! But if you have the stomach for it, then go to it, in God's name — you'll have me assisting you in everything. But will you really woo this wildcat?

[54] PETRUCHIO. Will I live?

[55] GRUMIO. Will he woo her? Yes — or I'll hang her.

[56] PETRUCHIO. Why did I come here, if not for that purpose? Do you think a little din can daunt my ears? Haven't I in my time heard lions roar? Haven't I heard the sea, puffed up with winds, rage like an angry boar slick with sweat? Haven't I heard great cannons in the field, and heaven's artillery thunder in the skies? Haven't I in pitched battle heard loud alarms, neighing horses, and trumpets' clang? And you tell me about a woman's tongue, which doesn't strike the ear half as hard as a chestnut popping in a farmer's fire? Pah, pah! Scare boys with bogeymen.

[57] GRUMIO.

[58] [_Aside_] Because he fears none.

[59] GREMIO. Hortensio, listen — this gentleman has happily arrived, I think, for his own good and yours.

[60] HORTENSIO. I promised we'd be contributors and cover his costs of wooing, whatever they are.

[61] GREMIO. And so we will, provided he wins her.

[62] GRUMIO. I wish I were as sure of a good dinner.

[63] Enter Tranio splendidly dressed, with Biondello.

[64] TRANIO. Gentlemen, God save you! If I may be so bold — tell me, I beg, which is the quickest way to the house of Signior Baptista Minola?

[65] BIONDELLO. The man with the two fair daughters — is that the one you mean?

[66] TRANIO. The very one, Biondello!

[67] GREMIO. Look here, sir — you don't mean her, do you—

[68] TRANIO. Perhaps him and her, sir — what's it to you?

[69] PETRUCHIO. Not her that scolds, sir — at any cost, I beg.

[70] TRANIO. I love no scolds, sir. Biondello, let's go.

[71] LUCENTIO.

[72] [_Aside_] Well begun, Tranio.

[73] HORTENSIO. Sir, a word before you go. Are you a suitor to the girl you speak of, yes or no?

[74] TRANIO. And if I am, sir — is that any offence?

[75] GREMIO. No — if without more words you'll get yourself out of here.

[76] TRANIO. Why, sir, please — aren't the streets as free for me as for you?

[77] GREMIO. But she is not.

[78] TRANIO. For what reason, I beg you?

[79] GREMIO. For this reason, if you must know — she's the chosen love of Signior Gremio.

[80] HORTENSIO. And she's the chosen of Signior Hortensio.

[81] TRANIO. Easy, gentlemen! If you're gentlemen, give me this courtesy — hear me out patiently. Baptista is a noble gentleman, and my father is not entirely unknown to him. And were his daughter even fairer than she is, she might have more suitors — and me for one. Fair Leda's daughter had a thousand wooers — surely one more may have fair Bianca. And so she shall: Lucentio will be one, though Paris himself came hoping to win alone.

[82] GREMIO. What — this gentleman will out-talk us all.

[83] LUCENTIO. Sir, give him his head — I know he'll prove a worn-out nag.

[84] PETRUCHIO. Hortensio, what's the point of all these words?

[85] HORTENSIO. Sir, let me be so bold as to ask — have you ever seen Baptista's daughter?

[86] TRANIO. No, sir, but I hear that he has two — the one as famous for a scolding tongue as the other is for beautiful modesty.

[87] PETRUCHIO. Sir, sir — the first is for me. Let her pass.

[88] GREMIO. Yes — leave that labour to great Hercules, and let it count as more than Alcides' twelve.

[89] PETRUCHIO. Sir, understand this from me, truly: the youngest daughter, the one you're after — her father keeps her from all access to suitors, and won't promise her to any man until the elder sister is first married. The younger is free then, and not before.

[90] TRANIO. If that's so, sir, and you are the man who must serve us all — and me among the rest — then if you break the ice and pull this off, achieve the elder and set the younger free for our access, whoever's lucky enough to have her won't be so graceless as to be ungrateful.

[91] HORTENSIO. Sir, you speak well, and you understand it well. And since you declare yourself a suitor, you must, as we do, show gratitude to this gentleman — to whom we'll all be generally indebted.

[92] TRANIO. Sir, I'll not be slack. As proof of it — if it pleases you, let's arrange this afternoon, and drink toasts to our lady's health, and do as adversaries do in law: fight mightily, but eat and drink as friends.

[93] GRUMIO, BIONDELLO. Oh, excellent suggestion! Fellows, let's be off.

[94] HORTENSIO. The suggestion is good indeed — so be it. Petruchio, I shall be your _ben venuto_.

[95] [_Exeunt._]
