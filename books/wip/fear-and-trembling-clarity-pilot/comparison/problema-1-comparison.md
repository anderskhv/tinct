# Problema I — Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical? — source / baseline / candidate v4.1

Columns per slot: **DA** = established source (served `original-da`, 1895 3rd ed.); **BASELINE** = served `modern-en` (sha256 152776f1…); **CANDIDATE** = this pilot; **WHY** = reason for the change. Word counts in brackets.

**Every** candidate paragraph is a fresh rendering from the Danish, not an edit of the baseline. WHY names the specific clarity, fidelity and alignment decisions. **BASELINE** [269w]

> The ethical as such is the universal, and as the universal it is what holds for everyone, which from another side may be expressed thus: that it holds at every moment. It rests immanent in itself, has nothing outside itself that is its *telos*, but is itself the *telos* for everything that has it outside itself; and once the ethical has taken this into itself, it goes no further. Determined as immediate sensuous and psychic being, the single individual is the single individual who has his *telos* in the universal, and this is his ethical task: continually to express himself in this, to annul his singularity in order to become the universal. As soon as the single individual would assert himself in his singularity over against the universal, he sins, and only by acknowledging this can he be reconciled again with the universal. Every time the single individual, after having entered into the universal, feels an impulse to assert himself as the single individual, he is in temptation, out of which he works himself only by repenting and giving up himself as the single individual into the universal. If this is the highest that can be said about a human being and his existence, then the ethical has the same character as a person's eternal blessedness, which through all eternity and at every moment is his *telos*, since it would be a contradiction that it could be given up (i.e., teleologically suspended), since it, as soon as it is suspended, is forfeited; while what is suspended is not forfeited, but precisely preserved in the higher, which is its *telos*.

**CANDIDATE** [306w]

> The ethical as such is the universal. As the universal, it applies to everyone — which can also be put another way: it applies at every moment. It rests immanently in itself: nothing outside it is its telos (its end: the purpose it exists for). Instead, it is itself the telos of everything outside it, and once the ethical has taken all of that into itself, it goes no further. Taken in his immediacy — as a being of senses and soul — the single individual is the individual who has his telos in the universal. His ethical task is to express himself in the universal continually: to cancel his particularity (whatever sets him apart as this one person) in order to become the universal. As soon as the single individual tries to assert himself in his particularity against the universal, he sins; the only way back to reconciliation with the universal is to acknowledge that he has sinned. Whenever the single individual, after having entered the universal, feels an urge to assert himself as the single individual, he is in a spiritual trial — an inner assault on him — and he can work his way out of it only by repenting and giving himself up, as the single individual, in the universal. If this is the highest thing that can be said about a human being and his existence, then the ethical has the same character as the eternal blessedness of a human being, which for all eternity and at every moment is his telos. For it would be a contradiction if eternal blessedness could be given up (that is, teleologically suspended). For the moment eternal blessedness was suspended, it would be forfeited — whereas whatever is teleologically suspended is not forfeited, but is preserved precisely in the higher thing that is its telos.

**WHY** — Baseline keeps 'rests immanent in itself', 'Determined as immediate sensuous and psychic being' and 'annul his singularity' in the Danish word order. The candidate splits the paragraph into 13 sentences. It defines telos at first use as 'its end: the purpose it exists for'. The immediacy clause is kept as a qualification ('Taken in his immediacy …'), not as a definition of the single individual. Enkelthed becomes 'particularity' with a five-word gloss, and ophæve becomes 'cancel'. The eternal-blessedness comparison is made explicit: the second 'it' is now 'eternal blessedness', where the baseline had 'since it … since it'. Anfægtelse becomes 'spiritual trial', glossed neutrally as 'an inner assault on him', where the baseline had 'temptation', which collides with Fristelse in ¶15. The logic is unchanged: blessedness cannot be suspended, because what is suspended is preserved in something higher.

## ¶1

**DA** [104w]

> Forholder det sig saaledes, da har Hegel Ret, naar han lader Mennesket i det Gode og Samvittigheden kun være bestemmet som den Enkelte, han har Ret i at betragte denne Bestemmethed som en ,moralsk Form af det Onde" (cfr. især Retsphilosophien), hvilken skal ophæves i det Sædeliges Teleologi, saaledes at den Enkelte, der forbliver i hiint Stadium, enten synder eller ligger i Anfægtelse: Hvad Hegel derimod ikke har Ret i, er at tale om Troen, Uret i ikke høit og lydeligt at protestere mod, at Abraham nyder Hæder og Ære som en Troens Fader, medens han burde hjemvises og udvises som en Morder. .

**BASELINE** [120w]

> If this is the case, then Hegel is right when he allows a human being in the good and in conscience to be determined only as the single individual; he is right to regard this character as a ‘moral form of evil' (cf. especially the *Philosophy of Right*), which must be annulled in the teleology of the ethical, so that the single individual who remains in this stage either sins or lies in temptation. What Hegel is *not* right about, however, is to speak about faith, not right in not protesting loudly and audibly when Abraham is given honor and glory as a father of faith, while he ought rather to be shown the door and expelled as a murderer.

**CANDIDATE** [139w]

> If that is how things stand, then Hegel is right when, at the stage of "the Good and Conscience," he defines a human being only as the single individual. He is right to regard this condition as a "moral form of evil" (see especially his Philosophy of Right), one that must be cancelled in the teleology of ethical life — the ethics lived out in a people's shared customs and institutions. On this view, the single individual who remains at that stage either sins or is in a spiritual trial. Where Hegel is wrong, however, is in speaking of faith. He is wrong not to raise a loud and public protest when Abraham is honored and celebrated as a father of faith, whereas Abraham ought instead to be sent back to a lower court and expelled as a murderer.

**WHY** — 'Moral form of evil' is kept as Hegel's phrase. 'The Good and Conscience' is marked as a title. det Sædelige is defined once as 'ethical life — the ethics lived out in a people's shared customs and institutions' (Hegel's Sittlichkeit), so that the later 'the ethical, that is, ethical life' can be followed. 'hjemvises og udvises' is kept as a two-step legal image. 'On this view' links the Hegel sentence to its consequence.

**DA** [142w]

Dersom dette ikke er Troen, såa er Abraham tabt, saa har Troen aldrig været til i Verden, netop fordi den altid har været til. Thi hvis det Ethiske 9: det Sædelige er det Højeste, og der intet Incommensurabelt bliver tilbage i Mennesket paa anden Maade, end at dette Incommensurable er det Onde 2: det Enkelte, der skal udtrykkes i det Almene, saa behøver man ikke andre Kategorier end hvad den græske Philosophi havde, eller hvad der ved en conseqvent Tænkning lader sig uddrage af disse. Dette burde Hegel ikke have lagt Skjul paa; thi han har dog havt græske Studier.

**BASELINE** [151w]

> For faith is precisely this paradox: that the single individual is higher than the universal, yet, mark well, in such a way that the movement repeats itself — that is, that having been in the universal, he now, as the single individual, isolates himself as higher than the universal. If this is not faith, then Abraham is lost; then faith has never existed in the world, precisely because it has always existed. For if the ethical, i.e., the moral, is the highest, and there is nothing incommensurable left in a person except in such a way that this incommensurable is evil, i.e., the singular which is to be expressed in the universal, then one needs no other categories than those Greek philosophy possessed, or those that can be drawn out of these by consistent thinking. This Hegel ought not to have concealed; for he had, after all, Greek studies behind him.

**CANDIDATE** [160w]

> For faith is precisely this paradox: that the single individual is higher than the universal. But note carefully how this is so: the movement is repeated. Having first been in the universal, the single individual now isolates himself, as the single individual, as higher than the universal. If faith is not this, then Abraham is lost, and there has never been faith in the world — precisely because there has always been faith. For suppose that the ethical — that is, ethical life — is the highest, and that nothing incommensurable remains in a human being (nothing that cannot be measured by that common standard) except in the sense that this incommensurable element is evil, namely the particular, which ought to be expressed in the universal. **WHY** — The 'mark well' clause is split off. 'The movement repeats itself' now leads into the sentence that explains it. The long 'if the ethical … then' conditional becomes 'suppose that … In that case'. 'Incommensurable' gets a 7-word gloss. 'det Enkelte' is rendered 'the particular'. The paradox 'never existed … because it has always existed' is kept unexplained.

**BASELINE** [170w]

> Faith is precisely this paradox: that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal, is justified over against it, not subordinate but superior — yet, mark well, in such a way that it is the single individual who, having been as the single individual subordinate to the universal, now through the universal becomes the single individual who, as the single individual, is superior to it; that the single individual as the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute. **CANDIDATE** [185w]

> Faith is precisely this paradox: that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal — is justified over against it, not subordinate to it but above it. But note carefully how this is so: it is the single individual who, after having been subordinate to the universal as the single individual, now, through the universal, becomes the single individual who as the single individual stands above it — that the single individual as the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute. This position cannot be mediated — that is, it cannot be brought under the universal — for all mediation takes place precisely by means of the universal. And yet either faith is this paradox, or else — these are the consequences I ask the reader to keep in mind at every point, even though it would become too long-winded for me to write them out everywhere — or else there has never been faith, precisely because there has always been faith, or else Abraham is lost.

**WHY** — The either/or/or-else structure of 'Og dog er Troen dette Paradox eller ogsaa … eller ogsaa' is restored. The baseline had turned the first member into a flat assertion. 'Cannot be mediated' gets a short definition: 'settled by being brought under the universal and explained there'. The rest of that definition is the source's own clause, 'all mediation takes place by means of the universal'.

## ¶6

**DA** [245w]

Deres Viisdom løber ud paa den skjønne Sætning, at i Grunden er Alt det Samme. Naar man vil see lidt nærmere til, tvivler jeg meget paa, at man i den ganske Verden skal finde en eneste Analogi, undtagen en senere, hvilket Intet beviser, naar det staaer fast, at Abraham repræsenterer Troen, og at den normalt er udtrykt i ham, hvis Liv ikke blot er det mest Paradoxe, der lader sig tænke, men saa paradox, at det slet ikke lader sig tænke. Han handler i Kraft af det Absurde; thi det er netop det Absurde, at han som den Enkelte er højere end det Almene. Dette Paradox lader sig ikke mediere; thi saasnart han begynder derpaa, da maa han tilstaae, at han var i en Anfægtelse, og naar saa er, kommer han aldrig til at offre Isaak, eller hvis han har offret Isaak, da maa han .angrende vende tilbage til det Almene. I Kraft af det Absurde faaer han Isaak igjen. Abraham er derfor i intet Øieblik en tragisk Helt, men noget ganske Andet, enten en Morder eller en Troende. Den Mellembestemmelse, der frelser den tragiske Helt, har Abraham ikke. **BASELINE** [251w]

> Now the story of Abraham contains just such a teleological suspension of the ethical. There has been no lack of clever heads and thorough scholars who have found analogies to it. Their wisdom amounts to the fine proposition that fundamentally everything is the same. If one will look a little more closely, I very much doubt that one will find a single analogy in the whole world, except a later one, which proves nothing, when it stands fast that Abraham represents faith, and that it is normally expressed in him, whose life is not only the most paradoxical conceivable, but so paradoxical that it cannot be thought at all. He acts by virtue of the absurd; for it is precisely the absurd that he, as the single individual, is higher than the universal. This paradox cannot be mediated; for as soon as he begins to do so, he must confess that he was in a temptation; and if so, he never gets to sacrifice Isaac; or if he has sacrificed Isaac, he must, repenting, return back to the universal. By virtue of the absurd he gets Isaac back again. Abraham, therefore, is at no moment a tragic hero, but something quite different — either a murderer or a believer. The mediating term that saves the tragic hero is what Abraham does not have. Therefore it is that I can understand a tragic hero, but cannot understand Abraham, even if in a certain demented way I admire him more than all others.

**CANDIDATE** [82w]

> Ethically expressed, Abraham's relation to Isaac is quite simply this: the father must love the son more than himself. But the ethical has several gradations within its own range. > * Lessing has somewhere said something similar from a purely aesthetic standpoint. What he actually wants to show in that passage is that grief, too, can find witty expression. To that end he quotes a remark made in a particular situation by the unhappy English king Edward II. In contrast to this, he cites, following Diderot, a story about a peasant woman and a remark of hers. He then continues: "Auch das war Witz, und noch dazu Witz einer Bäuerin; aber die Umstände machten ihn unvermeidlich. Und folglich auch muss man die Entschuldigung der witzigen Ausdrücke des Schmerzes und der Betrübniss nicht darin suchen, dass die Person, welche sie sagt, eine vornehme, wohlerzogene, verständige, und auch sonst witzige Person sey; denn die Leidenschaften machen alle Menschen wieder gleich: sondern darin, dass wahrscheinlicher Weise ein jeder Mensch ohne Unterschied in den nämlichen Umständen das nämliche sagen würde. Den Gedanken der Bäuerin hätte eine Königin haben können und haben müssen: so wie das, was dort der König sagt, auch ein Bauer hätte sagen können und ohne Zweifel würde gesagt haben." [That too was wit, and a peasant woman's wit at that; but the circumstances made it unavoidable. And consequently the excuse for witty expressions of pain and grief must not be sought in the fact that the person who says them is a distinguished, well-bred, intelligent and otherwise witty person; for the passions make all human beings equal again. It must be sought in the fact that, in all probability, every human being without distinction would say the same thing in the same circumstances. A queen could have had, and must have had, the peasant woman's thought, just as what the king says there a peasant too could have said, and no doubt would have said.] Cf.
