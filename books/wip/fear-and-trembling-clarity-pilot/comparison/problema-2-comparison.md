**BASELINE** [283w]

> The ethical is the universal, and as such, in turn, the divine. One is therefore right to say that every duty is fundamentally a duty toward God; but if one cannot say more, then one is also saying that I have, properly speaking, no duty toward God. Duty becomes duty by being referred to God; but in duty itself I do not enter into relation with God. Thus it is a duty to love one's neighbor. It is a duty insofar as it is referred to God; but in the duty I enter into relation not with God but with the neighbor whom I love. If I say in this connection that it is my duty to love God, then I am really saying only a tautology, insofar as ‘God' is here taken in an entirely abstract sense as the divine, i.e., the universal, i.e., duty. The whole existence of the human race rounds itself off completely as a sphere within itself, and the ethical is at once both that which limits and that which fills out. God becomes an invisible, vanishing point, an impotent thought; his power is only in the ethical, which fills out existence. So insofar as anyone should fall into wanting to love God in any other sense than the one indicated here, then he is overstrained; he loves a phantom which, if it had only enough power to speak, would say to him: I do not demand your love, only stay where you belong. Insofar as anyone should fall into wanting to love God otherwise, then this love becomes suspect, like the love Rousseau speaks of, by which a person loves the Kaffirs instead of loving his neighbor.

**CANDIDATE** [304w]

> The ethical is the universal, and as such it is also the divine. So it is right to say that every duty is at bottom a duty to God. But if one can say no more than that, then one is also saying that I really have no duty to God. What makes a duty a duty is that it is traced back to God; yet within the duty itself I stand in no relation to God. Loving one's neighbor, for instance, is a duty. It is a duty because it is traced back to God; but in this duty I enter into a relation not with God but with the neighbor I love. If I then say, in this context, that it is my duty to love God, I am really saying only a tautology — the same thing twice — insofar as "God" is here taken in a wholly abstract sense as the divine, that is, the universal, that is, duty. Then the entire existence of humankind is rounded off in itself as a perfect sphere, and the ethical is at once what bounds it and what fills it. God shrinks to an invisible, vanishing point, a thought without power; whatever power he has lies in the ethical, which fills existence. > The paradox of faith, then, is this: that the single individual is higher than the universal; that the single individual — to recall a distinction from dogmatics now less often heard — lets his relation to the absolute decide his relation to the universal, rather than letting his relation to the universal decide his relation to the absolute. Men som han selv ikke vandt, hvad han vandt, for godt Kjøb, saa sælger han det heller ikke ud for godt Kjøb, han er ikke ussel nok til at tage Menneskers Beundring og til Vederlag give dem sin tause Foragt, han veed, at hvad der i Sandhed er Stort, det er lige tilgængeligt for Alle.

**BASELINE** [282w]

> The knight of faith is referred to himself alone; he feels the pain of being unable to make himself understandable to others, but he feels no vain desire to want to instruct others. The pain is for him the assurance; the vain desire he does not know — for that he is too serious of soul. The inauthentic knight gives himself away easily by this mastery he has acquired in a moment. He does not at all grasp what it is about: that, insofar as another single individual is to walk the same way, he must in exactly the same way become the single individual, and then needs no one's instruction, least of all from one who would force himself upon him. Here, again, one leaps off; one cannot endure the martyrdom of unintelligibility, and instead of this one chooses, conveniently enough, the worldly admiration of mastery. The true knight of faith is a witness, never a teacher, and herein lies the deep humanity, which is worth more than this prattling sympathy for other people's woes and weals, which is honored under the name of sympathy, while it is nothing other than vanity. The one who only wants to be a witness thereby confesses that no person, not even the lowliest, needs another person's sympathy, or shall be debased thereby in order that another may be elevated. But just as he himself did not win what he won on the cheap, so he also does not sell it off cheaply; he is not wretched enough to take the admiration of people and in return give them his silent contempt — he knows that what is truly great is equally accessible to all.

## Footnote to ¶24 (absent from all served editions)

**CANDIDATE**

> * I want to shed light once more on the difference between the collision as it confronts the tragic hero and as it confronts the knight of faith. The tragic hero assures himself that the ethical obligation is wholly present in him by transforming it into a wish. Agamemnon can thus say: "The proof, for me, that I am not violating my duty as a father is that what is my duty is my only wish." So here we have wish and duty facing each other. The happy thing in life is when the two coincide, when my wish is my duty and vice versa, and most people's task in life is precisely to remain in their duty and, by their enthusiasm, to transform it into their wish. The tragic hero gives up his wish in order to fulfill his duty. For the knight of faith, too, wish and duty are identical, but he is required to give up both. So if he wants to resign himself by giving up his wish, he finds no rest; for it is, after all, his duty. If he wants to remain in his duty and in his wish, he does not become the knight of faith; for the absolute duty required precisely that he give it up. The tragic hero received a higher expression of duty, but not an absolute duty.
