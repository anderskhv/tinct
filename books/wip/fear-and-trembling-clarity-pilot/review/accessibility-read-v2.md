## Part 1: Paragraph by paragraph

### Problema I: Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?

- **[0]** Sets out the premise. The ethical applies to everyone at all times and has no purpose beyond itself. The individual's task is to give up what is private about him and live by the shared rule. Holding onto his private self against it is sin, or a "spiritual trial". If this is the whole truth, the ethical is like eternal blessedness: it can never be suspended, because suspending something means keeping it inside a higher purpose, and nothing is higher. *Connection:* the opening thesis, which the rest of the section tests.
- **[1]** *Consequence.* If [0] is right, Hegel is right to treat the merely private individual as a form of evil that must be absorbed into the ethical life of a community. But then Hegel should also condemn Abraham as a murderer instead of honoring him as the father of faith. *Ironic barb.*
- **[2]** *Counter-thesis.* Faith is the paradox that the individual stands higher than the universal, after first having belonged to it. If that is not faith, then either Abraham is lost or faith never existed. And if the ethical is the highest, Greek philosophy already had every concept we need.
- **[3]** *Ironic aside.* The narrator mocks people who say glibly that Christianity is light and paganism is darkness. Paganism did lack faith, but the phrase means nothing unless you know what faith is. Explaining everything without knowing what faith is wins you fools' admiration.
- **[4]** *Restatement and sharpening of [2].* The individual first belongs to the universal and then, through it, comes to stand above it, in an "absolute relation to the absolute". This cannot be mediated, because mediation always works through the universal. It stays a paradox, and the three-way either/or is stated formally.
- **[5]** *Concession.* The paradox can be confused with a spiritual trial, and it repels many people. Neither is a reason to hide it or water it down. People who have faith should give signs for telling the two apart.
- **[6]** *Application to Abraham.* There are no real analogies to Abraham. He acts "by virtue of the absurd". If he explained himself in universal terms, he would have to call his act a trial and repent. He is therefore never a tragic hero: he is either a murderer or a believer. He lacks the "middle term" that saves the tragic hero. The narrator can understand the tragic hero but not Abraham. *Confession.*
- **[7]** *Method.* In ethical terms, a father must love his son more than himself. The narrator will test whether some higher ethical duty could justify Abraham from inside the ethical.
- **[8]** *Example 1* (Agamemnon, not yet named). For the sake of a whole people, a king must sacrifice his daughter. The nation understands and honors him.
- **[9]** *Example 2* (Jephthah). A judge must keep a vow he made for Israel's victory, even though keeping it costs his daughter. All Israel understands him.
- **[10]** *Example 3* (Brutus). When the laws require it, a father must execute his guilty son. The whole nation admires him.
- **[11]** *Counter-test.* Remove the public reason: a fair wind, no vow, an innocent son. The same deeds become incomprehensible, and "it is a test" explains nothing.
- **[12]** *Further counter-test.* Everyone weeps for the heroes. But if they added "it will not happen; we believe this by virtue of the absurd", no one would understand. The absurdity is easy to see; believing it is not.
- **[13]** *Conclusion from the examples.* The tragic hero stays inside the ethical: one ethical duty gives way to a higher ethical duty. So there is no suspension of the ethical as such.
- **[14]** *Contrast.* Abraham leaves the ethical altogether for a telos outside it. His deed serves no nation, state or angry gods; it is purely private. The only ethical claim present is "the father must love the son", and the universal, hidden in Isaac, would cry out against him.
- **[15]** *Why does he do it?* For God's sake and, identically, for his own sake. Hence the word "test/temptation". Here temptation is inverted: the ethical is what tempts him away from God's will. This leaves the puzzle open, since duty is supposed to be God's will.
- **[16]** *Consequence.* A new category is needed. Paganism and the tragic hero have no private relation to God, because for them the ethical is the divine.
- **[17]** *Consequence.* Abraham cannot be mediated, which means he cannot speak. Speaking means using universal terms, and in those terms his situation can only be called a trial.
- **[18]** *Emotional turn and confession.* Abraham inspires admiration and horror. The tragic hero's sacrifice is secure; the one who gives up the universal might simply be wrong, with no rescue. People weep with the tragic hero, who comforts later mourners. No one can weep over Abraham, only feel holy dread. The narrator imagines Abraham going mad or being mistaken, and addresses Shakespeare, who never voiced this torment.
- **[19]** *Objection and reply.* An individual set against the universal is, in principle, in the form of sin. Unless that form can occur without being sin, Abraham is condemned. He had faith, and his only justification is the paradox itself.
- **[20]** *Question.* How does the individual know he is justified? If everything is reduced to the state or society, there is no paradox (the Pythagoras saying). Our age does sometimes offer an answer.
- **[21]** *Satire of that answer.* "Judge by the outcome" is the lecturers' formula. The narrator attacks it: the outcome comes last, and the hero is a hero because he begins, not because of how things turn out.
- **[22]** *Continuation.* The outcome belongs to a different order from the hero's existence. Would Abraham have been less justified if he had actually killed Isaac?
- **[23]** *Satire.* People want the happy ending without the anxiety. That is worse than temple robbery and worse than Judas.
- **[24]** *Digression on how to approach greatness* (the king's castle image). Neither servile distance nor impudent intrusion. What makes the great approachable is seeing the anxiety and distress they went through.
- **[25]** *Example: Mary.* Being "favored" does not make her great; people leave out her distress, anxiety and paradox. The narrator defends the purity of his own thought.
- **[26]** *Continuation.* Mary's pregnancy was a time of anxiety, and no one could understand her. Blessing and curse come together. She is great because she said "Behold, the handmaid of the Lord". She and Abraham are greater than heroes because of their distress, not in spite of it.
- **[27]** *Contrast.* The poet asks us to weep for the hero. The knight of faith says, "Do not weep for me, weep for yourself."
- **[28]** *Example: Christ's contemporaries.* People romanticize them and forget how terrifying it was. Eighteen centuries of outcome make it look easy. The narrator judges no one.
- **[29]** *Return to Abraham.* Before the outcome, he is either a murderer at every moment or the paradox.
- **[30]** *Summary of the section.* There is a teleological suspension, the individual is higher than the universal, and the paradox cannot be mediated. Anyone can become a tragic hero by his own strength, but no one can advise the knight of faith.
- **[31]** *Closing.* Faith is a miracle, yet it is open to everyone, because passion unites all human beings and faith is a passion. The footnote cites Lessing: passions make everyone equal.

### Problema II: Is there an absolute duty to God?

- **[0]** *Thesis to be tested.* If the ethical is the divine, every duty is a duty to God only indirectly. You never relate to God himself; you relate to your neighbor. "Duty to love God" becomes a tautology, and God becomes a powerless vanishing point. Loving God in any other way is suspect, like Rousseau's man who loves distant peoples instead of his neighbor.
- **[1]** *Consequence.* If nothing incommensurable exists, Hegel is right, but then he has no business praising Abraham or faith. Hegel ranks the outer above the inner (child/man example). Faith says the inner is higher.
- **[2]** *Consequence.* Ethically, the individual must express his inner life outwardly; holding back is sin or a trial. Faith is a new inwardness, not the first inwardness of feeling and mood.
- **[3]** *Warning against a confusion.* Philosophy has swapped "faith" for "the immediate" (feeling, mood), which is why it wants to go beyond faith. Real faith comes only after a movement of infinity (Socratic ignorance = infinite resignation). On philosophy's definition, Socrates would already be past faith.
- **[4]** *Restatement.* The individual defines his relation to the universal by his relation to the absolute, not the reverse. This is the same as saying there is an absolute duty to God. The ethical becomes relative but is not abolished: love for God can make love for one's neighbor take a form that looks like the opposite of duty.
- **[5]** *Either/or* (short form): otherwise faith is a trial and Abraham is lost.
- **[6]** *Consequence.* The paradox cannot be mediated. Once the individual tries to express it in universal terms, he sees it as a trial. If he resists the trial, he fails the absolute duty; if he gives in, he sins. Illustration: if Abraham said he loved Isaac, the listener would ask "then why?" or suspect hypocrisy.
- **[7]** *Application to Abraham.* The ethical relation to Isaac becomes relative. His only answer is "a test", which unites God's sake and his own sake. Seen from outside, that looks like extreme egoism or extreme devotion, with no middle term. No one can understand him, and no knight of faith can help another. Each must work out for himself what his own "Isaac" is. *Confession:* the narrator lacks the courage.
- **[8]** *Scriptural example.* Luke 14:26, "hate father and mother". The exegetes soften "hate" to "love less". The next verse (the tower builder counting the cost) suggests the word is meant to be taken as terribly as possible.
- **[9]** *Consequence of the softening.* A teaching that sounds terrible but only means "be a bit less attentive" is not worth defending.
- **[10]** *Concession.* The words can be understood without having the courage to act on them. Honesty requires admitting this; calling the lack of courage humility is really pride.
- **[11]** *Argument by analogy.* God demands absolute love. A human lover who wanted proof of love by making the beloved cold toward everyone else would be selfish and stupid. The husband example.
- **[12]** *Continuation.* A wife who loves her family well is a guarantee that she will love her husband well.
- **[13]–[14]** *Conclusion,* one sentence split across two slots: what would be selfish and stupid in a human being, the exegete asks us to accept as a worthy idea of God.
- **[15]** *How, then, "hate"?* As a paradox. Absolute duty may lead to what ethics forbids, but never to ceasing to love. Abraham must love Isaac even more; only that makes it a sacrifice. Ethically, the act is hatred and murder.
- **[16]** *Clarification.* The knight of faith has no higher universal to shelter in. If the Church demanded the sacrifice, the man would be a tragic hero, because the Church is like the state.
- **[17]** *Continuation.* Such an "ecclesiastical hero" is understood by everyone. He does not say "it is a test".
- **[18]** *Aside on why people avoid such passages.* They fear letting individuals loose, and they think being an individual is easy. The narrator: being an individual is the most terrible and therefore the greatest thing. Speak of it with fear and trembling so that it helps rather than traps.
- **[19]** *Distress of faith.* The tragic hero gives himself up for the universal; the knight gives up the universal to become the individual. The knight knows how good it is to be understood (the "edition of himself" image). He also knows the lonely path above, where he seems mad or a hypocrite.
- **[20]** *Abraham humanly told.* He might wish for an understandable task. He waited seventy years for a son, then was tested again. He accomplishes nothing for the universal. The paragraph ends by introducing the Cunctator.
- **[21]** *Continuation.* Abraham is a greater delayer than the Cunctator, but he saves no state. His contemporaries would call him mad, and his life is sealed by God.
- **[22]** *Consequence.* This is the terrible part. The tragic hero dances; the knight crawls. The knight's reward is to be God's intimate friend, who says "you" to God.
- **[23]** *Contrast.* The tragic hero is finished quickly and rests in the universal. The knight is tested constantly, and returning to the universal could be either temptation or truth. No human being can tell him which.
- **[24]** *The knight's passion.* He must gather the whole ethical claim he is breaking into one moment, to be sure he still loves Isaac, and must be able to renew that at once. The tragic hero has the universal's support for this; the knight has only himself. (A footnote on wish and duty belongs here.)
- **[25]** *Example.* Agamemnon must have this concentration and not hope vaguely for a way out. Abraham has it too, and then makes one more movement back toward the miracle.
- **[26]** *The marks asked for in P1 [5].* The true knight is isolated; the false one is sectarian. The sectarian wants tragic-hero comfort without paying for it, and uses noise to drown out anxiety.
- **[27]** *Continuation.* The true knight is a witness, not a teacher. He does not guide others, does not accept admiration, and knows that greatness is equally open to all.
- **[28]** *Final either/or* (four branches).
- **Footnote.** The tragic hero turns duty into his wish and then gives up the wish for a higher duty. For the knight, wish and duty are identical and he must give up both, so resignation gives him no rest.

## Part 2: Reconstructed arguments

### Problema I

1. The ethical is the universal. It holds for everyone at every moment and has no telos outside itself.
2. The individual's task is to give up his particularity and express the universal. Asserting particularity against it is sin or spiritual trial, curable by repentance.
3. If step 1 is the highest truth, the ethical can no more be teleologically suspended than eternal blessedness can.
4. On that view Hegel is consistent in condemning mere individuality, but then Abraham should be condemned as a murderer and not praised as the father of faith.
5. Faith claims the opposite: the individual, having first been inside the universal, comes to stand above it in an absolute relation to the absolute (God).
6. That position cannot be mediated, that is, explained through the universal, because all explanation uses the universal. So it is a paradox and the absurd.
7. The tragic heroes (Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus) suspend one ethical duty (to their child) for a higher ethical duty (to people, vow or state). They stay inside the ethical, are understood and are wept for.
8. Take away the higher ethical purpose, or add "it will not happen" (faith in the absurd), and they become incomprehensible.
9. Abraham serves no higher ethical purpose. His act is private, for God's sake and his own. It is a "test/temptation" in which the ethical itself is the temptation.
10. So Abraham cannot speak or be understood, and he has no middle term (see step 7).
11. Judging him by the happy outcome is illegitimate. He would have been no less justified if he had killed Isaac, and greatness lies in the beginning and the anxiety, as with Mary and Christ's contemporaries.
12. **Either/or conclusion:** either there is a teleological suspension of the ethical (the individual is higher than the universal: the paradox of faith), or Abraham is a murderer, not even a tragic hero (and there has never been faith / Abraham is lost).

### Problema II

1. If the ethical is the divine, every duty is referred to God, but I relate only to my neighbor, never to God. "Duty to God" is a tautology, and God is a vanishing point.
2. On that view nothing incommensurable exists; Hegel's ranking of outer over inner holds, and the individual must externalize everything.
3. Faith claims there is a new inwardness, higher than and incommensurable with the outward. This is not immediate feeling; it comes only after infinite resignation.
4. That is the same as saying there is an absolute duty to God: the individual sets his relation to the universal by his relation to God, not the reverse.
5. The ethical is then relativized, not abolished. Love of God can make love of neighbor take a form ethics calls its opposite ("hate").
6. The paradox cannot be mediated. In universal terms it is a trial, and resisting or yielding both fail. With no middle term, the act looks either like extreme egoism or like absolute devotion.
7. Luke 14:26 must be read literally, and the softening reading makes Christianity trivial. But "hate" must be understood paradoxically: Abraham must love Isaac more for the act to be a sacrifice. Ethically it remains murder.
8. A sacrifice demanded by the Church would make a tragic hero, not a knight of faith, because the Church is another universal.
9. The knight is alone. He cannot be understood, helped or guided. He must repeatedly concentrate the whole ethical claim into one moment and renew the movement toward the miracle. No human being can tell him whether he is in trial or in truth.
10. Marks: the false knight is sectarian and a teacher who seeks company and admiration; the true knight is isolated and a witness.
11. **Either/or conclusion:** either there is an absolute duty to God (the paradox: the individual as individual higher than the universal, in absolute relation to the absolute), or there has never been faith because it has always existed, or Abraham is lost, or Luke 14 must be explained away as the "tasteful exegete" does.

### Terms as these texts use them

| Term | Meaning as used here |
|---|---|
| **The ethical** | The binding moral order that applies to everyone at all times; its own end; "the divine" in P2 [0]. |
| **The universal** | Used interchangeably with the ethical: what is shared, expressible and understandable by all. In P1 [16] and P2 [16] the state, the people and the Church are forms of it. |
| **The single individual** | A person as this one person. Ethically, he is someone who should dissolve himself into the universal. In faith, he is the one who stands above it. The text uses the same phrase for both, and the difference is marked only by "as the single individual" and the "repeated movement". |
| **Particularity** | Whatever sets a person apart as this one person; ethically, to be cancelled (P1 [0]). Also "the particular" = the incommensurable = evil on the ethical view (P1 [2]). |
| **Telos** | End or purpose (glossed in P1 [0]). |
| **Teleological suspension** | Setting something aside for a higher purpose, where it is preserved inside that purpose and not lost (P1 [0], last sentence). "Teleological suspension of the ethical" = setting aside the whole ethical for a telos outside it (P1 [14]). |
| **Ethical life** | The morality lived in a people's shared customs and institutions (glossed P1 [1]). The tragic hero's sphere. The distinction from "the ethical" is not stable (see flags). |
| **Mediation** | Bringing something under the universal, explaining or justifying it in shared terms (glossed P1 [4]). "Cannot be mediated" = cannot be explained to anyone. |
| **The paradox** | The individual as individual is higher than the universal and stands in an absolute relation to the absolute. Cannot be thought or explained (P1 [4], P2 [4]). |
| **The absurd** | Glossed in P1 [6] as the paradox itself; in P1 [12] it is believing "it will not happen" and expecting Isaac back. |
| **Spiritual trial** | The urge to assert oneself as an individual against the universal, seen as an inner assault (P1 [0]). It is what faith looks like from the ethical side (P1 [5], [17]). In P2 [20] and [23], the trial runs the other way: wishing to return to the universal. |
| **Test / temptation / being put to the proof** | The one word used for Abraham's situation: God's proof of his faith. It unites "for God's sake" and "for his own sake" (P1 [15], P2 [7]). "Temptation" is inverted, because the ethical is what tempts. "Put to the proof" is the same notion, emphasizing examination. The text treats all three as one word. |
| **Absolute duty (to God)** | A duty to God directly, not through the ethical. Equivalent to the paradox (P2 [4]). |
| **Inwardness** | The inner life. The "first" inwardness is feeling or mood, which ethics requires to be expressed outwardly. Faith is a "new" inwardness that cannot be expressed outwardly (P2 [1]–[2]). |
| **Middle term** | The universal: the shared higher purpose that makes the tragic hero's act understandable (P1 [6]; identified only in P2 [7]). |
| **Tragic hero vs knight of faith** | The tragic hero sacrifices one ethical tie for a higher ethical purpose, stays in the universal, is understood and wept for, can speak, finishes and rests, and can be reached by his own strength. The knight of faith leaves the universal for a private absolute relation to God, cannot speak or be understood or advised, is never finished, and is a witness, not a teacher. |

## Part 3: Comprehension failures

### SEVERE

1. **P1 [2] (repeated in P1 [4], P2 [28]): "faith has never existed in the world — precisely because it has always existed."**
   On first meeting in Problema I, I could not reconstruct this branch of the either/or at all. Nothing in P1 says why "always existed" would imply "never existed". I understood it only in P2 [3] ("it is ridiculous to deny that faith has existed in every age" if faith = the immediate). I also brought outside knowledge: if faith is ordinary feeling, everyone has it, so real faith never existed. **Obstacle: wording/order.** The explanation comes one whole section after the formula is used, and P1 [2] and [4] both lean on it. It is a paradoxical formula, but it is not the paradox the text says is ungraspable. **[outside knowledge]**

2. **P2 [1]: "The child is das Innere, the inner; the man is das Äussere, the outer. That is why the child is determined precisely by what is outer — and why, conversely, the man, being das Äussere, is determined precisely by das Innere."**
   The example is supposed to illustrate "the outer is higher than the inner". Instead it reads as a contradiction: the inner one is ruled by the outer, and the outer one by the inner. I could not tell what it proves. The argument survives only because the next sentence ("faith is ... inwardness is higher than outwardness") is clear on its own. **Obstacle: the wording/example itself.** The glosses translate the German words but do not make the "That is why" follow. I relied on vague outside knowledge of Hegel to guess that it means potential vs. actualized. **[outside knowledge]**

### MODERATE

3. **P1 [0]: "For it would be a contradiction if eternal blessedness could be given up (that is, teleologically suspended). "Through the universal" is unexplained. "The absolute" is never said to mean God; I assumed it. **Mixed:** the wording piles up the phrase "the single individual" four times, and the idea is declared unthinkable. **[outside knowledge]** for absolute = God.

16. **P1 [24]: "at once wanting to exalt it and to cancel it by exalting it in a servile way"** and **"what will help him is precisely the anxiety and distress in which the great are put to the proof; for otherwise ... the great would only arouse his justified envy."** I had to reread both. 17. **P1 [25]: "My thought is as pure as anyone's, and whoever can think such things will surely find his thought becoming pure".** "Such things" has no clear referent (the young girl's question? Mary's anxiety?). Why the narrator suddenly defends his purity is unexplained. **Wording (referent).**

18. **P2 [1]: "for by the other he has pronounced judgment on both Abraham and faith."** "The other" presumably means the first half (that nothing is incommensurable), but it reads like a missing antecedent. 19. **P2 [3]: "then Socrates already went further, much further — instead of the reverse, that he did not reach it."** The final clause is hard to parse. "His ignorance is infinite resignation" assumes you know Socratic ignorance. **Wording**, plus **[outside knowledge]**.

20. **P2 [6]: "And then, if he does resist the spiritual trial, he will not come to fulfill the so-called absolute duty; and if he does not resist it, he sins, even if in reality his deed turns out to be what was his absolute duty."** Which is the trial here, the absolute duty or the urge to explain? The double bind depends on the scope of "As soon as ... he wants to express his absolute duty in the universal". I reread three times. **Mixed:** wording (scope of the condition) and idea.

21. **P2 [7]: "These two determinations also match each other in ordinary speech: ... 'He hardly did that for God's sake,' ... we mean that he did it for his own sake."** "Match" suggests agreement, but the example shows ordinary speech treating them as *opposites*. **Wording.**

22. **P2 [7]: "The single individual can only ever give himself any more precise explanation of what is to be understood by 'Isaac.'"** "Isaac" in quotation marks, as a stand-in for whatever each person's Isaac is, appears without warning, and the sentence is awkwardly built ("can only ever ... any more precise"). The parenthesis helps afterward. **Wording.**

23. **P2 [23]: "at every moment there is the possibility of turning back in repentance to the universal — and this possibility can just as well be a spiritual trial as the truth."** Here the trial is the pull *back to* the universal, the reverse of the P1 [0] definition (the pull *away* from it). This is coherent on reflection (P2 [20] prepares it: "such wishes are only spiritual trials"), but the shift in what "spiritual trial" points at is never marked. **Mostly idea**, with some wording.

24. **P2 [24]: "the passion to concentrate into a single moment the whole of the ethical that he is breaking, so that he can assure himself that he really loves Isaac".** Why concentrating the ethical proves love was unclear until the footnote (wish/duty). **Wording (order:** the key is in a footnote at the end of the page). ### Overall

Both sections are followable at the level of their main arguments. The contrast between the tragic hero and the knight of faith (P1 [7]–[17], P2 [15]–[27]) and both either/or conclusions came through clearly. P2 [4] is the single clearest statement of the thesis. The failures cluster in three places:

- The Hegel-facing paragraphs (P1 [0]–[2], P2 [1]–[3]).
- Undefined terms used before they are glossed: the middle term, the absolute, dialectic, and "always existed".
- The long rhetorical paragraphs (P1 [18], [24]–[25]), where referents drift.

Most of these are wording obstacles. The places where the difficulty is the idea itself (the paradox is unthinkable, and whether the pull toward the universal is trial or truth) are signposted by the text as such, and did not feel like reading failures.
