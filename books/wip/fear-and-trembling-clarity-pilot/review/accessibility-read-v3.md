## Problema I — Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?

### 1. Paragraph by paragraph

- **[0]** Sets up the view to be tested. The ethical is "the universal": it binds everyone, always, and has no purpose (telos) outside itself; everything else aims at it. The individual's task is to give up what is merely his own ("particularity") and live out the universal; standing apart is sin, and the pull to stand apart is a "spiritual trial". If this is the whole story, the ethical works like eternal blessedness: it can never be "suspended", because to suspend something is to keep it inside a higher purpose, and there would be nothing higher. The conclusion ("so the ethical could never be teleologically suspended") is left for the reader to draw.
- **[1]** If [0] is right, Hegel is consistent to treat the individual who stands apart as a "moral form of evil" that must be absorbed into "ethical life" (the shared customs of a people). His mistake is to praise Abraham as the father of faith. On his own terms he should be condemning Abraham as a murderer.
- **[2]** Faith is the paradox that the individual is *higher* than the universal. This comes *after* he has been inside the universal ("the movement is repeated"). If faith is not this, Abraham is lost and "there has never been faith… precisely because there has always been faith". If the ethical is the highest thing, we need nothing beyond Greek philosophy, and Hegel should have said so.
- **[3]** A digression mocking people who say "light over Christendom, darkness over paganism" without saying what faith is. Saying paganism lacked faith only means something if you define faith. This supports [2]: if faith is just ethics, the Greeks already had everything.
- **[4]** Restates the paradox more exactly. The individual first submits to the universal and then, *through* it, comes to stand above it, in an "absolute relation to the absolute". Mediation always works *through* the universal, so this cannot be mediated. It stays a paradox thought cannot reach. Then comes the three-way either/or that runs through both sections.
- **[5]** Concession: the paradox is easily confused with spiritual trial and it repels many people. But you should not water faith down to make it comfortable. Believers should supply marks that distinguish the two.
- **[6]** Applies this to Abraham: his story is a teleological suspension of the ethical. The analogies that scholars find are false. Abraham acts "by virtue of the absurd", meaning he as individual is higher than the universal. If he tried to justify himself in ordinary terms he would have to call it a spiritual trial and stop, or repent. So he is not a tragic hero. He is either a murderer or a believer. The tragic hero has a "middle term" and Abraham lacks it. The narrator can understand the hero but not Abraham.
- **[7]** Sets up the test. Ethically, a father must love his son. Could some *higher ethical* duty explain Abraham, keeping him inside ethics?
- **[8]** First tragic-hero case: a father (Agamemnon, not named until [11]) sacrifices his daughter so a whole people's expedition can go on. He is painful but admired, and the nation shares his deed.
- **[9]** Second case: Jephthah keeps a vow on which the people's victory depended, at the cost of his daughter. Everyone understands him.
- **[10]** Third case: Brutus, as judge, has his guilty son executed because the state's law requires it. Everyone admires him.
- **[11]** Take away the public reason (no windless calm, no vow, an innocent son) and nobody would understand these men. Saying "it is a test" would not help.
- **[12]** Add to the heroes' pain the words "But it will not happen" (i.e. the loved one will be kept), justified "by virtue of the absurd", and they become even less understandable. That is Abraham's position.
- **[13]** The difference: the tragic hero stays inside ethics. He puts a lower ethical duty (to a child) under a higher ethical duty (to the state or people). That is not a suspension of the ethical itself.
- **[14]** Abraham steps outside ethics altogether for a telos beyond it. His deed serves no people, state or public purpose; it is "purely private". Within his story the highest ethical fact is "the father must love the son". The universal is present only in Isaac, who stands for the future and would cry "don't".
- **[15]** Why, then, does Abraham do it? For God's sake (God wants proof of faith) and for his own (to give that proof). This double reason is what "test/temptation" means. Normally temptation pulls you away from duty. Here the ethical itself is the temptation, pulling him away from God's will.
- **[16]** So a new category is needed. The tragic hero has no private relation to God, since for him "the ethical is the divine". His paradox can therefore be resolved in the universal.
- **[17]** Abraham cannot be mediated, so he cannot *speak*. Speech is universal. The only universal words for his situation would be "I was in a spiritual trial".
- **[18]** The emotional core. The narrator admires Abraham and is horrified by him. The hero's sacrifice is on firm ground and draws tears that comfort sufferers. Abraham's might be a delusion with no rescue if he is wrong, and nobody can weep for him, only fear. There is an apostrophe to Shakespeare, who could put every torment into words except this one.
- **[19]** Formally, the individual standing against the universal is the shape of sin. Unless that shape can be "repeated" without sin, Abraham is condemned. He existed by faith, the paradox of an absolute relation to the absolute. His justification is itself paradoxical: it rests on his being the single individual, not on anything universal.
- **[20]** How can the individual know he is justified? If you fold everything into state and society you never reach the paradox. Some people today do give a quasi-answer.
- **[21]** That answer is "judge by the outcome." The narrator mocks the comfortable "lecturers" who sit in judgment on great men by results. Greatness must be learned from its *beginning*: the hero was a hero because he began, not because it came out well.
- **[22]** An outcome is a finite answer and a different kind of thing from the hero's existence. Would Abraham be less justified if he had actually killed Isaac?
- **[23]** People want the happy ending, like the end of a book, and skip the anxiety and paradox. Doing that robs the holy.
- **[24]** Greatness should be approached humanly: neither kept at an awed distance nor stormed without manners. Its anxiety and distress are what let an ordinary person come near it.
- **[25]** Mary as a parallel. What made her great is not simply being favored. People leave out her anxiety, distress and paradox.
- **[26]** Mary could not be understood by anyone. The angel explained nothing to others. God's blessing was also a curse. Her greatness lies in accepting through the distress, as with Abraham. Neither is a "hero".
- **[27]** A poet says of his hero "weep for him". The knight of faith says "don't weep for me, weep for yourself".
- **[28]** People who long to have seen Christ forget how terrifying and uncertain that would have been. Eighteen centuries of "outcome" make it look easy.
- **[29]** Back to Abraham. Before the outcome he is either a murderer at every moment or he stands in the paradox.
- **[30]** Conclusion: there is a teleological suspension of the ethical in Abraham's story, or he is a murderer and not even a tragic hero. You can become a tragic hero by your own strength, but not a knight of faith. Nobody can advise the knight of faith.
- **[31]** Coda: faith is a miracle yet open to all, because it is a passion and passion unites all people. The footnote shows through Lessing that passions make all people equal.

### 2. Argument chain

1. Suppose the ethical is the universal: binding on all, always, with no purpose beyond itself.
2. Then the individual's task is to give up his particularity and express the universal. Standing apart is sin, and the pull toward it is a spiritual trial.
3. On that view the ethical can never be "teleologically suspended", because there is nothing higher in which it could be preserved.
4. Hegel holds this view consistently, but he still praises Abraham, which is inconsistent.
5. Faith claims the opposite: the individual, after passing through the universal, stands above it in an absolute relation to God. This cannot be mediated (explained through the universal).
6. Test case: can Abraham be explained by a higher *ethical* duty, like the tragic heroes Agamemnon, Jephthah and Brutus?
7. No. The heroes sacrifice a private duty to a public one. Abraham's act serves no public good, is purely private, and is done for God's sake and his own ("a test").
8. So Abraham cannot speak or justify himself. Universal language would call his state a spiritual trial.
9. "Judge by the outcome" is not an escape: the outcome comes after, and greatness lies in the beginning, the anxiety and the paradox.
10. **Either/or:** either there is a teleological suspension of the ethical (faith is the paradox that the individual is higher than the universal), or Abraham is a murderer, faith has never existed ("because there has always been faith"), and Abraham is lost.

## Problema II — Is there an absolute duty to God?

### 1. Paragraph by paragraph

- **[0]** If the ethical is the universal and also the divine, then every duty is "a duty to God" only in the sense that God is its ground. In the duty itself I relate to my neighbor, not to God. "Duty to love God" becomes a tautology. God shrinks to an idle point, and loving God in any other way is suspect, like loving distant peoples instead of your neighbor.
- **[1]** If nothing in human life escapes common measure, Hegel is right, except when he talks about faith and Abraham. Hegel ranks the outer (self-expression) above the inner. Faith says the reverse: inwardness is higher (the "odd number" callback to Problema I [20]). The child/man example is the obscure part.
- **[2]** Ethically, the individual must express his inner life outwardly. Holding back in feeling or mood is sin or spiritual trial. Faith's inwardness is not that first, merely emotional inwardness but a *new* one that cannot be measured by the outward.
- **[3]** Modern philosophy has swapped "faith" for "the immediate" (raw feeling). That is why it can say faith is everywhere and must be got beyond. Real faith comes only *after* a movement of infinity (infinite resignation), unexpectedly, by the absurd. Socrates made that movement intellectually. This retroactively explains "never been faith because there has always been faith".
- **[4]** Restates the paradox: the individual's relation to God decides his relation to the universal, not the other way round. Put another way, there is an absolute duty to God. If so, "duty to love God" now means something new. The ethical becomes relative, not abolished, and love of God can make love of neighbor take a form ethics would call the opposite of duty.
- **[5]** Otherwise faith is a spiritual trial and Abraham, who yielded to it, is lost.
- **[6]** Cannot be mediated. Once he tries to express it universally he must see it as spiritual trial. If he resists, he fails the absolute duty; if he does not, he sins by universal standards. If Abraham explained his love for Isaac, the listener would ask "then why sacrifice him?"
- **[7]** In Abraham, the love of father for son is made relative to the relation with God. His only answer is "a test". Faith has lost the middle term (the universal) and so looks like either total egoism or total devotion. It cannot be communicated, even to another knight of faith. Each person must take on the paradox alone, and what counts as one's "Isaac" cannot be defined by others.
- **[8]** Luke 14:26, the command to hate father, mother and so on, is a scripture test case. Scholars soften "hate" to "love less", but the next verse (counting the cost of a tower) shows the words are meant at full strength.
- **[9]** The softened reading makes Christianity pitiful: a terrible saying that turns out to mean "be a bit less attentive".
- **[10]** You can understand the words without having the courage to do them. Admit it honestly, and don't call your cowardice humility.
- **[11]–[14]** Why the softened reading fails: a lover who wants his beloved to become lukewarm to everyone else is egoistic and stupid. A husband should be glad his wife loves her family well. So what would be stupid in a human is being attributed to God.
- **[15]** So how is one to "hate"? As a paradox. Absolute duty can make you *do* what ethics forbids but never make you *stop loving*. Abraham must love Isaac even more when God asks for him, since that love is what makes it a sacrifice. Outwardly, in the universal, he remains a murderer.
- **[16]** The knight has no higher *universal* to shelter in. If the Church demanded the sacrifice, the man would only be a tragic hero, since a Church reached by mediation is just another state.
- **[17]** Inside the paradox, the Church is no help. The "ecclesiastical hero" is understood by all and does not say "it is a test".
- **[18]** People avoid quoting Luke because they fear individuals running wild, and they think being an individual is easy. The narrator shares neither view: being the single individual is the hardest and strictest thing, and one should speak of it with fear and trembling, not suppress it.
- **[19]** The anxiety of faith. The hero gives himself up for the universal; the knight gives up the universal to be an individual. The knight *knows* how good it is to belong to the universal and be understood. His path is lonely and higher. Humanly he looks mad or a hypocrite.
- **[20]–[21]** Abraham could have wished for an ordinary, understandable task. Such wishes are themselves spiritual trials for him. His life of waiting and testing achieved nothing for the universal and could not be explained. It is "a sealed book".
- **[22]** This is the terror, and also the glory: the knight becomes God's intimate, addressing him as "you".
- **[23]** The hero finishes quickly and rests in the universal. The knight is tested constantly and can never be sure whether turning back would be temptation or truth.
- **[24]** The knight must gather the whole ethical claim he is breaking into one moment, to be sure he truly loves Isaac, and must be able to renew that at every instant. The hero does this too but with support. The knight is alone. (Footnote: for the hero, duty and wish coincide and he gives up the wish; the knight must give up both.)
- **[25]** Agamemnon makes the resigning movement and rests in the universal. Abraham makes that movement *and then* a further one, fixing his soul on the miracle. Without it he would only be an Agamemnon, and an unjustifiable one.
- **[26]** Only the individual can tell faith from spiritual trial, but there are outward marks. The true knight is absolutely alone; the false one forms a sect, a cheap imitation of the universal that drowns out anxiety with noise.
- **[27]** The true knight suffers from being misunderstood but does not try to lead others. He is a witness, never a teacher. The false knight quickly sets himself up as a master.
- **[28]** Final either/or.

### 2. Argument chain

1. If the ethical is the universal and the divine, every duty is grounded in God, but in each duty I relate to people, not to God.
2. Then "duty to God" is empty: God becomes a vanishing point and love of God beyond ethics is suspect.
3. Hegel (outer above inner) is right if nothing in a person is incommensurable. Faith claims an inwardness higher than outwardness.
4. That inwardness is not raw feeling ("the immediate"). It is a new inwardness that comes after infinite resignation, by the absurd.
5. So the paradox of faith can be put as: there is an absolute duty to God. The individual's relation to God determines his relation to the universal, which makes the ethical relative, not abolished.
6. This cannot be mediated or communicated, even to another knight. Outwardly it looks like egoism.
7. Luke 14:26 read at full strength confirms an absolute duty. Softening it makes God an egoistic fool.
8. The "hate" is paradoxical: one does what ethics forbids while loving more. Outwardly one is a murderer.
9. No higher universal (Church or state) can shelter the knight. If it could, he would be a tragic hero.
10. The knight lives in isolation, constant testing and anxiety. The false knight is recognizable by sect-forming and teaching.
11. **Either/or:** either there is an absolute duty to God (the paradox that the individual as individual stands above the universal in absolute relation to the absolute), or there has never been faith because there has always been faith, or Abraham is lost, or Luke 14 must be softened as the "tasteful exegete" does.

## Key terms as this text uses them

- **The ethical:** the moral order that binds everyone at all times, with no purpose beyond itself. Treated as identical with the universal and, in II [0], with the divine.
- **The universal:** what holds for all and can be said to all. Also the medium of speech, understanding and justification (I [17]).
- **The single individual:** the person as this one person. Ethically his task is to dissolve into the universal. In faith he stands above it.
- **Particularity:** "whatever sets him apart as this one person" (glossed in I [0]). Ethically it is to be cancelled.
- **Telos:** the end or purpose something exists for (glossed in I [0]).
- **Teleological suspension:** setting something aside for a higher purpose in which it is still preserved, not destroyed (I [0], final sentence). Here: the ethical set aside for the God-relation.
- **Ethical life:** "the ethics lived out in a people's shared customs and institutions" (I [1]). State, people, family.
- **Mediation / to mediate:** to explain or justify something by bringing it under the universal (glossed in I [4]). Also, from I [17], equivalent to being able to *speak* it.
- **The paradox:** that the individual, after being in the universal, is higher than it and relates absolutely to the absolute (God). Thought cannot resolve it.
- **The absurd:** the paradox as the basis of action. In practice, believing Isaac will be kept ("But it will not happen") while giving him up (I [6], [12]).
- **Spiritual trial:** "an inner assault". The urge to stand apart from the universal, which from the ethical side looks exactly like faith. Also, for the knight, the pull back toward the ethical (II [20], [23]).
- **Test / temptation / being put to the proof:** Abraham's only account of his act. It unites "for God's sake" and "for my own sake" (I [15]). Here the ethical itself is what tempts.
- **Absolute duty (to God):** a duty in which the individual relates directly to God, outranking and relativizing ethical duty (II [4]).
- **Inwardness:** the inner life. The ethical demands that it be expressed outwardly. Faith's "new inwardness" cannot be (II [2]).
- **Middle term:** the universal, which links an individual's act to others and justifies it. The tragic hero has it; Abraham does not. Glossed only in II [7].
- **Tragic hero vs knight of faith:** the hero sacrifices a lower ethical duty to a higher one, stays within the universal, is understood and wept for, and finishes. The knight suspends the ethical for God, is alone, cannot speak, cannot be wept for, and is continually tested.

## 3. Comprehension failures

### SEVERE

1. **"there has never been faith in the world — precisely because there has always been faith"** (I [2]; repeated I [4]; II [28]). In Problema I this is a knot the text never untangles. It is also a limb of the section's closing either/or. A newcomer cannot tell what "because" means here. It only becomes decodable in II [3] ("Once that is done, it would be absurd to deny that faith has been present in every age"), a whole section later. *Obstacle:* the idea is compressed. The wording is clear but gives no handle. In Problema I it is unrecoverable from the text alone. I knew the intended sense from outside knowledge **[OUTSIDE]**.

### MODERATE

2. **I [0] closing logic:** "For it would be a contradiction if eternal blessedness could be given up (that is, teleologically suspended). For the moment eternal blessedness was suspended, it would be forfeited — whereas whatever is teleologically suspended is not forfeited…" The gloss equates "given up" with "teleologically suspended", and the next sentence then says suspension is *not* forfeiting. On a first read this looks self-contradictory. The conclusion it drives at ("so on this view the ethical could never be suspended") is left unstated. Also "It rests immanently in itself" ("immanently" is unglossed) and "once the ethical has taken all of that into itself, it goes no further". *Obstacle:* wording plus a dense idea.
3. **"the movement is repeated"** (I [2]) and **"this form … can be repeated in such a way that it is not sin"** (I [19]). Which movement, and what "repeated" means, is only implied by "Having first been in the universal…". A reader can guess a two-stage path but not why "repetition" is the right word. *Obstacle:* wording, with an idea behind it.
4. **Hegel material in I [1]:** "at the stage of 'the Good and Conscience,' he defines a human being only as the single individual… a 'moral form of evil'… cancelled in the teleology of ethical life." For someone who knows no Hegel, "defines a human being only as the single individual" is puzzling: it sounds like praise of the individual, which is the opposite of what is meant. The gist ("Hegel says the individual standing alone must be absorbed into social ethics") comes through only by working backward from "cancelled". *Obstacle:* the idea (an unknown source). I relied on outside knowledge of Hegel **[OUTSIDE]**.
5. **"through the universal, becomes the single individual who as the single individual stands above it"** and **"an absolute relation to the absolute"** (I [4], I [19], II [4], II [28]). "Through the universal" is unexplained. "The absolute" is never said to mean God, though context makes that likely. *Obstacle:* wording.
6. **"except a later one, which proves nothing if it stands firm that Abraham represents faith"** (I [6]). What is the "later one"? A newcomer cannot identify it. *Obstacle:* the idea (an unexplained allusion).
7. **"The middle term that saves the tragic hero is something Abraham does not have"** (I [6]). "Middle term" is not glossed anywhere in Problema I. It is only identified as "the universal" in II [7]. In I it can be inferred loosely from [13]–[14]. *Obstacle:* wording.
8. **Unnamed tragic heroes, I [8]–[9]:** the Agamemnon paragraph never names him and cites "(line 687)" with no work named. Jephthah's vow ("binds God and himself, in one breath, by the same vow") assumes the reader knows its content. Both become clear only at [11], or through outside knowledge **[OUTSIDE]**. *Obstacle:* the idea (assumed knowledge of the stories).
9. **"He reduces the ethical relation between father and son… to a feeling whose dialectic lies in its relation to the idea of ethical life"** (I [13]). "Dialectic" is unglossed and the clause is opaque. The paragraph's point survives from the neighboring sentences. *Obstacle:* wording. The same issue appears in I [22]: "the outcome (insofar as it is finitude's answer to the infinite question) is, in its dialectic, entirely different in kind".
10. **"The unity of these two is expressed quite correctly in the word… it is a test, a temptation"** (I [15]; II [7]). In English these are two different words, so the claim that *one word* unites "for God's sake" and "for his own sake" does not land. The reader cannot see why "temptation" expresses that unity. *Obstacle:* wording (English lacks a single equivalent).
11. **I [18] syntax:** "Thanks, and thanks again, to the man who holds out to someone whom life's sorrows have assaulted and left naked the expression, the fig leaf of the word, to hide his wretchedness!" This is a garden-path sentence: the object "the expression" arrives after a long relative clause, and I had to reread it. *Obstacle:* wording.
12. **I [24]–[25] thread:** "at once wanting to exalt it and to cancel it by exalting it in a servile way"; "always more than a charwoman; anyone unwilling to be more than that will never be let in"; "if he has any backbone at all, the great would only arouse his justified envy"; "My thought is as pure as anyone's, and whoever can think such things will surely find his thought becoming pure; and if it does not, he has something terrible to expect." The general point (approach greatness humanly, through its anxiety) survives. These individual sentences do not parse into clear claims, especially the "pure thought" sentence, whose reference ("such things") is unclear. *Obstacle:* mostly wording.
13. **II [1] child/man example:** "The child is das Innere, the inner; the man is das Äussere, the outer. That is why the child is determined precisely by what is outer — and why, conversely, the man, being das Äussere, is determined precisely by das Innere." The chiasmus is baffling to a newcomer, and the German doubles the load. The main claim (Hegel ranks outer above inner; faith reverses this) survives because it is stated plainly after. Also "whatever incommensurable there is exists only by accident, from which nothing follows insofar as existence is viewed in terms of the idea" is very dense. *Obstacle:* the idea (Hegelian background), plus the extra German.
14. **"strip himself of the determination of inwardness"** (II [2]). "Determination" is technical and unglossed. *Obstacle:* wording.
15. **"Any closer explanation of what is to be understood by 'Isaac'…"** (II [7]). The text never says that "Isaac" here stands for whatever a given individual is asked to give up. A reader can infer it, but the shift from the person Isaac to a placeholder "Isaac" is abrupt. *Obstacle:* the idea (compressed).
16. **"And whoever demanded such a love would at the same moment be signing his own death sentence, insofar as his life lay in the love he desired"** (II [11]). Opaque: whose life, and why a death sentence? *Obstacle:* wording and idea.
17. **Split paragraphs II [12]–[14]:** "So: what one would regard in a human being as a sign of egoism and stupidity —" / "that, with the help of an exegete, one is supposed to regard as a worthy conception of the deity." One sentence is broken across two numbered slots, and [12] is a one-sentence fragment. In a reader view this looks like a rendering error. *Obstacle:* presentation (an alignment artifact).
18. **"This is the content of 130 years"** (II [21]) right after "He spends seventy years getting a son" (II [20]). The two numbers do not match and neither is explained, so the reader is left wondering which is right. *Obstacle:* the idea (unexplained biblical arithmetic) **[OUTSIDE needed to reconcile]**.
19. **"the passion to concentrate into a single moment the whole of the ethical that he is breaking, so as to make certain that he truly loves Isaac"** (II [24]). Why concentrating the broken ethical proves love is hard to see until the footnote. It is recoverable, but only with the footnote. *Obstacle:* the idea.
20. **"his words hardly become a snare for someone who has gone astray"** (II [18]). "Hardly" reads as "barely/scarcely" and confuses the sense ("do not become"). *Obstacle:* wording.

## 4. Wording obstacles noted (no explanations proposed)

The recurring wording obstacles are unglossed technical words: "immanently", "dialectic", "determination", "middle term" (in I), "the absolute" and "repeated". Another is the English split of a single concept into "test / temptation". Hegel's German terms in II [1] add load without adding clarity for this reader. The existing inline glosses (telos, particularity, mediation, ethical life, incommensurable, "in terms of the idea", Latin and Greek phrases) work well.

## 5. Verdict

**Problema I:** a newcomer can follow the backbone of the argument. The ethical as universal, the tragic-hero cases ([7]–[14]), the "Abraham cannot speak" step ([17]) and the stark either/or in [29]–[30] are clear, and the three heroes contrasted with Abraham carry the reasoning well. Understanding weakens in the opening definition ([0]), which states its conclusion only by implication and appears to contradict itself on "suspension". The Hegel paragraph ([1]) is opaque without background. One limb of the closing either/or, "never been faith because there has always been faith", cannot be decoded from this section alone.
