## Part 1 — Paragraph-by-paragraph paraphrase

### Problema I — Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?

- **[0]** Sets up the opposing position as a premise. The ethical is the universal: it binds everyone, and it needs no higher purpose because it is itself the highest purpose. The individual's task is to give up whatever makes him "this one person" and become the universal. Asserting himself against it is sin, and feeling the urge to do so is a "spiritual trial". If this is the whole truth, the ethical is like eternal blessedness: it cannot be "teleologically suspended", because suspending something means keeping it inside a higher purpose, and nothing is higher than it.
- **[1]** Draws the consequence. If [0] holds, Hegel is right that the individual who stands on his own is a form of evil, to be absorbed into family, society and state. But then Hegel is inconsistent to honour Abraham as the father of faith. On these premises Abraham should be tried as a murderer.
- **[2]** States the counter-thesis. Faith is the paradox that the single individual is higher than the universal, reached *after* first being inside the universal. Then an either/or: if this is not faith, Abraham is lost and faith never existed. If the ethical is the highest, Greek philosophy is enough, and Hegel should have said so.
- **[3]** An ironic aside against people who repeat stock phrases, such as "light over Christendom, darkness over paganism", without knowing what faith is. It ends with a jab from Boileau: fools who give easy explanations find bigger fools to admire them.
- **[4]** Restates the thesis more precisely. The individual stands in an "absolute relation to the absolute", which cannot be "mediated" (explained through the universal). It remains a paradox that thought cannot reach. Then the three-way either/or that the reader is asked to keep in mind throughout: faith is this paradox, *or* faith never existed, *or* Abraham is lost.
- **[5]** A concession. The paradox can be confused with a spiritual trial, and many people find it repellent. That is still no reason to water faith down, and believers should offer marks for telling the two apart.
- **[6]** Applies the thesis to Abraham. His story contains the suspension, and he has no real analogies. He acts "by virtue of the absurd". If he tried to mediate his act, he would have to call it a spiritual trial and repent. So he is not a tragic hero: he is either a murderer or a believer. The narrator can understand a tragic hero but not Abraham.
- **[7]** Announces the test: is there a higher *ethical* principle that could justify Abraham while staying inside the ethical?
- **[8]** First example of a tragic hero, a king (later named as Agamemnon) who sacrifices his daughter for the nation. He hides his pain, and the nation shares both his pain and his deed.
- **[9]** Second example, Jephthah, who sacrifices his daughter to keep a vow that won the victory for Israel. Everyone understands him.
- **[10]** Third example, Brutus, who executes his guilty son under Roman law and is admired for it.
- **[11]** Counterfactuals. Take away the public reason (fair wind, no vow, an innocent son) and nobody would understand these three. Saying "it is a test" would not help.
- **[12]** A second counterfactual. If the three heroes added "But it will not happen, we believe this by virtue of the absurd", they would be even less understood. People can see the absurd; they cannot see how anyone believes it.
- **[13]** Draws the distinction. The tragic hero stays inside the ethical: he places one ethical duty (to a child) under a higher ethical duty (to the state or people). So his case is no teleological suspension of the ethical *itself*.
- **[14]** Contrasts Abraham. His act has no link to the universal except that it breaks it. It is purely private, with no nation to save. His only ethical expression is "the father must love the son". Whatever universal there is lies in Isaac and would protest.
- **[15]** Asks why Abraham does it. For God's sake and for his own, which the text says is one thing. That is why it is called a "test/temptation". Here the temptation is the ethical itself, because it would hold him back from God's will. That creates a puzzle, since duty is supposed to *be* God's will.
- **[16]** Concludes that a new category is needed. The tragic hero has no private relation to God: for him the ethical is the divine, so his case can be mediated.
- **[17]** "Cannot be mediated" means the same as "cannot speak". To speak is to use the universal. If Abraham spoke, he would have to call his situation a spiritual trial.
- **[18]** A long lyrical passage. Abraham both awes and appalls the narrator. The man who gives up the universal for something higher may simply be deluded, and then nothing can save him. The tragic hero is wept for and consoles mourners, but nobody weeps over Abraham, only holy dread. The narrator imagines the lone climber on Moriah making a mistake. It ends with an aside to Shakespeare, who could say everything except this torment.
- **[19]** Asks how the individual exists once the ethical is suspended. He stands against the universal, which in principle is the form of sin (as with a child). Unless this form can "repeat" without being sin, Abraham is condemned. Abraham had faith: an absolute relation to the absolute, justified only as a single individual.
- **[20]** Asks how the individual can know he is justified. Reducing everything to state and society makes mediation easy and removes the paradox. The Pythagoras image is that the odd is more perfect than the even. Some modern people give an answer that "points toward" the paradox.
- **[21]** Satire. That answer is "judge by the outcome". A true hero-paradox might say it, but today it is said by "lecturers", safe people who judge great men from comfort. The real lesson of greatness is in the beginning, not the outcome, and the hero is a hero because he began.
- **[22]** The outcome is finitude's answer and differs in kind from the hero's existence. Getting Isaac back does not prove Abraham right. Would he have been less justified if Isaac had died?
- **[23]** People read for the ending as they read novels and skip the anxiety and paradox. Doing this robs the holy, and it is worse than Judas.
- **[24]** A personal credo. Greatness should be approached humanly, with confident respect, neither from a servile distance nor with impudence. This is illustrated by a commoner entering a king's palace. What makes the great approachable is knowing their anxiety and distress.
- **[25]** Example: Mary. Being "favoured" does not make her great. People leave out her distress, anxiety and paradox. Ends with a defensive aside about the purity of the narrator's thought.
- **[26]** Continues with Mary. Her pregnancy was a time of anxiety, and no one understood her. God blesses and curses at once. She is great through her "Behold the handmaid" and, like Abraham, becomes greater than a hero *through* the distress.
- **[27]** The poet can ask the crowd to weep for the tragic hero. The knight of faith says "do not weep for me, weep for yourself".
- **[28]** People sentimentally wish they had lived in Christ's time and forget how terrifying it would have been. The outcome (eighteen centuries) supports this self-deception. The narrator confesses he lacks the courage to wish he had been a contemporary.
- **[29]** Returns to Abraham. Before the outcome, he is either a murderer at every minute or stands in the paradox.
- **[30]** Summary. The story contains the suspension, and Abraham is higher than the universal. If not, he is a murderer, not even a tragic hero. Anyone can become a tragic hero by his own strength; no one can be advised on the road of faith.
- **[31]** Closing. Faith is a miracle, yet no one is excluded from it, because faith is a passion and passion unites people. The footnote cites Lessing on passion making all people equal.

### Problema II — Is there an absolute duty to God?

- **[0]** Sets up the opposing view. If the ethical is the divine, every duty is "to God" only in name. You love your neighbour, not God, and "duty to love God" becomes a tautology. God shrinks to a vanishing point, and loving him in any other sense is suspect, like Rousseau's love of distant peoples.
- **[1]** The consequence. If there is nothing incommensurable in human life, Hegel is right, but then he should not speak of faith or call Abraham its father. Hegel holds that the outer is higher than the inner, illustrated by child and man. Faith is the reverse: inwardness is higher, or "the odd is higher than the even".
- **[2]** For ethics, the task is to express the inner outwardly, and holding back in feeling is sin or spiritual trial. Faith's inwardness is a *new* inwardness, not that first one.
- **[3]** A warning. Philosophy wrongly equates faith with "the immediate" (feeling, mood). That makes faith common to every age and easy to "go beyond". Real faith comes *after* a movement of infinity, meaning infinite resignation. Socrates made that movement with his ignorance but did not reach faith. Faith can break through only after resignation.
- **[4]** Restates the paradox as a thesis about duty. The individual settles his relation to the universal through the absolute, not the other way round. So there is an absolute duty to God, and the ethical becomes relative. It is not abolished but can take a paradoxical form: love of God may require an act toward the neighbour opposite to ethical duty.
- **[5]** The either/or in brief: otherwise faith is a spiritual trial and Abraham is lost.
- **[6]** Why this cannot be mediated. Once the individual expresses the absolute duty in universal terms, he sees it as a spiritual trial. If he resists, he fails the absolute duty. If he doesn't, he sins. Example: Abraham explaining his love for Isaac would only sound self-contradictory.
- **[7]** Applies this to Abraham. The ethical relation is made relative, and his only answer is "a test", both for God's sake and his own. Ordinary speech treats "for God's sake" and "for his own sake" as the two readings of an odd act. Faith has lost the middle term, the universal, so it looks like extreme egoism and extreme devotion at once. No one can be understood, not even by another knight of faith. What "Isaac" means for a given person only that person can settle. Narrator's confession: he can understand the greatness but lacks the courage for it.
- **[8]** Scriptural evidence: Luke 14:26, "hate father and mother...". Scholars soften "hate" to "love less", but the next verses (the tower-builder counting the cost) suggest it should be taken at full strength.
- **[9]** Mocks the softening exegete. A teaching whose most solemn words turn out to mean "be a bit less attentive" would not be worth defending.
- **[10]** The words are terrible but understandable. Be honest about them even if you lack the courage. Calling that lack "humility" is really pride.
- **[11]** Argues that the passage must be read literally. A God (or husband) who wanted love proved by lukewarmness toward everyone else would be egoistic and stupid. A good husband wants a wife who is also a perfect daughter.
- **[12]** Finishes [11]'s example: her love as a daughter guarantees her love for him.
- **[13]–[14]** One sentence split across two slots. What we would call egoism and stupidity in a human, the exegete asks us to accept as a worthy idea of God.
- **[15]** Then how is one to "hate"? Only as a paradox. Absolute duty can lead to what ethics forbids but never to ceasing to love. Abraham must love Isaac even more, because that love is what makes the act a sacrifice. Ethically, and in outward reality, he remains a murderer.
- **[16]** The knight has no higher ethical universal to shelter in. If the Church demanded the sacrifice, the result would be a tragic hero, since the Church is just another state-like universal.
- **[17]** Inside the paradox there is no Church to reach. The individual finds blessedness or perdition there. The "ecclesiastical hero" is understood by all and does not say "it is a test".
- **[18]** Why such passages are avoided: fear of unleashing individuals, and a belief that being an individual is easy. The narrator rejects both. Being the single individual is the most terrible thing and so also the greatest. It must be spoken of with anxiety and reverence so that it helps rather than misleads.
- **[19]** The distress of faith. The tragic hero renounces himself for the universal, and the knight renounces the universal to become the individual. The knight knows how good it is to belong to the universal and be "legible to all", but his path is higher, lonely and steep. To others he looks mad or a hypocrite.
- **[20]** The knight could wish his task were the understandable one, and treats that wish as a spiritual trial. Example: Abraham waits seventy years for a son (with the Hagar episode), gets him, and is tested again. Sacrificing Isaac for the universal would have been "kingly" and restful, but that is not his task. Leads into Cunctator.
- **[21]** Abraham is a greater "delayer" than the Roman general but saves no state. Contemporaries would call him mad. His life is sealed, like a book seized by God and never made public.
- **[22]** Whoever does not see this terror is no knight of faith. The tragic hero "dances" compared with the knight, who crawls. The knight's reward is intimacy with God, calling him "you".
- **[23]** The tragic hero finishes quickly and rests in the universal. The knight stays sleepless, because turning back to the universal is always possible and could be either temptation or truth.
- **[24]** The knight's twofold passion: to gather the whole broken ethical into one moment, making sure he loves Isaac fully, and to renew that assurance instantly and completely. The tragic hero does something similar but with the universal's support. The knight is alone.
- **[25]** Contrast with Agamemnon, who concentrates and then rests. Abraham concentrates too, without support, and makes "one more movement" back upon the miracle. Without it he would be only an Agamemnon, and not even justifiable.
- **[26]** Only the individual can tell trial from faith, but outward marks can be drawn. The true knight is isolated, while the false one is "sectarian" and seeks a cheap tragic heroism among friends. The narrator confesses he admits his own inability. Sectarians drown out anxiety with noise.
- **[27]** More marks. The true knight feels the pain of being uncomprehended but has no wish to guide others. He is a witness, not a teacher. The false knight shows off sudden mastery and prefers admiration to martyrdom.
- **[28]** The final either/or: there is an absolute duty to God, meaning the paradox, *or* faith never existed because it always existed, *or* Abraham is lost, *or* Luke 14 must be explained away as the exegete does.
- **Footnote to [24]** For the tragic hero, duty and wish coincide, and he gives up the wish to keep the duty. The knight must give up both. So resigning the wish brings no rest (it is his duty), and keeping duty and wish fails the absolute duty.

## Part 2 — Reconstructed arguments

### Problema I

1. Premise under examination: the ethical = the universal = the highest. It has no telos outside itself.
2. So the individual's task is to cancel his particularity in the universal. Self-assertion against it is sin, and the urge to it is spiritual trial.
3. So the ethical cannot be teleologically suspended, since there is no higher telos in which it could be preserved.
4. Consequence 1: Hegel is right to call the self-standing individual a form of evil. Consequence 2: Abraham is a murderer, and Hegel is wrong to praise him as father of faith.
5. Counter-thesis: faith = the paradox that the individual, *after* passing through the universal, stands higher than it in an absolute relation to the absolute. This cannot be mediated.
6. Test: can Abraham be justified by a *higher ethical* principle, as tragic heroes are? Agamemnon, Jephthah and Brutus sacrifice a lower ethical duty to a higher ethical one (people, vow, state). They stay inside the ethical and are understood and wept for.
7. Remove the public reason, or add "by virtue of the absurd", and they become incomprehensible.
8. Abraham has no such higher ethical reason. His act is purely private, for God's sake and his own, and the ethical is itself the temptation. So only the category of faith (a private relation to God) could justify him.
9. Because he cannot be mediated, he cannot speak. He cannot be sure he isn't deluded, and no one can weep for or advise him.
10. The "outcome" (Isaac returned) cannot justify him. Justification must hold from the beginning, and judging by outcomes cheats greatness of its anxiety (compare Mary and Christ's contemporaries).
11. **Conclusion (either/or):** before the outcome, either Abraham is a murderer at every moment, or there is a teleological suspension of the ethical: the paradox higher than all mediation. Equivalently, either faith is this paradox, or faith never existed (because it "always existed"), or Abraham is lost.

### Problema II

1. Premise under examination: the ethical = the universal = the divine. Every duty is "to God" only by reference; the actual relation is to the neighbour.
2. So "duty to God" is a tautology, God is an empty vanishing point, and any other love of God is overwrought.
3. On that view, with nothing incommensurable, Hegel is right: the outer ranks above the inner, and the ethical task is to express oneself outwardly.
4. Counter-thesis: faith is a *new* inwardness, higher than the outer and not reducible to feeling or immediacy. It comes after infinite resignation.
5. Restated as duty: there is an absolute duty to God. The individual's relation to the universal is set by his relation to the absolute, and the ethical becomes relative but not abolished. It can take a paradoxical, reversed expression.
6. This cannot be mediated. Put into universal terms it looks like a spiritual trial (resist it and fail the absolute duty; yield and sin), and it combines the look of extreme egoism with absolute devotion. No one, not even another knight, can understand or help.
7. Scripture confirms the hard reading: Luke 14:26 must mean "hate" literally, because softening it makes God egoistic and stupid. "Hate" can only be understood paradoxically: Abraham must love Isaac most at the moment of sacrifice, yet outwardly he is a murderer.
8. No higher universal (such as the Church) can shelter him. That would make him a tragic hero.
9. The knight's distress: he sees the value of the universal, wishes for it, is endlessly tested, must concentrate and renew his assurance alone, and makes "one more movement" beyond the tragic hero's. Marks of the false knight: sectarianism, wanting to teach, sudden mastery.
10. **Conclusion (either/or):** either there is an absolute duty to God (= the paradox), or faith never existed because it always existed, or Abraham is lost, or Luke 14 (and similar passages) must be explained away like the tasteful exegete.

### Terms as these texts use them

- **The ethical**: the universal moral order that binds everyone at every moment. It is its own highest end, and on the view examined it is also the divine.
- **The universal**: the ethical seen as what is common to all and expressible to all. To speak is to use it.
- **The single individual**: the person as a particular human being. In P1[0] this is the "immediate" person with a task to merge into the universal. Faith's individual has passed *through* the universal and stands above it. The phrase "as the single individual" means "in his capacity as this one person, not as a representative".
- **Particularity**: whatever sets a person apart as "this one" (P1[0]). Ethically it is to be cancelled, and asserting it is sin.
- **Telos**: end or purpose (glossed in P1[0]).
- **Teleological suspension**: setting something aside for a higher end in a way that keeps (preserves) it within that end. A "teleological suspension of the ethical" would set ethical duty aside for a telos outside the ethical (God).
- **Ethical life**: glossed in P1[1] as morality lived out in family, society and state. P1[2] equates it with "the ethical", but P1[14] seems to separate the two (see flags).
- **Mediation**: settling or explaining something by bringing it under the universal (glossed in P1[4]). It is the same as being able to speak or be understood (P1[17]).
- **The paradox**: that the single individual is higher than the universal and relates absolutely to the absolute. It is also stated as "inwardness higher than outwardness" and as "there is an absolute duty to God". Thought cannot reach it.
- **The absurd**: in P1[6] it is identified with the paradox itself. In P1[12] and P1[3] it is the ground on which one believes the impossible (Isaac returned, "it will not happen").
- **Spiritual trial**: an inner assault or temptation to leave the universal (P1[0]). It is also what the believer's situation looks like when put in universal terms, and the constant possibility that the knight's faith is a delusion, or that turning back is a delusion.
- **Test vs temptation**: the text treats them as one word for Abraham's situation (P1[15]). "Temptation" normally means what pulls you *away* from duty. Here the ethical itself is the temptation, pulling Abraham away from God's will. "Test" names God's demand for proof.
- **Absolute duty**: a duty to God that is not just every duty referred to God, but a direct relation that makes the ethical relative (P2[4]).
- **Inwardness**: the inner. The *first* inwardness is feeling or mood, which ethics requires to be expressed outwardly. The *new* inwardness of faith cannot be measured against the outer (P2[2]).
- **Tragic hero vs knight of faith**: the tragic hero gives up a lower ethical duty for a higher ethical one. He stays in the universal, is understood and wept for, finishes quickly and rests. The knight of faith gives up the universal for a private absolute relation to God. He cannot speak or be understood, has no support, is always tested, and makes "one more movement" back upon the miracle.

## Part 3 — Comprehension failures

### SEVERE

1. **P1[2], repeated at P1[4] and P2[28]: "faith has never existed in the world — precisely because it has always existed."** [wording/order + idea] On first meeting, and again in [4], I could not make sense of this. It reads as a flat contradiction, and nothing in Problema I explains it. The explanation only arrives in P2[3] ("substitute 'the immediate'... for 'faith.' If one does that, it is ridiculous to deny that faith has existed in every age"). Because it is one of the three branches of the concluding either/or in *both* sections, not understanding it cost me a third of Problema I's conclusion. It is an order problem: the key comes a whole section later.
2. **P2[1]: "The child is das Innere, the man das Äussere; that is why the child is determined precisely by what is outer, and, conversely, why the man, as das Äussere, is determined precisely by das Innere."** [wording + outside] I could not parse the logic: the child *is* the inner, *therefore* it is determined by the outer? Keeping the German nouns (after only one gloss) forces me to translate while trying to follow a reversal. The main line of the argument survives, because the next sentence says plainly "inwardness is higher than outwardness". The example, which is supposed to illustrate, is opaque. It is the one sentence in either file I gave up on.

### MODERATE

3. **P1[0]: the definition of teleological suspension.** "For it would be a contradiction if eternal blessedness could be given up — that is, teleologically suspended — since the moment it was suspended, it would be forfeited. What is teleologically suspended, by contrast, is not forfeited but is preserved..." [wording] The first sentence equates "given up" with "teleologically suspended". The next says teleological suspension is precisely *not* giving up. It reads as self-contradiction until I worked out the hidden "therefore": nothing higher than blessedness exists to preserve it, so for it suspension would be pure loss. This is the title concept, and I got it only from a contrast placed at the end of a very long paragraph.
4. **P1[0]: "As the universal, it applies to everyone — which can also be put another way: it applies at every moment."** [wording/idea] I could not see why "everyone" and "every moment" are two ways of saying the same thing.

This matters, because P2[3] later makes "the immediate" a key word.
6. **P1[1]: "define a human being under 'the Good and Conscience' only as the single individual."** [outside] I don't know what "under 'the Good and Conscience'" refers to (a section of a Hegel book?). The rest of the paragraph is followable.

[wording/term slippage] P1[2] equates the ethical with ethical life. P1[13]–[14] treat ethical life as the higher civic level (state/people) *within* the ethical. I lost the subject. "Aulis" is unexplained (I needed outside knowledge that it is Agamemnon's port).
18. **P1[19]: "even though a child does not sin, because it is not conscious of its existence as such, its existence is nevertheless sin when seen in terms of the idea, and the ethical demands itself of the child at every moment. "This form can be repeated" relies on remembering "the movement repeats itself" from P1[2]. I had to reread several times.
19. **P1[20]: "which I can also express pointedly with a saying of Pythagoras: that the odd number is more perfect than the even."** [idea/outside] I could not see how odd/even maps onto individual/universal (the single, unpaired one over the paired one?). 20. **P1[20]→[21]: "Now and then in our time one does hear an answer that points in the direction of the paradox"**, followed by an attack on that answer. [order] P1[20] opens by asking how the individual can be sure he is justified. It then takes a detour through the state and Pythagoras, then introduces an answer "pointing toward" the paradox, which [21] ridicules. I lost track of whether the original question was ever answered. By reconstruction, the answer is "not by the outcome".
21. **P1[21]: "for just as our age does not produce heroes, which is its failing, it has the advantage that it produces few caricatures either. So when in our time one hears the words ... one knows at once whom one has the honor of addressing."** [wording] The "So" hides a step (no heroes or caricatures say it now, *so* whoever says it is a lecturer).
22. **P1[22]: "the outcome (insofar as it is finitude's answer to the infinite question) is, in its dialectic — in the way it works — entirely different in kind from the hero's existence."** [wording] "Dialectic" glossed as "in the way it works" did not help. I took the sentence on trust.
23. **P1[24]: "And what will help him is precisely the anxiety and distress in which the great are tested; for otherwise, if he has any backbone at all, the great would only arouse his justified envy. And whatever can be great only at a distance ... — that they destroy themselves."** [wording/referent] The reason is compressed (knowing their distress makes the great human, so they don't breed envy). In the last sentence, "they" could mean the people or the empty phrases.
24. **P1[25]: "And if it did not so strangely happen that those who listen can think just as inhumanly as those who speak, then surely every young girl would ask..."** and **"My thought is as pure as anyone's, and whoever can think such things will surely find his thought becoming pure..."** [wording] Negated conditional plus an unexplained "such things". The paragraph break between [25] and [26] ("And if he sins against them") splits the pronoun "them" (= the images) from its antecedent.
25. **P2[6]: "And then, if he does resist it, he will not come to fulfill the so-called absolute duty; and if he does not resist it, he sins."** [referent] "It" is the spiritual trial, but the spiritual trial *is* the pull toward the absolute duty. That only became clear on a third reading.
26. **P2[7]: "These two determinations also correspond to each other in ordinary speech. When we see someone do something that does not fit into the universal, we say, 'He hardly did that for God's sake,' and by this we mean that he did it for his own sake."** [wording] "Correspond" suggests agreement, but the example shows them as alternatives or opposites. I was unsure what point about ordinary speech was being made.
27. **P2[7]: "Any more precise explanation of what is to be understood by 'Isaac' the single individual can only ever give to himself."** [wording] A garden path: I first read "'Isaac' the single individual" as an apposition (Isaac, the individual). The object comes first and the subject is buried. The quotation marks around "Isaac" (meaning each person's own "Isaac") are never signalled.
28. **P2[11]: "It is easy to see now that if the passage is to make any sense, it must be understood literally."** It is followed by an argument that a love proved by lukewarmness is stupid. [order] I expected an argument *for* literal hate and got one against the *softened* reading. The link only closes at P2[13]–[14], where the sentence split across two slots delays it further.
29. **P2[15]: "I will not bring up here the human distinction 'either love or hate' — not because I have much against it, for it is at least passionate, but because it is egoistic and does not fit here."** [wording/idea] I could not see why "either love or hate" is egoistic.
30. **P2[16]→[17]: "Such an ecclesiastical hero..."** [order] P2[17] opens with a sentence about the paradox and then says "Such an ecclesiastical hero", which refers back to the Church case in P2[16]. The interposed sentence breaks the referent.
31. **P2[24]: "the passion to concentrate the whole of the ethical that he is breaking into a single moment."** [wording] A garden path: "breaking into a single moment" reads as one unit. It needs to be read as "the ethical that he is breaking" + "into a single moment". It sits in the knight's central "passion" sentence.
32. **P2[26]: "the lonely spiritual trials that await the knight of faith and that he dares not flee, precisely because it would be still more terrible if he presumptuously forced his way forward."** [wording] Fleeing and forcing forward are opposites, so "dares not flee because forcing forward would be worse" did not follow for me.

### Difficulty of the ideas vs difficulty of the wording

- **The ideas themselves (the text says so, and I accepted it):** that the paradox "cannot be thought", "cannot be mediated", and "just as inexplicable how he entered it as how he remains in it". Why the believer cannot know he isn't deluded (P1[18], P2[23]). The double requirement to love Isaac more and still sacrifice him (P2[15]). The knight giving up both wish and duty (footnote). These were hard but clearly *meant* to be hard, and the text told me so. I never felt the wording was hiding them.
- **The wording:** items 1 (order), 2, 3, 5, 7, 10–13, 16–18, 20–28 and 30–32 above are structural: circular definitions, unresolved referents, garden paths, examples introduced before names, glosses placed after first use, and key explanations placed one section too late.
- **Outside knowledge I relied on:** Agamemnon/Iphigenia/Aulis, Jephthah's vow, Brutus and lictors, Hegel's "Good and Conscience", Socratic ignorance, the Abraham chronology (70/130 years, Hagar), Cain, "the absolute" = God, and the promise hidden in "Isaac's loins".

### Where wording (not added explanation) seems to be the obstacle

- **Order.** Faith "never existed because it always existed" is used three times before the text gives its key (P2[3]). "Middle term" is used in P1[6] and only identified in P2[7], with a different label ("intermediate term"). The P1[8] hero is only named in P1[11].
- **Sentence structure.** P1[0] (teleological-suspension contrast; circular "single individual ... is the single individual"), P1[4] (four "single individual"s in one clause), P1[18] (nested "suppose"), P2[1] (child/man with German nouns), P2[7] ("'Isaac' the single individual"), P2[24] ("that he is breaking into a single moment").
- **Referents.** P1[6] "a later one", P1[18] "if it is possible", P1[24] "they destroy themselves", P1[25]/[26] "them" across a paragraph break, P2[6] "resist it", P2[17] "Such an ecclesiastical hero".
- **Term consistency.** "The ethical" vs "ethical life" (P1[2] vs P1[14]). "Spiritual trial" vs "test/tried" overlap. "Middle term" vs "intermediate term".
