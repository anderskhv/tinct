# Problema II — Is there an absolute duty to God?

_Fear and Trembling, reader section 6 — Modern English clarity pilot, candidate v2. Paragraph numbers are 0-based slots aligned with the served editions._

**[0]** The ethical is the universal, and as such it is also the divine. So it is right to say that every duty is at bottom a duty to God. But if one can say no more than that, then one is also saying that I really have no duty to God. A duty becomes a duty by being referred to God, but in the duty itself I do not enter into a relation with God. Thus it is a duty to love one's neighbor. It is a duty because it is referred to God; but in this duty I enter into a relation not with God but with the neighbor I love. If I then say, in this context, that it is my duty to love God, I am really saying only a tautology — the same thing twice — insofar as "God" is here taken in a wholly abstract sense as the divine, that is, the universal, that is, duty. The whole existence of the human race is then rounded off in itself, complete as a sphere, and the ethical is at once what bounds it and what fills it. God becomes an invisible, vanishing point, a powerless thought; his power is only in the ethical, which fills all of existence. So if anyone should take it into his head to love God in any sense other than the one just stated, he is overwrought: he loves a phantom which, if it only had strength enough to speak, would say to him, "I do not ask for your love; just stay where you belong." If anyone should take it into his head to love God in some other way, this love becomes suspect, like the love Rousseau speaks of, with which a person loves a distant African people instead of loving his neighbor.

**[1]** Now, if what has been set out here is correct — if there is nothing incommensurable in a human life, and whatever incommensurable there is exists only by accident, from which nothing follows insofar as existence is viewed in terms of the idea — then Hegel is right. But where he is not right is in speaking of faith, or in allowing Abraham to be regarded as its father; for by the other he has pronounced judgment on both Abraham and faith. In Hegel's philosophy, das Äussere (die Entäusserung) — the outer (the act of outwardly expressing oneself) — is higher than das Innere, the inner. This is often illustrated with an example. The child is das Innere, the inner; the man is das Äussere, the outer. That is why the child is determined precisely by what is outer — and why, conversely, the man, being das Äussere, is determined precisely by das Innere. Faith, however, is this paradox: that inwardness is higher than outwardness — or, to recall an expression used earlier, that the odd number is higher than the even.

**[2]** For the ethical view of life, then, the task of the single individual is to strip himself of the determination of inwardness and to express it in something outward. Every time the single individual shrinks from this, every time he wants to hold back in, or slip back into, the inward determinations of feeling, mood and so on, he sins; he is in a spiritual trial. The paradox of faith is this: that there is an inwardness that is incommensurable with the outward — an inwardness which, note carefully, is not identical with the first inwardness but is a new inwardness.

**[3]** This must not be overlooked. Recent philosophy has allowed itself, without further ado, to substitute "the immediate" — what is simply there before any reflection — for "faith." If one does that, it is ridiculous to deny that faith has existed in every age. Faith then ends up in rather common company, alongside feeling, mood, idiosyncrasy, vapeurs (fits of the vapors) and so on. To that extent philosophy may be right that one ought not to stop there. But nothing justifies philosophy in using language this way. Faith is preceded by a movement of infinity; only then does faith appear, nec opinate — unexpectedly — by virtue of the absurd. This I can well understand, without thereby claiming that I have faith. If faith is nothing more than what philosophy passes it off as, then Socrates already went further, much further — instead of the reverse, that he did not reach it. In an intellectual respect he made the movement of infinity. His ignorance is infinite resignation. This task is already one suited to human powers, even if our age disdains it. But only when it has been done, only when the single individual has exhausted himself in the infinite, only then has the point been reached where faith can break through.

**[4]** The paradox of faith, then, is this: that the single individual is higher than the universal; that the single individual — to recall a distinction from dogmatics now less often heard — determines his relation to the universal by his relation to the absolute, not his relation to the absolute by his relation to the universal. The paradox can also be put this way: there is an absolute duty to God. For in this relation of duty the single individual, as the single individual, relates himself absolutely to the absolute. So when it is said in this connection that it is a duty to love God, something different is being said from before. For if this duty is absolute, then the ethical is reduced to the relative. It does not follow that the ethical is to be abolished; rather, it receives an entirely different expression, the paradoxical expression — so that, for example, love for God can lead the knight of faith to give his love for his neighbor an expression that is the opposite of what, ethically speaking, is his duty.

**[5]** If this is not so, then faith has no place in existence; then faith is a spiritual trial, and Abraham is lost, since he gave in to it.

**[6]** This paradox cannot be mediated, for it rests precisely on the single individual's being only the single individual. As soon as this single individual wants to express his absolute duty in the universal and become conscious of it there, he recognizes that he is in a spiritual trial. And then, if he does resist the spiritual trial, he will not come to fulfill the so-called absolute duty; and if he does not resist it, he sins, even if in reality his deed turns out to be what was his absolute duty. So what was Abraham to do? If he had said to another person, "I love Isaac more than anything in the world, and that is why it is so hard for me to sacrifice him," the other would surely have shaken his head and said, "Then why sacrifice him?" Or, if the other had been a shrewd fellow, he might even have seen through Abraham and noticed that he was displaying feelings that flagrantly contradicted his deed.

**[7]** In the story of Abraham we find just such a paradox. Ethically expressed, his relation to Isaac is this: the father must love the son. This ethical relation is reduced to the relative, in contrast to the absolute relation to God. To the question "Why?" Abraham has no answer except that it is a test, a temptation — which, as noted above, is the unity of its being for God's sake and for his own sake. These two determinations also match each other in ordinary speech: when we see someone do something that does not fit into the universal, we say, "He hardly did that for God's sake," and by this we mean that he did it for his own sake. The paradox of faith has lost the middle term, that is, the universal. On the one side it has the expression of the most extreme egoism (doing the terrible thing it does for one's own sake); on the other side, the expression of the most absolute devotion (doing it for God's sake). Faith itself cannot be mediated into the universal, for that would cancel it. Faith is this paradox, and the single individual simply cannot make himself understood by anyone. People no doubt imagine that the single individual can make himself understood by another single individual who is in the same position. Such a view would be unthinkable if people in our time were not trying in so many ways to sneak slyly into greatness. One knight of faith cannot help another at all. Either the single individual becomes a knight of faith himself, by taking the paradox upon himself, or he never becomes one. Partnership in these regions is utterly unthinkable. The single individual can only ever give himself any more precise explanation of what is to be understood by "Isaac." And even if one could define ever so exactly, in general terms, what is to be understood by "Isaac" (which, incidentally, would be the most ridiculous self-contradiction: bringing the single individual, who stands precisely outside the universal, under universal definitions, when he is supposed to act precisely as the single individual who is outside the universal), the single individual could still never be assured of it by others, only by himself as the single individual. So even if a person were cowardly and wretched enough to want to become a knight of faith on someone else's responsibility, he would not become one; for only the single individual becomes one, as the single individual. And that is what is great — which I can understand without entering into it, since I lack the courage. But it is also what is terrible — which I can grasp even better.

**[8]** As is well known, Luke 14:26 sets out a remarkable teaching about the absolute duty to God: "If anyone comes to me and does not hate his own father and mother, and wife and children, and brothers and sisters — yes, even his own life — he cannot be my disciple." This is a hard saying; who can bear to hear it? For that reason it is heard very seldom. But this silence is only an evasion, and it does no good. The student of theology, meanwhile, learns that these words occur in the New Testament, and in some exegetical handbook he finds the information that μισεῖν (to hate), in this and a few other passages, is used per μείωσιν — by understatement — and means minus diligo, posthabeo, non colo, nihili facio: I love less, I put in second place, I do not honor, I count as nothing. The context in which these words occur, however, does not seem to support this tasteful explanation. For the very next verse tells how someone who wants to build a tower first calculates whether he is able to finish it, so that people will not laugh at him afterward. The close connection between this story and the verse quoted seems to indicate precisely that the words are to be taken as terribly as possible, so that each person may examine himself to see whether he can put up the building.

**[9]** Suppose that pious and tender-minded exegete, who thinks he can smuggle Christianity into the world by haggling it down in this way, managed to convince someone that this was — grammatically, linguistically and κατ' ἀναλογίαν (by analogy with other passages) — the meaning of that passage. Then he will hopefully also manage, in the same moment, to convince that same person that Christianity is one of the most pitiful things in the world. For consider a teaching that, in one of its most lyrical outbursts, where the consciousness of its eternal validity swells most powerfully within it, has nothing to say but a noisy word that means nothing and indicates only that one should be less kind, less attentive, more indifferent; a teaching that, at the very moment it makes as if to say something terrible, ends by soothing instead of terrifying — that teaching is surely not worth standing up for.

**[10]** The words are terrible, yet I do believe they can be understood — without it following that whoever has understood them therefore has the courage to do what they say. One should at least be honest enough to acknowledge what is written there, and to admit that it is great, even if one lacks the courage for it oneself. Whoever behaves this way need not shut himself out from a share in that beautiful story; for in a way it contains comfort even for the one who did not have the courage to begin building the tower. But he must be honest, and not pass off this lack of courage as humility; for, on the contrary, it is pride, whereas the courage of faith is the only humble courage.

**[11]** It is easy to see now that if the passage is to make any sense, it must be understood literally. God is the one who demands absolute love. Now, anyone who demands a person's love and thinks that this love should also prove itself by the person's becoming lukewarm toward everything else he held dear is not only an egoist but also stupid. And whoever demanded such a love would at the same moment be signing his own death sentence, insofar as his life lay in the love he desired. A husband, for example, requires his wife to leave father and mother. But if he were to regard it as proof of her extraordinary love for him that for his sake she became a lukewarm, listless daughter, and so on, then he is stupider than the stupidest. If he had any idea of what love is, he would wish to discover that she was perfect in love as a daughter and a sister.

**[12]** In that he would see a guarantee that his wife would love him as no one else in the kingdom does.

**[13]** So: what one would regard in a human being as a sign of egoism and stupidity —

**[14]** that, with the help of an exegete, one is supposed to regard as a worthy conception of the deity.

**[15]** But how, then, is one to hate them? I will not bring up here the human distinction "either love or hate" — not because I have much against it, for it is at least passionate, but because it is egoistic and does not fit here. If, however, I regard the task as a paradox, then I understand it — that is, I understand it in the way one can understand a paradox. The absolute duty can then lead one to do what ethics would forbid, but it can by no means lead the knight of faith to stop loving. Abraham shows this. At the moment he is about to sacrifice Isaac, the ethical expression for what he is doing is this: he hates Isaac. But if he really hates Isaac, he can rest assured that God does not require this of him; for Cain and Abraham are not the same. Isaac he must love with his whole soul. When God demands Isaac, Abraham must love him, if possible, even more — and only then can he sacrifice him. For it is precisely this love for Isaac that, by its paradoxical opposition to his love for God, makes his deed a sacrifice. But the distress and anxiety in the paradox is that, humanly speaking, he cannot make himself understood at all. Only at the moment when his deed is in absolute contradiction to his feeling does he sacrifice Isaac. But the reality of his deed is what makes him belong to the universal, and there he is and remains a murderer.

**[16]** The passage in Luke must further be understood in such a way that one sees that the knight of faith has no higher expression of the universal (such as the ethical) in which he could save himself. If, for example, the Church were to demand this sacrifice of one of its members, we would have only a tragic hero. For the idea of the Church is not qualitatively different from the idea of the state, as long as the single individual can enter it by a simple mediation.

**[17]** And as soon as the single individual has entered the paradox, he does not arrive at the idea of the Church; he does not get out of the paradox, but must find in it either his blessedness or his perdition. Such an ecclesiastical hero — the member whose sacrifice the Church demands — expresses the universal in his deed, and there will be no one in the Church — not even his father and mother, and so on — who does not understand him. But he is not a knight of faith, and he also gives a different answer from Abraham's: he does not say that it is a test or a temptation in which he is being put to the proof.

**[18]** People generally refrain from quoting passages like this one in Luke. They are afraid of letting people loose; they fear the worst will happen once the single individual takes it into his head to behave as the single individual. Moreover, people think that existing as the single individual is the easiest thing of all, and that this is exactly why people must be forced to become the universal. I can share neither that fear nor that opinion, and for the same reason. Whoever has learned that existing as the single individual is the most terrible thing of all should not be afraid to say that it is the greatest. But he must also say it in such a way that his words hardly become a snare for someone who has gone astray, but rather help him into the universal — even if his words make a little room for what is great. Whoever does not dare to mention such passages does not dare to mention Abraham either. And to think that existing as the single individual is easy enough contains a very dubious indirect admission about oneself. For whoever really has self-respect and concern for his soul is certain that the one who lives under his own supervision, alone in the whole world, lives more strictly and more chastely than a maiden in her chamber. It is true that there may be those who need compulsion — those who, if they were set free, would romp like unruly animals in selfish pleasure. But one must show that one is not one of them precisely by knowing how to speak with anxiety and trembling. And one should speak out of reverence for what is great, so that it is not forgotten out of fear of harm — harm that will surely not come if one speaks in such a way that one knows it is the great, knows its terrors; and without knowing those, one does not know its greatness either.

**[19]** Let us now consider the distress and anxiety in the paradox of faith a little more closely. The tragic hero renounces himself in order to express the universal; the knight of faith renounces the universal in order to become the single individual. As was said, everything depends on how one is placed. Whoever thinks it is easy enough to be the single individual can always be sure that he is not a knight of faith; for drifters and vagabond geniuses are not men of faith. The knight of faith, on the contrary, knows that it is glorious to belong to the universal. He knows it is beautiful and beneficial to be the single individual who translates himself into the universal — who, so to speak, himself produces a clean, elegant and as far as possible error-free edition of himself, legible to all. He knows it is refreshing to become understandable to himself in the universal, in such a way that he understands it, and that every single individual who understands him in turn understands the universal in him, and both rejoice in the security of the universal. He knows it is beautiful to be born as the single individual who has his home in the universal, his friendly dwelling place, which at once receives him with open arms whenever he wants to stay in it. But he also knows that higher up than this there winds a lonely path, narrow and steep. He knows it is terrible to be born in solitude out of the universal, to walk without meeting a single traveler. He knows very well where he is and how he stands in relation to other people. Humanly speaking, he is mad and cannot make himself understood by anyone. And yet "mad" is the mildest expression. If he is not regarded that way, then he is a hypocrite — and the higher he climbs on that path, the more dreadful a hypocrite he is.

**[20]** The knight of faith knows that it is inspiring to give oneself up for the universal, that it takes courage, but also that there is a security in it, precisely because it is for the universal. He knows that it is glorious to be understood by every noble mind, and in such a way that the onlooker himself is ennobled by it. This he knows, and he feels as if bound; he could wish that this were the task that had been set for him. So Abraham might now and then have wished that the task were to love Isaac as befits a father, understandably to all, memorably for all time; he could wish that the task were to sacrifice Isaac for the universal, so that he could inspire fathers to glorious deeds. And he is almost horrified at the thought that for him such wishes are only spiritual trials and must be treated as such; for he knows that it is a lonely path he walks, and that he accomplishes nothing for the universal but is himself only put to the proof and tested. Or what did Abraham accomplish for the universal? Let me speak humanly about it, really humanly! He spends seventy years getting a son in his old age. What others get quickly enough and enjoy for a long time takes him seventy years. And why? Because he is being tested and tempted. Is that not madness? But Abraham had faith, and only Sarah wavered and got him to take Hagar as a concubine — but for that reason he also had to drive Hagar away. He gets Isaac — and then he is to be tested again. He knew it is glorious to express the universal, glorious to live with Isaac. But that is not the task. He knew it is kingly to sacrifice such a son for the universal; he himself would have found rest in it, and everyone would have rested in praise of his deed, as a vowel rests in the silent letter that carries it. But that is not the task — he is being tested. There was a Roman general famous by the surname Cunctator, "the Delayer."

**[21]** He stopped the enemy by delaying — but what a delayer Abraham is compared with him! Yet Abraham does not save the state. This is the content of 130 years. Who can bear it? Would his contemporaries — if one could speak of his having any — not say: "With Abraham it is an eternal delaying. At last he got a son — it took long enough — and now he wants to sacrifice him. Is he not out of his mind? If only he could at least explain why he wants to do it; but it is always 'a test.'" Nor could Abraham explain any more than that; for his life is like a book that has been placed under divine seizure and never becomes publici juris — public property.

**[22]** This is what is terrible. Whoever does not see it can always be sure that he is no knight of faith. But whoever does see it will not want to deny that even the most sorely tried tragic hero moves as if in a dance compared with the knight of faith, who makes his way forward only slowly, crawling. And when he has seen this and has made sure that he lacks the courage to understand it, he will surely have some sense of the wondrous glory that knight attains: he becomes God's confidant, the Lord's friend — to speak quite humanly — so that he speaks to God in heaven as one speaks to an intimate, saying "you," while even the tragic hero addresses him only in the third person.

**[23]** The tragic hero is soon finished, and his struggle is soon over. He makes the infinite movement and is now secure in the universal. The knight of faith, on the other hand, is kept sleepless; for he is constantly being tested, and at every moment there is the possibility of turning back in repentance to the universal — and this possibility can just as well be a spiritual trial as the truth. He cannot get clarity about this from any human being; for then he would be outside the paradox.

**[24]** The knight of faith, then, has first and foremost the passion to concentrate into a single moment the whole of the ethical that he is breaking, so that he can assure himself that he really loves Isaac with his whole soul.* If he cannot do that, he is in a spiritual trial. Next, he has the passion to call up this whole assurance in an instant, and in such a way that it is just as fully valid as it was in the first moment. If he cannot do that, he gets nowhere; for then he must keep starting over from the beginning. The tragic hero also concentrates into one moment the ethical that he teleologically went beyond, but in this respect he has support in the universal. The knight of faith has only himself alone, and that is what is terrible. Most people live in an ethical obligation in such a way that they let each day have its own sorrow; but then they never arrive at this passionate concentration, this energetic consciousness. In a certain sense the universal can help the tragic hero attain it, but the knight of faith is alone in everything.

**[25]** The tragic hero does it and finds rest in the universal; the knight of faith is kept in constant tension. Agamemnon gives up Iphigenia and has thereby found rest in the universal; now he proceeds to sacrifice her. If Agamemnon has not made the movement — if at the decisive moment his soul, instead of passionate concentration, was lost in the common chatter about having several daughters and about how vielleicht das Ausserordentliche (perhaps the extraordinary) might still happen — then of course he is no hero but a case for the poorhouse. Abraham has the hero's concentration too, though in him it is far more difficult, since he has no support at all in the universal; but he makes one more movement, by which he gathers his soul back upon the miracle. If Abraham has not done that, he is only an Agamemnon — assuming one could even explain how it can be justified to want to sacrifice Isaac when doing so benefits the universal in no way.

**[26]** Whether the single individual is really in a spiritual trial or is a knight of faith, only the single individual himself can decide. Nevertheless, some marks can be constructed from the paradox that even someone who is not in it can understand. The true knight of faith is always absolute isolation; the false knight is sectarian. Sectarianism is an attempt to jump off the narrow path of the paradox and become a tragic hero on the cheap. The tragic hero expresses the universal and sacrifices himself for it. Instead of this, the sectarian Master Jackel — a puppet-show clown — has a private theater, a few good friends and comrades who represent the universal about as well as the court witnesses in The Gold Snuffbox represent justice. The knight of faith, on the contrary, is the paradox; he is the single individual, absolutely nothing but the single individual, without any connections or complications. This is the terrible thing that the sectarian weakling cannot endure. For instead of learning from it that he is unable to do what is great, and then plainly admitting it — something I naturally cannot but approve of, since it is what I do myself — the bungler thinks that by joining forces with a few other bunglers he will be able to do it. But that will not work at all; in the world of spirit no cheating is tolerated. A dozen sectarians go arm in arm; they know nothing at all of the lonely spiritual trials that await the knight of faith and that he dares not flee, precisely because it would be still more terrible if he presumptuously forced his way forward. The sectarians drown one another out with noise and clamor and keep anxiety away with their shouting; and a hooting fairground crowd like this thinks it is storming heaven and thinks it is walking the same path as the knight of faith — who, in the solitude of the universe, never hears a human voice but walks alone with his terrible responsibility.

**[27]** The knight of faith is thrown back on himself alone. He feels the pain of not being able to make himself understood by others, but he feels no vain desire to guide others. The pain is his assurance. The vain desire he does not know; his soul is too earnest for that. The false knight easily betrays himself by the mastery he has acquired in an instant. He does not grasp at all what is at issue: that if another single individual is to walk the same path, he must become the single individual in exactly the same way, and then he needs no one's guidance — least of all that of someone who wants to force himself on him. Here, again, people jump off the path: they cannot endure the martyrdom of being uncomprehended, and instead, conveniently enough, they choose the worldly admiration that mastery brings. The true knight of faith is a witness, never a teacher, and in this lies the deep humanity that is worth rather more than the silly concern for other people's weal and woe that is honored under the name of sympathy, though it is nothing but vanity. Whoever wants only to be a witness thereby confesses that no human being, not even the lowliest, needs another's sympathy or should be humbled by it so that the other can be raised up. But just as he did not win what he won on the cheap, he does not sell it cheap either. He is not base enough to accept people's admiration and pay them for it with his silent contempt. He knows that what is truly great is equally accessible to all.

**[28]** So either there is an absolute duty to God — and if there is, it is the paradox described here: that the single individual as the single individual is higher than the universal and as the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute — or else there has never been faith, because it has always existed; or else Abraham is lost; or else one must explain the passage in Luke 14 as that tasteful exegete did, and explain the corresponding passages, and similar ones, in the same way.

## Kierkegaard's footnote

_Attached to ¶24._ * I want to shed light once more on the difference between the collision as it confronts the tragic hero and as it confronts the knight of faith. The tragic hero assures himself that the ethical obligation is wholly present in him by transforming it into a wish. Agamemnon can thus say: "The proof, for me, that I am not violating my duty as a father is that what is my duty is my only wish." So here we have wish and duty facing each other. The happy thing in life is when the two coincide, when my wish is my duty and vice versa, and most people's task in life is precisely to remain in their duty and, by their enthusiasm, to transform it into their wish. The tragic hero gives up his wish in order to fulfill his duty. For the knight of faith, too, wish and duty are identical, but he is required to give up both. So if he wants to resign himself by giving up his wish, he finds no rest; for it is, after all, his duty. If he wants to remain in his duty and in his wish, he does not become the knight of faith; for the absolute duty required precisely that he give it up. The tragic hero received a higher expression of duty, but not an absolute duty.
