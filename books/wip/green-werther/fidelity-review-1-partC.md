# Fidelity Review — The Sorrows of Young Werther (modern-en) — Round 1, Part C

**Chapters covered:** 41–61 ("October 20, 1771" through "September 4,
1772" — the opening of Book Two through the peasant-lad's-tragedy letter).

## Method / Coverage statement

Every paragraph in chapters 41–61 read individually and in full, source
against candidate, in original order. None sampled or skimmed.

## Findings

### Ch. 48, para 1 — minor unlicensed title/name substitution (non-blocking)

- **Source:** "...the Chancellor N—, with his deaf wife; the
  shabbily-dressed I—, whose old-fashioned coat bore evidence of modern
  repairs..."
- **Candidate:** "...the court councillor R—, with his deaf wife; the
  shabbily turned-out J—, patching the gaps of his old-fashioned coat
  with new bits..."
- **What's wrong:** Both blanked-name placeholder letters changed (N—→R—,
  I—→J—), and the source's title "Chancellor" became "court councillor,"
  a different (lower) office. Since "N—" and "I—" are themselves
  editorial stand-ins for suppressed real names (the same device the
  book uses throughout, e.g. "S—", "Miss B—"), the letter substitution
  carries no factual content and is not itself a defect. The title
  change is the substantive part: "Chancellor" and "court councillor"
  are not interchangeable ranks, and nothing in context requires the
  swap.
- **Severity:** Non-blocking. It's a background walk-on character
  mentioned once, and the scene's meaning (a room full of minor nobility
  Werther is out of place among) is unaffected either way.
- **Fix (optional):** Restore "Chancellor" if the merge owner wants exact
  rank fidelity; otherwise leave as a documented, harmless variant.

No other defects found in this range. Specific checks:

- **Ch. 42 (ambassador friction / social-rank tirade / Miss B— aunt
  portrait).** Every clause of the aunt's cutting character sketch
  ("no decent fortune, no wit, and no support but the line of her
  ancestors...") lands in the same order with the same specific claims
  (beauty in youth, tormenting young men, the old officer, widowhood,
  dependence on the niece's looks) as the source.
- **Ch. 44 (the D— inn letter to Charlotte).** The puppet-show/peep-show
  metaphor for Werther's numbness, and the currency of feeling toward
  Miss B— as a Charlotte-substitute, both track the source's exact
  claims and qualifiers ("if anyone can be like you" / "wenn... irgend
  jemand dir ähnlich seyn kann" rendered faithfully as conditional, not
  asserted as fact).
- **Ch. 52 (pilgrimage to his birthplace).** Long nostalgic paragraph
  about the stream and stone-skipping preserved in full, including the
  closing aphorism about needing "only a few clods to be happy on, and
  fewer still to lie under" — meaning and word order of the final clause
  intact.
- **Ch. 61 (the peasant lad's ruin).** This is one of the chapters the
  task brief flagged for special attention (Werther's identification
  with a socially "lower" figure whose passion mirrors his own). The
  candidate preserves every step of the story exactly: the servant's
  infatuation, the assault attempt, his confession of "what little
  intimacies she'd allowed," the brother's intervention, his dismissal,
  and the closing line that he "won't live to see" her remarry — with
  Werther's own commentary on how he has "weakened," not
  "exaggerated," the story in retelling it (a self-aware unreliability
  that the candidate keeps intact rather than smoothing into a more
  confident narrator).

**Verdict for this range: ACCEPT AS-IS**, with one non-blocking note
(Ch. 48) recorded above.
