# Fidelity Review — The Sorrows of Young Werther (modern-en) — Round 1, Part B

**Chapters covered:** 21 (continued from Part A boundary — reconfirmed) through
40 ("July 13, 1771" through "September 10, 1771" — the close of Book One).

## Method / Coverage statement

Every paragraph in chapters 21–40 read individually and in full, source
against candidate, in original order, including the long single-paragraph
letters (Ch. 32's suicide debate with Albert, Ch. 34's nature-rapture
letter, Ch. 40's farewell-terrace scene). None sampled or skimmed.

## Findings

No fidelity defects found in this range — no actor swaps, no dropped or
added negations, no causality reversals, no altered conditions, no
omitted clauses, no unlicensed additions, no silent corrections, no
unmodernized-quotation islands.

Specific points checked and confirmed intact:

- **Ch. 32 (the pistol/suicide debate with Albert).** This is the
  chapter with the most philosophical load-bearing argument in the book,
  and it is the one place a fidelity reviewer would most expect a
  softened or "corrected" version of Werther's reasoning. It is not
  softened. The candidate preserves Werther's deliberately overreaching,
  self-serving analogies exactly as the source gives them (the burning
  house, the man who fights off six attackers, the drowned girl, the
  fevered man) — including the moments Albert calls them out as
  ill-fitting ("Pray forgive me, but I do not see that the examples you
  have adduced bear any relation to the question" → "Don't take it
  amiss, but the examples you bring up don't seem to fit the case at
  all") and Werther's own half-concession ("people have often told me my
  way of stringing things together verges on the muddled"). The
  candidate does not clean up Werther's logic to make it more valid than
  the source allows — the specious, self-justifying quality of the
  argument (flagged as a priority in the task brief) is intact.
- **Ch. 32, drowned-girl story.** Every clause of the girl's decline —
  her ordinary pleasures fading, her fixation on one man, her lover's
  abandonment, her drowning — survives with the same sequence of cause
  and effect (each "and then" / "until at last" hinge lands on the same
  step). Albert's rebuttal ("weakness," "easier to die than to bear a
  life of misery with fortitude") and Werther's counter-argument about
  the limits of human endurance are both preserved with the same
  degree of force and the same order of claims.
- **Ch. 34 (nature-as-devouring-monster letter).** The long
  cosmic-rapture-into-horror paragraph, one of the hardest in the book to
  render because of its cascading subordinate clauses, keeps every listed
  image (the ant's ruined world, the beetle freed by the sunset, the
  moss on the rock, the "monster, forever devouring, forever chewing its
  cud") in the same order with the same emotional arc from awe to dread.
- **Ch. 40 (the terrace farewell scene).** Three-way dialogue between
  Werther, Charlotte, and Albert is attributed correctly throughout,
  including Charlotte's speech about her mother's deathbed promise and
  Albert's two brief interruptions ("This moves you too much, dear
  Charlotte...").

**Verdict for this range: ACCEPT AS-IS.**
