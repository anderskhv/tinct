# Accessibility Review — The Sorrows of Young Werther (modern-en) — Round 1

**Reviewer:** blind pass, candidate only, no source consulted (per
`books/prompts/accessibility-review-prompt.md`).

**Coverage:** All 84 chapters read in full, start to finish, in the order
presented (Preface; 82 dated letters/entries, chapters 2–83; the editorial
narrative "The Editor to the Reader," chapter 84, all 122 paragraphs). No
chapter sampled or skipped. Read via `books/wip/green-werther/candidate.json`
rendered to `candidate-full.txt` and consumed in four sequential passes
covering paragraphs 1–100, 101–200, 201–300, 301–end (chapters 1–36, 36–57,
57–83, and the full editorial chapter 84).

## Overall verdict: substantially accessible

This is an unusually strong candidate. The prose reads as natural
contemporary English throughout — long sentences generally hold together
because clause order tracks the emotional logic of the thought (Werther's
mind visibly moving from image to image), not because they're grammatically
legal but overloaded. Dialogue is distinct and idiomatic per speaker
(Charlotte's brisk practicality, Albert's flat legalism, Werther's
run-on rapture). The one long block of archaic-register material — the
Ossian recitation in Ch. 84 — is *supposed* to read as a separate, older
kind of poetry embedded in the novel (Werther is reading aloud from his own
translation of Ossian to Charlotte), so its elevated diction is not a
modernization failure; see note below.

## Specific accessibility issues found

### Unglossed period/currency/technical terms a general reader will stumble on

- **Ch. 10, para 42:** "I gave each of the children a kreutzer, and one more
  for the baby" — "kreutzer" is an old Austro-German coin with no gloss
  anywhere in the book. A reader can infer "small coin" from context but
  the word itself will stop most readers momentarily.
- **Ch. 20, para 114:** "you insisted I run everything on seven florins a
  week" — same issue: "florins" is unglossed. The passage's math (seven
  florins vs. double that) is legible from context, but the unit itself is
  opaque.
- **Ch. 50, para 275:** "a parting gift of twenty-five ducats" — same
  currency-term pattern; less load-bearing here since the point (a generous
  gift) is clear without knowing what a ducat is worth.
- **Ch. 37, para 203:** "two little duodecimo volumes — Wetstein's small
  Homer" — "duodecimo" is a specialist bibliographic term (book-size
  format) that a general reader will not know and nothing in the sentence
  glosses it; the surrounding clause ("small... edition... so as not to lug
  the big... one about") does most of the explanatory work, so this is
  minor but still an exact stumbling point.

### Unglossed allusions that carry emotional weight but no explanation

- **Ch. 12, para 77:** "she laid her hand on mine and said, 'Klopstock!'"
  — the text does gloss this reasonably well one clause later ("At once I
  knew the magnificent ode in her mind"), so a reader gets that it's a poet
  and a shared emotional touchstone, but never learns *why* that one word
  carries such force. This is a case where the ambiguity may be
  deliberate (period readers would have caught the reference instantly;
  modern readers are meant to feel Werther's excess a little from the
  outside) — flagging for the record rather than as a clear defect.
- **Ch. 4, para 14:** "a spring — a spring that holds me under a spell,
  like Melusina and her sisters" — no gloss of who Melusina is; a reader
  gets "magical water-spirit" from tone alone but not the specific myth.
- **Ch. 84, para 487:** "as the feeling of his God's grace slowly fades
  from the heart of the believer" and the closing line of para 509, "the
  priest and Levite would pass... and the Samaritan would shed a tear" —
  Good Samaritan parable reference. Reasonably legible to most readers even
  without background, but a reader unfamiliar with the parable will miss
  that Werther is casting himself as the man left for dead by the road.
- **Ch. 84, para 518:** "Emilia Galotti lay open on his desk." This is
  presented completely flat, no gloss, immediately followed by the editor
  moving on. A general reader has no way to know this is a play about a
  father killing his daughter to save her honor from a tyrant, and that its
  presence on the desk is one of the most quietly loaded details in the
  whole book. This is the single largest missed-context risk in the text —
  worth a translator's note or an onboarding "Cast"/glossary entry even
  though the sentence itself is grammatically simple.

### Ossian recitation (Ch. 84, paras 458–481)

The embedded Ossian poem ("Star of the falling night!... Why do you wake
me, breath of spring?") is deliberately more elevated, apostrophic, and
compressed than the surrounding modern prose — short fragmentary
sentences, invocations, archaic-flavored imagery ("the flies of evening
swarm on their faint wings"). This *reads* as a distinct register shift
within the story (Werther performing his own translation of an older
poem), which is dramatically correct and should not be flattened to match
the surrounding prose. Flagging only because a reader without warning may
find the sudden shift disorienting; a brief in-app framing note ("Werther
reads from his own translation of Ossian") could help, but this is a
presentation/onboarding suggestion, not a defect in the sentence-level
prose.

### Flow

No passages read as mechanically chopped or "stitched together." Even the
longest single-paragraph letters (Ch. 12's ball scene, Ch. 32's suicide
argument with Albert, Ch. 84's confession letters) move associatively and
stay easy to follow because each clause is anchored to a concrete image or
a clearly-marked speaker change. Dialogue tags and quotation handling
inside long paragraphs (e.g. Ch. 16 para 97, Ch. 32 throughout, Ch. 84
paras 440–441) stay attributable — I did not lose track of who was
speaking in any exchange, including the three-way exchanges among Werther,
Charlotte, and Albert.

### What's already working especially well

- The register separation between Werther (effusive, associative,
  exclamation-heavy) and Albert (flat, qualifying, "to be sure" hedging,
  Ch. 32) and the vicar's household (plain, homely) is consistently held
  the whole book — this will matter a great deal for the fidelity reviewer
  checking voice distinction.
- The escalating repetition device ("I must be gone!" Ch. 39; "Tomorrow, I
  expect" Ch. 40; "It can't go on!" Ch. 84 para 440) reads naturally as
  obsessive repetition rather than as translation stiffness.
- The final chapter's shift from letters to third-person editorial
  narration is clearly marked in prose register — cooler, more procedural
  ("We grant that Albert often left his wife's room...", "A vein was opened
  in his arm, for good measure") — without losing readability.

## Summary

The candidate is a genuinely accessible, idiomatic modern-English reading
edition. The handful of unglossed period/currency terms and two or three
unglossed allusions (Melusina, Klopstock, Emilia Galotti, the Good
Samaritan) are the only concrete stumbling points found across 84 chapters
and roughly 354 paragraphs; none of them break comprehension of the plot
or emotional beats, though the "Emilia Galotti" line is the one place
where a reader is likely to miss real significance without outside help.
Verdict: **substantially accessible**, needs at most a few targeted
glosses/notes, not a broader prose pass.
