# Accessibility Review — Final (Reviewer A pass)

**Book / chapter:** Leviathan, edition chapter 10 (Hobbes's own Ch. 9, "Of the Several Subjects of Knowledge")
**File reviewed:** `candidate-sonnet.json`
**Coverage:** All 12 paragraphs read in full, start to finish, then re-read paragraph by paragraph. I was not shown the source text or any prior review notes; this judges the candidate purely as English prose a general adult reader would encounter today.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph notes

**Paragraphs 1–3 (the prose introduction)** — No accessibility issues found. These read naturally and clearly:
- Para 1 sets up the fact/science distinction with a concrete worked example ("if the figure shown is a circle, then any straight line through its centre divides it into two equal parts") that does real explanatory work rather than just restating the abstraction.
- Para 2's natural-history/civil-history split is plain and well-paced.
- Para 3's transition into "the table that follows" is a clean, unforced hinge into the outline that follows — it reads like something a writer would actually say, not a mechanical label.

These three paragraphs are the strongest prose in the chapter and can be held up as the standard the outline items are working toward.

**Paragraph 4** ("I. Science, that is, knowledge of consequences...") — No issue. Self-glossing, plain.

**Paragraph 5** ("A. Consequences from the accidents (that is, the properties and qualities) of natural bodies...") — No issue. "Accidents" is Aristotelian jargon a modern reader could easily misread (as "mishaps"), but it's immediately and correctly glossed in the same breath — "(that is, the properties and qualities)" — so it doesn't cost the reader anything. This is good practice and worth noting as something already working well.

**Paragraph 6** — No issue. Clear.

**Paragraph 7** ("a. Consequences from indeterminate quantity and motion; which, being the principles or first foundation of philosophy, is called Philosophia Prima...") — Minor stylistic wrinkle: "the principles or first foundation of philosophy" pairs a plural noun ("principles") with a singular one ("first foundation") in an "or" construction, which reads as two near-synonyms rather than a clean either/or. A reader might pause half a beat here. It's not a comprehension blocker — the sentence still lands — so I'd call this a very low-priority polish item, not a required fix.
- *Structural note (not a defect):* this item is the first of two consecutive outline entries both labeled "a." (this one and paragraph 8). Per the documented context, this duplicate lettering is an inherent structural quirk preserved from Hobbes's own text, not a translation error, and I'm not flagging it as fixable wording.

**Paragraph 8** ("a. Consequences from the qualities of transient bodies — those that sometimes appear and sometimes vanish") — No issue. "Transient bodies" is glossed cleanly in the same sentence.

**Paragraph 9** ("2) Consequences of the qualities from liquid bodies that fill the space between the stars — that is, the air, or the ethereal substance.") — No fixable wording issue. Per the documented context, both the "2)" numbering (breaking from the period-based scheme used elsewhere) and the inverted "qualities from liquid bodies" phrasing (rather than "qualities of liquid bodies") are faithful reproductions of Hobbes's own original and independently verified against source — I'm noting them here as an inherent structural/phrasing quirk of this outline item, not as a defect to fix. Setting that aside, "ethereal substance" is glossed by apposition with "the air," so a reader isn't left stranded on the term.

**Paragraph 10** ("B. Consequences from the accidents of political bodies; which is called POLITICS, and CIVIL PHILOSOPHY") — No issue.

**Paragraph 11** ("1. Of consequences from the institution, or founding, of COMMONWEALTHS, to the rights and duties of the body politic, or sovereign.") — One targeted item worth flagging: **"the body politic, or sovereign"** presents two distinct historical/political concepts (the commonwealth-as-entity, and its ruler) joined by "or" as if they were interchangeable. A general contemporary reader is unlikely to have "body politic" as active vocabulary, and the "or sovereign" doesn't function as a gloss so much as a second, different term — so instead of resolving the difficulty, it can read as two unfamiliar terms stacked together. This is the one place in the chapter where I'd want a second look: either a brief gloss on "body politic" (e.g., "the commonwealth as a whole") or restructuring so the two terms aren't presented as equivalents. Everything else in the paragraph is clear.

**Paragraph 12** ("2. Of consequences from that same institution, to the duty and right of the subjects.") — No issue. Clean parallel to paragraph 11, and "that same institution" refers back unambiguously.

## What's already working well

The three lead-in prose paragraphs (1–3) are genuinely good, natural contemporary English — not just "acceptable," but the kind of writing that could be pointed to as a model for other outline-heavy chapters. The outline items themselves are consistently good about immediately glossing potentially unfamiliar terms in the same sentence they're introduced ("accidents," "transient bodies," "ethereal substance"), which is the right technique for this kind of terse, taxonomic material and is doing real accessibility work throughout.

## Overall verdict: **Substantially accessible**

After three rounds of fixes, this chapter has reached substantially accessible. The remaining texture is either:
- **Unavoidable conceptual complexity** inherent to a 17th-century taxonomy of the sciences (the outline form itself, the density of nested classification, terms like "Philosophia Prima" that are being defined rather than assumed) — not fixable by rewording without changing what the chapter is; or
- **Documented preserved-source quirks** (the duplicate "a." labels at paragraphs 7–8, the "2)" numbering break and inverted phrasing at paragraph 9) — independently verified against source as faithful, not translation defects.

The one genuinely avoidable item is paragraph 11's "the body politic, or sovereign," where two distinct concepts are presented as though interchangeable without either being glossed — a small, targeted fix (add a short gloss or separate the two ideas) would close the loop, but it's a single low-stakes phrase in an otherwise clean chapter, not evidence of a broader pass being needed. Paragraph 7's "principles or first foundation" is a minor, optional polish item of the same character.

**Summary:** This is a short, structurally terse chapter — Hobbes's own outline of the sciences — and the candidate handles it well: the connective prose (paragraphs 1–3) reads naturally and does real explanatory work, and the outline entries consistently gloss potentially unfamiliar terms in-line rather than leaving the reader to guess. The only defect worth a targeted fix is the "body politic, or sovereign" phrasing in paragraph 11; everything else that remains difficult is either inherent to a 400-year-old taxonomy of knowledge or a documented, verified reproduction of Hobbes's own numbering and phrasing quirks, not something translation can or should smooth away.
