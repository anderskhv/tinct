# Accessibility Review — Round 2 (Reviewer A, fresh read)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 9. Of the Several Subjects of Knowledge (modern-English candidate, `candidate-sonnet.json`)

**Coverage:** All 12 paragraphs read in full, indices 0–11, start to finish as a reader, then re-read paragraph by paragraph. Source text, current modern-en, and any prior review notes were not consulted — this is a blind read of the candidate only.

## What reads well

- Paragraph 0 is clean and does real work: it defines "knowledge of fact" vs. "science," gives the witness/philosopher pairing plainly, and the geometry example ("if the figure shown is a circle, then any straight line through its centre divides it into two equal parts") is genuinely easy to follow.
- Paragraph 1's natural history / civil history split is stated with no friction.
- Paragraph 4 handles a real terminology risk well: "accidents (that is, the properties and qualities)" glosses the term inline before it's used, so a reader isn't stopped cold.
- Paragraph 6 does the same for "Philosophia Prima (First Philosophy)."
- The list/table paragraphs (3–11) are, sentence by sentence, short and grammatically simple — no dense embedded clauses, no archaic constructions. The vocabulary load ("accidents," "commonwealth," "body politic") is inherent to the subject and is handled about as gently as it can be.

## Issues found

**Paragraph 2** — "The records of science are the books that contain the demonstrations of the consequences of one affirmation to another; these are commonly called books of philosophy."
The chain "demonstrations of the consequences of one affirmation to another" stacks three prepositional links in a row with no relief. It's grammatically legal but a reader has to hold "demonstrations → of consequences → of one affirmation → to another" in the air before the clause resolves. Likely a re-read on first pass. This is avoidable — the same content could be unpacked into two shorter clauses.

**Paragraph 2** — "...may be divided in the manner I have divided them in the table that follows."
Two problems: (1) "divided ... in the manner ... divided them" repeats the verb awkwardly in the same sentence, which reads as a stumble rather than a stylistic choice; (2) it tells the reader to expect "the table that follows," but paragraphs 3–11 render as a flat list of short lines, not a table — a reader looking for a literal table (columns, headers) may be briefly disoriented before realizing the outline structure *is* the table. This is fixable wording, not an unavoidable concept.

**Paragraphs 6 and 7 — duplicate "a." labels.**
Paragraph 6 begins "a. Consequences from indeterminate quantity and motion..." and paragraph 7 begins "a. Consequences from the qualities of transient bodies..." — two consecutive items both labeled "a." under what reads as the same level of the outline. As presented, a reader has no way to tell whether these are alternates, a hierarchy reset, or an error. This is a real stumbling point: outline labels are a navigation aid, and here the aid misleads instead of orienting. This reads as fixable — the outline can be relettered/renumbered for a flat, unambiguous sequence without touching the content's meaning.

**Paragraph 8 — numbering style break.**
"2) Consequences of the qualities from liquid bodies..." switches to a "2)" style, while every other list item in the chapter uses "1.", "a.", or "A." (period-based, not parenthesis-based). This is a small thing on its own, but combined with the paragraph-6/7 duplicate "a." it compounds the reader's sense that the outline levels aren't reliable. Fixable by conforming to one numbering convention.

**Paragraph 8** — "Consequences of the qualities from liquid bodies that fill the space between the stars — that is, the air, or the ethereal substance."
Compare paragraph 7's parallel construction: "Consequences from the qualities of transient bodies." Paragraph 8 inverts the prepositions — "of the qualities from liquid bodies" instead of "from the qualities of liquid bodies" — which breaks the pattern the reader has just been trained on two lines earlier and reads as a minor grammatical stumble on a re-read. Fixable wording; the glossed aside ("that is, the air, or the ethereal substance") itself works fine.

**Paragraph 10** — "...to the rights and duties of the body politic, or sovereign."
It's not clear on a first pass whether "the body politic, or sovereign" means the body politic *is* the sovereign (appositive, "or" = "in other words"), or whether these are two separate things sharing rights and duties. A reader has to pause and decide. Minor, but avoidable — a small rephrasing ("of the sovereign, that is, the body politic" or similar apposition marker) would resolve it. (Note: paragraph 4 does exactly this kind of inline apposition correctly for "accidents," so the pattern for how to do it clearly already exists elsewhere in the chapter.)

**Paragraph 10 vs. 11 — minor order swap.**
Paragraph 10 says "the rights and duties of the body politic"; paragraph 11 says "the duty and right of the subjects" — order flipped (rights/duties vs. duty/right) between two structurally parallel list items. Very minor, borderline not worth a separate fix, but it's the kind of small inconsistency that a careful reader moving quickly through a symmetrical list will notice as slightly off.

## Verdict

**Needs targeted fixes** — not yet "substantially accessible" as a clean pass, and not because of any unavoidable conceptual density. Most of the chapter (paragraphs 0, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9) reads as clearly as this kind of taxonomy list can read, and the two glossed technical terms ("accidents," "Philosophia Prima") are handled well. The remaining difficulty is concentrated in a small, specific set of problems, all of them fixable by wording/formatting rather than being inherent to the 17th-century taxonomy content itself:

1. A stacked prepositional chain in paragraph 2 ("demonstrations of the consequences of one affirmation to another").
2. An awkward self-referential, verb-repeating clause in paragraph 2 ("divided ... in the manner I have divided them in the table").
3. A genuine navigational defect: duplicate "a." outline labels in paragraphs 6–7.
4. An inconsistent numbering style at paragraph 8 ("2)" vs. the period-based scheme used everywhere else).
5. A broken parallel-structure preposition order in paragraph 8 ("of the qualities from liquid bodies").
6. Ambiguous apposition in paragraph 10 ("the body politic, or sovereign").

None of these require reinterpreting or simplifying Hobbes's actual classification of the sciences — they are sentence- and label-level wording/formatting fixes. Once addressed, I'd expect this chapter to clear as substantially accessible; as it stands, it does not because of these specific, listable problems rather than any residual conceptual difficulty.
