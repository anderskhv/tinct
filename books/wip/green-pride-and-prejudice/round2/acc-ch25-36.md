# Accessibility review: Pride and Prejudice (modern-en), chapters 25–36

## Coverage
I read all 301 candidate paragraphs in full: every chapter straight through, then again paragraph by paragraph.

- Ch 29: 29.0–29.41 (42) · Ch 30: 30.0–30.12 (13) · Ch 31: 31.0–31.29 (30) · Ch 32: 32.0–32.30 (31)
- Ch 33: 33.0–33.40 (41) · Ch 34: 34.0–34.30 (31) · Ch 35: 35.0–35.5 (6; 35.4 is the full letter, about 2,000 words) · Ch 36: 36.0–36.13 (14)

The only file I opened was the candidate file.

## Findings in brief (20 total, 3 blocking)
**Blocking**
- **30.2:** garbled duplicated clause: "that there might be that the family might have other church posts".
- **30.6 → 30.7:** the paragraph breaks mid-sentence ("in order to have" / "the earliest confirmation of it.").
- **36.3 → 36.4:** the same split-sentence defect ("what he'd told" / "them himself.").

**Referent / ambiguity**
- **28.17:** "she will do for him": the "him" is Darcy, who is not in the scene.
- **31.28:** "married _her_": means Miss Bingley but reads as Miss de Bourgh.
- **35.4:** "that Mr. Darcy never had": Darcy means his father, but it reads as him naming himself.
- **30.7:** "requiring them": "them" has no clear antecedent.
- **34.4:** "of it being beneath him": "it" has no antecedent.
- **29.37:** "such grand impertinence": reads as if the impertinence is Elizabeth's.
- **34.1:** "no intentions at all": too bare to be clear.

**Overloaded**
- 30.5: stacked double negative.
- 31.2: a "that"-clause used as the subject.
- 36.4: "classify … as being" knot.
- 36.6: a long chain of "that" clauses, with "his blamelessness" misattributable.
- 35.4: structural. The whole letter is one paragraph and should be split at topic boundaries if alignment permits.

**Vocabulary / archaic / small grammar**
- 29.40: quadrille, casino.
- 31.12: "in such state".
- 35.1: subjectless clause.

**Jarring**
- 36.x: the narration suddenly uses contractions throughout chapter 36, which it does not do in chapters 25–35.
- 36.8: "useless or pointless suspicion" is redundant and flat.

## Passages that read especially well
- **25.3, 25.12:** Mrs. Bennet's grievance and Elizabeth's Gracechurch Street irony keep their full comic edge.
- **26.29:** Elizabeth's letter on not having been in love. Crisp, and the irony is intact.
- **27.8–27.22:** the mercenary-vs-prudent exchange and the "rocks and mountains" outburst.
- **29.14–29.39:** Lady Catherine's interrogation. Fast, clear, and very funny.
- **31.11–31.25:** the piano scene. The rapid banter is easy to follow, and each speaker is clear.
- **33.9–33.37:** the conversation with Colonel Fitzwilliam. The revelation lands cleanly.
- **34.3–34.29:** the first proposal. Tense, lucid, and it keeps its formality without obstructing the reader.
- **36.7–36.8:** "Until this moment, I never truly knew myself." A strong climax.

## Verdict
- **Ch 25–29:** substantially accessible. Only one real referent issue (28.17) and one piece of vocabulary.
- **Ch 30–31:** need targeted fixes. There is one garbled sentence (30.2), a split paragraph (30.6/7), and two referent problems (30.7, 31.28).
- **Ch 32–34:** substantially accessible. There are two small referent fixes (34.1, 34.4).
- **Ch 35–36:** need targeted fixes. There is a split paragraph (36.3/4), the 36.4 knot, the 36.6 chain, the 35.4 "Mr. Darcy" referent, the letter's single-paragraph layout, and the chapter 36 contraction register.

**Readability summary.** Across these twelve chapters the edition mostly does what it promises. The prose is clear and modern in syntax, while Austen's irony, social distinctions and the characters' formal voices survive intact. The Rosings dinner, the piano scene, the Fitzwilliam conversation and the proposal all read smoothly aloud. The problems are concentrated and mechanical rather than systemic:
- two paragraphs split mid-sentence, which is probably a segmentation or alignment artifact;
- one garbled clause;
- a handful of pronouns that point to the wrong person in context (28.17, 31.28, 35.4);
- the densest reflective paragraphs in chapter 36, where long periodic sentences survive nearly untouched;
- chapter 36's switch to contracted narration.

Darcy's letter is lucid sentence by sentence, but as a single 2,000-word paragraph it is hard going on screen and in audio. None of this needs a broader pass. Targeted fixes to the 20 items above would make chapters 25–36 fully accessible.
