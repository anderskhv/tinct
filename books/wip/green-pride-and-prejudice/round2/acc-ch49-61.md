## Findings in brief

There are 26 findings. One is blocking. Full detail and suggested replacements are in `acc-ch49-61.json`.

**Blocking**
- **55.20:** The passage contradicts itself. "The latter" (Mr. Bennet) is said to be more agreeable than Bingley expected. The same sentence then says Bingley was "more talkative and less eccentric than Mr. Bennet had ever seen him". That reverses who is observing whom, and "eccentric" only fits Mr. Bennet. The same paragraph also has the garbled phrase "wouldn't be missed to counteract her mother's plots".

**Unclear referent or speaker** (the largest group)
- 50.22: "His daughter's request" comes straight after Mrs. Bennet's speech.
- 53.17: "seen him in Derbyshire" (Bingley or Darcy?).
- 54.11: "He" is Darcy, then "his eyes" are Bingley's.
- 56.61: "she answered angrily" follows Lady Catherine's speech.
- 60.27: "her husband" means Charlotte's, not Elizabeth's.
- 61.12: "her uncle and aunt" means Elizabeth's, not Lady Catherine's.

**Overloaded or tangled sentences**
- 50.3: the ten-pounds-a-year arithmetic.
- 50.9: a long "That his anger..." subject.
- 50.11: "to whose existing objections would now be added".
- 58.8: Lady Catherine's report to Darcy.

**Garbled or leftover archaic wording**
- 50.3: "In terms of grateful acknowledgment".
- 51.35: "put it out of her power".
- 54.13: dangling "Anxious and restless, the period...".
- 55.17: "grateful to the daughter", in the old sense of "pleasing".
- 55.20: "wouldn't be missed to counteract".

**Ambiguous**
- 49.28: "may I ask?" with no question following.
- 50.11: "personal harm".
- 55.7: "went upstairs" when they are already upstairs.
- 57.3: "she added" with no preceding speech.
- 61.9: "after peace was restored", which means the war ending.

**Vocabulary where the meaning matters**
- 52.3: "debts of honor" (gambling debts).
- 52.29: "living" (a church post).
- 53.48: "Michaelmas".
- 56.16: "wilderness" (a garden feature; "hermitage" in 56.17 is similar).
- 59.44: "special license".

The following terms were deliberately not flagged because context carries them: entail, commission, steward, pin-money, St. James's, Scarborough, livery and chaise and four. The "thirteen at table" joke in 53.25 is also left alone.

## Passages that read especially well

- **49.0–49.12:** The run across the lawn to Mr. Bennet has brisk, clear movement.
- **51.26–51.31:** Lydia's wedding monologue. Her breathless voice ("Well, and so...") and the slip about Darcy are well paced.
- **52.3:** Mrs. Gardiner's long letter. Despite its length it stays lucid and warm, and the closing lines about the ponies and Pemberley park land well.
- **56.22–56.71:** The Lady Catherine confrontation. The formality is kept, and the exchanges are crisp and easy to follow aloud.
- **57.13–57.19:** Mr. Bennet reads Collins's letter. The irony comes through cleanly.
- **58.2–58.23:** The second proposal and Darcy's self-accounting ("from eight to twenty-eight") are moving and plain.
- **60.0–60.15:** Elizabeth teasing Darcy is light, modern-sounding and still in period.
- **60.23:** Mr. Bennet's three-line letter to Collins.

**Readability summary.** The edition reads fluently and keeps its register. Austen's irony, the characters' voices and the period formality all survive, and I found no slang, therapy-speak or modern attitudes. Dialogue is the strongest part throughout. Most problems are local:

- a pronoun whose referent shifts to another person within a sentence;
- a handful of long periodic sentences in Chapter 50, where the narrator stays close to Elizabeth's thoughts;
- a few period words ("living", "debts of honor", "wilderness") where a two- or three-word gloss would help.

The only real comprehension problem is 55.20, where Mr. Bennet and Bingley are swapped mid-sentence. Fixing that, plus about a dozen one-line referent and phrasing touch-ups, would make chapters 49–61 fully accessible to a general reader or listener.
