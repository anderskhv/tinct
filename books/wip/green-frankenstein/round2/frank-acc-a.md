## Findings

1. **Ch4 ¶38** — "terrible the storm that struck the gallant vessel on its course and wrecked it — thus!" Archaic inverted word order dropped into modernized prose at the exact hinge into the frame narrator's tale; a listener may need a beat to parse it. Non-blocking.

2. **Ch5 ¶8** — Untranslated Italian phrase "schiavi ognor frementi," glossed immediately after ("the 'ever-chafing slaves'"). Fine on the page; slightly awkward read aloud with no pronunciation cue. Non-blocking.

3. **Ch6 ¶1** — One sentence stacks four separate historical/legendary references (Roncesvalles, the Round Table, the Crusades, the Holy Sepulchre), each individually glossed but heavy to track by ear in one breath. Non-blocking; would benefit from a sentence break.

4. **Ch7 ¶16** — "cried the soul of Frankenstein" — momentary lurch from first-person narration to third-person self-reference and back, which could make a first-time reader think a second character has appeared. Non-blocking (recoverable from surrounding "I").

5. **Ch8 ¶11** — "Cæsar" uses a classical ligature spelling found nowhere else in the reviewed chapters (inconsistent with the otherwise fully modernized spelling), and the Caesar reference itself is asserted without any explanation of why it fits the sentence's point. Non-blocking.

6. **Ch9 ¶8** — The Coleridge citation "[Coleridge's 'Ancient Mariner.']" is dropped in as its own bare paragraph right after the quoted verse — inconsistent with how the same poem was glossed earlier in Letter 2 (folded into a full explanatory sentence). Reads oddly as a stray footnote, especially in audio. Non-blocking.

7. **Ch10 ¶8** — "He already has one or two little _wives_" — the old idiom for a small child's playmates/crushes could genuinely read as literal and confusing until the next clause resolves it. Non-blocking.

8. **Ch14 ¶3 — BLOCKING.** A full stanza of quoted verse (Percy Shelley's "Mutability") is inserted directly into first-person prose narration with no lead-in, no attribution, and no signal that a quotation/different register is starting. The prose is running along clearly, then abruptly becomes dense, syntactically inverted verse ("be it joy or sorrow," "nothing may endure but mutability"), then the narrative resumes on the next paragraph as though nothing happened. A first-time reader — and especially a listener with no visual/typographic cue that this is a poem — would likely be genuinely confused about who is speaking and why the language suddenly becomes so much harder to follow. This needs at least a framing clause (e.g., "the poet's words came back to me:") to read clearly.

## What reads well
The bulk of the text flows naturally and reads as clean, contemporary prose: Walton's letters (chapters 1–3), Victor's account of his childhood and Elizabeth's arrival (chapter 5), the Cornelius Agrippa / Professor Krempe / Professor Waldman sequence (chapters 6–7, including the professors' dialogue, which lands with real voice), the creation-night sequence (chapter 9), and the storm/first-sighting-of-the-creature sequence (chapter 11) are all clear, vivid, and easy to follow by ear despite their length. Foreign/period references (Ancient Mariner in Letter 2, Cornelius Agrippa, galvanism, chamois, syndics) are consistently and helpfully glossed inline the first time they appear — the one exception is the Ch9/Ch14 quoted-verse handling flagged above.
