## Findings

1. **Ch. 15, ¶3 — `[The moon]`** (flow, **blocking**). A bracketed gloss is dropped mid-sentence to identify "a radiant form rising from among the trees." In print this reads as an editorial footnote; read aloud (this text is also played as audio) it lands as an abrupt, unexplained fragment with no grammatical connection to the sentence around it. This is the one finding I'd call blocking for audio: the sentence should either fold "the moon" into the prose naturally (e.g., with a dash) or drop the gloss since "a radiant form" is later confirmed as the moon anyway.

2. **Ch. 22, ¶21 — `[Wordsworth's "Tintern Abbey."]`** (consistency, non-blocking). Same print-footnote convention appears again as its own paragraph right after a quoted verse. Not blocking on its own (a listener can infer it's a citation), but it's part of an inconsistent pattern — see next two findings — in how borrowed/quoted language is marked across this stretch.

3. **Ch. 22, ¶19 — `"the very poetry of nature"`** (consistency, non-blocking). Quotation marks around this phrase with no speaker or source given, unlike the Wordsworth line two paragraphs later which does get a citation. A general reader has no way to know this, too, is a borrowed phrase.

4. **Ch. 19, ¶5 — `'The path of my departure was free,'`** (reference, non-blocking). Another unmarked, uncited quotation dropped into the narrator's own reflection with a jarring shift in register. Combined with findings 2–3, this is a recurring inconsistency in how quoted/borrowed phrases are handled: sometimes bracket-cited, sometimes quoted with no explanation, sometimes (implicitly) folded in unmarked. Worth a single pass to standardize, though none of these three individually blocks comprehension.

5. **Ch. 23, ¶7 — `the likable Falkland, the arrogant Goring, his queen, and his son`** (reference, non-blocking). Falkland and Goring are named with only one adjective each and no anchoring beyond "Charles I had gathered his forces" earlier in the same paragraph. A listener unfamiliar with the English Civil War will likely not place who these people are, though the adjectives carry enough sense that the sentence isn't actively confusing — a minor stumble, not a blocker. (The related "the great Hampden" reference two paragraphs later, ch. 23 ¶9, is self-explanatory via "that patriot fell" and needs no fix.)

6. **Ch. 22, ¶20 — Wordsworth "Tintern Abbey" excerpt** (archaic, non-blocking, flagged for awareness only). The inverted syntax and archaic diction ("Unborrow'd from the eye") is genuinely a stumble on a single listen. I'm not proposing a rewrite — it's a verbatim quotation of a real historical poem, and modernizing Wordsworth's own lines would misrepresent the quotation. Noting it only so the merge owner is aware this passage will read/sound dense regardless.

## What's working well

The prose modernization across this stretch is strong overall:
- Character names (Felix, Agatha, Safie, De Lacey, Victor, Elizabeth, Clerval, Walton) are used with complete consistency; no spelling drift anywhere in the 549 paragraphs.
- Deliberately preserved place-name spellings (Chamounix, Strasburgh, Havre-de-Grace) and the Creature's epithets (fiend, demon, wretch) read naturally and don't trip the ear.
- Long, information-dense passages — the cottagers' backstory (ch. 18), the Creature's negotiation with Frankenstein (ch. 21), the pursuit across the ice (ch. 28) — are broken into clear, well-paced sentences that read well aloud despite covering complex plot and emotional shifts.
- In-line glossing is mostly done well and could be a model for the flagged spots above: e.g. "a dull, ugly sirocco — the hot desert wind" (ch. 21) and "Volney's *Ruins of Empires* ... its grand, declamatory style was modeled on Eastern authors" (ch. 17) both explain unfamiliar terms smoothly within the sentence rather than via a bracket aside.
- Dialogue throughout the trial (ch. 25) and the final confrontation between Walton and the Creature (ch. 28) is clean, distinct between speakers, and free of archaic constructions.

## Verdict

**CLEAN** (needs targeted fixes only) — one blocking finding (the bracketed moon gloss in ch. 15), five non-blocking items clustered around inconsistent treatment of quoted/borrowed phrases and two unexplained historical-figure references. No broader pass is warranted; the modernization reads naturally start to finish across all 14 chapters.
