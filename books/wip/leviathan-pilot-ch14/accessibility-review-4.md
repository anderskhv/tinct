# Accessibility Review — Round 4 (Reviewer A, fresh/blind)

**Coverage:** I read all 14 paragraphs (indices 0–13) of
`candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to finish, then went back through
paragraph by paragraph. I was not shown the source text, any prior review,
or any drafter's notes.

**Book/chapter context given to me:** Hobbes's "state of nature" chapter
(Ch. 13 of Leviathan), modern-English reading edition, round 4 of review
(after 3 prior rounds of fixes).

---

## Paragraph-by-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 1** — "But this proves rather that men are equal in that point
than unequal."
Inverted comparative construction ("proves rather X than Y"). Grammatically
legal but not how a contemporary reader phrases a comparison; causes a
small hitch before the sense lands. A plain contemporary order — "this
actually proves that men are equal in that point, not unequal" — would
remove the hitch.

**Paragraph 2** — "From this equality of ability arises equality of hope in
attaining our ends."
Inverted subject/verb order (verb-then-subject: "arises equality of
hope..."). Legible on a second pass but a general reader will likely
re-read it once to find the subject. This construction recurs a few times
in the chapter (see paragraph 3 below) — worth fixing as a pattern, not
just in one spot.

**Paragraph 3** — "there is no way for any man to make himself secure so
reasonable as Anticipation"
Same inverted-comparative pattern as paragraph 1 ("no way ... so
reasonable as X"). Minor but adds up alongside the paragraph-2 instance.

**Paragraph 3** — "Also, because there are some who take pleasure in
contemplating their own power in acts of conquest, and pursue it further
than their security requires, if others did not increase their own power
through invasion, though they would otherwise be glad to remain at ease
within modest bounds, they would not be able to survive for long by
relying on defense alone."
This is the hardest sentence in the chapter. It stacks a causal clause
("because there are some who...") inside a conditional ("if others did
not...") inside a concessive ("though they would otherwise be glad...")
before finally reaching the main clause. The pronoun reference also drifts:
it's unclear whether "others" and the first "they" refer to the
power-hungry conquerors just mentioned, or to the modest, defense-minded
majority — and whether the second "they" (who "would not be able to
survive") is the same group as the first "they." A reader has to stop and
work out who is doing what to whom. This reads as genuinely overloaded,
not just dense — it needs restructuring into two sentences with the actors
named explicitly, not just simplified vocabulary.

**Paragraph 4** — "men take no pleasure (but on the contrary a great deal
of grief) in keeping company where there is no power able to overawe them
all"
"Overawe" is uncommon in everyday contemporary usage; most readers will
recognize it but it's a small stumble ("dominate" or "keep them in check"
would land faster).

**Paragraph 6** — "whether aimed directly at their own persons or
reflected onto their kindred, their friends, their nation, their
profession, or their name."
"Kindred" is a mildly archaic word choice next to the otherwise plain list
("friends," "nation," "profession"). Not a hard stumble but slightly
inconsistent register — "family" would match the rest of the list.

**Paragraph 7** — "For WAR consists not in battle only, or the act of
fighting, but in a stretch of time during which the will to contend by
battle is sufficiently known."
"The will to contend by battle is sufficiently known" is abstract and
passive-feeling ("known" to whom?). A reader has to work to convert this
into the plain idea — that both sides are known to be willing to fight.
This is the one place in an otherwise strong paragraph (the War/Weather
analogy right after it reads very well) that still asks for extra
decoding work.

**Paragraph 8** — "In such a condition there is no place for Industry,
because its fruit is uncertain"
Capitalized "Industry" risks a false-friend misread: today "industry"
overwhelmingly means the manufacturing/business sector, not
"industriousness" or "hard, purposeful work." The list that follows
("cultivation," "navigation," "building") eventually disambiguates it, but
a reader hits the false reading first. The same word recurs uncapitalized
with the same intended meaning in paragraph 13 ("a hope, by their
industry, of obtaining them") — same risk there, smaller because it's
already framed by "hope ... of obtaining."

**Paragraph 10** — "except for the government of small families, whose
harmony depends on natural lust — that is, natural appetite"
"Lust" today reads overwhelmingly as sexual desire. The appositive gloss
("that is, natural appetite") does repair it, but only after the reader
has already momentarily parsed the wrong meaning. A reader coming to this
cold will likely do a double-take before the gloss corrects course.

**Paragraph 11** — "But because by this they uphold the industry of their
subjects, that misery which accompanies the liberty of individual men does
not follow from it."
This sentence is dense and its pronouns are hard to anchor. "By this" and
the final "it" both need to be traced back to "the state and posture of
gladiators" two sentences earlier, across an intervening clause about
forts/garrisons/spies — by the time the reader reaches "it," the
antecedent is no longer close at hand. The causal claim itself (why
maintaining a war posture "upholds industry" and thereby prevents a
specific "misery") is also compressed to the point of being hard to
reconstruct on a first read. This is a candidate for splitting into two
sentences with the antecedents named directly.

**Paragraph 12** — "To this war of every man against every man, this also
follows: that nothing can be unjust."
The preposition is slightly off for contemporary usage — "to this ... this
follows" reads oddly; "from this" is the construction a modern reader
expects. Small, but it's a stumble at the very top of an otherwise strong,
punchy paragraph ("Force and fraud are, in war, the two cardinal
virtues.").

**Paragraph 12** — "no distinction between mine and thine"
Archaic pronoun pair, but this phrase ("mine and thine") is a
well-recognized idiom even in contemporary English (commonly used in
discussions of property/ethics), so I'm flagging it only as a minor note,
not a real stumble.

## What's working well

- The famous line in paragraph 8 — "the life of man, solitary, poor,
  nasty, brutish, and short" — lands with full force and needed no
  softening.
- Paragraph 7's War/Weather analogy ("as the nature of foul weather lies
  not in a shower or two of rain but in an inclination to it over many
  days together...") is genuinely elegant and reads as natural
  contemporary prose while preserving the original's logic.
- Paragraph 9's cascade of rhetorical questions ("What opinion does he
  have of his fellow subjects, when he rides armed? ... Does he not
  thereby accuse mankind by his actions as much as I do by my words?") is
  vivid, well-paced, and one of the strongest passages in the chapter.
- Paragraph 11's gladiator image ("in the state and posture of gladiators,
  with their weapons pointing and their eyes fixed on one another") is
  concrete and immediately visual — it's only the sentence that follows it
  (flagged above) that loses the thread.
- The chapter's terms of art — Competition, Diffidence, Glory (paragraphs
  5–6) — are each immediately and cleanly glossed at first use, which is
  good practice carried consistently through the whole chapter.
- Overall pacing (short paragraphs building toward the "war of every man
  against every man" formulation) is well judged; nothing feels
  mechanically chopped or stitched-together.

---

## Verdict

**Needs targeted fixes** — not yet "substantially accessible" in the sense
of "all remaining difficulty is unavoidable conceptual complexity."

To be explicit about what's avoidable vs. not: Hobbes's underlying
argument (equality → competing claims → war of all against all → the
passions/reason that point toward peace) is inherently abstract
political-philosophy content, and that conceptual difficulty is
unavoidable and appropriate to preserve. But several of the issues above
are wording problems, not concept problems, and are fixable without losing
meaning:

1. The tangled, ambiguous-pronoun sentence in paragraph 3 ("Also, because
   there are some who take pleasure...") — the hardest sentence in the
   chapter, needs restructuring with named actors.
2. The similarly tangled, ambiguous-pronoun sentence in paragraph 11
   ("But because by this they uphold the industry...").
3. A recurring inverted-syntax pattern ("proves rather X than Y" in
   paragraph 1; "arises equality of hope" in paragraph 2; "no way ... so
   reasonable as" in paragraph 3) that could be flattened to
   subject-first, contemporary order throughout.
4. The false-friend risk on capitalized "Industry" (paragraph 8, echoed in
   13), which invites a wrong first reading before the surrounding list
   corrects it.
5. Smaller vocabulary/register spots: "overawe" (paragraph 4), "kindred"
   (paragraph 6), the "lust"/"natural appetite" gloss (paragraph 10), and
   "to this war... this also follows" (paragraph 12).

None of these require touching the chapter's ideas or its more successful
passages (paragraphs 7's weather analogy, 9's rhetorical questions, and
the "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" line should all be left
exactly as they are). The fixes are localized — two sentences that need
restructuring for clarity, a handful of word swaps, and flattening one
recurring inverted-syntax habit — which is consistent with a chapter that
has already been through three rounds of cleanup and is close, but not
quite at, a clean pass.
