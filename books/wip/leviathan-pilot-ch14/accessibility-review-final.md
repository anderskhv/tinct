# Accessibility Review — Final Pass (Reviewer A)

**Book / chapter:** Leviathan, edition chapter 14 ("Chapter 13. Of the Naturall Condition of Mankind, as Concerning Their Felicity, and Misery" — the "state of nature" chapter)

**Coverage:** Read all 14 paragraphs (indices 0–13) of `candidate-sonnet.json`, in full, start to finish, then re-read paragraph by paragraph. No source text or prior review notes were consulted.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph notes

**Paragraph 0** — Clear. The opening claim (rough equality of strength and wit) reads naturally; "by secret scheming or by joining with others who are in the same danger as himself" resolves the logic without strain.

**Paragraph 1** — Two spots worth flagging, both minor:
- "nor is it acquired, as Prudence is, while pursuing something else" — the sentence elides a verb ("acquired") the reader has to mentally supply a second time to parse "as Prudence is [acquired], while pursuing something else." It's grammatically legal and resolves on a re-read, but it is a genuine small hitch, not just density.
- "which almost all men think they have in a greater degree than the common run of people — that is, than everyone but themselves and a few others whom they approve of — either by reputation, or because those others agree with them." The final "either by reputation, or because those others agree with them" is a little detached from its referent (why the "few others" are approved of); a reader has to hold the sentence open an extra beat to land it. Still resolvable without real confusion.
Neither of these caused a misreading on my pass — both are "read it once more, carefully" rather than "get it wrong." I'd call them stylistic rather than blocking.

**Paragraph 2** — Clear. "Equality of hope in attaining our ends arises from this equality of ability" reads well; the invader/invaded logic that follows is easy to track.

**Paragraph 3** — Clear. "Anticipation" is introduced and defined in the same breath ("by force or by cunning to master the persons of all men he can"), so the technical term doesn't strand the reader.

**Paragraph 4** — Clear. Natural, well-paced; "expects his companion to value him at the same rate he sets on himself" lands cleanly.

**Paragraph 5** — Clear. The three-part list (Competition, Diffidence, Glory) is crisp, and "Diffidence (that is, mutual distrust)" is a good in-line gloss.

**Paragraph 6** — Clear. "The first uses violence to make themselves masters of other men's persons, wives, children, and cattle" has a singular/plural pronoun wobble ("the first ... themselves") but this is idiomatic modern English (singular "themselves") and did not slow me down.

**Paragraph 7** — Reads unusually well. The definition of WAR as a "stretch of time during which the will to fight by battle is plain to see," and the weather analogy that follows ("the nature of foul weather lies not in a shower or two of rain but in an inclination to it over many days together"), is genuinely elegant — clear, concrete, and it preserves the force of the original argument without archaism.

**Paragraph 8** — Clear, and notably strong. The famous closing clause ("solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short") is kept verbatim, which is correct here — it's the best-known line in the book and any paraphrase would be a worse reading experience, not a better one. The list construction before it ("no cultivation of the earth; no navigation... no arts; no letters; no society") reads naturally as a rhetorical list, not as mechanically chopped prose.

**Paragraph 9** — Clear. The "locks his doors / locks his chests" argument-from-experience is vivid and easy to follow; the direct-address rhetorical questions land naturally in modern English.

**Paragraph 10** — Clear. "whose harmony depends on natural lust — that is, natural appetite" defines the potentially confusing word "lust" (which now reads as narrower/more sexual than Hobbes meant) right where it's needed, which is the correct fix.

**Paragraph 11** — Clear. The gladiator image ("in the state and posture of gladiators, with their weapons pointing and their eyes fixed on one another") is vivid and immediately legible; the parenthetical translation into forts/garrisons/spies resolves any ambiguity about what the metaphor means concretely.

**Paragraph 12** — Clear. The Justice/Injustice argument is dense in content (this is Hobbes's actual philosophical move) but the sentences are short and sequential — "Where there is no common power, there is no law; where no law, no injustice" — which is exactly the right shape for a hard idea.

**Paragraph 13** — Clear. Short, clean bridge to the next chapters.

## Overall readability

This chapter reads as clear, natural contemporary prose almost throughout. The famous passages (the definition of war, the weather analogy, "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," the gladiator image) all land with real force in modern English — several paragraphs (7, 8, 9, 11) read as genuinely well-crafted, not merely acceptable. The chapter's difficulty, where it exists, is the difficulty of the argument itself (an unusual definition of "war" as a standing disposition rather than an event; the move from psychological equality to a state of mutual threat; the derivation of "no injustice" from "no common power") — that is Hobbes's own philosophical content, not an accessibility problem, and no rendering of this chapter could remove it without cutting the argument.

## Verdict: substantially accessible

The two items flagged in paragraph 1 are the only wording-level friction I found across all 14 paragraphs, and on reflection I do not think either rises to a blocking defect. Both resolve correctly on the reader's own re-read, neither produces a wrong reading, and both are the kind of compression that shows up in careful expository prose generally (not archaism, not unresolved 17th-century syntax, not unglossed vocabulary). Given this chapter has already been through 6 rounds of fixes with shrinking returns, I would not hold it for these — they read as taste-level tightening opportunities, not comprehension stumbles. Everything else that makes this chapter hard is Hobbes's own conceptual density, correctly preserved rather than dumbed down, and it comes through in prose that is otherwise unusually smooth and often genuinely good.

**Recommendation:** accept as substantially accessible. No further accessibility-driven revision needed.
