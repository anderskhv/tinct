# Accessibility Review — Round 5 (Reviewer A, fresh read)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 13 — "Of the Naturall Condition of Mankind, as
Concerning Their Felicity, and Misery" (pilot ch. 14 slot)

**Coverage:** I read all 14 paragraphs (indices 0–13) of
`candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to finish, then went back
paragraph by paragraph. I was not shown the source text, prior review
notes, or drafter's notes.

---

## Paragraph-by-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 1** — "Set aside, for now, skills that depend on the use of words..."
- *"nor is it acquired, as Prudence is, while pursuing something else"* — the
  parenthetical "as Prudence is" briefly derails the clause; a reader parses
  "acquired, as Prudence is" as one unit and then has to backtrack to attach
  "while pursuing something else" to "acquired." Reads as two ideas welded
  together rather than one clean thought.
- *"than the common run of people — that is, than everyone but themselves and
  a few others whom they approve of — either by reputation, or because those
  others agree with them"* — three levels of qualification stacked in one
  sentence (comparison → redefinition of the comparison → reason for the
  redefinition). A reader can lose track of what "either...or" is modifying
  and has to re-read to confirm it attaches to "approve of," not to "wiser."

**Paragraph 7** — "...they are in that condition which is called War..."
- *"but in a stretch of time during which the will to fight by battle is
  plain to see"* — "the will to fight by battle" is an odd, slightly
  redundant collocation ("fight by battle") that reads as a stumble rather
  than a deliberate phrase. A reader has to pause and reparse to get the
  intended meaning ("a known readiness to fight").

**Paragraph 9** — "It may seem strange to someone who has not carefully weighed these things..."
- *"Nor are the actions that proceed from those passions sinful, until men
  know a law that forbids them — which, until laws are made, they cannot
  know; nor can any law be made until they have agreed upon the person who
  is to make it."* — three chained "until" conditions plus a "nor...nor"
  structure in one sentence. The logic itself (no sin without known law → no
  law known until made → no law made until a lawmaker is agreed on) is
  Hobbes's real chain of reasoning, but the sentence's grammar makes the
  chain harder to follow than the reasoning requires — a reader is likely to
  need a second pass to keep the three conditions straight.

**Paragraph 11** — "But though there had never been any time in which individual men..."
- *"But because this posture of war upholds the industry of their subjects,
  the misery that accompanies the liberty of individual men does not follow
  from it."* — the pronoun "it" (referring back to "posture of war," two
  clauses earlier) is not obviously anchored, and the sentence asks the
  reader to hold two comparisons at once (individual-state-of-war vs.
  kings'-state-of-war) without restating which is which. A general reader
  will likely have to re-read to confirm what "it" points to and why the
  earlier-described misery doesn't apply here.

**Paragraph 12** — "From this war of every man against every man, this also follows..."
- *"If they were, they might be in a man who was alone in the world, just as
  well as his senses and passions."* — grammatically legal but elliptical:
  the comparison's second half ("as well as his senses and passions [are
  present in a solitary man]") is implied, not stated, which asks the reader
  to reconstruct a whole clause. Minor, but a genuine stumble point.

---

## What's working well

- **Paragraph 0** (opening) reads cleanly and naturally start to finish —
  good, unforced modern syntax with no loss of Hobbes's claim.
- **Paragraph 1**, *"They see their own wit close at hand, and other men's at
  a distance."* — this is a small gem: vivid, literary, and completely clear.
  Exactly the kind of line that should stay untouched.
- **Paragraph 5**, the three-causes list ("Competition... Diffidence...
  Glory") and its paragraph-6 elaboration read crisply — good scaffolding,
  easy to track.
- **Paragraph 7**, the weather metaphor — *"as the nature of foul weather
  lies not in a shower or two of rain but in an inclination to it over many
  days together"* — is smooth, natural, and lands the analogy well.
- **Paragraph 8**, the famous closing image ("solitary, poor, nasty,
  brutish, and short") is preserved intact and reads as intended — this is a
  case where the historical phrasing *is* the right choice, not an
  accessibility problem.
- **Paragraph 11**, the gladiators image — *"in the state and posture of
  gladiators, with their weapons pointing and their eyes fixed on one
  another"* — is vivid and immediately legible.

---

## Overall verdict: needs targeted fixes (not yet fully "substantially accessible")

This chapter is close, and round 4's evident work shows: I did not find any
unresolved archaic verb forms, unglossed technical terms, or chapter-wide
syntax problems. Most paragraphs (0, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13, and most of 11)
read as clean, natural modern prose.

But it has **not yet reached the point where all remaining difficulty is
unavoidable conceptual complexity**. There are five identifiable sentences —
quoted above, in paragraphs 1 (x2), 7, 9, and 11 (with a minor sixth in
paragraph 12) — where the *difficulty is in the wording itself*, not in
Hobbes's underlying idea: an odd collocation ("will to fight by battle"), an
ambiguous pronoun referent ("does not follow from it"), and stacked
parentheticals/conditionals that could be unpacked into more linear sentences
without losing meaning or literary register. These are narrow, sentence-level
fixes, not a sign the chapter needs a broader rewrite — but they are still
avoidable, and a general reader will genuinely stumble or re-read at each of
these five points.
