# Accessibility Review — Round 2 (Reviewer A, fresh/blind)

**Book / chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 13 ("Of the Naturall Condition of Mankind, as Concerning Their Felicity, and Misery") — modern-English reading edition, candidate-sonnet.

**Coverage:** Read all 14 paragraphs (indices 0–13) in full, start to finish, then re-read paragraph by paragraph. Did not read source.json, current-modern-en.json, or any prior review file — this is a blind, fresh pass with no knowledge of what Round 1 flagged.

---

## Paragraph-by-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 1** ("And as for the faculties of the mind...")
- *"setting aside the arts grounded on words"* — vague for a general reader. It's not clear what this refers to (rhetoric/grammar/logic); the phrase doesn't parse on its own and the reader has to guess from context.
- *"nor acquired, as Prudence is, while we are pursuing something else"* — the syntax here is compressed to the point of ambiguity. It takes a re-read to work out that this means Prudence/experience is picked up incidentally, in the course of doing other things — the sentence doesn't say that plainly.
- *"whom, by reputation or for agreeing with themselves, they approve of"* — "for agreeing with themselves" is awkward; a reader has to stop and work out that it means "because those people agree with them," not some other relation.

**Paragraph 3** ("And from this mutual distrust...")
- *"if others, who would otherwise be glad to be at ease within modest bounds, did not by invasion increase their own power, they would not be able, for any length of time, to survive by standing on the defensive alone."* — this is a single overloaded conditional with the subject ("others") reintroduced as "they" across a long embedded clause. On first read it's easy to lose track of who is invading whom and who "would not be able to survive." This needs a re-read to parse correctly.
- *"such enlargement of dominion over other men, being necessary to a man's preservation, ought to be allowed him."* — "allowed him" (rather than "allowed to him") is an archaic word order that will read as slightly off/unfinished to a contemporary reader.

**Paragraph 4** ("Again, men take no pleasure...")
- *"he naturally endeavors, so far as he dares (which, among those who have no common power to keep them at peace, is far enough to make them destroy one another), to extort a greater value from those who show him contempt"* — the parenthetical is inserted between the verb ("endeavors") and its object ("to extort"), so the reader has to hold the main clause in suspension across a fairly long aside. This is the kind of grammatically-legal-but-overloaded construction the prompt calls out — reads as dense/stitched rather than smooth.

**Paragraph 7** ("From this it is plain...")
- *"they are in that condition which is called War, and such a war as is of every man against every man"* — "such a war as is of every man against every man" is an archaic relative construction that doesn't read as natural contemporary prose; a reader will likely stumble on "as is of."

**Paragraph 8** ("Whatever, therefore, follows...")
- *"Whatever, therefore, follows from a time of war, in which every man is enemy to every man, the same is consequent to the time in which men live with no other security than what their own strength and their own invention will furnish them with."* — "the same is consequent to the time" is a legalistic inversion (normal order would be something like "the same holds for the time..."). This is the single hardest sentence in the chapter to parse on a first read; a reader plausibly has to go back and reread it to recover the logical structure (whatever follows from war-time follows equally from the state-of-nature time).

**Paragraph 10** ("It may perhaps be thought...")
- *"except for the government of small families, whose harmony depends on natural lust — that is, instinctive desire, not romance — have no government at all"* — the "except for..." clause is separated from the verb phrase it modifies ("have no government at all") by a long insertion, so on first pass it can read as though family government is being contrasted with something else rather than being the sole exception noted. Needs a re-read to see that the sentence is saying: these peoples have no government at all, except for family government.
- *"it may be perceived what kind of life there would be where there was no common power to fear, from the kind of life that men who have formerly lived under a peaceful government tend to degenerate into, in a civil war."* — the governing phrase ("from the kind of life...") is placed far from the verb it completes ("it may be perceived... from the kind of life..."), with a further embedded relative clause in between. This is grammatically legal but genuinely hard to hold together in one pass.

**Paragraph 11** ("But though there had never been...")
- *"there does not follow from it that misery which accompanies the liberty of individual men."* — inverted subject-verb order ("there does not follow from it that misery," rather than "that misery does not follow from it"). Reads as archaic/legalistic and will cause at least a momentary stumble.

**Paragraph 12** ("To this war of every man against every man...")
- *"but only that to be every man's which he can get, and for as long as he can keep it."* — archaic inverted construction ("that... which" fronted); a contemporary reader will likely need to reread this clause to parse it as "only what he can get is every man's."
- Minor/borderline: *"though with a possibility of coming out of it, lying partly in the passions and partly in his reason"* — the participial phrase's referent (the possibility) is not restated, so it reads as a slightly dangling modifier. Milder than the items above but worth a look.

## What reads well (worth preserving as-is)

- Paragraph 5–6: "Diffidence (that is, mutual distrust)" and the Competition/Diffidence/Glory framing — the glosses are placed exactly where needed and don't interrupt the sentence. Very clean.
- Paragraph 3: "Anticipation — that is, by force or by cunning to master the persons of all men he can" — same technique, works well.
- Paragraph 7: the weather/war analogy ("For as the nature of foul weather lies not in a shower or two of rain but in an inclination to it over many days together...") is genuinely clear and reads naturally despite being a complex analogy — this paragraph is a model for the rest of the chapter.
- Paragraph 8: "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" and the surrounding list ("no cultivation of the earth; no navigation... no arts; no letters; no society") is appropriately left dense and rhythmic rather than smoothed out — this is a case where the famous cadence should not be broken up, and it isn't.
- Paragraph 9: the sequence of rhetorical questions ("What opinion does he have of his fellow subjects, when he rides armed? Of his fellow citizens, when he locks his doors? And of his children and servants, when he locks his chests?") is the clearest, most natural-sounding stretch in the chapter — nothing to fix here.
- Paragraph 12: "Where there is no common power, there is no law; where no law, no injustice." — crisp, aphoristic, and fully accessible while keeping Hobbes's famous parallel structure intact.

## Verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

Explicit answer to the round-2 question: the chapter has **not yet** reached the point where all remaining difficulty is unavoidable conceptual complexity. There is a real, identifiable cluster of avoidably-difficult wording left — concentrated in a handful of archaic subject-verb inversions and overloaded parenthetical/conditional sentences:

- Archaic inverted constructions that read as legalistic rather than contemporary: "the same is consequent to the time..." (P8), "there does not follow from it that misery..." (P11), "but only that to be every man's which he can get..." (P12), "ought to be allowed him" (P3).
- Overloaded sentences where a parenthetical or subordinate clause is wedged between a verb and its object/complement, forcing the reader to hold the main clause in suspension: the endeavor/extort sentence (P4), the "if others... did not by invasion increase..." conditional (P3), the "it may be perceived... from the kind of life..." sentence (P10).
- A couple of vague/compressed phrases that don't parse cleanly on their own: "the arts grounded on words" (P1), "nor acquired, as Prudence is, while we are pursuing something else" (P1), "for agreeing with themselves" (P1), the "except for the government of small families..." clause (P10).

None of these are conceptually hard — Hobbes's actual ideas here (equality of hope leading to conflict, the three causes of quarrel, the definition of war as a disposition rather than continuous fighting, the state of nature having no justice) come through clearly once the sentences are parsed. The difficulty is syntactic: leftover period word order (fronted clauses, "consequent to," "allowed him," "that... which" constructions) that a contemporary reader has to mentally reorder before the sentence resolves. That's avoidable — these can be rewritten into contemporary word order without touching the ideas, several proper nouns/terms (Diffidence, Anticipation, WAR/PEACE) or the deliberately-preserved famous lines ("solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"; "Where there is no common power, there is no law; where no law, no injustice").

**Summary:** This is a substantially stronger draft than the prompt's baseline complaint categories would suggest — most of the chapter (the causes-of-quarrel taxonomy, the war/weather analogy, the armed-traveler passage, the closing aphorisms) reads as natural, confident contemporary prose, and several of the hardest conceptual moves in the original (the Latin-inflected legal reasoning about property and injustice) have been rendered clearly. But roughly ten sentences, concentrated in paragraphs 1, 3, 4, 8, 10, 11, and 12, still carry inverted or overloaded 17th-century syntax that will cost a general reader a re-read. A targeted pass on those specific sentences — not a broader rewrite — should be enough to close the gap.
