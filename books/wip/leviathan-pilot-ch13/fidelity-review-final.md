## The two round-3 edits (the reason for this pass)

### 1. "Thumomancy, or Presage" → "Thumomancy, or foreboding" — ACCURATE

Source context: "Sometimes in their own hopes and feares, called Thumomancy, or
Presage". The divinatory source here is explicitly the diviner's *own* hopes and
fears, i.e. an internal premonitory feeling, not an external sign. "Presage" in
this construction is the noun sense *a presentiment / a feeling that something is
about to happen*. "Foreboding" carries exactly that sense in plain modern English.

- Claim unchanged: the practice is still named (Thumomancy), still glossed with an
  alternative name, still sourced to "a person's own hopes and fears".
- No change in certainty, actor, or causality.
- The mild negative skew of "foreboding" (bad-news presentiment) is not a
  distortion here — the paired term in Hobbes's own sentence is "feares", and the
  whole catalogue is presented as credulous superstition.
- The appositive structure "called Thumomancy, or foreboding" preserves the
  source's own "called X, or Y" pattern; no editorial voice added.

### 2. "by the Schoole-men" → "the schoolmen — that is, the scholastic theologians —" — ACCURATE, NO OVERREACH

Source: "and partly from bringing of the Philosophy, and doctrine of Aristotle into
Religion, by the Schoole-men; from whence there arose so many contradictions..."

- Identification is correct: in Hobbes's usage throughout *Leviathan*, the
  "Schoole-men" are the scholastic theologians of the medieval universities, and
  the specific charge here — importing Aristotle into religion, producing
  contradictions that discredited *the Clergy* — is a charge against theologians
  specifically, not against secular Aristotelians. The gloss narrows correctly
  rather than guessing.
- The original term "schoolmen" is retained, so nothing is silently substituted;
  the gloss is additive and marked as a gloss ("that is,").
- No overreach: the gloss does not add an evaluation, a date, an institution, a
  named figure, or a doctrinal characterization. It identifies who, and stops.
- Causality preserved exactly: schoolmen → Aristotle into religion →
  contradictions and absurdities → clergy's reputation for ignorance *and*
  fraudulent intent → people revolt, against their princes' will (France, Holland)
  or with it (England). Both branches of that final condition are intact.

Both edits are fidelity-neutral. Accessibility improved, claims untouched.

## Hobbes's blunt theses — NO SOFTENING

1. **Fear-origin of religion (paras 4–5, 9–10).** The Prometheus figure, the
   "perpetual fear... as if groping in the dark", "the gods were first created by
   human fear", and the endorsement "which, said of the gods (that is, of the many
   gods of the pagans), is very true" are all intact, including the crucial
   restriction of that endorsement to the pagan gods and the separate, *non*-fear
   derivation of the one eternal God from causal reasoning. The four-part "natural
   seed of religion" (ghosts, ignorance of secondary causes, devotion toward what
   is feared, chance events taken as omens) is complete and in order.
2. **Deification catalogue (paras 12–15).** Delivered at full length and full
   bluntness, including the onion and leek, the genitals-as-Priapus line, and
   "there was nothing a poet could bring into his poem as a character that they did
   not turn into either a god or a devil."
3. **Religion-as-statecraft (paras 11, 16, 23–26).** "The religion of the first
   kind is a branch of human politics"; the founders "whose whole purpose was
   simply to keep the people obedient and at peace"; the three-part technique
   (divine attribution, laws = the gods' will, appeasement ceremonies); the result
   that the people "were less inclined to rise up against their rulers" and
   "needed nothing else but bread"; Roman toleration and the single Jewish
   exception; and the summary line "And this is how the religion of the pagans
   functioned as a part of their statecraft." All present, none hedged.
   "Pretended to receive / pretended himself" is rendered as "claimed", which is
   the standard modern equivalent of the 17th-century sense and keeps the implied
   falsity carried by the surrounding frame ("whose whole purpose was simply to
   keep the people obedient").
4. **Necromancy as fraud (para 23).** "juggling and confederate knavery" →
   "trickery and organized fraud" — equal force, no softening.
5. **Closing indictment (para 31).** "unpleasing Priests" → "priests who make
   themselves disliked"; the whole list of Roman claims (coronation, priestly
   marriage, legitimacy of princes, release from allegiance, deposition of kings,
   clerical criminal immunity, mass fees and purgatory payments) is intact as a
   rhetorical question series, as is the concession that civil authority and custom
   sustain faith more than any belief in the clergy's sanctity, and the final
   barb — "not only among Catholics, but even within the church that has claimed
   the most credit for reforming itself." Not blunted.
