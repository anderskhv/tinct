## Paragraph-by-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 3** — "lacking the observation and memory needed to track the order, sequence, and dependence of the things they see." "Dependence" used as a bare noun here ("track ... the dependence of the things they see") is a slightly unnatural collocation — a reader has to mentally supply "causal dependence" to parse it smoothly. Minor stumble, one re-read at most.

**Paragraph 6** — "so called them ghosts — as the Romans called them Imagines and Umbrae — and believed them to be spirits..." The Latin terms *Imagines* and *Umbrae* are dropped in with no translation, only the surrounding gloss "ghosts." A reader who doesn't know Latin gets the general sense but not what those two specific words mean or how they differ from each other. This is very likely a required proper-noun/historical-term preservation rather than a wording choice — flagging for completeness, but I read it as largely unavoidable given the constraint to preserve historical terms.

**Paragraph 7** — "as when the Athenians, for their war at Lepanto, called for another Phormio, or the Pompeian faction, for their war in Africa, called for another Scipio." Four proper nouns (Lepanto, Phormio, Pompeian faction, Scipio) land in one clause with zero gloss. A general contemporary reader will not know who Phormio or Scipio were, or what these wars refer to, and the sentence gives no foothold — you can follow the grammatical point ("they wanted a repeat hero") only by inference, not because the names mean anything. This reads as inherent to the passage's content (a historical example Hobbes reaches for) rather than a rewording problem — the names themselves are almost certainly required to stay as proper nouns per the translation protocol. Still, this is a genuine stumbling point for readers and worth flagging.

**Paragraph 15** — "the plains with Pan and the Panises, or satyrs; the woods with fauns and nymphs; the sea with Tritons and other nymphs; every river and spring with a ghost bearing its name, and with nymphs of its own; every house with its Lares, or household spirits; every man with his own guardian genius; the underworld with ghosts and spirit officials such as Charon, Cerberus, and the Furies; and, at night, every place with Larvae, Lemures, the ghosts of the dead, and a whole kingdom of fairies and bogeymen." This is the densest proper-noun catalogue in the chapter. Most terms get a quick appositive gloss ("Panises, or satyrs"; "Lares, or household spirits"), which is good technique and genuinely helps. But "Larvae, Lemures, the ghosts of the dead" is ambiguous: is "the ghosts of the dead" restating what Larvae/Lemures are, or naming a fourth, separate category alongside them? A reader can't tell without outside knowledge, and the sentence doesn't resolve it. This one spot is a real (if small) accessibility gap that a light rewrite could close (e.g., "Larvae and Lemures — restless ghosts of the dead —").

**Paragraph 18** — the divination survey ("sometimes in the ambiguous or nonsensical answers given by the priests at Delphi... sometimes in the writings of the Sibyls... sometimes in the meaningless utterances of madmen... sometimes in the position of the stars... sometimes in a person's own hopes and fears... sometimes in the claims of witches... sometimes in the chance flight or feeding patterns of birds... sometimes in the entrails of a sacrificed animal... sometimes in dreams; sometimes in the croaking of ravens...; in chance remarks...; sometimes in monstrous or unusual occurrences...; and sometimes in pure lottery..."). This entire catalogue is one sentence, roughly 300+ words, held together by a chain of "sometimes in..." clauses. Each individual clause is well-glossed (the divination term is defined right where it appears, which is the right technique), but the sheer length and clause-count means a reader is likely to lose the thread of the governing verb ("They led people to believe they could learn their fortunes...") well before reaching the end, and will have to re-read the opening to reconnect it to "sometimes in pure lottery." This is a genuinely overloaded sentence by the prompt's own definition (grammatically legal, but too many coordinate clauses stacked without a break) — and, unlike the proper-noun issues above, this one looks avoidable: nothing here requires it to be a single sentence rather than three or four shorter ones (paragraph/order integrity wouldn't be affected by adding sentence breaks within the paragraph).

**Paragraph 23** — "For since every organized religion is founded, at the start, on the trust a large number of people place in a single person — someone they believe to be not only wise and working for their happiness, but also holy, someone to whom God himself chooses to reveal his will directly — it necessarily follows that once those who administer religion come under suspicion for their wisdom, their sincerity, or their goodwill, or once they can no longer produce any convincing sign of divine revelation, the religion they wish to maintain will likewise come under suspicion — and, wherever the civil authorities do not enforce it by fear, it will be contradicted and cast aside." One sentence, three nested em-dash asides, and a "since... it necessarily follows that..." structure spanning the whole thing. It's parseable but demanding — a reader has to hold the "since" clause active across two long interruptions before reaching "it necessarily follows." Like paragraph 18, this reads as an avoidable overload rather than unavoidable content difficulty; splitting after "divine revelation" (before "the religion they wish to maintain...") would preserve every idea while relieving the load.

**Paragraphs 4, 19, 21** (bracketed marginal notes: "[The natural cause of religion: anxiety about the future.]", "[The designs of the authors of the religion of the heathen.]", "[The true religion, and the laws of God's kingdom, are the same.]") — These are marginal-note-style headers folded into the start of the paragraph body as plain bracketed text. Without any typographic distinction (italics, a line break, a label) from the sentence that follows, a reader unfamiliar with the convention of inline marginal glosses may briefly read the bracket as part of the first sentence rather than as a heading, causing a momentary stumble at the top of each of these three paragraphs. I'm flagging this as a formatting/presentation issue rather than a wording issue — it may be outside what this candidate file (plain paragraph strings) can fix on its own, so I'd defer to the merge owner on whether it's addressable at this stage.

No other paragraphs produced a stumble, misread, or need-to-reread on this read-through.

## Where the chapter reads unusually well

- **Paragraph 4** (the Prometheus passage) is the standout of the chapter. The classical reference is fully unpacked in-line ("Prometheus — whose name, translated, means 'the prudent man' — was chained to Mount Caucasus, a place with a wide view over the land, where an eagle feeding on his liver ate by day exactly as much as grew back by night"), so a reader with zero prior knowledge of the myth still gets the full image and the point it's making, with real narrative propulsion. This is the model for how the other proper-noun-heavy passages could be brought up to the same standard.
- **Paragraph 27** ("scandalous, because they are stumbling blocks that trip people up on the path of religion") — the etymological aside lands naturally and adds clarity rather than friction.
- **Paragraph 37 [index 31]**, the closing rhetorical-question list ("For who fails to see whose interest is served by getting people to believe: that a king has no authority from Christ unless a bishop crowns him? That a king who is also a priest cannot marry?...") is genuinely propulsive and clear despite covering dense material — the parallel question structure carries the reader through a long list without strain. One of the better passages in the chapter.
- The divination-term glosses throughout paragraph 18 (defining each term immediately after naming the practice — "called Theomancy," "called horoscopy," "which is called necromancy... and is nothing but trickery and organized fraud") are individually excellent technique; the paragraph's only problem is cumulative length, not the glossing itself.
- The chapter's overall register is consistent and readable — declarative, unpretentious modern prose that doesn't reach for false elegance. Sentence-level word choice throughout is plain and correctly calibrated for a general adult reader; the difficulty that remains is concentrated in a handful of specific, locatable spots rather than spread evenly across the chapter.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes** — but narrowly. Of the issues above, only two are clearly avoidable through further editing without touching content or proper nouns: the single mega-sentence in **paragraph 18** and the triple-nested sentence in **paragraph 23**; both could be split into two or three sentences each with no loss of meaning or paragraph-count violation. The "Larvae, Lemures, the ghosts of the dead" ambiguity in **paragraph 15** is a small, fixable clarity gap. Everything else flagged — the untranslated Latin terms in paragraph 6, the unglossed historical names in paragraph 7 (Lepanto/Phormio/Scipio/Pompeian faction), and the dense proper-noun catalogue in paragraph 15 more broadly — is unavoidable conceptual/historical complexity inherent to a 17th-century survey of pagan religious practice, and is already handled about as well as prose can handle it (the drafter glosses most terms in-line where possible, as in "Pan and the Panises, or satyrs").

**Summary:** This is a strong, largely accessible rendering of a genuinely dense chapter — Hobbes surveying the psychological origins of religion and then cataloguing pagan deities and divination practices. The prose is plain, declarative, and consistently well-calibrated for a general reader; where names and Latin/Greek terms appear unglossed, that reflects the chapter's subject matter (a name-dense historical survey) rather than a translation shortcoming, and the drafter has already mitigated much of it with well-placed appositive glosses (the Prometheus passage in particular is excellent). The remaining friction is concentrated in two long, multi-clause sentences (paragraphs 18 and 23) that could be split for easier tracking, plus one small referential ambiguity (paragraph 15) — none of which require touching content, proper nouns, or paragraph structure to fix.
