## Paragraph 19 — the divination-survey paragraph (restructured this round)

This is the paragraph that catalogues oracles, Sibyls, Nostradamus, Enthusiasm/Theomancy, horoscopy, Thumomancy, necromancy, augury, Aruspicina, Metoposcopy, Portenta/Ostenta, and lottery-divination (Cross and Pile).

**Verdict: the restructuring works.** It now reads as seven distinct sentences rather than one overloaded run-on:

1. Intro sentence (natural vs. supernatural foretelling).
2. Delphi/Delos/Ammon oracle sentence.
3. Sibyls/Nostradamus sentence.
4. "Sometimes they looked to..." — Enthusiasm/Theomancy, horoscopy, Thumomancy, necromancy (four items on semicolons).
5. "And sometimes they looked to..." — augury, Aruspicina, dreams, raven/bird-chatter, Metoposcopy, palm-reading, omens, Portenta/Ostenta (the longest remaining sentence, but each item is followed by its own plain-English gloss, so it reads as a deliberate catalogue rather than a tangle).
6. "and sometimes to pure lottery..." — Cross and Pile, sieve-holes, bibliomancy with Homer/Virgil.
7. Closing sentence: "So easily are people led to believe anything..."

Each divination method is now immediately glossed in plain English at first mention (e.g., "the entrails of a sacrificed animal, called Aruspicina," "the features of a person's face, called Metoposcopy"), so a reader can follow the catalogue without needing outside knowledge of the Latin/Greek terms, even though the terms themselves are retained (correctly, per what I'd expect a translation protocol to require for proper-noun/technical-term preservation). The remaining density here is the unavoidable kind — it's a long list of named things, not a syntactically tangled sentence. This is a clear improvement over what a single 300+-word sentence would have read like.

**One residual issue in this paragraph:** "to a person's own hopes and fears, called Thumomancy, **or presage**" — the gloss word chosen for "Thumomancy" is itself an uncommon, archaic-flavored word. A general reader who doesn't already know "presage" (more often encountered as a verb, "to presage," rather than this nominal sense) won't be much better off than before the gloss was added. This is the one place in the paragraph where the fix-the-catalogue-with-a-plain-gloss strategy doesn't fully land, because the substitute word isn't actually plain. Suggest a clearer gloss such as "called Thumomancy, or fortune-telling from one's own wishes and worries" (or similar) — something a reader could parse without a dictionary.

## Other paragraphs — issues found

**Paragraph 31:** "why the schoolmen brought Aristotle's philosophy and doctrine into religion, giving rise to so many contradictions and absurdities" — "schoolmen" (meaning medieval scholastic theologians) is used with no gloss or contextual cue to its meaning. A modern reader is likely to picture literal schoolteachers rather than scholastic philosophers, which would garble the sentence's meaning (that academic theologians overcomplicated doctrine with imported Aristotelian logic). This reads as avoidable — a brief in-line gloss ("the schoolmen — the university theologians —") would fix it without disrupting flow.

**Paragraph 16:** "at night, every place with Larvae, Lemures, and the ghosts of the dead" — "Larvae" and "Lemures" (Roman terms for malevolent/wandering spirits of the dead) are left completely unglossed, unlike most of the neighboring terms in this same paragraph (Lares *is* glossed as "household spirits" a few words earlier). The immediate context ("and the ghosts of the dead") gives a plausible-enough clue that these are also spirit-related, so this is a minor stumble rather than a real comprehension failure — but it's inconsistent with how carefully other terms in the same list are glossed, so it's worth a look. This is the kind of name-catalogue paragraph where I'd expect proper nouns to be retained by design; flagging only the inconsistency in glossing, not the retention itself.

**Paragraph 8:** "as when the Athenians, for their war at Lepanto, called for another Phormio, or the Pompeian faction, for their war in Africa, called for another Scipio" — Phormio and Scipio are historical figures a general reader is unlikely to recognize, with no gloss. This reads as inherent historical-reference density rather than avoidable wording — the sentence is clear grammatically, the difficulty is not knowing who these people were, which is the kind of thing a proper-noun-preservation rule would call for keeping as-is.

**Paragraph 4:** "lacking the observation and memory needed to track the order, sequence, and dependence of the things they see" — "dependence" is used in the sense of causal dependency, which is slightly abstract on first read but is adequately supported by the surrounding words ("order, sequence"). Minor, not flagging as a fix-needed item.

No other paragraphs produced a stumble on this read-through. Paragraphs 1–3, 6, 9–15, 17–18, 20–30, and 32 read cleanly at a normal pace with no re-reads needed.

## Where the chapter reads unusually well

- **Paragraph 5** (Prometheus): the myth is explained economically and clearly within the sentence itself ("chained to Mount Caucasus... where an eagle feeding on his liver ate by day exactly as much as grew back by night") — a reader with zero prior knowledge of the myth can still follow the argument.
- **Paragraph 18**: "hybrid gods who were mere lodgers in heaven, such as Bacchus and Hercules" is a vivid, natural modern phrase that lands the point (demigods as half-belonging) without sounding anachronistic.
- **Paragraph 26**: "they are stumbling blocks that trip people up on the path of religion" is a clean, natural rendering of the "scandal" etymology — reads as if written in contemporary prose, not translated.
- **Paragraphs 9 and 25** read with notably good rhythm — short, declarative sentences that carry Hobbes's enumerations (types of worship; what destroys reputation for wisdom) without feeling choppy or list-like.
- **Paragraph 22**: the "general who commands an army may also have his own personal regiment" analogy for God's dual kingship is clear and well-paced.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** The paragraph 19 restructuring resolves what was clearly the chapter's hardest accessibility problem — the long, single-sentence divination catalogue now reads as a sequence of manageable, individually-glossed sentences. The remaining friction in the chapter is almost entirely the unavoidable kind: proper-noun catalogues (pagan deities, divination-method names, historical figures like Phormio/Scipio/Chilperic/Pope Zachary) that a translation protocol preserving historical content and allusions would require keeping in place. These are inherent conceptual/structural density, not fixable wording.

There is, however, a small amount of genuinely avoidable wording left:

1. **Paragraph 19** — "or presage" as the plain-English gloss for Thumomancy is itself obscure; needs a clearer substitute.
2. **Paragraph 31** — "the schoolmen" is unglossed and risks being misread by a general reader as literal schoolteachers.
3. **Paragraph 16** — "Larvae, Lemures" are left unglossed while a neighboring term (Lares) is glossed in the same sentence; minor inconsistency worth a look.

None of these are structural — they're single-word/phrase fixes. Once addressed, I'd expect this chapter to be fully clear of avoidable accessibility issues, with only the inherent catalogue-density remaining (which is expected and appropriate for this text).

**Summary:** The chapter reads clearly and holds together as an argument across all 32 paragraphs — Hobbes's chain of reasoning (fear/curiosity → invented causes → pagan religion as statecraft → why faith erodes) comes through without requiring the reader to reconstruct 17th-century syntax. The round-1 target paragraph (19) is fixed. Three small, isolated gloss-wording issues remain (listed above); everything else is either clean prose or unavoidable proper-noun density consistent with the translation protocol's preservation rules.
