# Accessibility Review 1 — Julius Caesar (`julius-caesar`, modern-en)

**Reviewer:** Claude Sonnet 5, blind pass (candidate.json only, no source.json consulted).
**Coverage statement:** Read all 18 chapters (Act/Scene units) of `candidate.json` in full,
start to finish, paragraph by paragraph — all 997 paragraphs read individually, none
skimmed or sampled.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** This is a genuine modern-English rendering, not a
mechanical cleanup: archaic verb forms, inversions, and vocabulary are consistently
rebuilt into ordinary contemporary sentence structure ("Doth not Brutus bootless
kneel?" → "Doesn't Brutus kneel in vain?"; "an eye ... shall not / gnaw ... my hair"-
type mangled syntax nowhere reproduced; blank-verse line breaks are prose-flowed
without losing sense units). Register, imagery, and rhetorical patterning (Antony's
"honorable men" refrain, Cassius's flattery of Brutus, Casca's blunt commentary) all
read naturally as spoken English.

## Specific wording flagged for difficulty

1. **Ch2 ¶41 (Cassius's Tiber-swimming speech)** — this is a long, information-dense
   single paragraph (Aeneas/Anchises allusion, the fever anecdote, "Titinius"). A
   general reader will likely have to re-read once for the two nested similes
   (Cassius-and-Caesar-in-the-river / Aeneas-and-Anchises), but the sentence
   structure itself is clear English, not archaic — the difficulty is the allusion
   density inherent to the scene, not the modernization.
2. **Ch4 ¶7 (Brutus's soliloquy — "It has to be by his death...")** — the serpent's-egg
   argument is abstract political reasoning; it reads as clear modern prose but
   requires attention to track the conditional logic ("since the quarrel can't be
   justified by what he is right now, frame it like this..."). Not a wording defect;
   flagging for the reader's benefit.
3. **Ch9 ¶35 (Antony's "Friends, Romans, countrymen" oration)** — reads very
   naturally, but the ironic refrain ("Brutus is an honorable man") depends on the
   reader tracking accumulating irony across a single long paragraph; a reader
   skimming could miss the turn. This is inherent to the rhetorical structure of the
   scene, not a modernization defect — flagging only as a natural point where a
   reader might slow down.
4. **Ch13 ¶39 (Cassius's "Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come...")** — dense
   emotional speech with the "Plutus's mine" allusion (god of wealth). "Plutus" is
   named but not glossed; a reader unfamiliar with the reference may not immediately
   grasp "richer than gold" refers to Brutus's love, but the surrounding text ("Inside,
   a heart dearer than Plutus's mine, richer than gold") supplies enough context that
   the sentence still lands even without recognizing the allusion.
5. **Ch16 ¶36 (Messala's "O hateful Error, child of Melancholy!")** — one of the few
   places where personification/apostrophe register stays elevated ("O Error,
   conceived so quickly, you never come to a happy birth, but kill the mother who
   engendered you"). This reads as deliberately poetic/eulogistic in context (funeral
   speech for Cassius) rather than as unmodernized archaism — noting it because its
   density stands out against the surrounding prose rhythm.

None of the above are vocabulary or archaic-construction defects — no sentence in the
candidate left an unresolved archaic verb form, an unglossed obsolete word, or a
mechanically chopped sentence. The five notes above are places where the *content*
(allusion density, rhetorical structure, argument complexity) asks more of a reader
than the wording does, which is a property of the play, not a modernization failure.

## What reads unusually well

- The Act 3 Scene 1 assassination sequence and Antony's funeral oration (Ch9) carry
  their tension and irony cleanly in modern syntax without losing the theatrical
  build.
- The Brutus/Cassius quarrel scene (Ch13) reads as natural heated conversation —
  short clipped exchanges land with real rhythm ("I am." / "I say you are not.").
- Working-class dialogue (Ch1 Cobbler/Carpenter, Ch10 Cinna-the-poet mob scene) keeps
  its comic timing and menace without becoming stilted.

## Summary

The chapter is genuinely accessible modern prose throughout. No blocking
accessibility defects found in this pass. The five items above are noted for the
merge owner's awareness (inherent difficulty, not wording problems) rather than as
required fixes.
