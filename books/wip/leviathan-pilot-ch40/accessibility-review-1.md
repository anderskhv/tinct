# Accessibility Review — Reviewer A (fresh, candidate-only)

**Chapter:** 40 / "Chapter 39. Of the Signification in Scripture of the Word Church"

## Coverage

I read all 5 paragraphs (indices 0–4) in full, start to finish, then went

Judged purely as English prose.

## Per-paragraph issues

### Paragraph 0

- **"the Greek fathers call it *Kuriake*, the Lord's house; and from that, in our language, it came to be called *Kyrke*, and Church."**
  Two unglossed foreign/archaic terms back to back (Kuriake, Kyrke). The
  sentence is doing etymology, so some unfamiliarity is the point, but a
  general reader may need a second pass to see that "Kyrke" is just an old
  spelling of "Church" and not a third, separate word being introduced.
  Minor stumble, not a blocker.

### Paragraph 1

- **"signifies the same thing Ecclesia signified in the Greek commonwealths: a congregation, or an assembly of citizens, called together to hear the magistrate speak to them—what the Roman commonwealth called Concio, the speaker himself being called Ecclesiastes, or Concionator."**
  One long sentence stacking four foreign terms (Ecclesia, Concio,
  Ecclesiastes, Concionator) across a dash-appended clause. A reader has to
  hold "Greek term / Roman term / Roman term for the speaker" all in one
  breath. This is the densest sentence in the chapter and likely needs a
  re-read.

- **"it was Ecclesia Legitima, a lawful church, Ennomos Ecclesia."**
  "Ecclesia Legitima" (Latin) and "Ennomos Ecclesia" (Greek) are presented
  as if in sequence without anything marking that they're two languages'
  versions of the same phrase — a reader could easily take the second term
  as a *different* or *further* category rather than a synonym. The English
  gloss "a lawful church" sits between them, which actually increases the
  risk of misreading the third term as unrelated.

- **"it was a confused church, Ecclesia Sugkechumene."**
  Same pattern as above (foreign term trailing an English gloss), plus
  "seditious clamor" just before it is elevated vocabulary a general reader
  may need to slow down on.

### Paragraph 2

- **"meaning the church triumphant, or the church to come."**
  "Church triumphant" is a piece of theological jargon (paired implicitly
  with "church militant," which isn't mentioned) that isn't defined
  anywhere in this paragraph. A general reader with no theology background
  will likely not know what "triumphant" is doing here versus "to come,"
  which is glossed by proximity but triumphant is not.

Otherwise this paragraph reads cleanly — the three Bible citations
(Acts 8:3, Col. 4:15, Matt. 18:17) are each introduced and closed simply,
and the "Tell it to the church..." quotation is easy to follow despite
being direct scripture.

### Paragraph 3

- **"whatever act is done in a gathering of people is the particular act of each one of those present who lent their aid in carrying it out, and not the act of all of them together, as of one body; much less the act of those who were absent, or who, being present, were not willing it should be done."**
  A single sentence carrying three stacked negatives/exceptions (act of
  each person → not the act of all together → much less the act of those
  absent/unwilling). Grammatically legal but the reader has to track which
  clause is being denied at each step; this is a strong candidate for
  needing a re-read even though no individual word is hard.

- **"a company of men professing the Christian religion, united in the person of one sovereign, at whose command they ought to assemble, and without whose authority they ought not to assemble."**
  "United in the person of one sovereign" uses "person" in the
  political/legal sense (a single representative office), not the literal
  sense of one human being. Since this sentence is presented as a formal
  definition (capitalized "CHURCH"), a reader unfamiliar with that usage of
  "person" may misread it as saying the church is united around one
  specific human ruler rather than one sovereign office/authority.

The opening of this paragraph — "can be said to have the power to will, to
declare, to command, to be obeyed, to make laws, or to do any other action
whatsoever" — is a long list but reads smoothly because of its parallel
verb structure; flagging it as a positive example, not a problem (see
below).

### Paragraph 4

- **"nor any teaching of doctrine lawful to any subject which the governor, both of the state and of religion, forbids to be taught."**
  Double-negative-shaped construction ("no other government... nor any
  teaching... lawful... which the governor... forbids") that a reader has
  to mentally untangle — it takes a beat to work out that this means "no
  subject may lawfully teach what the governor forbids."

- **"between the Christian and the man."**
  This closing phrase of the "civil war...in every Christian man's own
  breast" sentence is rhetorically striking but genuinely ambiguous on a
  first read — "the man" has no clear referent distinct from "the
  Christian" earlier in the same sentence, so a reader may pause to work
  out that it means the same person's religious self versus civic self,
  not two different people.

- **"The teachers of the church are called pastors; so also are civil sovereigns."**
  This sentence is easy to misparse on first read. The natural reading of
  "so also are civil sovereigns" is ambiguous between "civil sovereigns are
  *also* called pastors" (the intended meaning) and "civil sovereigns are
  *also* teachers of the church." It arrives with no transition explaining
  why civil sovereigns would be called pastors at all, so a general reader
  is likely to reread this sentence at least once.

- **"of which both could be false, and at least one must be false."**
  Compact logical statement (a version of "not both can be true, but both
  could be false") that reads more like a logic-puzzle sentence than
  ordinary prose. Not wrong, just dense enough to warrant a pause.

## What reads well

- Paragraph 0's etymological walk from "Ecclesia" → metaphorical
  congregation → the building itself → "Kuriake" → "Kyrke" → "Church" is
  well-paced and easy to follow as a narrative of how a word's meaning
  shifted, despite the foreign terms noted above.
- The four-way sense breakdown in paragraph 2 (building → citizens'
  assembly → whole body of Christians → the elect → professing
  congregation) is clearly signposted with "Sometimes... Sometimes,
  too... Sometimes it means..." — the repetition of "Sometimes" acts as a
  helpful structural cue rather than feeling repetitive.
- "'Temporal' and 'spiritual' government are only two words brought into
  the world to make men see double, and mistake who their lawful
  sovereign is." — this is a genuinely vivid, memorable turn of phrase and
  reads naturally, not archaically.
- The list "to will, to declare, to command, to be obeyed, to make laws, or
  to do any other action whatsoever" (paragraph 3) is long but its
  parallel infinitive structure makes it easy to read at speed rather than
  choppy or overloaded.
- The final two sentences of paragraph 4 ("Who that one chief pastor
  is... And to whom Scripture has assigned that office...") land cleanly
  as a forward-looking close to the chapter — clear signposting for what
  comes next.

---

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

The chapter is readable start to finish and its overall argument (the word
"Church" has several distinct senses in Scripture, and only one of those
senses lets "the church" act as a single legal person) comes through
clearly. The problems are concentrated in a handful of specific spots
rather than throughout: the pile-up of unglossed foreign terms in
paragraph 1, a couple of genuinely dense multi-clause sentences (paragraph
3's "whatever act is done...", paragraph 4's "nor any teaching of
doctrine..."), and two ambiguous/confusing short sentences in paragraph 4
("between the Christian and the man" and "so also are civil sovereigns")
that are likely to cause a real stumble or misreading on first pass. None
of these require restructuring the chapter — they're the kind of thing a
light targeted pass (a clarifying half-clause, reordering a phrase, or
separating a stacked term list) would resolve without touching the
chapter's voice or content.
