# Accessibility Review — Final (Reviewer A, fresh/blind)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 39 (pilot ch. 40 slot) — "Of the Signification in Scripture of the Word Church"

**Coverage:** I read all 5 paragraphs in full, in order (indices 0–4, as they appear in `candidate-sonnet.json`). I was not shown the source text, any prior draft, or any earlier review notes.

---

## Paragraph-by-paragraph findings

**Paragraph 0**
No accessibility stumbles. The etymology chain (congregation → building → "Kuriake" → "Kyrke" → "Church") is laid out in a clear line the reader can follow one step at a time. "Idolaters" is slightly elevated vocabulary but well within general adult reading level and clear from context.

**Paragraph 1**
- *"the speaker was called Ecclesiastes, and Concionator"* — this is a likely stumble. Most contemporary readers know "Ecclesiastes" only as the biblical book. Meeting it here glossed as "the speaker [at an assembly]" creates a moment of false recognition — the reader has to consciously override the more familiar meaning before the sentence makes sense. Worth a beat of re-reading.
- The paragraph otherwise handles its cluster of foreign terms well — each (*Concio*, *Ecclesia Legitima*, *Ennomos Ecclesia*, *Ecclesia Sugkechumene*) is immediately translated in-line ("a lawful church," "a confused church"), so despite the density of foreign vocabulary, a reader can track meaning without needing outside knowledge. This is a paragraph that could easily have read as a list of unglossed jargon, and it doesn't — worth flagging as something that's working well.

**Paragraph 2**
No accessibility stumbles. The five senses of "church" are cleanly numbered by "sometimes... sometimes... sometimes," each with its scripture example and a plain-English paraphrase (e.g., "church triumphant" is immediately unpacked as "the church still to come: the church in its final, glorified state"). This is the clearest paragraph in the chapter.

**Paragraph 3**
No major stumbles. One small friction point: *"Here 'person' is used in its political sense: a single sovereign authority, not necessarily one individual ruler"* — the parenthetical does real work to head off a misreading, and it succeeds, but a reader still has to hold "person ≠ individual human being" in mind through the rest of the paragraph and the next one. Not an error, just a concept that asks for a second's extra attention. Not flagging as a fix-needed item, just noting where attention is required.

**Paragraph 4**
This is the densest paragraph and carries most of the chapter's remaining friction:
- *"'Temporal' and 'spiritual' government are only two words brought into the world to make men see double, and mistake who their lawful sovereign is."* — "make men see double" is a live metaphor (mistaking one thing for two) rather than a dead/familiar idiom, and it lands a beat late: the reader gets the image before the referent ("who their lawful sovereign is") clarifies what's being doubled. A reader is likely to pause and back up once the second half arrives.
- *"between the sword of justice and the shield of faith; and (what is more) in every Christian man's own breast — between the Christian and the man"* — the external metaphor (sword/shield) is clear enough, but "between the Christian and the man" is genuinely ambiguous on first pass: it reads as though "Christian" and "man" are being opposed as two different kinds of people, when the intended sense is an internal split within one person (the same man's loyalty to faith vs. to civil duty). This is the single hardest sentence fragment in the chapter for a general reader.
- *"The teachers of the church are called pastors — and so are civil sovereigns."* — this is short and grammatically simple but conceptually abrupt: it asserts, without preparation, that civil sovereigns are *also* called "pastors," which will read as a non sequitur to a reader who hasn't already absorbed the chapter's argument that sovereign and chief-pastor are one office. A reader is likely to reread this sentence to check they haven't missed a step.
- *"of which both could be false, and at least one must be false"* — the logical structure (both-could-be-false vs. at-least-one-must-be-false) is correct but compressed enough that it asks for a re-read to parse correctly; a reader has to hold two different modal claims (possibility vs. necessity) in the same short clause.
- Structurally, this paragraph carries four separable moves (no universal church → temporal/spiritual is a false split → the sovereign must be singular or civil war follows, including *internal* conflict → pastors must have one chief pastor, who is the sovereign) with no paragraph break between them. Each individual sentence is readable, but the cumulative effect by the end of the paragraph is of several distinct arguments run together, which asks more sustained attention than the rest of the chapter.

---

## What's working well
Paragraphs 0–2 are genuinely strong: technical/etymological material and multiple scripture citations are handled with plain glosses at every turn, and the "sometimes... sometimes..." structure in paragraph 2 in particular reads naturally rather than mechanically. The chapter does not lean on unresolved archaic constructions anywhere — sentences are grammatically modern throughout, which is a real accomplishment given the source material.

## Overall summary
This chapter reads clearly for the first three paragraphs and the sovereign-definition paragraph (3), with only minor, easily-skimmed friction points (the "Ecclesiastes" homonym trap, the "person"/individual distinction). The final paragraph (4) is where the remaining accessibility load concentrates: one metaphor that lands its meaning a beat late ("see double"), one genuinely ambiguous phrase ("between the Christian and the man"), one abrupt unprepared claim ("so are civil sovereigns"), and a compressed logical clause ("of which both could be false, and at least one must be false") — plus an overall sense, by the end of the paragraph, of several arguments run together without a breath between them. None of these are archaic-construction problems or vocabulary problems; they're compression and pacing issues concentrated in one paragraph.

**Verdict: substantially accessible.** The chapter as a whole reads as clear, contemporary prose; the issues above are targeted (three sentence-level items and one pacing observation, all confined to paragraph 4) rather than pervasive, and none of them would stop a general reader from following the chapter's argument — at most they'd cause a re-read of a clause. I would not send this back for a broader pass; the paragraph-4 items are worth a light targeted polish if another round is planned, but the chapter does not require it to be considered done.
