# Accessibility Review — Round 3 (Reviewer A, fresh read)

**Coverage:** Read all 5 paragraphs (indices 0–4) of `candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to finish, then paragraph by paragraph. Did not read source.json, current-modern-en.json, or any prior review file. Blind to the source text and to any drafter's notes.

**Book/chapter context given:** Leviathan, Chapter 39 — "Of the Signification in Scripture of the Word Church."

---

## Issues found

### Paragraph 2 — significant, likely newly introduced

> "Sometimes, too, it means only the elect, as in Eph. 5:27: 'A glorious church, without spot, or wrinkle, holy, and without blemish'—meaning what Hobbes calls the 'church triumphant,' or — in other words — the church still to come: the church in its final, glorified state."

The phrase **"meaning what Hobbes calls the 'church triumphant'"** breaks the text's voice. This chapter is written in the first person by the author himself elsewhere in the same passage ("I define a CHURCH to be...", paragraph 3). A reader has no way to parse why the author would suddenly refer to himself in the third person as "Hobbes" mid-sentence — it reads as though a different, unnamed commentator has broken in to gloss the author's own words, or as an editing artifact that slipped through. This is the single most disorienting moment in the chapter and would stop most readers cold, if only for a beat, to ask "wait, who's talking?" This looks like it was introduced by the latest edit rather than carried over — the rest of the sentence (the "church still to come... final, glorified state" gloss) is a legitimate, helpful clarification of an archaic term, but the "Hobbes calls" framing undermines it.

**Recommendation:** Replace with something that keeps the definition in the same voice, e.g. "meaning what is called the 'church triumphant'..." or "—that is, the church triumphant..."

### Paragraph 1 — moderate

> "The Roman commonwealth called this same kind of assembly Concio, and the speaker was called Ecclesiastes, and Concionator."

Two problems compound here. First, **"Ecclesiastes"** is very likely to be misread by a general reader as a reference to the biblical book of Ecclesiastes — it's a much more common association than "one who addresses an assembly," and nothing in the sentence heads that off. Second, the sentence gives two untranslated foreign terms back-to-back for "the speaker" (Ecclesiastes, Concionator) without translating either one or explaining why there are two — a reader can't tell if these are Greek/Latin synonyms, a title and a description, or something else. Compare the very next sentence, which does the work of translating its foreign terms ("Ecclesia Legitima... both meaning simply 'a lawful church'") — this sentence doesn't, so it reads as a step down in clarity right next to a paragraph that otherwise handles this well.

**Recommendation:** Gloss at least one term, e.g. "...the speaker was called, in Greek, Ecclesiastes, and in Latin, Concionator — both simply meaning 'speaker.'"

### Paragraph 4 — minor, worth a look

> "The teachers of the church are called pastors — and so are civil sovereigns."

As written, this can be misread as saying civil sovereigns are literally referred to as "pastors" in everyday usage, which will strike a modern reader as odd with no explanation given (pastor now reads almost exclusively as "clergyman"). The chapter's larger argument — that Hobbes is deliberately extending the shepherd/pastor metaphor to secular rulers to make his point about the sovereign as "one chief pastor" — doesn't quite land because the leap isn't bridged. It's not incomprehensible, and the payoff a few sentences later ("Who that one chief pastor is... the civil sovereign") does retroactively clarify it, but a reader has to hold the odd claim in suspension for three more sentences before it resolves.

**Recommendation:** Optional — something like "The teachers of the church are called pastors, or shepherds — and, in this same sense, so are civil sovereigns" would flag the metaphor up front.

### Paragraph 3 — trivial, stylistic only

> "...a church too, assembled in any commonwealth that has forbidden it to assemble, is an unlawful assembly."

Mildly repetitive ("assembled... to assemble... assembly") — reads a little clunky on a close pass but isn't actually confusing. Not a comprehension blocker; flagging only because the prompt asks about "stitched-together" prose, and this clause has a slight tongue-twister quality.

## What's working well

- **Paragraph 3's parenthetical on "person"** — "(Here 'person' is used in its political sense: a single sovereign authority, not necessarily one individual ruler.)" — is a clean, well-placed piece of scaffolding. It heads off exactly the kind of stumble a modern reader would otherwise hit on the word "person," and it doesn't feel bolted-on.
- **Paragraph 4's "who governs both state and religion"** aside, attached to "the governor," is similarly effective — quietly reminds the reader what "governor" means in this argument at the exact moment it's needed, without restating the whole thesis.
- **Paragraph 4's closing image** — "between the Christian and the man," capping the list of civil-war fault lines — is genuinely strong, literary writing. It lands as a real rhetorical point, not archaic filler, and reads naturally aloud.
- The scripture citations throughout (paragraphs 0, 2, 3) are handled consistently and clearly, with the quoted verse text itself left legible rather than over-modernized.
- Paragraph 0's etymology of "Church" (Kuriake → Kyrke → Church) is well-paced and easy to follow despite being inherently about foreign-language history.

---

## Overall readability

This chapter is close. Four of five paragraphs read cleanly and would give a general reader no trouble at all — the prose is plain, the sentences are well-bounded even when they're long (paragraph 4 has several long sentences that nonetheless hold together), and two spots that read like round-2 fixes (the "person" and "governor" asides) are doing real, unobtrusive work. The one clear blocker is the "Hobbes calls" slip in paragraph 2, which is jarring enough on its own to keep this chapter out of "substantially accessible" until it's fixed; the Ecclesiastes/Concionator line in paragraph 1 and the unglossed "pastors... civil sovereigns" line in paragraph 4 are smaller, more forgivable stumbles that a reader can push through but that are still worth a pass.

## Verdict

**Needs targeted fixes** — not a broader pass. One clear-cut error (the third-person "Hobbes" reference in paragraph 2) plus two smaller clarity gaps (paragraph 1's untranslated title pair, paragraph 4's unglossed pastor/sovereign line). All three are small, surgical edits, not structural rewrites.
