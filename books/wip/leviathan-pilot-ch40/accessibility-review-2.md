# Accessibility Review 2 — Leviathan, Chapter 39 (pilot file "ch40"), candidate-sonnet.json

**Reviewer role:** fresh reader, blind to source and to any drafter's notes or the prior review. This is a second, independent accessibility pass — not a diff against round 1.

**Coverage:** read all 5 paragraphs (indices 0–4) of `candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to finish, then re-read paragraph by paragraph. No other file in the directory was opened.

---

## Paragraph 0

> "But this use is metaphorical, standing for the congregation assembled there; and the word has since come to be used for the building itself..."

**Issue:** Referent/sequencing confusion. The paragraph first tells the reader "Church" was used "for God's house, meaning a temple," gives the 1 Cor. 14:34 example, and then immediately says "this use is metaphorical, standing for the congregation" — i.e., the very use just illustrated didn't originally mean the building at all, it meant the people. A reader has to stop and work out that "this use" reaches back past the temple gloss to the underlying congregation-sense, before the next clause pivots forward again to say the word *later* came to mean the building literally. Three different states of the word (literal-congregation → metaphorical-building → literal-building) are compressed into two sentences without a signal like "originally" or "at first" to anchor the timeline. Likely to require a re-read.

> "to distinguish the temples of Christians from those of idolaters"

**Issue (minor):** "idolaters" is a loaded, slightly archaic word. Meaning is recoverable from context (non-Christian worshippers), but it's the kind of word a general reader today doesn't use or hear often — flagging as a vocabulary friction point, not a blocker.

## Paragraph 1

No major stumbles. The paragraph front-loads several foreign/technical terms (Concio, Ecclesiastes, Concionator, Ecclesia Legitima, Ennomos Ecclesia, Ecclesia Sugkechumene), but every one is immediately glossed in English ("meaning simply 'a lawful church'," "a confused church"), so the density is manageable — this is inherent to an etymological argument about the word "Church" and reads as intentional, not accidental clutter.

## Paragraph 2

> "meaning the 'church triumphant' — that is, the redeemed in heaven, as distinct from the church still on earth — or the church still to come."

**Issue:** Ambiguous attachment. It's not clear whether "or the church still to come" is a third alternative sense of "church" being listed alongside "still on earth," or whether it's meant to be read as also inside the "distinct from" clause (i.e., "distinct from [the church still on earth, or the church still to come]"). As written, the dash before "or" makes it read like a new item tacked onto the sentence rather than part of the list it belongs to. This needs a re-read to parse correctly.

> "as in Acts 8:3, where it says that 'Saul made havoc of the church.'"

**Issue (minor):** "made havoc of" is an archaic verb construction (a modern reader would say "wreaked havoc on" or "ravaged"). It's presented as a direct quotation, but per review instructions quotation status doesn't exempt it — flagging because the phrasing itself, not just the citation, could cause a brief stumble. Meaning is still recoverable from context.

> "let him be to you as a Gentile, or a tax collector"

No issue — "tax collector" reads as an already-modernized substitution and lands clearly; "Gentile" is common enough to pass without a gloss.

## Paragraph 3

Reads well. The chain of definition — congregation → "in this last sense" → the formal CHURCH definition — is easy to follow, and the parenthetical "(Here 'person' is used in its political sense: a single sovereign authority, not necessarily one individual ruler.)" does real work: it heads off a plausible misreading of "united in the person of one sovereign" as meaning literally one human being, before that misreading can take hold. This is the clearest paragraph in the chapter — worth flagging as something that's working, not just as an absence of problems.

## Paragraph 4

> "'Temporal' and 'spiritual' government are only two words brought into the world to make men see double, and mistake who their lawful sovereign is."

**Issue:** The idiom "see double" is vivid but its logical connection to the point (two words creating a false impression of two separate governments where there is really one) isn't self-evident on first pass. A reader has to infer that "seeing double" = perceiving two authorities where there's only one. Not opaque, but it asks for a beat of interpretive work that the surrounding prose doesn't fully set up.

> "between spiritualists and temporalists"

**Issue:** "Spiritualists" is a false-friend risk for a contemporary reader — today the word's dominant association is with séances/communicating with the dead, not with "those who claim authority over spiritual/church matters" (the intended 17th-century sense, parallel to "temporalists"). A reader could momentarily import the wrong meaning before context corrects them.

> "men will be taught contrary doctrines, of which both could be false, and at least one must be false"

**Issue:** Dense logical construction packed into a short clause. The reader has to hold two distinct claims at once — "both could be false" and "at least one must be false" — and recognize these are different (weaker/stronger) statements about the same pair of doctrines, without any signposting that they're two separate logical points rather than a redundancy. Short sentence, but conceptually overloaded; likely to cause a re-read even though it isn't grammatically complex.

> "And that governor must be one, or else faction and civil war must inevitably follow in the commonwealth—between church and state, between spiritualists and temporalists, between the sword of justice and the shield of faith; and (what is more) in every Christian man's own breast — between his loyalty as a Christian and his loyalty as a citizen."

**Note, not a flag:** this is a long sentence stacking three parallel "between X and Y" clauses, a semicolon, a parenthetical, and a fourth "between X and Y" — but the strict parallelism ("between... and...", repeated) actually carries the reader through it. Flagging it here for visibility (it's the single longest, most structurally loaded sentence in the chapter) but it reads more like a controlled rhetorical build than a stitched-together tangle. "The sword of justice and the shield of faith" and "in every Christian man's own breast" are genuinely effective, natural phrasing — worth calling out as strong writing, not a problem.

---

## What's working

- Paragraph 3's parenthetical gloss on "person" (political sense vs. individual ruler) is a good model for how the chapter handles a term that could otherwise mislead — it's specific, brief, and placed exactly where the ambiguity would otherwise land.
- The foreign-term glosses in paragraph 1 (Ecclesia Legitima / "a lawful church," etc.) are handled consistently and don't require the reader to hold untranslated terms in mind.
- The rhetorical build in paragraph 4's "faction and civil war" sentence, despite its length, stays legible through parallel structure and lands on vivid, concrete imagery rather than abstraction.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

The chapter is largely accessible and several passages (paragraph 3 especially) read very well. But there are a handful of concrete, fixable spots: the metaphorical/literal sequencing in paragraph 0's opening argument, the dangling "or the church still to come" in paragraph 2, the "spiritualists" false-friend risk and the compressed "both could be false / at least one must be false" logic in paragraph 4, and the "see double" idiom that could use a touch more scaffolding. None of these require a structural rewrite — they're each a sentence-level clarity fix — but as they stand, a general contemporary reader would likely need to re-read multiple sentences per paragraph to track the argument, which is a real accessibility cost in a chapter whose entire content is a careful five-way disambiguation of one word's meanings.

## Summary of chapter readability

This is a dense definitional chapter — Hobbes walking through five senses of "Church" and then building a political argument on top of the last one — and the candidate mostly succeeds at making that walk followable in modern prose, especially by the time it reaches the formal definition in paragraph 3, which is the clearest writing in the sample. The friction that remains clusters around two things: (1) places where the original's compressed logical moves (paragraph 0's building/congregation switch, paragraph 4's "both could be false, at least one must be false") haven't been given quite enough connective scaffolding for a first-pass modern reader, and (2) a couple of individual word choices ("spiritualists," "see double," "made havoc of") that either risk a false modern reading or ask for more inferential work than their neighbors. Fixing these would move the chapter from "needs targeted fixes" to "substantially accessible" without touching its voice or content.
