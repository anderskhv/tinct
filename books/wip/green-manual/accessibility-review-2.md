## Section-by-section findings

### Section 11 — reads clean now
"For as long as he allows you, treat it as a traveller treats an inn — care for it, but as something that belongs to another." "He" resolves back to "the giver" two sentences earlier without trouble. This section (flagged in round 1 as tangled) now reads smoothly and the traveller/inn image lands well. No issue.

### Section 12 — awkward, overloaded sentence
> "But he is not so well placed — and you are entirely so — that your freedom from disturbance should lie in his power."

This is grammatically legal but genuinely hard to parse on a first pass. The "not so X — and you are entirely so — that Y" construction forces the reader to hold two suspended comparisons (his position vs. yours) before the payoff clause arrives, and the parenthetical "and you are entirely so" briefly reads as if it's still describing the slave rather than pivoting to "you." A reader will likely have to re-read this once to get "he isn't in a position, and you are fully in a position, for your peace of mind to depend on him." Worth a light rewrite for clarity (e.g., "He is not in a position to threaten your peace of mind — you are, entirely, and you should not hand that power to him.").

### Section 24 — one confusing comparative
> "If lack of honor is an evil, another person can no more involve you in evil than in anything shameful."

The "no more X than Y" construction normally contrasts two different things of equal implausibility, but here "evil" and "anything shameful" are near-synonyms in the surrounding argument (the passage is arguing dishonor isn't really evil or shameful at all), so the comparison reads as circular rather than clarifying — a reader has to stop and figure out what's actually being compared to what. This is the one place in an otherwise well-handled section (the "covered walkways lined with columns," "Roman citizens," and "blacksmith/shoemaker" passages all read naturally) that snags.

### Section 29 — one odd word choice, otherwise strong
> "Do you think you can take this on and still eat, drink, and dislike things just as before?"

"Dislike things" is an odd verb choice in a list otherwise about ordinary appetites (eat, drink); it's presumably standing in for "have aversions" but reads as a non-sequitur item in the list on first encounter. Minor — most readers will infer the sense and move on, but it's a small speed bump in an otherwise vivid, well-paced section (the Euphrates reference is now cleanly identified as "a celebrated philosopher of the day," and the athletic-training extended metaphor reads naturally throughout).

### Section 36 / 42 — logic aside, now consistent but still abstract
The "either-or" / "both-and" terminology is aligned between the two sections as claimed (Section 36: "works as an either-or statement, but not as a both-and statement"; Section 42: "a true both-and statement"). That consistency helps. The underlying analogy in Section 36 — jumping from a disjunctive-logic example ("It is day or it is night") to table manners — is inherently a stretch in the source material, and a careful reader may still pause over how the logic point maps onto the dinner point. But this is the content being genuinely compressed, not a translation defect; the terminology fix has done what it can, and I don't think this needs further rewriting.

### Section 47 — statue reference, confirmed still ungrounded
> "Do not embrace statues."

Read cold, with no gloss, this is a genuine non-sequitur — it sits between two clearly-motivated ascetic examples (living frugally, taking a mouthful of water without announcing it) and gives the reader nothing to anchor it to. A fresh reader will stumble here and likely reread the sentence looking for context that isn't there.

That said — consistent with round 1's assessment — I would **not** hold the book for this alone. It's a single five-word imperative in a list of otherwise-clear ascetic practices; the reader loses one data point, not the argument of the section (which is about not performing your self-discipline for an audience, and that point survives intact with or without this line). Given that no accurate gloss can be built without inventing unverified historical specifics (the standard readings — cold-bath statues, or a reference to embracing/kissing temple statues as an endurance stunt — aren't solidly enough attested to state as fact in a reader-facing edition), leaving it bare is the more honest choice than fabricating a confident explanatory clause. I'd note it in the edition's back matter or a footnote if the format allows one, but it is not, on its own, a publication blocker.

### Unglossed proper names — consistent pattern, not blocking
- **Diogenes and Heraclitus** (Section 15) — no gloss, but the surrounding banquet passage carries the sense (people who renounced worldly appetite and so were "deservedly divine"), same as round 1's judgment.
- **Polynices and Eteocles** (Section 31) — no gloss, but the sentence is self-contained: "The belief that royal power was good made [them] enemies" tells the reader what happened without requiring the Theban backstory.
- **Anytus and Melitus, and Crito** (Section 52, not previously flagged) — Socrates' accusers and his friend, named without introduction in the closing quotation. Same pattern as above: the philosophical point ("they can kill me but not harm me") is fully intelligible without knowing who they are. I'm noting this only for completeness since it wasn't in round 1's list; I don't think it needs a gloss any more than Polynices/Eteocles did — the logic is the same.
- **Chrysippus** (Section 49) — the passage itself supplies enough ("If Chrysippus had not written so obscurely..."; later "explain Chrysippus rather than Homer") to place him as a famously difficult philosophical author. No gloss needed.

None of these four cases require intervention; they're all cases where the sentence carries its own sense without the name needing definition, which is the same standard round 1 applied to Diogenes/Heraclitus and Polynices/Eteocles.

### Everything else
Sections 1–10, 13–23, 25–28, 30, 32–35, 37–41, 43–46, 48, 50, and 51 all read cleanly on a fresh pass — no unresolved archaic constructions, no ambiguous referents, no overloaded syntax. Section 52's two verse quotations scan naturally and the closing Crito line lands cleanly.

## What reads especially well

The extended metaphors are the strongest material in the book and read as natural literary English, not translated aphorism: the ship-and-harbor passage (Section 7), the banquet passage (Section 15), the "two handles" image (Section 43), the shoe/possessions metaphor (Section 39), and the sheep/wool digestion metaphor (Section 46) all land cleanly and are genuinely pleasurable to read. Section 24's dialogic back-and-forth with the imagined friend ("Then acquire money," "But my country...") reads like real conversation, not stilted rhetorical setup, and the lettuce/obolus example in Section 25 is a model of concrete, ungenerlized explanation.

## Verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

The book is overwhelmingly accessible — the large majority of its 52 sections read as clean, natural contemporary English with no stumbling blocks. Two sentences (Section 12's "he is not so well placed..." construction, Section 24's "no more...than" comparison) are genuinely hard to parse on a first read and would benefit from a light rewrite; one word choice in Section 29 ("dislike things") is a minor speed bump. Section 47's bare "Do not embrace statues" remains a real, unresolved non-sequitur for a cold reader, but — as in round 1 — I would not hold the book for it, since no accurate gloss can be built without inventing historical specifics that aren't reliably attested; it's a single-line loss in an otherwise intact section, not a comprehension failure for the chapter or the book. The various unglossed proper names (Diogenes/Heraclitus, Polynices/Eteocles, Anytus/Melitus/Crito, Chrysippus) all pass the same test round 1 applied — the sentences carry their own sense without the names needing definition — and I don't think any of them need further work.
