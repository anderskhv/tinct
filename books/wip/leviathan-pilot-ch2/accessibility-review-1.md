# Accessibility Review — Reviewer A (fresh read)

**Book/chapter:** Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Part I — Of Man, Chapter 1: "Of Sense" (modern-English reading edition)

**What I read:** All 5 paragraphs of `candidate-sonnet.json`, in full, in order (indices 0–4). I did not read `source.json`, `current-modern-en.json`, or any drafter notes. I read the chapter once straight through, then went back paragraph by paragraph.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph findings

### Paragraph 0
No accessibility issues. Reads cleanly and naturally — this is some of the strongest prose in the chapter. "Something commonly called an Object" is a nice unforced gloss of the technical term.

### Paragraph 1
No accessibility issues. Short, clear, well-paced.

### Paragraph 2
No accessibility issues. Reads like ordinary, transparent prose.

### Paragraph 3 (the long paragraph — this is where the difficulty concentrates)

- **"an effort of the heart to push itself free of it"** — The final "it" is ambiguous on first pass: does "it" refer to the resistance/counter-pressure just named, or to the original pressure from the object? A reader has to loop back through the sentence to resolve the pronoun. This is avoidable — the antecedent could be named directly ("push itself free of that pressure") without adding length.

- **"exist in the object that causes them as nothing but a number of different motions of matter, by which it presses on our organs in different ways"** — Grammatically legal but overloaded: one sentence carries the definition of "sensible qualities," their true nature (motion), and the mechanism (pressing organs), with "it" doing double duty for "the object." A reader will likely need a second pass. This is a case where the underlying idea (secondary qualities are really just motion) is genuinely a hard 17th-century concept, but the sentence *construction* — cramming three moves into one clause chain — is a wording/structural choice, not an unavoidable feature of the idea itself. It could be split without losing content.

- **"so the bodies we see or hear likewise produce their effects on us through a strong but unnoticed action of their own"** — "a strong but unnoticed action of their own" is vague enough that a reader may not be sure what's being claimed (what action? unnoticed by whom, how?). This is the point of the analogy (external bodies act on us the way rubbing the eye does), but the phrase stating the parallel is fuzzier than it needs to be. Avoidable wording softness, not conceptual necessity.

- **"For if those colors and sounds actually resided in the bodies or objects that cause them, they could not be separated from those objects — yet we see that they can be, by lenses, and in echoes by reflection, where we know that the thing we see is in one place and its appearance in another."** — This is the single hardest sentence in the chapter to parse on a first read. It's long, has an embedded conditional, and the supporting examples ("by lenses, and in echoes by reflection") are compressed almost to the point of not explaining themselves — a reader who doesn't already know the "lenses separate the appearance of color from its object" argument will likely have to re-read this once or twice to see how lenses/echoes prove the point. The underlying inference is legitimately dense (a compact 17th-century empiricist argument), so some difficulty here is unavoidable — but the sentence could be broken into two sentences (one stating the conditional, one giving the two examples) with real accessibility gain and zero loss of content or nuance.

- **"the real object itself seems to be clothed in the very image it produces in us"** — "clothed in" is a literary/archaic-flavored metaphor. It's evocative and not incomprehensible, but it stands out as more ornamental than the plain, transparent register of the surrounding sentences (e.g., paragraphs 0–2). This is a register inconsistency rather than a comprehension blocker — a reader will get it, but it reads like a different, older voice dropped into an otherwise contemporary sentence.

### Paragraph 4

- **"throughout every university in Christendom"** — "Christendom" is a real but slightly dated word for a general contemporary reader; most readers will infer meaning from context (universities, Europe) but it's not a word in everyday use. Minor stumble, low severity.

- **"an Audible Species, that is, an Audible Aspect, or Audible Being Seen, which enters the ear and produces hearing"** and **"an Intelligible Species, that is, an Intelligible Being Seen, which, coming into the understanding, makes us understand"** — These are the sharpest stumbling points in the whole chapter. "Being Seen" is visual vocabulary; applying it to *hearing* and to *understanding* reads, on first encounter, like an inconsistency or an error rather than a deliberate technical term, because "seen" directly contradicts the sense being described. A reader will likely stop and reread, unsure whether this is intentional. I can't judge from this candidate alone whether "Being Seen" is meant to reproduce a fixed piece of period jargon (in which case some awkwardness may be irreducible) or whether it's a wording choice within this rendering — but as pure reader experience, this is the passage most likely to cause a reader to stop and double back, more than anything in paragraph 3.

- **"I must point out along the way, wherever it comes up, what in them would need correcting"** — Slightly clunky rhythm ("along the way, wherever it comes up") but not a comprehension problem; readable on first pass.

## What reads unusually well

Paragraphs 0–2 are genuinely strong: plain, contemporary, well-paced sentences that carry Hobbes's definitions (thought, Object, Sense) without any loss of precision. The glosses inserted in-line — "something commonly called an Object," "Fancy — that is, mental image" — are handled exactly right: unobtrusive, not condescending, and they do real work for the reader. The opening of paragraph 4 (schools of philosophy / Aristotle) is also clear and easy to follow up until the "Species" terminology begins. The overall voice is consistent and the vocabulary level is well-judged outside of the specific problem spots above.

---

## Unavoidable conceptual complexity vs. avoidably-difficult wording

Both are present, and they're distinguishable:

**Unavoidable (conceptual):** Hobbes's actual argument in paragraph 3 — that "sensible qualities" like color and sound are not really in objects but are motions, and that this can be shown because appearances can be separated from objects (lenses, echoes) — is intrinsically demanding 17th-century epistemology. Any accessible rendering of *this idea* will ask something of the reader. Likewise, paragraph 4's parody of Scholastic "Species" jargon is supposed to sound like empty technical language — that's Hobbes's rhetorical point, not a translation flaw.

**Avoidable (wording/construction), specifically:**
1. The pronoun ambiguity in "push itself free of it."
2. The single overloaded sentence defining sensible qualities as motion (could be split in two).
3. The vague "strong but unnoticed action of their own."
4. The lenses/echoes sentence's compression — splittable into two sentences with no loss of meaning, only gain in clarity.
5. The register-inconsistent "clothed in" metaphor.
6. "Audible/Intelligible Being Seen" — genuinely confusing because "seen" collides with hearing and understanding; this reads as an internal contradiction to a fresh reader regardless of whether it's deliberate period terminology.

None of these six are cases where the idea itself demands the current wording — each could plausibly be tightened or clarified without sacrificing Hobbes's meaning, which is why I'm calling them avoidable rather than folding them into "the chapter is just hard."

---

## Overall verdict: **needs targeted fixes**

## Summary

This candidate is mostly strong and reads naturally for long stretches — paragraphs 0, 1, and 2 in particular could stand as-is. The difficulty concentrates almost entirely in paragraph 3 (one very long, multi-clause paragraph carrying Hobbes's core mechanistic argument about sense) and, within paragraph 4, the two "Being Seen" phrases. Most of paragraph 3's difficulty comes from real conceptual density that any modern rendering has to carry, but several individual sentences and phrases inside it are harder than the ideas require — a pronoun left ambiguous, one sentence doing the work of two or three, a vague summarizing phrase, and one archaic-flavored metaphor that breaks the otherwise contemporary register. The "Audible/Intelligible Being Seen" phrases in paragraph 4 are the most reader-stopping moments in the chapter because the word "seen" actively fights the sense being named. A handful of targeted, local fixes — splitting two overloaded sentences, resolving one pronoun, and reconsidering the "Being Seen" phrasing — would move this from "needs targeted fixes" to "substantially accessible" without touching the paragraphs that already work well.
