# Accessibility Review — Round 2 (Reviewer A, fresh read)

**Chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 15 ("Of Other Laws of Nature") — pilot file labeled ch16
**Source reviewed:** `candidate-sonnet.json` only. No source text, no prior review notes, no drafter's notes were read.

**Coverage:** Read all 43 paragraphs, in full, start to finish, then a second pass paragraph by paragraph. Paragraph indices covered: 1–43 (all), including the three bracketed structural notes (indices 3, 23, 37) and all embedded Latin/Greek/legal terms.

## Issues found

Very few genuine accessibility problems remain. Notes below, roughly in descending order of severity.

**Paragraph 30** — "And because, however willing people may be to observe these laws, questions can still arise about a man's action — first, whether it was done at all, and second, if it was done, whether it violated the law or not (the first kind of question being a question of fact, the second a question of right) — unless the parties to the dispute agree to abide by the judgment of a third party, they remain as far from peace as ever."
This is the one sentence in the chapter that's genuinely overloaded rather than just conceptually dense: the main clause ("unless... they remain as far from peace as ever") is separated from its subject by a very long em-dash aside that itself contains a parenthetical inside it. A reader has to hold the "because... unless" frame open across three nested interruptions. This is a legitimate candidate for a light restructure (e.g., breaking the aside into its own sentence before returning to the "unless" clause) — not just unavoidable content density.

**Paragraph 22** — "and when men who consider themselves wise try to force their will on people who have no confidence in their own wisdom, they don't always — or even often, or hardly ever — come out the winners."
The stacked, self-correcting qualifiers ("don't always — or even often, or hardly ever") ask the reader to process three successive downgrades in one breath. It reads as a deliberate ironic escalation (and probably is, in keeping with Hobbes's tone here), but a reader will likely have to re-read it once to parse the logic of the list. Minor — flagging for awareness rather than recommending a fix, since flattening it would probably lose the rhetorical effect.

**Paragraph 4** — "The kingdom of God is taken by force — but what if it could be taken by unjust force?" and the "Coke's Commentary on Littleton" aside later in the same paragraph.
Both are references a general contemporary reader is unlikely to recognize (a scriptural allusion to Matthew 11:12, and a citation to a specific 17th-century legal commentary). Neither is a wording problem — the surrounding sentence gives enough context to follow the argument without knowing the reference — so this is noted rather than flagged as something to fix; there isn't an accessible way to gloss "Coke's Commentary on Littleton" without adding explanatory content that isn't in the text.

**Paragraph 36** — "when weighing other men's actions against his own and finding them too heavy, put them on the other side of the scale, and his own actions in their place, so that his own passions and self-love add nothing extra to the weight."
The scale/weighing metaphor is coherent but asks for a beat of visualization before it clicks — worth a glance if a future pass is looking for smoothing opportunities, but not a stumble on the level of paragraph 30.

Nothing else in the 43 paragraphs struck me as requiring a re-read purely because of wording. The technical/legalistic vocabulary that remains (commutative/distributive justice, primogeniture, arbitrator, foro interno/foro externo) is uniformly and immediately glossed in-line ("that is, ..." or a parenthetical translation) at first use, which is the right pattern and makes the terminology accessible despite being unfamiliar.

## What's working well

- The consistent glossing convention for named laws and foreign/technical terms (JUSTICE, COMPLAISANCE, EQUITY, pleonexia, Prosopolepsia, kleronomia, contumely, foro interno/externo) is a real strength — every one of these lands its definition in the same sentence, so unfamiliar vocabulary never becomes an obstacle.
- Paragraph 17 (the "stones gathered for building a structure" simile for social fitness) and paragraph 21 (the servant/master example clarifying injury vs. damage) are both genuinely clear and vivid — concrete illustrations doing real explanatory work rather than just restating the abstract rule.
- The "fool has said in his heart" passage (paragraph 4) keeps its rhetorical energy — sarcastic, propulsive — while staying easy to follow sentence by sentence, which is a hard balance for this kind of material.
- The golden-rule paragraph (36) lands cleanly at the sentence level even where the scale metaphor that follows it is a little denser.
- Bracketed structural notes (paragraphs 3, 23, 37) read as useful section markers, not clutter.
- Per the given context: the ordinal jump to "the eighteenth law" in paragraph 32 after several unnumbered laws is an inherent oddity carried from the source's own inconsistent numbering, not a defect — noting it here only for completeness, not recommending any renumbering.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** The remaining difficulty in this chapter is almost entirely the unavoidable conceptual density of Hobbes's argument itself — long chains of legal/philosophical reasoning, historical and scriptural references without modern equivalents, and inherently abstract distinctions (commutative vs. distributive justice, foro interno vs. foro externo) that no amount of rewording will fully dissolve for a general reader. Avoidable wording problems are minimal: paragraph 30 is the one sentence I'd call a genuine candidate for a targeted structural fix (its nested aside separates subject from verb by too much), and paragraph 22's stacked qualifiers are a minor, arguably intentional, rhetorical wrinkle. Everything else reads as clear, well-paced modern prose that keeps Hobbes's voice and rigor intact — glossing of unfamiliar terms in particular is handled consistently and well throughout all 43 paragraphs.
