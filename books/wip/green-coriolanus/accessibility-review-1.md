# Accessibility Review 1 — Coriolanus (`coriolanus`, modern-en)

**Reviewer role:** Reviewer A (fresh, candidate-only, blind to source.json).
**Coverage:** All 29 chapters (Act 1 Sc.1 – Act 5 Sc.6), all 1,379
paragraphs of `candidate.json`, read in full, not sampled.

This review reads `candidate.json` only, as a general contemporary reader
would encounter it, before any comparison against `source.json`.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** The candidate consistently rebuilds
Shakespeare's dense syntax (long periodic sentences, inverted word order,
suspended subjects) into ordinary contemporary sentence structure while
keeping register, imagery, and the play's political bite intact. The
political rhetoric — Coriolanus's contempt for the plebeians ("you common
cry of curs," "the mutable... many," "scabs") and the tribunes'
class-conscious argument for the people's power — reads as clear,
deliberate, frank argument, not smoothed over or hedged.

## Notable strengths

- The belly fable (Ch1 ¶32–48) is rendered as a coherent, followable
  allegory rather than a series of disconnected archaic clauses.
- Coriolanus's set-piece speeches on mixed authority (Ch14 ¶55, ¶61, ¶64)
  — long, syntactically nested arguments in the source — are broken into
  readable clause sequences without losing the argument's logical chain
  (if/then, therefore, concession).
- The servingmen's comic prose scene (Ch21 ¶62–101) keeps its joking
  rhythm and specific images (rabbits after rain, cudgels, cannibalism
  banter) instead of flattening the humour.
- Volumnia's two great persuasion speeches (Ch15 ¶28, Ch26 ¶36, ¶41) keep
  their emotional escalation and specific imagery (the ripest mulberry,
  the eagle in the dovecote, the mother's womb) legible without losing
  their length or rhetorical build.

## Points a general reader might still stumble on

These are noted for completeness; none felt severe enough to call the
chapter inaccessible, and several are inherent to the play's dense
classical-allusion density rather than fixable by rewording:

- **Ch12 ¶27** (Cominius's battle narration): a long single-paragraph
  speech listing military exploits across Coriolanus's career (Tarquin,
  the dictator, seventeen battles). Dense with proper nouns and telescoped
  chronology; a reader may need to reread to track "he" across clauses.
  This density is inherent to the source's own single unbroken speech —
  splitting it further would misrepresent the source's rhetorical
  structure (one continuous eulogy).
- **Ch3 ¶33** and **Ch26 ¶45** (Valeria's Penelope allusion; Aufidius's
  reference to Coriolanus's Volscian mother): both assume unstated
  classical/legendary background (the Penelope myth; the implication about
  Coriolanus's ancestry). The candidate does not gloss these — correctly,
  since source itself doesn't explain them and adding an explanatory gloss
  would be inventing content not licensed by source.
- **Ch14 ¶53** ("this Triton of the minnows"): a compressed classical
  metaphor (Triton = minor sea-god, here mocking Sicinius's outsized
  self-importance among lesser men). Kept as-is; any paraphrase would
  either lose the image or add an interpretive gloss source doesn't offer.
- Several of the servingmen's/citizens' malapropisms and coinages
  ("directitude," "empiricutic," a "Jack guardant") are deliberately odd
  words in source itself. The candidate correctly keeps them rather than
  normalizing them to plain English — flagged here only so a reader isn't
  surprised to hit an invented word; this is not a defect.
