JASON. (Jason enters.) This is far from the first time I have seen the terrible harm a stubborn temper can cause. You could have stayed here, in this country and this home, if you had quietly accepted your rulers' decisions. Instead, your reckless outbursts have brought you exile. I am not offended: keep shouting, "Jason is the worst of men!" as long as you please. But after what you said against the rulers, consider yourself fortunate that banishment is your only punishment. I kept trying to calm the anger of the royal family; I wanted you to remain. You would not restrain your foolishness, but went on insulting those in power, and that is why you must leave. Still, I have not grown tired of caring for my own people. I have come, woman, out of concern that when you and the children are exiled, you should lack neither money nor anything else. Exile brings plenty of troubles with it. You may hate me, but I could never wish harm upon you.

MEDEA. Scoundrel beyond all scoundrels! That is the darkest name my tongue can find for your cowardice. You come to me—you actually come, after proving yourself hateful to heaven, to me and to the whole human race! To injure those who love you and then look them in the face is neither boldness nor courage. It is shamelessness, the worst disease that infects mankind. Still, I am glad you came. I shall lighten my heart by abusing you, and listening will make you suffer. Let me begin at the beginning. I saved your life. Every Greek who sailed beside you in Argo knows it. You were sent to put the fire-breathing bulls under the yoke and sow a field of death. I killed the dragon that guarded the Golden Fleece, coiling round it without ever sleeping, and brought the light of rescue to you. I abandoned my own father and my home, and followed you to Iolkos beneath Pelion, more devoted to your cause than wise. I killed Pelias by the hands of his own children, the worst of deaths, and so relieved you of every fear. After all I did for you, you vilest man, you deserted me for a new bride, though I had borne your children. If you had been childless, your hunger for another marriage might have been forgivable. But faith in oaths has disappeared. I do not know whether you think the ancient Gods still rule, or whether new laws have been made for men. Your own heart must tell you that you have broken your promises to me. Shame on this right hand you so often clasped, and these knees! How uselessly a worthless man embraced us; how he has cheated our hopes! Come, I will speak with you as a friend, though what fair treatment can I expect from you? Still, I shall question you, and your answers will expose you further. Where can I go now? To my father's house, which I deserted along with my country for your sake? To the unfortunate daughters of Pelias? How warmly they would welcome their father's murderer! That is how things stand: I have made my own family my enemies, and to serve you I have turned against me people I should never have hurt. What happiness you have given me among the women of Hellas in repayment for it all! What a marvellously faithful husband I possess—miserable woman that I am!—if I must leave this country friendless, alone in exile with my lonely children. A splendid disgrace for our new bridegroom: his babies and the woman who saved him wandering in poverty! Zeus, why have you given men clear ways to recognise counterfeit gold, but left no natural stamp on a man's body by which we can identify the worthless?

CHORUS. Anger is a terrible thing, beyond the reach of any healing remedy, when people who once loved one another meet as bitter enemies.

JASON. It seems I must speak skilfully. Like a cautious sailor with his canvas reefed, I must run before the storm of abuse coming from your tongue, woman. You pile your services as high as a tower, but I credit Kypris alone, no other God or human being, with saving my voyage. You are clever; must you force me to explain how Love's arrows, which never miss, compelled you to preserve my life? Still, I will not press that point too closely. You saved me, and that was a good deed. Yet you received more than you gave by rescuing me, as I shall show. First, you now live in Hellas instead of a barbarian country. You understand justice and have learned to live under law rather than force. All the Greeks have heard of your wisdom; you have a famous name. Had you remained at the world's remote edge, no one would be telling your story. I would want neither a house full of gold nor a voice sweeter than Orpheus's if fame never heard about my good fortune. That is enough about my great struggles, since you challenged me to this dispute. As for your attacks on my royal marriage, I shall show, first, that I acted wisely; second, with self-control; and third, as the best friend you and my children could have. No, listen until I finish. When I arrived here from Iolkos, followed by so many desperate misfortunes, what greater stroke of luck could an exile find than marriage to a princess? It was not, as you resentfully imagine, because I hated your bed and desired a new bride. Nor did I want a larger number of children: those I have are enough, and I find no fault in them. I wanted us to live respectably, a matter of real importance, and without hardship. I know very well how friends keep their distance from a poor man. I wanted to raise our sons as befits my family, and give your children brothers, joining them all into one household so that we could live happily. What need have you for more children? But it helps me to use sons yet to be born to support the ones already living. Was that such a bad plan? You would not say so if the loss of your marriage bed did not sting you. You women are so unreasonable: while your marriage rights remain intact you believe everything is well, but if anything goes wrong in bed, you turn bitterly against even the finest, most fortunate circumstances. If only people could have children by some other means, without the existence of women, mankind would never have suffered this curse.

CHORUS. Words, Jason, cleverly dressed-up words. Yet to my mind—and I know you will dislike hearing it—you are behaving unjustly by betraying your wife.

MEDEA. On many subjects my judgement differs from most people's. To me, a villain with a clever tongue loses far more than he gains. Certain that his eloquence can cover his wrongdoing, he becomes bolder in wickedness. That is no great wisdom. So do not try your handsome appearances and cunning speeches on me. One statement will defeat you: if you were not a scoundrel, you would have obtained my agreement to this marriage instead of hiding it from those close to you.

JASON. Yes, you would have been a wonderful help with my plans if I had mentioned marriage, when even now you cannot control this enormous rage!

MEDEA. That was not what stopped you. A foreign wife would bring you no honour as you grew older.

JASON. Understand this clearly: I did not marry the princess I have won out of desire for her. As I told you, I wanted to protect you and father royal children, brothers for my sons and a strong defensive tower for my household.

MEDEA. I want no prosperous life overshadowed by sorrow, no good fortune with the thorns of conscience piercing my heart!

JASON. Can you change your prayer to something wiser? Pray that you never mistake a benefit for a grief, or think yourself unfortunate when good luck comes.

MEDEA. Yes, go on insulting me! You have a place of safety, while I am driven from this country abandoned and alone.

JASON. This was your own choice. Put the blame on no one else.

MEDEA. My choice? By marrying someone else and betraying you, I suppose!

JASON. By uttering wicked curses against the royal family.

MEDEA. Quite so. And in the same way I am cursing your household now?

JASON. I will argue with you about this no further. But if you are willing to accept money from me to help you or the children in exile, say so. I am ready to give generously, and provide tokens of hospitality that will secure you friends. You would be foolish to refuse. Restrain your anger, and you will find yourself better off.

MEDEA. Your friends! I want nothing to do with friends of yours. I will accept nothing, and you must offer nothing. A scoundrel's gifts bring no benefit.

JASON. At any rate, I call the Gods as witnesses that I am willing to give you and your sons every assistance. But you dislike what would help you; your stubborn pride rejects friends. You will suffer all the more for it.

MEDEA. Go away! While you linger at these doors, impatience for your newly captured bride is burning you up! Marry her. Perhaps—and may God make my words come true—you will have a wedding you would gladly undo. (Jason leaves.)

CHORUS. Love brings men neither glory nor honour when it arrives in unchecked excess. But when Kypris descends and lets her joy fall in moderation, no Goddess is more enchanting. Queen, do not aim at me from your golden bow that inescapable arrow poisoned with desire—never at me! Let self-restraint protect me, that loveliest gift of the immortal Gods. May Love's Queen never visit me in terror, bringing bitter conflict and unforgiving feuds, striking my soul with frenzied restlessness over a mismatched bed. May she honour the peace of marriage instead, and with her sharp sight choose the best bonds for us. My country, my home, may exile never be my fate! May my feet never be led down the hard roads of poverty! That would be suffering most worthy of pity. Before that, let death, let death end the struggle of living and close life's brief day! There is no sharper agony than being cut off like that from one's homeland. We have seen it ourselves and need no one else to teach us: no city, no friend has shown you compassion in your dreadful affliction. But the man who has no regard for those who love him, who will not unlock his heart honestly for the unfortunate, may he perish cursed and hated. Such a man will never be my friend!
