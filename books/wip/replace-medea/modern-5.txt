AIGEUS. (Aigeus enters.) Greetings, Medea! No one knows a better word than that for welcoming a friend.

MEDEA. And greetings to you, Aigeus, son of wise Pandion. Where have you come from on your journey through this country?

AIGEUS. I have just left the ancient prophetic shrine of Phœbus.

MEDEA. What took you to that prophetic centre, the navel of the earth?

AIGEUS. I went to ask how I might become the father of children.

MEDEA. By heaven! Have you really remained without children all this time?

AIGEUS. I have none; some God's will has brought me that fortune.

MEDEA. Are you married, or have you never shared a marriage bed?

AIGEUS. No, I too have been yoked to the bed of marriage.

MEDEA. And what did Phœbus tell you about having descendants?

AIGEUS. Profound, wise words that no human being can explain.

MEDEA. Could I hear the God's answer without committing a sin?

AIGEUS. Certainly. Indeed, it needs a wise mind more than anything.

MEDEA. What were his words? Tell me, if hearing them is permitted.

AIGEUS. He said I must not loosen the projecting foot of the wine-skin.

MEDEA. Until you had done what, or arrived in which country?

AIGEUS. Until I had returned to the hearth of my ancestors.

MEDEA. Then what purpose brought you sailing to this coast?

AIGEUS. There is a man named Pittheus, the king of Trœzen.

MEDEA. Pelops' son, reputed to be a man of great religious devotion.

AIGEUS. I want to repeat the God's answer to him.

MEDEA. Yes, he is wise and understands matters of this kind.

AIGEUS. And he is the dearest of my allies in war.

MEDEA. Then may you prosper and obtain what your heart longs for.

AIGEUS. Why are your eyes lowered? Why do you look so pale and worn?

MEDEA. Aigeus, my husband is the most contemptible of all men.

AIGEUS. What do you mean? Explain plainly what is hurting you.

MEDEA. Jason is wronging me, though I have done him no wrong.

AIGEUS. What has he done to you? Tell me more clearly.

MEDEA. He is marrying another woman to make her mistress of his home.

AIGEUS. What! Has he actually dared to do something so shameful?

MEDEA. Yes. He once loved me; now he treats me with contempt.

AIGEUS. Has he fallen in love again, or come to hate you?

MEDEA. In love? Yes, with the highest things: he has betrayed love itself!

AIGEUS. Then let him go to ruin if he is so worthless!

MEDEA. What he fell in love with was a connection to royalty.

AIGEUS. Who is giving him a daughter? Finish telling me the story.

MEDEA. Kreon, the man who rules this country of Corinth.

AIGEUS. Lady, you certainly have good reason for your sorrow.

MEDEA. It is killing me! And on top of that I am being banished.

AIGEUS. By whom? This is another misfortune you are telling me about.

MEDEA. Kreon is driving me out of Corinth into exile.

AIGEUS. Does Jason let him do it? I cannot approve of that.

MEDEA. He pretends to object, but as for standing firm—never! Now I beg you, taking hold of your beard and clasping your knees as your suppliant: have pity, pity on my unhappy fate. Do not stand by while I am driven out without a home. Give me a place at the hearth in your land and in your house. May heaven then bless your marriage with children, and bless you also at your death. You do not know what good fortune you have encountered. I will put an end to your childlessness and make your seed grow into sons: I know medicines that can do this.

AIGEUS. There are many reasons, lady, why I am inclined to grant this favour. First, respect for the Gods; then, the children you promise, for in that respect the name of Aigeus is entirely extinguished. Here is my offer: if you come to my country, I will undertake your defence within the bounds of justice. But I must warn you of this. I will not agree to take you away from this land myself. If you reach my home by your own efforts, you will remain in safety, and I will hand you over to no one. You must make your own escape from here, because I also wish to remain blameless towards my allies.

MEDEA. I accept. But if you would pledge this to me with an oath, I would have everything I ask of you.

AIGEUS. What, do you not believe me? Or what difficulty still troubles you?

MEDEA. I do believe you. But Kreon and the family of Pelias are my enemies. Bound by an oath, you could never surrender me to them when they tried to drag me out of your country. If you only promised, without making a pledge to the Gods, you might become their friend and readily obey a herald's demand. My cause has no strength. They have wealth and a royal household on their side.

AIGEUS. Your words show extraordinary foresight, lady! Still, if you wish it, I will not refuse. It is safer for me too, giving me a reason I can present to your enemies, while making your position more secure. Name the Gods by whom I should swear.

MEDEA. Swear by the wide Earth, and by the Sun, my father's father, and include the entire company of Gods.

AIGEUS. What am I promising to do, or to refrain from doing? Tell me.

MEDEA. That you will never expel me from your country yourself, and if any enemy tries to drag me away, you will never, while you live, consent to surrender me.

AIGEUS. I swear by Earth, by the Sun in his pure majesty, and by every God, to keep the promise you have described.

MEDEA. That is enough. What punishment if you break your pledge?

AIGEUS. Whatever punishment falls on men who hold the Gods in contempt.

MEDEA. Continue happily on your journey; everything is settled. I too will come to your city as quickly as I can, once I have accomplished my purpose and obtained my desire. (Aigeus leaves).

CHORUS. May Maia's son, the ruler of travellers, bring you safely home. Aigeus, may the dream in your heart, the sweet visions that give wings to your feet, come to fulfilment. What you have done today has shown me the nobility of your nature.

MEDEA. Zeus, Justice born of Zeus, and radiance of the Sun, now, my friends, we shall overcome our enemies. We have set our feet upon the road. Now there is hope of taking revenge. Where my plans were weakest, this man has appeared as a harbour in the storm. I shall fasten my ship's stern-rope to him when I reach the city and stronghold of Pallas. Now I will tell you my entire plan, though I do not expect it to please you. I shall send someone from my household to ask Jason to come and see me. When he arrives, I shall speak gently, saying, "I want what you want," and, "This is a good thing." I shall say his royal marriage, his betrayal of me, benefits us and was very wisely arranged. I shall ask for my sons to be allowed to stay. Not because I would leave my children in an enemy country to be trampled on, but because I mean to use this deception to kill the king's daughter. I shall send them to the bride with gifts in their hands, a finely woven robe and a golden crown, to win exemption from banishment. If she accepts my ornaments and puts them on, she will die horribly, and so will anyone who touches her. Such terrible poisons will coat my gifts. But I pass over that part now and mourn the deed I still must carry out. I shall kill my children—yes, my own. No one will tear them from my hands. Then, when I have destroyed Jason's entire household, I shall leave this country, fleeing the blood of my dear little ones, having dared an utterly unholy act. I cannot endure the laughter of my enemies. Let everything go. What does life mean to me? I have no country, no home, no shelter from my troubles. My mistake was made that day when, deceived by this Greek's words, I abandoned my father's house. With God's help, he will pay me back. Never again will he look upon the sons I bore him alive, and he will father no son by his new bride. The wretched woman is doomed to a wretched death from my poisons. Let no one think me powerless, feeble or submissive! I am the opposite: fierce towards my enemies, kind towards those who love me. Such people live the most glorious lives.

CHORUS. Now that you have shared your plan with me, I speak both as someone who wants to help you and as a defender of human laws: do not do this.

MEDEA. It must be done. Still, it is natural for you to say that; you have not been injured as I have.

CHORUS. Woman, will you truly find the heart to kill your own sons?

MEDEA. Yes. That is how I can hurt my husband most deeply.

CHORUS. But you would become the most miserable wife of all.

MEDEA. Let it be so. Every word meant to stop me is wasted. You there! (To the Nurse.) Go and bring Jason to me, you whom I trust with every confidential errand. See that you tell no one anything of my intentions, if you are a loyal servant and a woman. (Medea and the Nurse leave).

CHORUS. How fortunate the ancient race of Erechtheus, descendants of the blessed Gods, living in a land untouched by devastation and sheltered in peace. They drink continually from Wisdom's splendid wine, moving lightly through the shining clear air as if carried on wings. There, the story says, golden-haired Harmonia gave birth to the nine stainless Pierid Muses. And they tell how the Lady of Cyprus drew from the beautifully flowing Cephisus and breathed the waters' dew far across the land on the sweet winds of Zephyr. She sends her Loves, seated in glory beside Wisdom, to shape the whole story of virtue, while over her hair she throws, throws fresh garlands of fragrant roses. (Medea returns.) How could that holy city, the city of sacred waters, which stretches out a protecting hand to every friend travelling through its country, receive an accursed murderess who pitilessly slaughtered her babies, a pollution among its daughters? Set the thought before your mind: to murder the children of your own womb! Think what it means to kill your sons, what a deed you would commit today! We beg you by your knees, we implore you by earth and heaven: do not bring such a fate upon your little ones! Where, where will you find the desperate hardness to serve so fiendish a purpose, to stiffen your heart and steady your hand so that it does not turn aside from the merciless act that will stain you with the horror of children's blood? When you turn your eyes upon your little ones, how will you restrain the mother within you and stop the tears from rising? Can you harden your breast when your children kneel before you, and redden your hand without your heart yearning for the darlings you have killed?
