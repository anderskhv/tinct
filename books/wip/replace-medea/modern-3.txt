MEDEA. (Medea comes outside.) Women of Corinth, I have left my house so that you will have no reason to find fault with me. I know many people thought arrogant, some because they carry themselves with proud reserve, others because they shrink from strangers. Some who walk quietly through life are accused of laziness. Human eyes cannot judge fairly: before we have learned another person's heart, we hate them on sight, though they have done us no injury. A newcomer must learn the city's ways. But neither do I admire those born here who make their own laws, rude people who treat their neighbours with contempt. As for me, this blow came without warning and broke my heart. I am ruined; life has lost every pleasure, and I want to die, my friends. The man who meant everything to me—you know it well—my husband, has become the worst of men. Of all living creatures with understanding, surely we women are the most miserable. First we must buy ourselves a husband as though competing at an auction, and in doing so purchase a master for our bodies, which is a still deeper evil. And the risk is enormous: will the man we get be bad or good? A woman is disgraced if she changes husbands, and she cannot reject the one she has. Entering unfamiliar habits and customs, she needs a prophet's knowledge of what she has never been taught: what sort of man will share her bed. If we learn that lesson well, and our husband lives beside us without struggling against the yoke, we are fortunate. Otherwise death is the only remedy. When life at home irritates a man, he goes out and relieves his disgust by turning to a friend or someone his own age. We have only one person, one heart to turn to for consolation. Men say that we remain safely at home while they face spears in battle. They are wrong: I would stand behind a shield twice before facing the danger of childbirth once. But your circumstances and mine are different. This city is yours; you have your father's home, the happiness of daily life and the companionship of friends. I am alone, without a city, insulted by the man who carried me off from a foreign shore. I have no mother, brother or relative to give me a safe harbour against disaster. So this is the favour I ask: if I discover a way, some scheme, to avenge these injuries on my husband, his new bride and the father giving her to him, keep it secret. A woman shrinks from danger, and loses courage at the sight of battle and a sword. But wrong her in the rights of marriage, and no spirit will be thirstier for blood.

CHORUS. I will keep silent. Medea, you would be justified in repaying your husband; your grief is no surprise. But here is Kreon, the country's ruler, coming towards us to announce some fresh command. (Kreon enters.)

KREON. Medea, with that dark, threatening look and your anger against your husband, I order you to leave this country in exile, taking both sons with you. Do not delay. I am the judge in this matter, and I shall not return home until I have driven you beyond the borders.

MEDEA. Ah, my misery! I am utterly destroyed! My enemies are chasing me under full sail, and there is nowhere to land out of these waves of disaster. Yet, injured as I am, let me ask one thing: Kreon, why are you sending me into exile?

KREON. I am afraid of you; there is no need to disguise it. I fear you will do my daughter some irreparable harm. Several things give me reason for this fear. You are clever, and skilled in many dangerous arts. You are bitter at being deprived of your husband's bed. And people bring me reports of your threats against the father, the bridegroom and the bride. I mean to protect my head before the blow falls. Better to have your hatred now, woman, than show pity once and bitterly regret it when it is too late.

MEDEA. This is hardly the first time, Kreon, that gossip has done me a serious injury. Any man with sense should never have his sons taught to be wiser than ordinary people. They acquire learning that brings them no profit, and earn other people's envy and resentment. Offer unfamiliar wisdom to fools, and they will call you useless, not wise. Let your reputation rise above those who flatter themselves on their intelligence, and the city will hate you. I too have suffered this fate. My knowledge makes some people jealous; others think I do nothing, or interfere too much, or avoid society. Yet I am not so very wise. And now you are frightened that I will spoil your happiness. Kreon, have no fear of me: I am hardly someone who would dare offend against kings. What injury have you done me? You gave your daughter to the man you chose. It is my husband I hate. And you, no doubt, acted wisely in doing this? No, I do not begrudge your good fortune. Marry, and prosper. Only let me remain in this country of yours. However badly I have been treated, I will hold my tongue and submit to those who are stronger.

KREON. Your words sound gentle, but the fear remains in my heart that you are planning harm all the while. I trust you even less than I did before. A woman—or a man—who openly rages is easier to guard against than someone who plots in silence. Leave at once, and spare me your appeals. This is settled. No trick will let you stay among us when you are my enemy.

MEDEA. No, I beg you by your knees, and by your daughter, the bride!

KREON. Your appeals are useless. You will never persuade me.

MEDEA. Will you turn me out, with no respect at all for my pleas?

KREON. Yes. I do not love you more than I love my own family.

MEDEA. My country, how intensely I remember you at this moment!

KREON. Yes, Corinth is dear to me too, after my children.

MEDEA. Alas! What a curse love brings upon human beings!

KREON. A blessing or a curse, I suppose, according to one's luck.

MEDEA. Zeus, let the man responsible for this never escape your notice!

KREON. Get away, you raging fool, and stop causing me trouble.

MEDEA. I have troubles enough already; I do not need any more.

KREON. My servants will soon take hold of you and throw you out.

MEDEA. No, no, do not do that, Kreon, I implore you!

KREON. You seem determined to make a scene of this, woman.

MEDEA. I will leave the country. Staying is not the favour I ask.

KREON. Then why resist? Why not free Corinth of your presence?

MEDEA. Allow me just this one day to remain, to make some plans for our exile and find a place of safety for my little ones, since their father takes no care to provide for them. Have pity on them. You too are a father, and it is fitting that you should show kindness to these children. It is not my own banishment that torments me. I am mourning the disaster that has fallen on them.

KREON. Cruelty is the last thing in my nature. Giving way to pity has spoilt many of my plans. Even now, woman, I know I am making a mistake; still, I will grant your request. But take this warning: if the Sun-god's returning torch finds you and your sons anywhere inside this country's borders, you will die. That promise will not be broken. So, if you must stay, remain for one day. That is too short a time for the deeds I fear. (He leaves.)

CHORUS. Unhappy woman! I grieve for your misery, for all the distress and suffering that confront you! Where can you turn? What friendly hand will welcome you among strangers? Where will you find a home, a country willing to receive you and free you from these evils? Medea, with God's hand upon your helm, your ship has been steered into waves of despair that will engulf and wreck you!

MEDEA. Things have gone badly on every side; no one can deny it. But in this matter they have not gone badly at all. Do not suppose that yet. The bride and bridegroom still have a bitter struggle ahead, and those who arranged this marriage have no small troubles waiting for them. Do you think I would ever have humbled myself before that man unless I had something to gain or some deception to practise? I would not have spoken to him or touched his hand. But he has been so foolish that, when exile could have prevented every plan of mine, he has granted this day's reprieve. Before it is over I shall have killed three enemies: the father, his daughter and my husband. I have so many ways to bring them death that I cannot decide which to attempt first, my friends. Shall I burn the bridal chamber, or steal quietly through the house to their bed and drive a sharpened knife into their hearts? One obstacle stands in that path: if they catch me crossing the threshold with my murderous purpose, I shall die and give my enemies a reason to laugh. Best to take the direct route that suits my special skill, and destroy them with poison. Yes. But suppose they are dead: what city will receive me? What host will offer a refuge, a safe home, and protect my life from those who seek revenge? There is no one. I shall wait a little. If some stronghold of safety appears, I will accomplish these deaths through secrecy and cunning. But if misfortune forces me out with no hope left, I shall seize the sword myself, even at the cost of my life, and kill them, daring the reckless deed with my own strong hand. By the Queen of Night, whom I honour above all and have chosen as my partner, Hekatê, who lives beside the dark shrine at my hearth, none of them will torment my heart without regretting it. I shall make their wedding bitter and miserable, their marriage promises bitter, and my banishment bitter for them too. Come on, Medea! Use every scrap of your magical knowledge, every plan and trick. Forward to the terrible deed! This is the moment for courage. Look at the wrongs you have suffered. You must not let the sons of Sisyphus or Jason's bride make a laughing-stock of you, a king's daughter, descended from the Sun! You know what means to use. Yes, they say it is women's nature to be helpless in doing good, but wonderfully skilful in devising evil.

CHORUS. The sacred rivers are creeping uphill, back towards their springs. Justice has become injustice, and the old order has dissolved into confusion. Men's hearts think only of betrayal; belief in the Gods reels from its ancient foundations and turns to falsehood. Changes, changes! The people's voice will now bestow honour on me. Glory will shine across my life. The old story about women is over: its age-worn slanders will no longer bind us like chains. Those songs of earlier generations, forever accusing women of treachery and broken faith, will falter in shame. Alas, why have our lips not been touched with the fire of song from the altar of Phœbus, king of the harp and giver of inspiration! Then I would raise my own clear song to challenge men's. Through all the ages there are women to make poets proud, heroines worthy of their verses. But you sailed far across the foam from your ancient home, driven by a heart gone wild. You saw the gates of the sea, the Twin Rocks, open apart. Now in a stranger's country you must wake to an empty marriage bed, abandoned by your husband, overtaken by misery, and driven out in disgrace. Oaths have lost their binding power. Wide Hellas has no shame left for promises betrayed; shame has flown away to heaven. You have no father's house to offer harbour when the storms of trouble gather. Another woman has seized your bridal chamber, and now, in the pride of her power, rules over you, unhappy one.
