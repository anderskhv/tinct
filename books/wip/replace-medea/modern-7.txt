MEDEA. Friends, I have waited a long time to learn what fortune will bring from that place. Look, I see one of Jason's servants coming towards us. My expectant heart tells me he is bringing news of fresh disaster. (A Messenger enters).

MESSENGER. Medea, you who have committed a dreadful, unlawful act, escape, escape. Once you have boarded a ship or a chariot that races over the plain, do not leave it for a moment.

MEDEA. What has happened to make such an escape necessary?

MESSENGER. The princess has just died, and her father Kreon is dead as well, both killed by your poisons.

MEDEA. What splendid news you bring. From this moment I count you among my friends and benefactors.

MESSENGER. What are you saying? Are you sane, or have you lost your mind? You hear that the royal household has been destroyed and feel pleasure, with no fear at all?

MEDEA. Yes, I could answer you with an argument of my own. But do not hurry away, my friend. Tell me how they died. You will make me twice as happy if their deaths were especially horrible.

MESSENGER. When your two children arrived with their father and entered the rooms decorated for the wedding, those of us servants who grieved for your troubles were pleased. News of a truce in the old quarrel between you and your husband immediately began to whisper from ear to ear. One person kissed your sons' hands, another their golden heads. Happiness drew me after them into the women's rooms. The woman we now honoured in your place, before she saw your pair of sons yoked together like a chariot team, kept turning longing eyes towards Jason. Then she drew her veil across her face and twisted her pale neck away contemptuously, disgusted by the approach of your children. Your husband tried to soften the girl's anger and hostility. He said, "Do not treat your friends as enemies. Stop being angry and turn your head back. Regard as friends the people your husband regards as friends. Accept their presents, and ask your father to cancel their exile, for my sake." When she saw the finery, she could not resist and agreed to everything her husband asked. Before the father and your boys had gone far from her room, she took the richly worked clothes and dressed herself, setting the golden crown around her curls. She arranged her hair before a bright mirror, smiling at the phantom image of herself. Then she rose and walked through the rooms, taking dainty steps on ivory feet, delighted with her gifts and repeatedly glancing down from her neck to the hem at her ankles. What followed was terrible to see. Her colour changed suddenly. She staggered backwards, her limbs trembling, and barely managed to drop onto a couch before she fell to the floor. An old serving-woman, thinking perhaps that Pan or another God had sent this seizure, raised a cry of prayer. That was before she noticed the white foam bubbling from the girl's mouth, her rolling eyes and her bloodless face. Then the servant gave a different cry, a long, loud scream. At once one person rushed to her father's rooms, another to her new husband, to tell them the bride had been stricken. The whole house echoed with hurrying feet. By then a fast runner straining every limb could have reached the finish of a six-plethra course. She woke from that speechless trance, her eyes having been shut, and gave a shrill, horrible scream, poor woman! Her agony attacked like two armies charging at once. The golden band round her head began shooting out an astonishing stream of ravenous flame, while the delicate clothes your children had given her devoured her pale flesh. She leapt from the couch and ran, all on fire, shaking her head and hair from side to side to throw off the crown. But the gold clasp gripped her firmly. Whenever she shook her locks, the fire blazed with twice the fury. At last, overcome by suffering, she fell to the floor, so disfigured that only a father could have recognised her. The regal calm of her eyes and her lovely features had disappeared. Blood dripped from the top of her head, mingled with sparks of fire. The flesh melted off her bones like resin dripping from a pine, consumed by that mysterious poison. A dreadful sight! Everyone was afraid to touch the body, warned by her hideous fate. Her miserable father, still knowing nothing of what had happened, suddenly entered and threw himself upon her corpse. He began to wail, wrapped his arms around her body, kissed her and cried, "My unhappy child, what God has destroyed you so horribly? Who has robbed this old man, already a tomb, of you? Ah, if only I could die beside you!" When his tears and groans subsided, he tried to raise his aged body, but he clung to those fine robes as ivy clings around laurel branches. Then came a ghastly struggle. As he tried to lift his knee, her body pulled against him. If he dragged himself away by force, he tore his old flesh from his bones. At last he gave up and breathed his last, the unfortunate man unable to resist his destruction any longer. The bodies lie there, the daughter held in her old father's embrace. Tears must mourn that suffering; words cannot. I shall say nothing about your part in it. You will find your own way to escape punishment. But now, as often before, I consider human life a shadow. I am not afraid to say that those who seem wise among us, those skilled in clever speech, are guilty of the deepest foolishness. No mortal is happy. At the high tide of fortune a man may be more prosperous than his neighbour. But happy? No! (He leaves).

CHORUS. I think the God will surround Jason with many disasters today, and justly so. But how pitiful your fate, daughter of Kreon: you have gone down into the halls of Hades because Jason wanted you for his wife!

MEDEA. Friends, I have decided. I shall kill my children quickly and leave this country, instead of delaying and surrendering my sons to death at hands still more merciless. They must die; and since that cannot be avoided, I who gave them life shall also give them death. Come, my heart, prepare yourself for the fight! Why hesitate over the dreadful evil deeds that must be done? My miserable hand, take up the sword. Take it, and move towards this bitter starting-line of life. Do not become cowardly. Do not think of your little ones, of how dearly you love them, or how you gave them birth. Forget your sons for this one short day, then mourn them afterwards. Even if you kill them, they remain beloved, and I remain a wretched woman. (Medea leaves).

CHORUS. Earth, and the Sun's radiance that reveals everything, look down upon an accursed woman before this mother satisfies her thirst for murder, striking with her own hands the tender fruit of her womb. Look down: she comes from your golden lineage, and the offspring of the Gods is gripped by human terror and the shadow of doom. Light born of heaven, stop her, hold her back. We beg you to drive from this house the miserable, blood-stained Erinnys pursued by demons! Your pains in childbirth were wasted, wasted the bearing of those near and dear to you, woman who escaped through the Pass of Fear and sped in flight past the dark-blue Clashing Crags! Alas for her! Why has savage anger stirred the depths of her soul until only pitiless murder can repay her injuries? Vengeance falls harshly upon mortals who spill their family's blood. It cries out from the earth, a voice from the Gods, terrifying the killers upon whose homes it will descend. (The Children cry out from inside).

CHILD 1. What can I do? How can I escape from my mother's hands?

CHILD 2. I do not know, my dearest brother. Death has come for us!

CHORUS. That cry! Can you hear it, the children's cry! Miserable creature! Woman with an accursed fate! Should I go inside? My heart is shouting, "Save the children from the murder closing in on them!"

CHILD 1. Yes, by the Gods, come and help us! We desperately need you.

CHILD 2. The sword has caught us in its deadly net, and there is no escape!

CHORUS. Wretched woman! What stone is your breast made of, what steel was used to shape your heart, that you are determined to kill the babies you bore with the very hands that lovingly embraced them? I have heard of only one woman in the past who attacked her own loved ones: Ino, driven mad by the Gods when Zeus's bride chased her from her home to wander lost and alone. Standing at the edge of a sea-cliff, she fell, poor woman! Guilt for her children's blood dragged her feet down into the salt water, and she died beside both her children. What more dreadful horror can still be done? Marriage bed of women, heavy with anguish, what harm you have already brought upon humanity, how many forms of ruin! (Jason enters).

JASON. Women standing near this house, is Medea, who has done these terrible things, still inside, or has she fled? She will have to hide beneath the earth or lift herself on wings into the farthest heaven, or else face the royal family's revenge. Does she imagine she can kill the country's rulers and walk out of this house unharmed? But it is my sons I care about, not her. Those she has injured will repay her injury. I have come to save my children's lives, so that the relatives of the dead do not, to my sorrow, take revenge upon them for their mother's wicked murders.

CHORUS. Unhappy Jason, you do not know how deep into misery you have fallen, or you would never have spoken like that.

JASON. What is it now? Does she intend to kill me as well?

CHORUS. Your sons have died. Their own mother killed them.

JASON. My misery! What are you telling me? Woman, your words have killed me!

CHORUS. Think of your children as no longer living. That is the truth.

JASON. What? She killed them? Where did it happen, inside the house or outside?

CHORUS. Open the doors and you will see the bodies of your children.

JASON. Servants, pull back the bolts at once! Unbar these doors so that I can see the double horror, my dead children and their mother, and kill her in return. (Medea appears above them in a chariot drawn by dragons).

MEDEA. Why are you shaking the doors and trying to break them open, searching for your dead children and the woman who killed them? Stop trying. If you want something from me, say it. Your hands will never reach me. My father's father, the Sun, has given me this chariot to protect me from an enemy's grasp.

JASON. Abomination! Woman most hateful to the Gods, to me and to all humanity, you dared plunge a sword into the little ones you bore and leave me ruined and childless! After doing this, after daring an act so unholy, can you still look upon the earth and sun? May destruction take you! Now I see clearly what I failed to see when I brought you out of a barbarian country and household into a Greek home: a total curse, a traitor to your father and the land that raised you! The Gods have hurled the thunderbolt of your guilt against me. You murdered your own brother beside his hearth before you boarded Argo with its beautiful prow. Those were your beginnings. Then you married me and became my sons' mother, only to murder them for the sake of your marriage rights. No Greek woman would have dared such a deed. Yet I lowered myself to marry you instead of one of them, a hateful, savage bride, a tigress instead of a woman, with a fiercer nature than Scylla of the Tyrrhenian sea. But no amount of abuse could pierce you; your nature is too hardened. Get away, you criminal stained with your children's blood! I can only weep for my destiny. I shall have no happiness with my newly married bride, and never speak alive to the sons I fathered and raised. Lost, lost to me!

MEDEA. I could answer your words in a long argument if Father Zeus did not already know how I have treated you and how you have treated me. You were not going to dishonour my marriage bed and then live happily, laughing at me! Nor were your princess and Kreon, your new relation by marriage, going to banish me without suffering! So call me a tigress if you like, or Scylla haunting the Tyrrhenian coast. I have torn your heart, as you deserved.

JASON. But you are grieving too; you share in my suffering!

MEDEA. Yes. Yet sorrow is a gain to me if it stops your laughter.

JASON. My children, what a wicked mother was yours!

MEDEA. My sons, your own father's lust brought you to destruction!

JASON. It was certainly not my hand that killed them.

MEDEA. No, it was your arrogance and the new marriage bonds you forged.

JASON. What, you claim a right to kill them over a marriage!

MEDEA. Do you consider that a trivial injury for a woman?

JASON. For a sensible woman, yes. But nothing could satisfy you.

MEDEA. They are alive no longer, and that will pierce your heart!

JASON. They live, alas! They will bring vengeance down upon you.

MEDEA. The Gods understand who first set this misery in motion.

JASON. Indeed they do: they understand your loathsome nature.

MEDEA. You are the loathsome one. Your bitter tongue disgusts me.

JASON. And yours disgusts me. Yet we could easily be rid of one another.

MEDEA. How? What would I have to do? I would welcome that.

JASON. Give me my dead children so I may bury them and mourn.

MEDEA. Never. I shall bury them with my own hands, carrying them to the sanctuary of Mountain Hêrê, so that no enemy can outrage them by violating their grave. I shall require this land of Sisyphus to hold a solemn festival and rites of atonement for this unholy murder. As for me, I am going to the land of Erechtheus to live with Aigeus, Pandion's son. You, foul man, will meet the foul death you deserve: a piece of Argo's wreck will strike your skull, now that you have seen the bitter outcome of this marriage.

JASON. May the Fury who avenges children strike you, and Justice watching over murder repay you!

MEDEA. What God, what spirit would listen to your plea, you oath-breaking scoundrel who betrays a guest?

JASON. Away with you, foul creature whose own deed killed her children!

MEDEA. Go back into your house and lead your bride from there to her grave!

JASON. I go, a desolate father, robbed of both sons from my home!

MEDEA. You do not yet know true grief. Wait until your old age arrives.

JASON. My children, whom I love above everything!

MEDEA. Their mother loved them. You did not.

JASON. And yet their mother killed them!

MEDEA. So that you would be caught in the net you had spread for me.

JASON. How miserable I am! In my grief I long to press my lips against my sons' beloved lips.

MEDEA. So now you call for them, now you want to kiss them, after rejecting them before?

JASON. By the Gods, give me just this much: let me touch my children's sweet, soft bodies!

MEDEA. No. Every plea you make is wasted on the air.

JASON. Zeus, do you hear how she rejects me, what outrage I am suffering from that loathsome woman who murdered her children, that mother tigress? Yet in my helplessness and disgrace I mourn my loved ones. I call high heaven to bear witness; I ask God to hear this declaration: you have killed my sons and prevent my hands from touching them or laying their bodies in the earth! If only I had never fathered them, rather than live to see them destroyed by you today!

CHORUS. Every destiny belongs to Zeus on Olympus, and he reveals it. The Gods bring many things about that no one hoped for. What we expected, they choose not to fulfil; they open roads our eyes could never see. This astonishing event has happened so. (All leave).
