JASON. (Jason enters.) I have come because you sent for me. Though you are my enemy, I will not refuse you this favour. I will listen, woman, to whatever fresh request you wish to make.

MEDEA. Jason, forgive what I said before, and bear with that temper of mine. The memory of our former love should make that possible. I have called myself to account and reproached myself: "Miserable woman, why am I behaving like a mad creature? Why am I raging against good advice, quarrelling with the rulers of this country and with my husband, who is acting for my real benefit by marrying into royalty and giving my sons brothers? Why do I not put an end to my anger? What is wrong with me, when the Gods are offering blessings? Have I no children? Do I not know that we are exiles from our own country and have no friends?" Thinking it over, I realised I had been feeding enormous foolishness and anger without reason. Now I approve of you. You seem wise to have secured this family connection for us, while I was senseless. I should have supported your plans, helped with everything, decorated the marriage bed and gladly waited upon the bride. But we are women; there is no need for a harsher name. You should not repay wrong with wrong, or set your own foolishness against mine. I give way and admit I was unwise then, but have now come to a better judgement. Children, my children, leave the house and come here. (The Children enter.) Come and greet your father; join me in saying goodbye to him. With your mother, put the old hatred aside and make peace with those who love you. We have called a truce; bitterness has given way. Take his right hand. Oh, the evils lying in ambush! The shadow of things hidden keeps haunting me! My children, will you live for many years and still stretch loving arms towards him like this? Ah, how quickly my tears come, how frightened I am! The quarrel with your father is over—so late, so late!—and my softening eyes have filled with tears.

CHORUS. Tears of frightened sorrow are springing into my eyes as well. May nothing worse than what has happened yet befall us!

JASON. Woman, I welcome this mood, though I do not condemn the earlier one. It is natural for women to grow angry when their husband negotiates another marriage. But your heart has turned towards better thoughts now, and though late, you have cast your vote for the winning side. That is a wise woman's action. As for you, children, your father has taken considerable thought for you, carefully, and may heaven help him. I believe you and your brothers will one day stand foremost in this land of Corinth. Grow strong; your father, with whatever God favours us, will arrange the rest. May I see you grown tall and handsome, in the prime of manhood, victorious over my enemies. But why are you wetting your eyes with sorrowful tears, turning your pale face away from the children? Why do you not welcome what I say with happiness?

MEDEA. It is nothing. My heart is simply dwelling on these children.

JASON. Do not be afraid. I will make every arrangement for their good.

MEDEA. I will try. It is not that I doubt your promises. But a woman is only a woman, born to weep.

JASON. Poor woman, why are you making these laments over them?

MEDEA. I gave birth to them. When you prayed for their lives, pity came over me, whispering, "Will it happen?" But I have told you only part of what I asked you here to discuss. Let me say the rest. Since the king wishes to expel me from this country, I know perfectly well that leaving is best for me too. I should not remain as an obstacle to you and the rulers, whose household regards me as an enemy. So I will go into exile. But plead with Kreon not to banish my sons, so that you can raise them yourself.

JASON. I may not succeed in persuading him, but I must make the attempt.

MEDEA. Then ask your bride to appeal to her father, so that your sons are not driven out of this land.

JASON. Certainly. And I think I can persuade her, if she has anything in common with other women.

MEDEA. I will help you in that effort. I shall send her gifts whose beauty, I know, far surpasses anything people see today: a finely woven robe and a golden crown. Our sons will carry them. An attendant must bring the ornaments here immediately. Her blessings will be countless, not merely one: she gains you as her husband, a matchless spouse, and possesses ornaments the Sun, my father's father, once gave to his own descendants! Take these wedding presents in your hands, my sons. Carry them to the fortunate princess-bride and give them to her. These gifts will surely satisfy her.

JASON. But, foolish woman, why strip yourself of these treasures? Do you think a royal household lacks robes, or gold? Keep them; do not give them away. If my wife values me at all, I am certain she will prefer my request to any treasures.

MEDEA. Do not say that. Gifts persuade even the Gods, so people say. Gold weighs more with human beings than endless words. Fortune is hers; God favours her cause now, and her power is newly born. I would pay with my life to buy my sons' release from exile, not merely with gold. Now, children, go into that wealthy house. Appeal to your father's new wife, my lady and queen. Ask not to be exiled, and give her my ornaments. The most important thing is that she should take the gifts into her own hands. Hurry, and return to your mother with happy news that she has obtained what she longs for. (Jason and the Children leave).

CHORUS. My hope for the children's lives has changed into despair. There is no hope left! Already they are walking the road to slaughter! The bride will accept that garland of a crown, its glittering beauty concealing destruction for the unhappy woman. She will take it between her hands to place an ornament of Hades upon her golden hair. Its dazzling loveliness, its unearthly splendour, will quickly tempt her to dress herself in the robe and the crown worked in gold. Soon she will stand arrayed as a bride before phantoms risen from Hades. Such a terrible trap will catch her feet. The poor woman will be swallowed by her appointed death, and never again steal out of Doom's dark prison. And you, wretched bridegroom, cursed in your eagerness for a royal alliance, you are bringing destruction on your sons' lives without knowing, without thinking! Your bride will pledge herself to a hideous death. How far you are sinking from your old good fortune! Amid my laments I grieve too for your anguish, unhappy mother, preparing to slaughter your own little ones in revenge upon the man who would marry another unlawfully and abandon you to live beside a king's daughter. (The Children's Guardian returns with the Children).

CHILDREN'S GUARDIAN. Mistress, your sons are freed from the sentence of exile! The royal bride gladly took your present into her hands. Your sons have peace now.

MEDEA. What misery!

CHILDREN'S GUARDIAN. Why are you standing there stunned when things have gone well? Why turn your face away, instead of listening happily to the news I bring?

MEDEA. How wretched I am!

CHILDREN'S GUARDIAN. That cry does not fit the news I have brought you.

MEDEA. Misery, and misery again!

CHILDREN'S GUARDIAN. Have I unknowingly brought bad news? Was I mistaken in thinking this a happy message?

MEDEA. Your news is what it is. I do not blame you for it.

CHILDREN'S GUARDIAN. Why are your eyes cast down? Why are you shedding tears?

MEDEA. They must fall, old man. The Gods and I together—fool that I am!—have arranged these matters badly.

CHILDREN'S GUARDIAN. Take courage. Your children may still bring you back to your home.

MEDEA. Before that happens, I in my misery shall send others home.

CHILDREN'S GUARDIAN. You are not the only mother parted from her sons. Human beings must bear misfortune with submission.

MEDEA. I will. But go inside and prepare what the children need for their daily life. (The Children's Guardian leaves.) Children, children, you have a city and a home where you will live, leaving your wretched mother behind, deprived of her forever. I must go into exile in another country before I have enjoyed you and seen your happiness, before I have prepared your marriage beds, dressed your brides and bridal rooms, and lifted the wedding torch. What a curse this merciless temper has brought upon me! For nothing, for nothing, my babies, I raised you. All for nothing I wore myself out in labour and endured the sharp pains of giving you birth. Ah, the hopes I had, unhappy woman! I hoped your hands would care for me in old age, that I would die enclosed in your loving arms, as everyone desires! But that sweet dream is over, over! Deprived of you, I shall waste away a bitter, sorrowful life. You will pass into another life and never again look lovingly upon your mother. Woe! Woe! Why are your eyes fixed on me, my darlings? Why are you smiling at me for the very last time? Alas! What am I to do? My heart gives way when I look at my children's laughing eyes! Women, I cannot do it! Goodbye to those plans! I shall take my children out of this land. Why should I wound their father's heart through their suffering, only to bring twice as much suffering on myself? I will not, I will not! Farewell, my plans! But—but what is happening to me? Am I willing to be laughed at, to let my enemies slip from my hands without punishment? I must dare to act. Shame on the cowardice that let these tender words escape my heart! Children, go indoors. (The Children leave.) Anyone who cannot witness my sacrifice without sin must answer for himself. My hand will not tremble. Oh! Oh! My heart, my heart, do not do this deed! Let them live, miserable heart; spare your babies! Living there with me, they will bring you joy. No! By the demons below who dwell with Hades, I will never leave my children for my enemies to trample upon. They have to die. And since it has to happen, I who gave them life will kill them. All of this is fixed destruction: she will not escape! Even now the crown is on her head, and the royal bride is dying in my robe. I know it. But since I must set out on a most unhappy journey, and send these children on one still more unhappy, I want to speak to my sons. (The Children return.) Give me your right hands, my babies; let your mother kiss them. Hands I love so dearly, lips so dear to me, my children's bodies and noble faces, may blessings be yours there! Your father has robbed you of everything here. Sweet, sweet embrace! Your rose-petal skin, your fragrant breath! Go away, go away! I have no strength left to look at you; evil has overwhelmed me. (The Children leave.) Now, now I understand the horrors I intend. But passion masters clear judgement, and this is what brings the most dreadful evils upon humanity.

CHORUS. Often before now my mind has climbed lonely heights of thought, the steep paths of heaven, or plunged deep into darkness where a woman's weaker heart has failed. But why should it fail? Should no inspiration stir a woman's breast? Should she never welcome that sweet visitor, Song, which gives voice to Wisdom's thought? Alas! Not every woman can! Few, very few—perhaps you might find one in a thousand—have an inward daylight kindled by poetry's sun. Now I say this: quiet happiness that has never known love's fever in the blood, or the pains and joys of motherhood, surpasses the joy mingled with worry that parents know. Those without children never discover whether babies bring sunshine or cloud to human lives; the labour and grief of parental love lie far beyond their knowledge. But those whose houses ring with children's sweet laughter I see constantly fretted by anxiety, always working to provide a fitting upbringing for those they love. A person labours with love stronger than death. Yet, yet, who can know whether the heir to whom all that wealth will pass will be wise or foolish? Last comes the worst thing of all. Even if you acquire enough wealth, even if your sons grow into fine, good men, what if cruel Death carries their lives down and disappears into Hades? What benefit is there in Heaven laying this upon mankind, on top of everything else, this crowning sorrow?
