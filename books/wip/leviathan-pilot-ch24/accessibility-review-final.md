# Accessibility Review — Final (Reviewer A, blind/candidate-only)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 23 ("Of the Public Ministers of Sovereign Power") — modern-English reading edition, pilot chapter 24 slot.

**Coverage:** Read candidate-sonnet.json in full, all 13 paragraphs (indices 0–12), start to finish as a continuous read, then again paragraph by paragraph. No source text, drafter's notes, or prior review file was read, per instructions.

---

## Paragraph-by-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 1** (the long definitional paragraph beginning "A PUBLIC MINISTER is someone...")

- Quote: *"So neither ushers, nor sergeants, nor any other officers who attend the assembly merely for the convenience of the men assembled — in an aristocracy or a democracy — nor stewards, chamberlains, cofferers (household treasurers), nor any other officers of a monarch's household, are public ministers in a monarchy."*
  Why it's hard: this sentence lists two separate groups — assembly attendants (which belong to an aristocracy or democracy) and household officers (which belong to a monarchy) — but closes with a single shared predicate, "are public ministers in a monarchy." A reader who has just been told the first group belongs to "an aristocracy or a democracy" will stumble on the sentence asserting something about them "in a monarchy." It reads as if the whole sentence is only about monarchies, which contradicts the aristocracy/democracy clause a few words earlier. This requires a re-read to untangle, and even then the logic of why "in a monarchy" governs the whole sentence isn't obvious from the wording alone.
- Quote: *"any other officers who attend the assembly merely for the convenience of the men assembled — in an aristocracy or a democracy —"*
  Why it's hard: the em-dash aside "in an aristocracy or a democracy" is dropped in immediately after "the men assembled," so on first pass it can read as a location/setting for the assembling ("the men assembled in an aristocracy or a democracy") rather than as a label for which kind of commonwealth this class of officer belongs to. Minor stumble, compounds with the issue above.

**Paragraph 2** ("Of public ministers, some are entrusted with a general administration...")

- Quote: *"an infant king's predecessor may entrust a protector or regent with the whole administration of the kingdom during his minority"*
  Why it's hard: "predecessor" is an unusual word choice here — a general reader's first association with "predecessor" is simply "the previous title-holder," and it takes a beat to work out that this means something like the late king (the infant's father) making arrangements for a regency, rather than, say, an adjacent official. Not a blocking error, but a small comprehension hitch a plainer phrase (e.g., "the infant king's late father" or "the previous king") would avoid.

**Paragraph 3** ("Others have a special administration...")

- Quote: *"Those with authority over tributes, impositions, rents, fines, or any other public revenue"*
  Why it's hard: "tributes" and especially "impositions" are unfamiliar/archaic terms for a contemporary reader (impositions = duties/taxes). The surrounding list ("rents, fines, or any other public revenue") does supply enough context to infer the general sense, so this is a mild vocabulary stumble rather than a real block.

**Paragraph 4** ("Second, those who have authority over the militia...")

- Quote: *"a soldier without a command... does not for that reason represent its person, because there is no one for him to represent it to"*
  Why it's hard: "there is no one for him to represent it to" is compressed — the reader has to reconstruct that representing the commonwealth means representing it *to subordinates one commands*, and a rank-and-file soldier has none. The very next sentence ("Everyone who holds a command represents the commonwealth only to those he commands") retroactively clarifies it, but the reader has to hold the ambiguity for a beat before the payoff sentence resolves it.

**Paragraph 12** (final paragraph, "Neither a counsellor nor a council of state...")

- Quote: *"Neither a counsellor nor a council of state — considered simply as having no authority of judicature or command, but only of giving advice to the sovereign when it is asked for, or offering it when it is not — is a public person."*
  Why it's hard: this is a legally-legal but overloaded construction — the subject ("Neither a counsellor nor a council of state") and its verb ("is a public person") are separated by a long qualifying clause in em-dashes. A reader has to hold the subject in memory across the whole interruption to land on the verb. Not wrong, but the kind of sentence that invites a second pass.
- Quote: *"no one can represent him to himself while he is present in person"*
  Why it's hard: "represent him to himself" is a slightly abstract reflexive construction. It's decodable from context (the sovereign doesn't need an advisor to stand in for him when he's already there), but it asks a small extra effort of the reader.

## What's working well

- The chapter is consistently good about glossing potentially opaque terms in-line and immediately: "judicature — the administration of justice," "the bench — in their seats of justice," "cofferers (household treasurers)," "Dei Gratia in the strict sense — that is, by the favor of God alone," and the Latin phrases in paragraph 5 are each followed immediately by a plain-English translation in parentheses. This is the single biggest accessibility strength of the draft — a general reader is never left alone with unexplained jargon or Latin.
- The recurring "Ministers, because...; public, because..." etymological aside (paragraphs 3, 5) is handled consistently and reads naturally each time, not mechanically repeated.
- The body-part analogies closing several paragraphs (nerves and tendons, organs of voice, hands, an eye, the public ear) land clearly and give the reader a concrete image to anchor each abstract category of "public minister."
- Paragraph 8 (the England Common Pleas / Public Pleas / jury passage) is the strongest paragraph in the chapter — dense historical/legal content but delivered in short, sequential, well-signposted sentences ("By Common Pleas I mean...", "First...", "So, having judges of his own choosing..."). It reads smoothly despite covering a lot of ground.
- Paragraph 7's three-part enumeration of how a defendant's judge comes to be "agreed on" (challenge, appeal, appeal to the sovereign) is logically dense material but is broken into clearly numbered steps ("First," "Second," "Third"), which keeps it readable despite the underlying legal reasoning being intricate.
- No evidence of truncation or dropped content anywhere in the 13 paragraphs — every paragraph reads as a complete thought from opening to closing period, consistent with the earlier paragraph-1 fix having taken.

---

## Overall verdict: **needs targeted fixes**

The chapter reads smoothly for the great majority of its length, and its handling of archaic/technical vocabulary (Latin phrases, legal and administrative terms) is genuinely strong — glosses are supplied consistently and unobtrusively. The chapter is not in "needs a broader pass" territory: there is no pervasive stiffness or systemic difficulty. But it isn't yet "substantially accessible" as-is, because paragraph 1 contains one sentence (the ushers/sergeants/stewards sentence) that actively misleads a reader about which kind of commonwealth is being discussed, which is a real comprehension trip rather than just added density. Paragraph 12's opening sentence, paragraph 4's "represent it to" clause, and the paragraph 2 "predecessor" phrasing are smaller, lower-priority items that would tighten the chapter further but are not blocking. Fixing paragraph 1's structure is the one change that would matter most before sign-off.
