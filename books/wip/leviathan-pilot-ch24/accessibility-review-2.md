## Paragraph-by-paragraph issues

**Paragraph 0**
- "the similar parts of a commonwealth" — "similar" is an unusual word choice here (presumably meaning "homogeneous" or "alike," i.e., citizens who are interchangeable units). It's set up by contrast with "organic parts" (which *is* glossed via the appositive "that is, the parts with a distinct function, like organs in a body"), but "similar parts" itself gets no equivalent gloss, so a reader may pause wondering "similar to what?" Minor, but asymmetric — one technical term is explained, its paired opposite isn't.

**Paragraph 1**
- "A monarch bears the person not only of the commonwealth but also of a man; a sovereign assembly bears the person not only of the commonwealth but also of the assembly." — "bears the person of" is a technical, archaic construction (meaning roughly "acts in the role/capacity of," not "carries a human being"). It's used twice in this sentence and recurs throughout the chapter ("represent the person of the commonwealth," "represents the person of the sovereign," etc.) but is never directly defined anywhere in this chapter. A reader without prior context may read "bears the person... of a man" as a near category error — isn't a monarch already a man? — and have to reverse-engineer the technical sense of "person" as "role" or "legal capacity" from context alone. This is the single most load-bearing piece of unglossed vocabulary in the chapter, since almost every paragraph depends on understanding it.
- "So neither ushers, nor sergeants, nor any other officers who attend the assembly merely for the convenience of the men assembled — in an aristocracy or a democracy — nor stewards, chamberlains, cofferers (household treasurers), nor any other officers of a monarch's household, are public ministers in a monarchy." — A four-way "neither... nor... nor... nor" list is stretched across a long sentence, and a dash-set aside ("in an aristocracy or a democracy") is dropped into the middle of the list rather than at a natural boundary. By the time the sentence reaches "are public ministers in a monarchy" at the very end, the reader has to reassemble which items belong to which regime. Likely requires a second pass.

**Paragraph 2**
- "an infant king's predecessor may entrust a protector or regent with the whole administration of the kingdom during an infant king's minority" — This is confusing on its face: a "predecessor" is, by definition, someone who came before and is presumably no longer reigning (or living), yet the sentence has him actively "entrusting" a regent "during an infant king's minority" — i.e., during the *current* reign. Read literally, it sounds like a dead king is still acting after his own succession. The intended meaning (a father/prior sovereign makes this arrangement before he dies or leaves the throne, effective once the child becomes king) doesn't come through from the wording as written; a reader will likely have to guess at the timeline.

**Paragraph 3**
- "At home, first, for the commonwealth's economy — that is, the management of its affairs, not money in the modern sense: those who have authority over the public treasure — tributes, impositions, rents, fines, or any other public revenue — to collect, receive, disburse, or audit it, are public ministers." — This is a garden-path sentence. It opens with a prepositional fragment ("At home, first, for the commonwealth's economy...") that reads like it's heading toward a verb of its own, then a colon restarts the sentence with a new subject ("those who have authority..."), and the actual verb ("are public ministers") doesn't land until the very end, after two more dash-set insertions. A reader has to hold a lot open before the sentence resolves.
- The recurring construction "Ministers, because...; public, because..." (first appearing here, repeated in paragraphs 5 and elsewhere) is a sentence fragment used as a rhetorical device to split the compound word "public minister" into its two halves and define each. On first encounter it may read as an ungrammatical fragment rather than a deliberate figure; it becomes easier to parse once the pattern repeats, but the first instance could use a beat more signaling (e.g., "They are called ministers because...; and public because...").

**Paragraph 6**
- "in their seats of justice" — a mildly archaic metaphor for "on the bench" / "in court." Context makes it inferable, but it's unglossed and slightly likely to cause a half-beat stumble.

**Paragraph 7**
- "If the defendant is then allowed to challenge any of the remaining judges whose interest makes him suspect them, the judges he does not challenge are ones he has, in effect, agreed to himself." — Grammatically legal but pronoun-dense: "him," "them," "he," "himself" all thread through a single sentence carrying an intricate legal-logical claim (that non-objection counts as tacit agreement). Likely needs a re-read to keep the referents straight, even though each pronoun is technically resolvable.

**Paragraph 8**
- "There were two orders of men, one the lords and the other the commons." — Abrupt shift into flat past tense with no connective ("Historically...", "Under this system...", etc.) linking it to the previous sentence about English courts. The reader has to infer that this is now historical background rather than a continuation of the argument.
- "The lords had the privilege that in all capital crimes their judges could be none but lords — as many of them as chose to attend — and since this was always acknowledged as a privilege granted them as a favor, their judges were none but those they themselves wanted." — This sentence says essentially the same thing twice ("their judges could be none but lords" ... "their judges were none but those they themselves wanted") with a causal aside in between that doesn't add new information between the two near-identical clauses. It reads as circular and may send the reader back to check whether they missed something.
- "may fitly be compared to the organs of voice" — "fitly" (meaning "fittingly" or "aptly") is archaic and unglossed. Likely to register as unfamiliar to a general reader, though the sentence is still parseable.

**Paragraph 9**
- "authority from the sovereign to procure the execution of judgments given" — "procure" here is used in its older sense of "bring about" / "see to," but a modern reader's default sense of "procure" is "obtain, acquire" (as in "procure supplies"). "Procure the execution of judgments" is likely to cause a momentary misreading — as though something is being acquired rather than carried out.

**Paragraph 12**
- "Neither a counsellor nor a council of state — considered simply as having no authority of judicature or command, but only of giving advice to the sovereign when it is asked for, or offering it when it is not — is a public person." — A long parenthetical dash clause sits between the subject ("Neither a counsellor nor a council of state") and the verb that completes it ("is a public person"), which doesn't arrive until the very end of the sentence. Grammatically legal, but the subject-to-verb gap is wide enough that a reader is likely to lose the thread and need to re-read from the top once the verb finally appears.

## What's working well

- The extended body-organ metaphor that runs through the whole chapter — "nerves and tendons" (protectors/viceroys, paragraph 2), "organs of voice" (judges/instructors, paragraph 8), "hands" (executive officers, paragraph 9), and "an eye" (the secret agent, paragraph 11) — is vivid, consistently deployed, and does real work making an abstract taxonomy of offices feel concrete. This is the chapter's strongest accessibility feature.
- Latin tags are glossed immediately and cleanly: "Dei Gratia in the strict sense — that is, by the favor of God alone," and both "Dei Gratia et Regis (by the grace of God and the king)" and "Dei Providentia et Voluntate Regis (by the providence of God and the will of the king)" carry inline translations right where they appear (paragraph 5).
- Legal/historical English terms are defined in place rather than left opaque: "cofferers (household treasurers)" (paragraph 1), "judicature — the administration of justice" (paragraph 6), "Common Pleas" / "Public Pleas (also called Pleas of the Crown)" (paragraph 8), and the jury description in paragraph 8 ("until at last twelve men were agreed on without objection — a jury —") all land clearly.
- The closing sentence of the chapter — "the council of state is the sovereign assembly itself, and gives counsel to no one but itself" — is crisp, almost epigrammatic, and reads as a genuinely satisfying close rather than a translated fragment.
- Paragraphs 4, 9, 10, and 11 (militia officers, executive officers, ambassadors abroad, private vs. public ministers abroad) are largely clean, parallel, and easy to follow in one pass.

---

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.**

Most of the chapter reads clearly for its genre — a handful of paragraphs (4, 9, 10, 11) are genuinely smooth, and the running body-organ metaphor is a real strength that should be preserved as-is. But there is one systemic issue (the unglossed technical sense of "bears/represents the person of," which nearly every paragraph leans on) plus a small cluster of local sentence-level problems: a logically confusing "predecessor" timeline (paragraph 2), two or three garden-path sentences with long subject-to-verb gaps (paragraphs 3 and 12), a near-circular restatement (paragraph 8), and a couple of archaic word choices that carry a misleading modern default sense ("procure," "fitly"). None of these require a rewrite of the whole chapter, but each is a concrete, fixable spot rather than an inherent feature of the material's difficulty.

## Summary

This is a philosophically dense chapter — Hobbes cataloguing every kind of public officer in a commonwealth — and the candidate mostly carries that density well, especially through its consistent, well-glossed use of the body-as-commonwealth metaphor and its habit of translating Latin and archaic legal terms in place. The rough edges cluster around a few specific things: the chapter's central technical term ("person," in the sense of role or representative capacity) is used a dozen times but never explained, which is likely to leave general readers guessing at the core concept from paragraph 1 onward; a couple of sentences delay their main verb so long that readers will need to re-read them; and a couple of individual word choices ("procure," "predecessor," "fitly") carry a modern default meaning that actively misleads rather than just sounding old-fashioned. Fixing those specific spots, without touching what already works, should bring this chapter to fully accessible.
