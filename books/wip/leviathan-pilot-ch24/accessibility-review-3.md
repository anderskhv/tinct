# Accessibility Review — Round 3 (Reviewer A, fresh read)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 23 ("Of the Public Ministers of Sovereign Power") — modern-English reading edition, candidate-sonnet.

**Coverage:** I read all 13 paragraphs of `candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to finish, then went back through paragraph by paragraph. I did not read the source text, any prior draft, or any other review file — this is a blind read of the candidate prose only, judging general-reader accessibility. Paragraphs are numbered 1–13 in array order below.

---

## Issues found

**Paragraph 4** ("Others have a special administration...")
> "Ministers, because they serve the representative person and can do nothing against his command, or without his authority; public, because they serve him in his political capacity."

"The representative person" has no clear antecedent at this point in the paragraph — the sovereign hasn't been named in this sentence, and "representative person" is a compressed technical phrase a reader would have to reconstruct from paragraph 2's "bear the person of the commonwealth." A reader hitting this cold is likely to pause and backtrack to figure out who "the representative person" is. A light gloss or a restated "the sovereign" would remove the stumble.

**Paragraph 5** ("Second, those who have authority over the militia...")
> "But a soldier without a command, though he fights for the commonwealth, does not for that reason represent its person, because there is no one for him to represent it to."

The double use of "represent" in close succession, with "it" as the object the second time, makes the sentence briefly ambiguous — "represent it to" reads oddly on first pass (represent the commonwealth's person *to* whom?). It resolves on a second read but costs the reader a re-read, which is exactly the kind of "grammatically legal but overloaded" case the review is meant to flag.

**Paragraph 13** ("Neither a counsellor nor a council of state...")
> "Neither a counsellor nor a council of state — considered simply as having no authority of judicature or command, but only of giving advice to the sovereign when it is asked for, or offering it when it is not — is a public person."

This is a long parenthetical (about 25 words) wedged between the subject ("Neither a counsellor nor a council of state") and its verb ("is a public person"). A reader has to hold the subject in mind across the full aside before the sentence resolves. It's grammatically sound but reads as a stumble point — a candidate for either shortening the aside or moving it to a following sentence.

Same paragraph, a few sentences later:
> "For the advice is addressed to the sovereign alone — and no one can represent him to himself while he is present in person."

"Represent him to himself" is a compressed formulation — the sense (no one needs to stand in for the sovereign when the sovereign is right there) is recoverable, but it takes a beat to parse, and "in person" landing right after "himself" reads almost like a stutter. Minor, but worth a look.

**Paragraph 9** ("Considering these features of just and rational judicature...")
> "So, having judges of his own choosing, the party had no grounds to object to the sentence being final."

"The party" arrives as a new label after several sentences that used "he"/"the subject" — a small referent shift that could cause a half-beat of confusion about who "the party" is (it's the same generic litigant discussed throughout, not a new figure). Minor.

## What's working well

- The technical apparatus is handled cleanly throughout: "bear the person" gets an explicit, well-placed parenthetical gloss right where it's introduced (paragraph 2); "judicature" is glossed in-line as "the administration of justice" (paragraph 7); the commonwealth's "economy" is glossed as "its public treasure" (paragraph 4); and the Latin tags (*Dei Gratia*, *Dei Gratia et Regis*, *Dei Providentia et Voluntate Regis*) are all translated immediately in parentheses (paragraph 6). This is exactly the right pattern and it's applied consistently.
- The numbered "First... Second... Third..." structuring of the judge-challenge argument in paragraph 8 makes an inherently intricate piece of reasoning easy to follow.
- The organic-body metaphors that close several paragraphs (nerves and tendons, organs of voice, hands, an eye, "the public ear") land clearly and give the reader a concrete image to hang each category of minister on.
- Paragraph 10's discussion of the England court system (Common Pleas / Public Pleas, lords' privilege, the jury of twelve) is dense material handled in plain, readable sentences.

---

## Verdict

**Substantially accessible.** This reads as a chapter in good shape: the archaic-term glossing pattern is applied thoroughly and correctly, sentence lengths are generally well-managed for the density of the material, and the organizing metaphors do real work for the reader. The remaining issues are a small number of localized stumbles — an ambiguous or compressed referent in two places (paragraphs 4 and 13), one doubled-"represent" construction (paragraph 5), and one long subject-verb-separating parenthetical (paragraph 13) — rather than any systemic problem with vocabulary, sentence construction, or flow. If round 2 targeted similar issues, these read like the last few stragglers, not evidence of a fresh regression; nothing here suggests the latest edit introduced new systemic trouble. A light targeted pass on the four spots above would likely close this out.
