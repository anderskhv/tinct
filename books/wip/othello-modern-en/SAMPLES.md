# Ten before/after speeches for Anders

*Original* = served `othello-original-en`. *Before* = current live `othello-modern-en`. *After* = this candidate, shown with the proposed line breaks (each original verse line’s counterpart starts a new line).


## 1.20 · 1.1 — Iago wakes Brabantio (“old black ram”)

**Original:** IAGO. Zounds, sir, you’re robb’d, for shame put on your gown, Your heart is burst, you have lost half your soul; Even now, now, very now, an old black ram Is tupping your white ewe. Arise, arise, Awake the snorting citizens with the bell, Or else the devil will make a grandsire of you: Arise, I say.

**Before:** IAGO. Damn it, sir, you've been robbed. For shame, put on your gown. Your heart's been broken, you've lost half your soul. Right now, this very moment, an old black ram is mounting your white ewe. Get up, get up. Wake the snoring citizens with the bell, or else the devil will make a grandfather of you. Get up, I say.

**After:**

IAGO. By God's wounds, sir, you've been robbed! For shame, put on your robe!  
Your heart is broken, you've lost half your soul.  
Right now, now, this very minute, an old black ram  
is mounting your white ewe. Get up, get up!  
Ring the bell and wake the snoring citizens,  
or else the devil will make you a grandfather.  
Get up, I tell you!


## 1.33 · 1.1 — “the beast with two backs”

**Original:** IAGO. I am one, sir, that comes to tell you your daughter and the Moor are now making the beast with two backs.

**Before:** IAGO. I'm one, sir, who's come to tell you that your daughter and the Moor are at this very moment making the beast with two backs.

**After:**

IAGO. I'm someone, sir, who has come to tell you that your daughter and the Moor are, right now, making the beast with two backs — having sex.

## 9.7 · 3.3 — Desdemona: “His bed shall seem a school” (the calibration line)

**Original:** DESDEMONA. Do not doubt that. Before Emilia here I give thee warrant of thy place. Assure thee, If I do vow a friendship, I’ll perform it To the last article. My lord shall never rest, I’ll watch him tame, and talk him out of patience; His bed shall seem a school, his board a shrift; I’ll intermingle everything he does With Cassio’s suit. Therefore be merry, Cassio, For thy solicitor shall rather die Than give thy cause away.

**Before:** DESDEMONA. Don't doubt that. In front of Emilia here, I give you guarantee of your place. Be assured, if I vow a friendship, I'll perform it to the last article. My lord shall never rest. I'll watch him tame, and talk him out of patience. His bed shall seem a school, his board a confession. I'll mix everything he does with Cassio's suit. So be cheerful, Cassio — your advocate would rather die than give your cause away.

**After:**

DESDEMONA. Don't worry about that. Here, with Emilia as witness,  
I guarantee you'll get your post back. Believe me,  
if I swear to a friendship, I'll carry it out  
to the last clause. My lord will never get any rest;  
I'll keep him awake till he's tame, like a hawk being trained, and talk till he runs out of patience;  
his bed will feel like a classroom and his dinner table like a confessional;  
I'll work into everything he does  
Cassio's request. So cheer up, Cassio,  
because your advocate would rather die  
than give up your case.


## 9.74 · 3.3 — Iago: “the green-eyed monster”

**Original:** IAGO. O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; It is the green-ey’d monster which doth mock The meat it feeds on. That cuckold lives in bliss Who, certain of his fate, loves not his wronger; But O, what damned minutes tells he o’er Who dotes, yet doubts, suspects, yet strongly loves!

**Before:** IAGO. Oh, beware, my lord, of jealousy — it is the green-eyed monster that mocks the meat it feeds on. That cuckold lives in bliss who, certain of his fate, doesn't love the woman who wronged him. But oh, what damned minutes does he count over who dotes, yet doubts, suspects, yet strongly loves!

**After:**

IAGO. Oh, beware, my lord, of jealousy;  
it is the green-eyed monster, which mocks  
the meat it feeds on. The cuckold lives in bliss  
who knows for sure he's been betrayed and doesn't love the one who wronged him;  
but oh, what damned minutes a man counts out  
who adores, yet doubts; suspects, yet loves intensely!


## 9.139 · 3.3 — Othello: “Farewell the tranquil mind”

**Original:** OTHELLO. I had been happy if the general camp, Pioners and all, had tasted her sweet body, So I had nothing known. O, now, for ever Farewell the tranquil mind! Farewell content! Farewell the plumed troops and the big wars That make ambition virtue! O, farewell, Farewell the neighing steed and the shrill trump, The spirit-stirring drum, the ear-piercing fife, The royal banner, and all quality, Pride, pomp, and circumstance of glorious war! And, O you mortal engines, whose rude throats The immortal Jove’s dread clamours counterfeit, Farewell! Othello’s occupation’s gone!

**Before:** OTHELLO. I would have been happy if the general camp, pioneers and all, had tasted her sweet body, so long as I had known nothing. Oh, now, forever farewell the tranquil mind! Farewell content! Farewell the plumed troops and the big wars that make ambition virtue! Oh, farewell — farewell the neighing steed and the shrill trumpet, the spirit-stirring drum, the ear-piercing fife, the royal banner, and all quality, pride, pomp, and circumstance of glorious war! And, oh, you mortal engines, whose rude throats the immortal Jove's dread clamors counterfeit, farewell! Othello's occupation is gone!

**After:**

OTHELLO. I'd have been happy if the whole army camp,  
down to the lowest ditch-diggers, had tasted her sweet body,  
as long as I knew nothing. Oh, now, forever  
farewell the peaceful mind! Farewell contentment!  
Farewell the plumed troops and the great wars  
that turn ambition into a virtue! Oh, farewell,  
farewell the neighing warhorse and the shrill trumpet,  
the spirit-stirring drum, the ear-piercing fife,  
the royal banner, and all the splendor of rank,  
the pride, pomp and ceremony of glorious war!  
And oh, you deadly cannons, whose rough throats  
imitate the dreadful thunder of immortal Jove,  
farewell! Othello's life's work is gone!


## 10.40 · 3.4 — Othello: the handkerchief’s magic

**Original:** OTHELLO. ’Tis true. There’s magic in the web of it. A sibyl, that had number’d in the world The sun to course two hundred compasses, In her prophetic fury sew’d the work; The worms were hallow’d that did breed the silk, And it was dyed in mummy, which the skillful Conserv’d of maiden’s hearts.

**Before:** OTHELLO. It's true. There's magic in the web of it. A sibyl, who had numbered in the world the sun running two hundred courses, sewed the work in her prophetic fury. The worms were hallowed that bred the silk, and it was dyed in mummy, which the skillful preserved from maidens' hearts.

**After:**

OTHELLO. It's true. There's magic woven into it.  
A prophetess who had lived long enough in this world  
to watch the sun complete two hundred yearly circuits — two hundred years —  
stitched it in a fit of prophetic frenzy.  
The silkworms that made the silk were holy,  
and it was dyed in fluid from embalmed bodies, which experts  
had extracted from virgins' hearts.


## 13.48 · 4.3 — Emilia on husbands and wives

**Original:** EMILIA. Yes, a dozen; and as many to the vantage as would store the world they played for. But I do think it is their husbands’ faults If wives do fall: say that they slack their duties, And pour our treasures into foreign laps; Or else break out in peevish jealousies, Throwing restraint upon us. Or say they strike us, Or scant our former having in despite. Why, we have galls; and though we have some grace, Yet have we some revenge. Let husbands know Their wives have sense like them: they see, and smell And have their palates both for sweet and sour, As husbands have. What is it that they do When they change us for others? Is it sport? I think it is. And doth affection breed it? I think it doth. Is’t frailty that thus errs? It is so too. And have not we affections, Desires for sport, and frailty, as men have? Then let them use us well: else let them know, The ills we do, their ills instruct us so.

**Before:** EMILIA. Yes, a dozen — and as many to the surplus as would store the world they played for. But I do think it is their husbands' faults if wives do fall. Say that they slack their duties and pour our treasures into foreign laps. Or else break out in peevish jealousies, throwing restraint upon us. Or say they strike us, or scant our former having out of spite. Why, we have galls; and though we have some grace, yet have we some revenge. Let husbands know their wives have sense like them. They see, and smell, and have their palates both for sweet and sour, as husbands have. What is it they do when they change us for others? Is it sport? I think it is. And does affection breed it? **After:**

EMILIA. Yes, a dozen; and on top of that, enough to fill up the world they played for with their children.  
But I do think it's the husbands' fault  
when wives fall: say they neglect their marital duties  
and pour what's ours into other women's laps;  
or else they break out in petty jealousies  
and lock us up. Or say they hit us,  
or cut back what they used to give us, out of spite.  
Why, we have tempers too; and though we can forgive,  
we can also take revenge. Let husbands know  
their wives have senses just like them: they see and smell  
and have a taste for both the sweet and the sour,  
as husbands do. What is it they're doing  
when they swap us for other women? Is it for sexual sport?  
I think it is. And is it desire that drives it?  
I think it is. Is it weakness that goes astray like this?  
That too. And don't we have desires,  
a desire for sexual sport, and weakness, just as men do?  
Then let them treat us well; or else let them know  
the wrongs we do are wrongs they taught us.


## 15.2 · 5.2 — Othello: “Put out the light”

**Original:** OTHELLO. It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul, Let me not name it to you, you chaste stars! It is the cause. Yet I’ll not shed her blood, Nor scar that whiter skin of hers than snow, And smooth as monumental alabaster. Yet she must die, else she’ll betray more men. Put out the light, and then put out the light: If I quench thee, thou flaming minister, I can again thy former light restore, Should I repent me. But once put out thy light, Thou cunning’st pattern of excelling nature, I know not where is that Promethean heat That can thy light relume. When I have pluck’d thy rose, I cannot give it vital growth again, It must needs wither. I’ll smell it on the tree.

**Before:** OTHELLO. It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul. Let me not name it to you, chaste stars! It is the cause. Yet I will not shed her blood, nor scar that skin of hers, whiter than snow and smooth as polished alabaster. But she must die, or she will betray more men. Put out the light, and then put out the light. If I extinguish you, flaming candle, I can restore your former light again, should I repent. But once I put out your light, you most cunning pattern of nature's perfection, I do not know where that Promethean fire is that can rekindle your flame. Once I have plucked your rose, I cannot give it life again. It must wither. I will smell it on the stem.

**After:**

OTHELLO. It's the cause — the crime on trial — it's the cause, my soul;  
don't make me say its name to you, you pure, chaste stars!  
It's the cause. Still, I won't spill her blood,  
or scar that skin of hers, whiter than snow  
and smooth as the alabaster of a marble tomb.  
And yet she has to die, or she'll deceive more men.  
Put out the light, and then put out the light — her life:  
if I snuff you out, you flaming candle that serves me,  
I can bring your earlier light back again  
if I change my mind. But once your light is out,  
you most intricate masterpiece of nature at her best,  
I don't know where to find the heavenly fire Prometheus stole  
that could relight you. Once I've picked your rose,  
I can't make it live and grow again;  
it's bound to wither. I'll smell it while it's still on the bush.


## 15.190 · 5.2 — Othello’s last speech

**Original:** OTHELLO. Soft you; a word or two before you go. I have done the state some service, and they know’t. No more of that. I pray you, in your letters, When you shall these unlucky deeds relate, Speak of me as I am. Nothing extenuate, Nor set down aught in malice. Then must you speak Of one that loved not wisely, but too well; Of one not easily jealous, but being wrought, Perplex’d in the extreme; of one whose hand, Like the base Judean, threw a pearl away Richer than all his tribe; of one whose subdu’d eyes, Albeit unused to the melting mood, Drop tears as fast as the Arabian trees Their medicinal gum. Set you down this. And say besides, that in Aleppo once, Where a malignant and a turban’d Turk Beat a Venetian and traduc’d the state, I took by the throat the circumcised dog, And smote him, thus.

**Before:** OTHELLO. Wait. A word or two before you go. I have done the state some service, and they know it. No more of that. I beg you, in your letters, when you relate these unhappy deeds, speak of me as I am. Do not minimise anything, nor record anything from malice. Then you must speak of one that loved not wisely, but too well; of one not easily made jealous, but, when worked upon, troubled in the extreme; of one whose hand, like the base Judean, threw a pearl away richer than all his tribe; of one whose hardened eyes, though unused to weeping, drop tears as fast as the Arabian trees their medicinal gum. Set this down. And say also that once in Aleppo, where a malicious and turban-wearing Turk beat a Venetian and slandered the state, I took the circumcised dog by the throat, and struck him, thus.

**After:**

OTHELLO. Wait a moment; a word or two before you go.  
I've done the state some service, and they know it.  
Enough about that. I ask you, in your letters,  
when you report these unhappy events,  
speak of me as I am. Don't make excuses for anything,  
or write anything down out of malice. Then you must speak  
of one who loved not wisely, but too well;  
of one who wasn't easily jealous, but once worked on,  
was distraught beyond all measure; of one whose hand,  
like the ignorant Judean, threw away a pearl  
worth more than his whole tribe; of one whose lowered eyes,  
though they're not used to melting into tears,  
drop tears as fast as the Arabian trees drip  
their healing gum. Write this down.  
And say as well that in Aleppo once,  
when a vicious Turk in a turban  
was beating a Venetian and insulting the Venetian state,  
I took the circumcised dog by the throat  
and struck him — like this.
