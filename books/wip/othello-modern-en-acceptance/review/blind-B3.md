## Act 4, Scene 3: Cyprus, another room in the castle

### 1. Beats

- Othello is seeing out the departing guest Lodovico. Lodovico says he needn't bother and thanks Desdemona as hostess, but Othello insists on the walk.
- As he leaves, Othello abruptly orders Desdemona to go straight to bed and to send her attendant away. He says he'll be right back.
- Once they are alone, Emilia says he seems gentler than before. Desdemona passes on his order to dismiss her, asks for her nightclothes, and says they mustn't displease him now.
- Emilia wishes Desdemona had never met him. Desdemona says she loves him so much that even his stubbornness, scolding and frowns seem graceful, while Emilia starts unpinning her.
- Emilia says the sheets Desdemona asked for are on the bed. Desdemona mocks her own silly mind, then asks to be wrapped in one of those sheets if she dies first. Emilia calls that nonsense.
- Desdemona remembers her mother's maid Barbary, who was abandoned by her lover and died singing an old "willow" song. The song keeps coming back to her tonight.
- She suddenly remarks on Lodovico's good looks and fine speech. Emilia jokes that a Venetian lady would walk barefoot to Palestine for one touch of his lip.
- Desdemona sings the willow song while undressing. She breaks off to hand things over and to tell Emilia to hurry, because Othello will be back soon.
- She sings a line forgiving the man's scorn, then realizes it isn't the song's next line. She thinks she hears knocking; Emilia says it's the wind.
- She sings the lover's retort ("If I court more women, you'll bed more men") and tells Emilia to go. She asks whether itchy eyes mean she will cry, and Emilia says they mean nothing.
- With "these men" on her mind, Desdemona asks whether any women really betray their husbands so grossly. Emilia says there certainly are.
- Desdemona asks whether Emilia would do it "for the whole world". Emilia jokes that she wouldn't do it by heavenly light, only in the dark. Then she says the world is a big prize for a small sin.
- Emilia says she wouldn't cheat for trinkets, but would for the world, since it would make her husband a king. And if you owned the world, you could make the wrong right.
- Desdemona doesn't believe such women exist. Emilia says there are plenty, and blames husbands for it: they neglect and cheat on their wives, grow jealous, lock them up, hit them, and cut their allowance.
- Emilia argues that wives have the same senses, appetites and weaknesses as husbands. When husbands mistreat their wives, the wives' wrongs are lessons the husbands taught.
- Desdemona says good night and prays to learn goodness, not badness, from bad treatment. They go out.

### 2. Meaning and intent

- **13.1** — Lodovico means "no need to see me out". He wants to take a polite leave without troubling his host.
- **13.2** — Othello means "excuse me, I insist; a walk will do me good". He wants to accompany Lodovico. I suspect he also wants time away before whatever he is planning.
- **13.7** — Othello means "bed now, dismiss your attendant, I'll be right back — make sure of it". He wants Desdemona alone and in bed when he returns. The curtness is ominous.
- **13.10** — Emilia means he seems calmer than he was earlier. She wants reassurance that the trouble between them is easing.
- **13.11** — Desdemona means he has ordered her to bed and Emilia away. She wants Emilia to understand that she has to go.
- **13.13** — Desdemona means "those are his orders; help me undress, then go". She wants to obey exactly and not provoke him.
- **13.14 (turning line)** — Emilia means she wishes Desdemona had never met Othello. She wants to side with her mistress against him, and the line turns the talk to the marriage.
- **13.15** — Desdemona means she loves him so much that even his harshness looks graceful. She wants to defend him, and her own choice.
- **13.16 (turning line)** — Emilia means the bed is made up with the requested sheets. She is only reporting a job done, but it tips Desdemona into thoughts of death.
- **13.17** — Desdemona means "never mind, our minds are foolish, but if I die first, shroud me in those sheets". She wants to voice a premonition while half-laughing at it.
- **13.18** — Emilia means "stop talking like that". She wants to break the morbid mood.
- **13.19** — Desdemona means the song of a jilted maid, who died singing it, keeps coming back to her, and she feels like that maid. She wants Emilia to understand her sadness, and to hurry.
- **13.21** — Desdemona means "undress me here" and that Lodovico is handsome. She wants a lighter topic, as a distraction from her dread.
- **13.24** — Emilia means women are wild about Lodovico. She wants to cheer Desdemona up with a joke.
- **13.25** — Desdemona is singing about a forsaken woman weeping by a tree. She is half-involuntarily voicing her own grief.
- **13.26 / 13.28** — Desdemona (the lines are printed in brackets) means "put these away; hurry, he'll be here soon". She wants to be ready before Othello returns.
- **13.29–13.30 (turning line)** — Desdemona sings that no one should blame the man and that she accepts his scorn. Then she catches herself ("that's not the next line") and hears knocking. She is on edge, and her own situation is leaking into the song.
- **13.31** — Emilia means it's only the wind. She wants to calm her.
- **13.32** — Desdemona is singing: when the woman called her lover false, he answered that she would be just as unfaithful. The song's accusation mirrors what Desdemona is facing.
- **13.33** — Desdemona means "go now; do itchy eyes mean I'll cry?" She wants to dismiss Emilia and to get some reassurance.
- **13.35** — Desdemona means "these men! Do women really betray their husbands so grossly?" She wants Emilia's frank view, because she can hardly believe the thing she has apparently been accused of.
- **13.37 (turning line)** — Desdemona asks whether Emilia would betray her husband for the whole world. She wants to test whether any woman would.
- **13.40** — Emilia means "not by this light; I'd do it in the dark". She wants to deflect with a joke.
- **13.41** — Desdemona asks the same question again. She wants a serious answer this time.
- **13.42** — Emilia means the whole world is a huge prize for a small sin. She wants to argue that the trade is worth it.
- **13.44** — Emilia means she would do it and make amends afterwards. Not for trinkets, but to make her husband a king she would risk purgatory. She wants to shock and amuse Desdemona with worldly realism.
- **13.45** — Desdemona means she never would. She wants to affirm her fidelity.
- **13.46** — Emilia means a wrong is only a wrong by the world's rules, so if you own the world you make the rules. She wants to win the argument with a clever twist.
- **13.47** — Desdemona means no woman would really do it. She wants to keep her faith in wives.
- **13.48** — Emilia means plenty would, and husbands are to blame: they stray, get jealous, lock their wives up, hit them and cut their allowance. Wives have the same senses, desires and weaknesses, so wives' wrongs are lessons taught by husbands. She wants Desdemona to see wives as men's equals who answer mistreatment in kind. It is an implicit indictment of Othello.
- **13.49** — Desdemona means good night, and may heaven help her learn goodness rather than badness from bad treatment. She wants to close the subject with forgiveness, not revenge.

### 3. Unclear points

- **13.9, 13.50** — "[Exeunt Othello, Lodovico and Attendants.]" / "[Exeunt.]" (**Low**)
  - "Exeunt" is a Latin stage word. I guessed "they go out" from context, but a first-time listener hears a foreign term.
- **13.2** — "Oh, forgive me; it will do me good to walk." (**Low**)
  - "Forgive me" sounds like an apology for something. I took it as "excuse me, but I insist".
- **13.15** — "My love thinks so well of him" (**Med**)
  - "My love" normally names a person, so for a moment it sounds as if someone else, perhaps Othello, thinks well of "him".
  - I worked out that it means "my love for him makes me approve of him so much that…".
- **13.13 vs 13.20–13.21** — "give me my nightclothes" … "Shall I go and get your nightgown?" / "No, unpin me here." (**Low**)
  - She has just asked for her nightclothes, and now she refuses her nightgown, which sounds contradictory.
  - I assumed the nightgown is a separate robe, but the text doesn't say so. Later, 15.161 has her dead "pale as your nightgown".
- **13.16–13.17** — "I've put the sheets you asked for on the bed." / "please wrap my body in one of those same sheets" (**Med, prior knowledge**)
  - The text never says which sheets these are or why she asked for them.
  - I know from the play that they are her wedding sheets, and that is what makes the shroud request so pointed. Without it, the exchange feels random.
- **13.17** — "It doesn't matter. Honestly, how silly our minds are!" (**Low**)
  - "It doesn't matter" sounds like a brush-off about the sheets.
  - "How silly our minds are" comes before the thought it is mocking, so by ear it seems to be about the sheets too. I took it as "never mind — what foolish thoughts we have".
- **13.19** — "the man she loved turned wild and abandoned her" (**Low**)
  - "Turned wild" is vague: did he go mad, or turn faithless and dissolute? I took it as "went off the rails".
- **13.19, 13.29** — "She had a song about “willow”" / "Sing all a green willow must be my garland" (**Med, prior knowledge**)
  - Nothing explains why the song is about a willow. I know from outside the play that willow, and a willow garland, was the emblem of forsaken love.
  - The text gives the sad mood but not the symbol.
- **13.25** — "Sing all a green willow." (**Low**)
  - The refrain keeps old syntax that doesn't parse as modern English.
  - That's tolerable in a song, but I can't say what "Sing all a green willow" literally means.
- **13.25 vs 13.27** — the first verse carries no "[Sings.]" (**Med**)
  - The first verse is marked only by italics, which vanish when read aloud. Later lines carry "[Sings.]" but no italics.
  - A listener may take 13.25 as recited rather than sung, and can't cleanly tell song from speech.
- **13.26** — "[Put these away —]" (**High**)
  - This is Desdemona speaking, but it is printed as a bracketed stage direction with no speaker.
  - I also can't tell what "these" are: jewellery? clothes?
- **13.28** — "[Please hurry. He'll be here soon.]" (**High**)
  - Speech printed as a stage direction.
- **13.30** — "[No, that's not the next line. Listen! Who's that knocking?]" (**High**)
  - Speech printed as a stage direction. An exclamation and a question inside direction brackets contradict the format, and a narrator would probably read it in the wrong voice.
- **13.33** — "[So off you go; good night. My eyes are itching; does that mean I'm going to cry?]" (**High**)
  - Speech printed as a stage direction. Emilia's reply at 13.34 proves it was spoken.
- **13.29** — "Let nobody blame him — his scorn I approve —" (**Low**)
  - The inverted "his scorn I approve" is old-fashioned.
  - I understood that this is Desdemona slipping her own situation into the song only because 13.30 says "that's not the next line". It's subtle, but a listener can recover it.
- **13.32** — "If I court more women, you'll bed more men." (**Low**)
  - The logic is knotty on first hearing: is it a threat, a prediction or an excuse?
  - I took it as "if I chase other women, you'll sleep with other men": he answers her accusation with one of his own.
- **13.35** — "I've heard people say so." (**Med**)
  - It comes straight after Emilia's "It doesn't mean a thing either way", so "so" sounds like agreement with Emilia.
  - I think it actually means "I've heard it said that itchy eyes mean tears", but by ear the referent points the wrong way.
- **13.41** — "Would you do such a thing for the whole world?" (**Low**)
  - This is a word-for-word repeat of 13.37, and aloud it can sound like a duplicated line.
  - I read it as Desdemona pressing for a serious answer after the joke.
- **13.44** — "and undo it once I'd done it" (**Low**)
  - How do you undo adultery? It stays unclear until 13.46 explains that owning the world lets you declare the wrong right.
- **13.44** — "for a cheap lover's ring" (**Low**)
  - By ear, "cheap" could attach to "lover", meaning a ring from a cheap lover. I took it as a cheap love-token ring.
- **13.44** — "make her husband a cuckold" (**Low**)
  - "Cuckold" is an archaic word. I know it means a man whose wife is unfaithful, and the context helps, but not every listener will know it.
- **13.48** — "enough to fill up the world they played for with their children" (**Med**)
  - By ear this is a garden path: "played for with their children" sounds like playing games with children.
  - On a second pass I read it as "the world they gambled for", which such women could populate with their (illegitimate) children.
- **13.48** — "Is it for sexual sport?" (**Low**)
  - The register is slightly clinical and odd, especially since "sexual sport" is repeated a few lines later. The meaning is clear.
- **13.21** — "This Lodovico is a good-looking man." (**Low, tonal**)
  - This is an abrupt swerve from death and songs to a man's looks. I assume it's meant as a distraction, but for a moment it makes me wonder whether she is attracted to him.
  - The scene never establishes who Lodovico is (a visitor from Venice? a relative?).

### 4. Images and jokes

- **13.19** — "hang my head over to one side": the posture of grief. **Got it first time.**
- **13.24** — "walked barefoot to Palestine just for one touch of his lower lip": comic exaggeration, a barefoot pilgrimage for a kiss. **Got it first time.**
- **13.25** — the song's images: the forsaken woman by a sycamore, hand on breast and head on knee, streams murmuring her moans, tears softening stones. **I got the mood first time. I did not get the willow symbolism from the text**; I had it only from prior knowledge. "green willow must be my garland" (13.29) marks a jilted lover.
- **13.29–13.30** — the song going wrong: she sings forgiveness of "his scorn", then notices it's not the next line. **Got it on reflection:** her own situation bleeds into the song.
- **13.32** — the lover's retort mirrors Othello's accusation. **Got it first time.**
- **13.33** — itchy eyes as an omen of weeping. **Got it first time**, because the line spells it out.
- **13.17** — the sheets as a shroud. **I got the shroud part. The wedding-sheet irony I only got from prior knowledge.**
- **13.40** — "by this heavenly light — I could do it just as well in the dark": a pun on swearing "by this light" versus doing something in the light. **Got it first time**, and it is the best-landed joke in the packet.
- **13.42** — "The world's a huge thing. It's a big reward for a small sin.": "the whole world" used first as an idiom, then as a literal prize. **Got it first time.**
- **13.44** — "who wouldn't make her husband a cuckold if it made him a king?": cheeky logic that adultery could crown him. **Got it first time.**
- **13.46** — "it's a wrong in your own world, and you could quickly make it right": wordplay on "world", meaning that if you own the world you write its rules. **Got it on second thought.**
- **13.48** — "pour what's ours into other women's laps": sexual innuendo. Husbands spend on other women the attention or money owed to their wives. **Got it first time.**
- **13.48** — "the world they played for": a gambling image. **Only half-got it on first hearing.**
- **13.48** — "a taste for both the sweet and the sour": appetite as sexual desire. **Got it.**
- **13.49** — "not to learn bad from bad, but to let the bad teach me better": a moral turn on "bad". **Got it.**

### 5. Scene rating: **4 / 5**

The dialogue is mostly plain modern English, and the scene's arc of dread, song and Emilia's frank argument about wives comes through clearly. The biggest obstacle is formatting: four of Desdemona's spoken lines (13.26, 13.28, 13.30, 13.33) are printed as bracketed stage directions with no speaker, so by ear they sound like directions or lose their speaker. Beyond that, the willow song is only partly marked as sung and its symbolism is never explained, and the sheets are never identified as wedding sheets, so the shroud request loses its point; a few phrasings also trip the ear ("My love thinks so well of him", "I've heard people say so", "the world they played for with their children").

## Act 5, Scene 2: Cyprus, a bedchamber in the castle

### 1. Beats

- It is night, and Desdemona lies asleep with a light burning. Othello enters, bracing himself with "the cause" that he won't name. He won't shed her blood, but she must die so that she can't deceive more men.
- He compares snuffing the candle, which can be relit, with ending her life, which can't. He kisses her again and again and weeps, and she wakes.
- He asks whether she has prayed and tells her to ask pardon for any unforgiven sin, because he won't kill her soul unprepared. She realizes he means to kill her.
- She is frightened by his rolling eyes and trembling, and insists she has done nothing wrong. He says her love is the reason she dies.
- He accuses her of giving Cassio the handkerchief he gave her. She swears she didn't and begs him to send for Cassio.
- He says Cassio has confessed to sleeping with her, and that "honest Iago" has silenced Cassio for good. She cries that Cassio is betrayed and she is ruined, and he takes her tears as grief for a lover.
- She begs to be banished instead, then to live till tomorrow, then for half an hour, then for one prayer. He smothers her.
- Emilia calls urgently from outside. Othello finds Desdemona still alive and finishes her. He dithers over letting Emilia in, is struck that "I have no wife", draws the bed-curtains and unlocks the door.
- Emilia reports a murder outside: Cassio has killed Roderigo. Cassio is alive, so Othello's revenge has gone "out of tune".
- Desdemona cries out that she was wrongly murdered. Dying, she tells Emilia that nobody did it but herself, sends her love to her "kind husband", and dies.
- Othello first hides behind her words, then declares she was a whore and that he killed her. Emilia calls Desdemona an angel and him a devil.
- Othello names Emilia's husband, Iago, as his source. Emilia keeps repeating "My husband?" and calls Iago a liar. She defies Othello's threat and shouts "Murder!".
- Montano, Gratiano and Iago arrive. Iago admits he told Othello that Desdemona was unfaithful with Cassio. Emilia says his stories caused the murder and refuses his orders to go home.
- Othello collapses by the bed, then tells Gratiano, Desdemona's uncle, that he killed her. Gratiano says her father died of grief over the marriage. Othello cites the handkerchief, a family keepsake, seen in Cassio's hand.
- Emilia reveals that she found the handkerchief and gave it to Iago, who had begged her to steal it. Iago threatens her and calls her a whore and a liar, then stabs her and runs.
- Montano gives Gratiano the weapon taken from Othello, posts him outside the door and chases Iago. Othello, disarmed, feels his courage and honor are gone. Emilia dies singing "willow", swearing Desdemona was chaste and loved Othello.
- Othello finds another sword and calls Gratiano in. He tells him not to be afraid, because this is his journey's end. He addresses dead Desdemona and begs devils to torment him.
- Lodovico arrives with Montano, the wounded Cassio in a chair, and Iago as a prisoner. Othello wounds Iago and is disarmed. He calls himself "an honorable murderer", admits plotting Cassio's death and asks Cassio's pardon, and Iago vows never to speak again.
- Letters from Roderigo's pocket and Cassio's account expose Iago's plot. Iago planted the handkerchief in Cassio's room and set Roderigo on to kill Cassio, and before dying Roderigo named Iago as the one who stabbed him. Lodovico strips Othello of command, makes Cassio governor and orders Iago tortured.
- Othello asks to be remembered as one who "loved not wisely, but too well". He tells of killing a Turk in Aleppo "like this", stabs himself, and dies on a kiss. Lodovico curses Iago, gives Othello's estate to Gratiano, leaves Iago's sentence to the governor, and sails to report to Venice.

### 2. Meaning and intent

- **15.2** — Othello means "the cause" (her crime, which he won't name before the stars) compels him. He won't mar her skin, but she must die or deceive more men. A candle can be relit and her life can't, so he'll kiss her while she lives. He wants to steel himself and frame the killing as justice.
- **15.4** — Othello (the speech is printed in brackets) means her breath almost makes him give up justice, and that he'll love her after death. His tears are cruel, because his grief punishes what it loves. He wants to savour her one last time while still carrying out what he sees as a sacred punishment.
- **15.5–15.7** — Desdemona means "Is that you? Are you coming to bed?". She wants her husband, affectionately.
- **15.8 (turning line)** — Othello means "have you prayed?". He wants her soul ready, and the question signals that he intends to kill her.
- **15.10** — Othello means she should pray now for any unforgiven sin. He wants her soul prepared.
- **15.12** — Othello means "be quick; I'll step away; I won't damn your soul". He wants to kill her body but "save" her soul, and he sees himself as merciful.
- **15.13–15.14 (turning line)** — Desdemona asks whether he is talking about killing, and Othello says yes. The threat is now explicit.
- **15.16** — Othello means "amen to mercy for you". He wants her soul saved, while still meaning to kill her.
- **15.17** — Desdemona means that if he prays for mercy he won't kill her. She wants to hold him to that.
- **15.19** — Desdemona means his rolling eyes terrify her even though she knows she is innocent. She wants him to see her innocence and calm down.
- **15.20–15.22 (turning line)** — Othello says "think of your sins", Desdemona answers that her only sin is loving him, and Othello replies "that's why you die". He turns her love into her crime.
- **15.23** — Desdemona means that killing for love is unnatural, and his lip-biting and trembling are bad omens that she hopes aren't aimed at her. She wants to understand and to plead.
- **15.26 (turning line)** — Othello means she gave Cassio his handkerchief. He wants a confession. The charge is now named.
- **15.27** — Desdemona means "I swear I didn't; ask Cassio". She wants a witness called.
- **15.28** — Othello means she shouldn't perjure herself on her deathbed. He wants a confession.
- **15.30** — Othello means she dies now, and should confess, because her denials can't shift his conviction. He wants a confession to justify what he is doing.
- **15.33** — Desdemona means she asks for mercy: she never wronged him, never loved Cassio beyond ordinary goodwill, and never gave him a token. She wants to be believed and spared.
- **15.34** — Othello means he saw the handkerchief in Cassio's hand, and her lying hardens him, turning this from a sacrifice into a murder. He wants to punish her denial, and her refusal to confess angers him.
- **15.35** — Desdemona means Cassio must have found it, so Othello should send for him to tell the truth. She wants Cassio as a witness.
- **15.36 (turning line)** — Othello means "he has confessed". He wants to crush her denial.
- **15.38** — Othello means Cassio confessed to sleeping with her. He wants to confront her with it.
- **15.42 (turning line)** — Othello means Cassio has been silenced, killed, and Iago saw to it. He wants to cut off her appeal to a witness.
- **15.44** — Othello means his revenge would have taken a life for every hair on Cassio's head. He wants to show how deep his hatred goes.
- **15.45** — Desdemona means Cassio was betrayed and she is lost. It is despair, since her only witness is gone.
- **15.46** — Othello means she is weeping for her lover in front of him. For him this is the last straw.
- **15.47–15.53** — Desdemona asks to be banished, then killed tomorrow instead, then for half an hour, then for one prayer. She wants any delay that lets her survive.
- **15.52, 15.54** — Othello means there's no stopping now and it's too late. He wants to finish it.
- **15.56** — Emilia means "My lord!". She wants urgent admission, because she has news.
- **15.57** — Othello means "What's that? She's still alive? I'll end her pain." He wants to finish killing her "mercifully".
- **15.61** — Othello tells Emilia to wait and tells himself Desdemona is dead. Emilia is probably bringing news of Cassio's death, so should he let her in? He thinks Desdemona might be stirring, and if Emilia comes in she'll speak to his wife. Then he realizes he has no wife and feels the heavens should convulse. He wants to control the situation, but horror breaks through.
- **15.63** — Othello means "come in; wait, let me close the bed-curtains". He wants to hide the body.
- **15.66** — the line is bracketed, presumably Othello's, and means "what's wrong?". He wants to act normal.
- **15.67** — Emilia means there has been a foul murder outside. She wants to report it to the general.
- **15.70** — Othello means the moon is driving men mad. He wants to explain the violence away, and perhaps his own.
- **15.71** — Emilia means Cassio has killed Roderigo. She is reporting news.
- **15.72–15.74** — Othello means "Cassio isn't dead? Then my revenge is spoiled." He wanted Cassio dead, and nearly gives himself away.
- **15.75 (turning line)** — Desdemona means she has been unjustly murdered. She wants to proclaim her innocence.
- **15.78** — Emilia means "that's my lady's voice; help!". She wants help, and wants Desdemona to speak.
- **15.79** — Desdemona means she dies innocent. She wants to be believed.
- **15.81 (turning line)** — Desdemona means nobody did it but herself, and asks Emilia to give her love to her kind husband. Even now she wants to protect Othello.
- **15.83, 15.85** — Othello means "How could she be murdered? You heard her; it wasn't me." He wants to hide behind her lie.
- **15.86** — Emilia means "she said so; I'll have to report it truthfully". She wants to be honest, and may be hinting at suspicion.
- **15.87 (turning line)** — Othello means she lied her way to hell, and he killed her. He wants to own the act as justice.
- **15.88** — Emilia means that makes Desdemona more of an angel and him a blacker devil. She wants to condemn him.
- **15.89–15.92** — Othello says she was a whore, false as water. Emilia answers that he slanders her, that he is as rash as fire, and that she was true.
- **15.93** — Othello means Cassio slept with her, Iago will confirm it, and Othello would deserve hell if he'd acted without proof. He wants to justify himself with evidence.
- **15.94–15.101** — Emilia keeps repeating "My husband?". She is trying to take in something unbelievable. Othello insists and grows impatient.
- **15.97** — Othello means yes, with Cassio, and that had she been faithful he wouldn't have traded her for a world made of one jewel. He wants to show how much he prized her.
- **15.102** — Emilia means villainy has made a mockery of love, and asks whether her husband really said it. She wants confirmation.
- **15.103** — Othello means "yes, your husband, my friend, honest Iago". He wants to end her doubt.
- **15.104** — Emilia means that if Iago said it he is a liar and his soul should rot, and that Desdemona loved Othello too much. She wants to defend Desdemona and condemn both men.
- **15.106** — Emilia means "do your worst; you never deserved her". She wants to defy him.
- **15.107** — Othello means "be quiet or else". It is a threat.
- **15.108** — Emilia means she can bear more than he can inflict, he is a fool, and she'll expose him whatever it costs. She wants to raise the alarm.
- **15.110** — Montano means "what's going on, general?". He wants an explanation.
- **15.111** — Emilia sarcastically means "fine work, Iago, men are blaming their murders on you". She wants Iago to deny it.

### 3. Unclear points

### 5. Scene rating: **3 / 5**

The main action is always clear — the murder, Desdemona's protective lie, Emilia's discovery, Iago's exposure through the handkerchief, and Othello's suicide — and most of the big speeches (the candle speech, "loved not wisely, but too well") land well. The rating reflects how many local stumbles there are: three spoken passages are printed as stage directions (15.4, a whole Othello speech; 15.66, with no speaker; 15.146, with the speaker's name inside the bracket), and pronouns or addressees tangle at several points (15.61, 15.145, 15.151, 15.180). On top of that there are register misfires ("Hello there!", eyes that "roll", "There, there", "offers to stab", "whippersnapper"), logic slips caused by glosses or word order (15.39, 15.97, 15.158, 15.193, 15.196), and context the packet never supplies ("the cause", "the Moor" / "blacker devil", "the ignorant Judean", and missing stage business around Emilia at the door and Othello being disarmed).

## Five biggest comprehension obstacles (both scenes)

1. **Spoken lines printed as bracketed stage directions.**
   - Lines: 13.26, 13.28, 13.30, 13.33, 15.4 (a whole Othello speech), 15.66 (no speaker) and 15.146 (speaker label inside the bracket).
   - By ear these read as directions or lose their speaker. This is systematic: speech that resumes after a mid-speech direction or song gets swallowed into brackets.
2. **Pronoun and addressee tangles the ear can't resolve.**
   - 15.61: "She's dead. She's probably coming…", and then "No.".
   - 15.145: "such a good wife".
   - 15.151: "him" versus "that villain".
   - 15.180: "that's the best thing you can do" sounds like Othello endorsing torture.
   - 15.184 and 15.188: "that damned villain" versus Iago, and "he spoke".
3. **Modern-idiom and register misfires.**
   - "Hello there!" (15.56, 15.58), "your eyes roll like that" (15.19) and "There, there" (15.57).
   - "[Iago offers to stab his wife.]" (15.139), "You've done well for yourself" (15.111) and "whippersnapper" (15.153).
   - "mistress" (15.102, 15.108) and "as if by a spell" (15.118).
4. **Logic slips created by glosses or word order.**
   - 15.38–15.39: "What? Unlawfully?" after "used you for sex".
   - 15.158: "unarmed as I am" just after he finds a sword.
   - 15.196: "I thought he had no weapon, because he was so great-hearted".
   - 15.97: "Yes, with Cassio. No —".
   - 13.35: "I've heard people say so".
   - 15.193: "Everything he said is ruined".
5. **Context the text never supplies, which I understood only from prior knowledge.**
   - "It's the cause — the crime on trial" (15.2).
   - Willow as the emblem of forsaken love, with the song only partly marked as sung (13.19, 13.25, 13.29).
   - The sheets are never called wedding sheets (13.16–13.17).
   - "the Moor" and "blacker devil": Othello's race is never established.
   - "the ignorant Judean" and "Spartan dog".
   - Missing stage business: Emilia outside a locked door, Othello's second smothering, Othello being disarmed, and Othello's death.

Minor recurring item: the Latin "Exeunt" (13.9, 13.50, 15.198).
