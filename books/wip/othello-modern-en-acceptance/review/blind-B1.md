# Blind comprehension review: reader B1

**Packet:** *Othello* (Tinct Modern English), Act 1 Scene 1 and Act 1 Scene 3.
**Stance:** I read as a first-time listener hearing each scene read aloud. I assume speaker names are voiced (or the voices are distinct) and that bracketed stage directions are read in a narrator voice. Scene 2 is not in this packet, so I judge Scene 3 as if heard straight after Scene 1.

**Legend for unclear points**
- **H**: likely to derail a listener or to attach a speech to the wrong speaker
- **M**: needs a second hearing, or leaves a real gap
- **L**: a brief stumble that context resolves
- **PK**: I could resolve this only from prior knowledge of the play, its original wording or theatre convention. The brief counts these as unclear.

## Act 1, Scene 1: Venice. A street

### 1. Beats
- At night in a Venice street, Roderigo attacks Iago. Iago has been spending Roderigo's money freely, yet he "knew about this" and said nothing. Iago swears he never dreamed of it. The scene never says what "this" is.
- Roderigo reminds Iago that he claimed to hate "him". Iago explains why. Three important men asked his general to make Iago lieutenant. The general brushed them aside and chose Michael Cassio, a Florentine "bookkeeper" who has never fought. Iago, a proven veteran, stays a mere ensign. Only at this point do we learn that the general is "the Moor".
- Iago blames a system that promotes by favouritism rather than seniority. He asks whether he has any reason to love the Moor.
- Roderigo says he would quit. Iago answers with his creed. Loyal servants are fools. Clever men only *seem* dutiful while serving themselves, and he is one of them. He serves the Moor only to use him. "I am not what I am."
- Roderigo sneers that "the thick-lips" is lucky if he can "pull this off". Iago tells him to wake "her father" and poison the Moor's joy with public scandal.
- At Brabantio's house they shout "Thieves!", warning him about his house, his daughter and his money. Brabantio appears at a window.
- Iago stays anonymous in the dark and tells Brabantio crudely that his daughter is at this moment having sex with the Moor ("old black ram… white ewe", "the devil will make you a grandfather").
- Brabantio recognises Roderigo, a suitor he has rejected. He takes the noise for a drunken prank and threatens him.
- Iago escalates: "Barbary stallion", grandchildren neighing, "the beast with two backs". Brabantio calls him a villain, and Iago retorts "And you're — a senator."
- Roderigo gives a formal account. The daughter left in the middle of the night with only a hired gondolier and went to the Moor. If Brabantio did not consent, she has rebelled. Roderigo tells him to check her room.
- Brabantio remembers a dream that matched this. He calls for lights and goes in to look.
- Iago slips away. He cannot be seen as a witness against the Moor, because Venice needs the Moor for the Cyprus war and will not dismiss him. So Iago must fly a false "flag" of love. He tells Roderigo to bring the search party to the Sagittary inn, where Iago will be with the Moor.
- Brabantio returns with servants. She is gone. He laments and fires questions at Roderigo, and asks whether they are married. Roderigo thinks they are.
- Brabantio suspects that magic spells have corrupted her. He now wishes Roderigo had married her, and he organises an armed search with the night watch, with Roderigo guiding them to the Moor.

### 2. Meaning and intent
- **1.1**: Roderigo means Iago has been living off his purse and yet knew about "this" and hid it. He wants an explanation. At this point I can't tell what "this" is.
- **1.2**: Iago means he never imagined it. He wants to calm Roderigo and keep his trust, and his money.
- **1.3**: Roderigo means Iago has claimed to hate an unnamed "him". He wants proof of whose side Iago is on. *(This line turns the scene to Iago's grievance.)*
- **1.4**: Iago means his general ignored powerful backers and gave the lieutenancy to Cassio, a bookish Florentine with no battle experience, leaving Iago, a proven soldier, as a mere ensign. We learn only at the end that the general is "the Moor". Iago wants Roderigo to believe his hatred is real and justified.
- **1.5**: Roderigo means, I think, that he would sooner have been the Moor's executioner than serve under him. He wants to show solidarity.
- **1.6**: Iago means promotion runs on favouritism, not seniority, so he owes the Moor no love. He wants Roderigo to agree the grievance is fair.
- **1.7**: Roderigo means Iago should quit. *(This prompts Iago's creed.)*
- **1.8**: Iago means he serves the Moor only to exploit him. He despises loyal servants and admires men who fake duty for profit, and he counts himself one of them. He will never show his real heart. He wants Roderigo to see him as a clever ally, while openly admitting that he fakes loyalty.
- **1.9**: Roderigo means the Moor ("thick-lips") is in luck if he gets away with "this". He wants to vent jealous resentment. What "this" is remains unclear. *(This line turns the scene towards action.)*
- **1.10**: Iago means wake the woman's father and ruin the Moor's happiness with scandal. He wants Roderigo to raise the alarm while Iago stays in the background.
- **1.11**: Roderigo means they have reached the father's house. He agrees to shout.
- **1.14**: Iago means the house has been "robbed", and the daughter is one of the stolen goods. He wants to panic Brabantio.
- **1.16**: Brabantio wants to know what the racket is.
- **1.20**: Iago means that right now an old black man is having sex with Brabantio's white daughter and will get her pregnant. He wants to shock and humiliate Brabantio into acting at once.
- **1.25**: Brabantio means Roderigo is a rejected suitor, drunk and making trouble. He wants him gone.
- **1.29**: Brabantio means a robbery is absurd in a city like Venice. He wants to dismiss them.
- **1.31**: Iago means Brabantio is refusing help because he dislikes the messengers, while his daughter is being mated by an African stallion. He wants to provoke Brabantio with ever cruder images.
- **1.33**: Iago means it plainly this time: the daughter and the Moor are having sex. He wants to leave no room for doubt. *(Turn.)*
- **1.35**: Iago means "senator" is as bad an insult as "villain", or he bites back a worse word. He wants to sneer while staying anonymous.
- **1.37**: Roderigo means the daughter has gone to the Moor in the night with only a gondolier. If Brabantio allowed it, they have wronged him by making noise. If not, she has rebelled, and he should check her room. Roderigo wants to be believed and to look honourable, and to discredit the Moor.
- **1.38**: Brabantio means this matches a dream he had and he already half believes it. He wants lights and his household roused. *(Turn: he believes it.)*
- **1.40**: Iago means he cannot be seen testifying against the Moor, because Venice needs the Moor for the Cyprus war and will only reprimand him. So Iago will fake loyalty, and the search should go to the Sagittary inn. He wants Roderigo to carry on without exposing him.
- **1.43**: Brabantio means she is gone and his life is ruined. Between cries of grief he fires questions at Roderigo. He wants the facts: where she is, with whom, and whether they are married.
- **1.44**: Roderigo means he believes they are married. *(Turn: this is a marriage, not a fling.)*
- **1.45**: Brabantio means his own child has betrayed him, and perhaps spells corrupted her. He wants Roderigo to confirm such spells exist. *(This sets up the witchcraft charge.)*
- **1.47**: Brabantio means he now wishes Roderigo had married her, and that the searchers should spread out. He wants to arrest her and the Moor.
- **1.49**: Brabantio means he will raise armed men from every house, plus the night watch. He wants Roderigo to lead, and he promises a reward.

### 3. Unclear points
- **1.1**: "knew about this". **M, PK.** The scene does not say what "this" is until much later. It recurs as "such a thing" (1.2) and "if he can pull this off" (1.9). A listener learns that it concerns the daughter and the Moor only at 1.20, 1.33 and 1.37, and that it is a *marriage* only at 1.43–1.44. I knew at once only because I know the play. Starting mid-conversation is deliberate, but heard aloud, the first nine speeches have nothing to anchor them.
- **1.1**: "who've had the run of my purse as if its strings were yours to open". **L.** The image is clear: Iago spends Roderigo's money. *Why* Roderigo funds Iago is never stated. I inferred it (he is a rejected suitor, 1.25, and presumably pays Iago to help), but only by piecing it together.
- **1.3**: "You told me you hated him." **M, PK.** "Him" has no antecedent. 1.4 shows that he is Iago's commanding officer. That he is "the Moor" comes only in the last words of 1.4 ("His Moorship's ensign"). His name, Othello, is never spoken in this scene.
- **1.4**: "Three important men of this city, pleading in person for him to make me his lieutenant, took their hats off to him". **L.** Today "hats off to him" means "I admire him". I took it to mean they removed their hats in humble petition, but for a moment it sounds as if the three men were praising the Moor.
- **1.4**: "a man half-damned already by a pretty wife". **M, PK.** The image is opaque: why would a pretty wife half-damn a man? I took it as a misogynist sneer: a beautiful wife will make a fool or a cuckold of him, or he is besotted with her. It also tells the listener, as fact, that Cassio is married. Only prior knowledge tells me this is a well-known puzzle line. On this text alone a listener files "Cassio has a pretty wife" (see 3.95).
- **1.4**: "one Michael Cassio, a Florentine". **L.** It is delivered as a sneer, but I can't tell why being from Florence counts against him. Perhaps he is an outsider in Venice, or perhaps Florence suggests bankers, to go with "bookkeeper".
- **1.4**: "But he, sir, got the promotion, and I — whose service his own eyes had seen proved at Rhodes, at Cyprus, and on other battlefields". **M, PK.** The pronouns tangle. "He" is Cassio, but "his own eyes" must be the general's, since Cassio has never been in battle. Heard aloud, "his" points back to Cassio. The same juggling recurs in "He, if you please, gets to be his lieutenant" (he = Cassio, his = the Moor). Across 1.4 the listener has to track two unnamed "he"s. Logic can sort them out on reflection; I sorted them instantly only because I know the play.
- **1.4**: "this adder-up of debits and credits". **L.** Heard aloud, "adder" first suggests the snake. "Debits and credits" corrects it.
- **1.4**: "His Moorship's ensign". **L/M, PK.** This is the first sign that the general is a Moor, and it is buried in a mock title (I hear an echo of "His Worship"). "Ensign" registers only as a rank below lieutenant. The text never says that an ensign carries the flag, so Iago's "hang out a flag and signal of love" (1.40) doesn't connect to his rank. I made that link only from prior knowledge.
- **1.5**: "By heaven, I'd rather have been his hangman." **L.** The comparison is left out: rather than what? I took it as "I'd sooner have hanged the Moor than serve under him", but it can also be heard as a wish to hang Cassio.
- **1.8**: "I serve him so I can serve my own turn on him." **L.** The phrasing is odd. I understood "I serve him in order to use him, or to get my turn at him", but the idiom is old and only half heard.
- **1.8**: "and when they're old, they're fired". **L (tone).** An anachronistic office word. It is clear, but it jars a little in a Renaissance military setting.
- **1.8**: "For, sir, as sure as you are Roderigo, if I were the Moor, I wouldn't want to be Iago." **M, PK.** The riddle is left unsolved. Possible readings: "if I were in the Moor's place, I wouldn't want a man like me serving me"; "I'm nothing like the Moor"; "anyone would rather be master than servant". I couldn't settle on one. The next sentence gives the general drift (he serves only himself) but doesn't decode the line. Knowing the original is the only reason I didn't take it for a slip.
- **1.8**: "For when my outward actions put on display the true workings and shape of my heart for all to see, it won't be long before I wear my heart on my sleeve for jackdaws to peck at." **L.** The sentence is long and its "when … it won't be long before" logic is slightly off. I understood "if my actions ever showed my real heart, I'd be as exposed as a man with his heart pinned to his sleeve for birds to peck". A listener may not know "jackdaws", but "peck" shows they are birds.
- **1.8**: "I am not what I am." **L.** Taken literally, it contradicts itself. I got "I'm not what I seem" from the lines before it.
- **1.9**: "What a stroke of luck the thick-lips has in his hands if he can pull this off!" **M.** It doesn't follow from Iago's creed. "This" is still unexplained, and a listener knows "the thick-lips" is the Moor only because he was the last man mentioned. I got the racist sneer, but not what he would be "pulling off".
- **1.10**: "Call up her father." **H, PK.** No woman has been mentioned, so "her" has no antecedent. Only "your daughter" (1.14) and the ram and ewe (1.20) reveal who "she" is. This is the biggest obstacle in the opening.
- **1.10**: "Wake him, go after the Moor, poison his joy, denounce him in the streets, stir up her relatives' rage, and though he's living in a rich, fertile paradise, plague him with flies." **L.** A run of pronouns: "him" is first the father, then the Moor. Naming "the Moor" in the middle makes it resolvable, but it is busy to follow aloud. "His joy" means the Moor's happiness with "her", who is still unexplained.
- **1.20**: "For shame, put on your robe!" **L.** Why "for shame", and which robe: a dressing gown to come down in, or his senator's robe? I took it as "Get dressed and get up; it's disgraceful to lie in bed while this happens."
- **1.31**: "you're one of those people who won't serve God just because the devil tells you to". **L/M.** This takes a moment. I understood "you'd refuse good advice because you dislike the people giving it". On first hearing, Iago casting himself as "the devil" confused me.
- **1.31**: "so you'll have your daughter covered by a Barbary stallion" and "Spanish ponies for relatives". **L.** Not every listener will know "covered" in the breeding sense, or that "Barbary" means North Africa, or why the ponies are "Spanish". The crude horse-breeding insult to the Moor still lands.
- **1.33**: "making the beast with two backs — having sex." **M (tone).** The tacked-on "— having sex" sounds like an editor's footnote spoken by Iago. It is flat and clinical straight after a vivid image, and the image was already clear from the ram and stallion lines. This is the most jarring line in the scene.
- **1.37**: "(as I'm partly starting to think it is)". **L.** Why would Roderigo suspect that Brabantio consented? I took it as a sarcastic jab at his slow reaction. Buried in a very long sentence, it is confusing to follow aloud.
- **1.37**: "But I beg you: if it's by your wish and with your wise consent … then we've done you a bold and insolent wrong." **M.** This is one sentence of about 80 words with a parenthesis and nested clauses. On one hearing I kept the gist (she went to the Moor at night with only a gondolier) but not the if/then logic.
- **1.37**: "a vagabond, drifting foreigner who belongs here and everywhere". **L.** "Belongs here and everywhere" sounds like praise ("at home anywhere"). The context wants a sneer ("belongs nowhere, rootless"). "Vagabond, drifting" signals the intent, but the phrase works against it.
- **1.38**: "Strike a light, there!" **L.** It can be heard as the old exclamation of surprise "Strike a light!". "Give me a candle!" corrects it.
- **1.40**: "because he's been launched, for such loud and pressing reasons, to the Cyprus wars, which are under way right now, that, to save their souls, they've no one else of his depth and ability to run their campaign". **M.** The "such … that" frame is split by three interruptions, so by the time "that" arrives the thread is lost. "To save their souls" can be heard literally. This is the first mention of the "Cyprus wars", with no context: who is fighting whom? (Scene 3 answers this.)
- **1.43**: "(Oh, unhappy girl!)", "(Who would ever be a father!)" and "(Oh, she deceives me beyond belief!)". **L.** On the page, the parentheses make these look like asides or stage directions, and aloud they are invisible. The speech flips between questions to Roderigo and cries to himself. It works as distraught speech only if the performer marks the shifts.
- **1.50**: "[Exeunt.]" **M for the target reader, PK (theatre convention).** It is Latin and never explained. I know it means "they all leave" only from theatre convention. It recurs at 3.42, 3.70 and 3.72, whereas "Exit" is plain English.

### 4. Images and jokes
- **1.1** Purse strings: Iago has spent Roderigo's money as if it were his own. *First hearing: yes.*
- **1.4** "a great mathematician … this bookkeeper, this adder-up of debits and credits": sarcasm, casting Cassio as a desk theorist rather than a soldier. *Yes.*
- **1.4** "an old maid at her spinning wheel": Cassio knows as much about battle order as a spinster. *Yes.*
- **1.4** "any senator in his civilian robe": any politician could lecture on theory as well as Cassio. *Yes.*
- **1.4** "the wind stolen out of my sails, dead in the water": Iago's career has stalled. *Yes.*
- **1.4** "His Moorship": a mock honorific. *Yes as a sneer; the "His Worship" echo only for listeners who know the phrase.*
- **1.4** "half-damned already by a pretty wife": a misogynist jibe. *No: I couldn't unpack it.*
- **1.6** "the old ladder of seniority, where each number two stood next in line to number one". *Yes.*
- **1.8** "just like their master's donkey, for nothing but their feed": servants as beasts of burden. *Yes.*
- **1.8** "lined their coats with money": feathering their own nests. *Yes.*
- **1.8** "wear my heart on my sleeve for jackdaws to peck at": to show true feelings is to be pecked apart. *Yes; the modern idiom helps.*
- **1.8** "I am not what I am": a paradox meaning "I'm not what I seem". *Yes from context; the literal words don't parse.*
- **1.9** "the thick-lips": a racist nickname for the Moor. *Yes, as a slur.*
- **1.10** "though he's living in a rich, fertile paradise, plague him with flies": spoil his happiness with pests. *Yes.*
- **1.12** A fire spotted at night in a crowded city: the pitch of panic Roderigo should use. *Yes.*
- **1.14** "your house, your daughter and your moneybags": the daughter listed as stolen property, a dark joke. *Yes.*
- **1.20** "an old black ram is mounting your white ewe": a crude racial and sexual animal image. *Yes (shocking, as intended).*
- **1.20** "the devil will make you a grandfather": the Moor, cast as the devil, will get her pregnant. *Yes.*
- **1.31** "won't serve God just because the devil tells you to": refusing good advice because of who brings it. *Partly.*
- **1.31** "Barbary stallion … grandchildren neighing at you … racehorses for cousins and Spanish ponies for relatives": the Moor as a stud horse, and mixed-race grandchildren as horses. *Yes for the gist; I missed what "Barbary" and "Spanish" add.*
- **1.33** "the beast with two backs": two bodies having sex. *Yes, already clear before the gloss.*
- **1.35** "And you're — a senator.": Iago pauses as if about to insult him, then uses the title as the insult. It also tells us Brabantio is a senator. *Yes.*
- **1.38** "not unlike a dream I had": foreboding. *Yes.*
- **1.40** "launched … to the Cyprus wars": the Moor sent off like a ship. *Yes.*
- **1.40** "hang out a flag and signal of love, which is really nothing but a signal": a show of loyalty that is only show. *Yes; the link to his rank of ensign (flag-bearer) came only from prior knowledge.*
- **1.46** Roderigo's instant "Yes, sir, I certainly have." about magic spells: a comic eagerness to agree. *Yes.*
- **1.47** "Oh, I wish you'd had her!": irony, as the rejected suitor is suddenly the preferred one. *Yes.*

### 5. Rating: **4 / 5**
Most of the modern English is fluent and vivid, and from 1.20 onward the scene is easy to follow by ear. The main obstacle is the withheld subject. "This" (1.1), "him" (1.3) and "her father" (1.10) have no antecedents, so the first nine speeches float until "your daughter" (1.14) and the ram and ewe (1.20), and the marriage itself surfaces only at 1.43–1.44; meanwhile Iago's grievance speech juggles two unnamed "he"s ("his own eyes"). The remaining stumbles are riddles left unsolved ("if I were the Moor, I wouldn't want to be Iago", "half-damned already by a pretty wife"), two very long sentences (1.37, 1.40), the self-glossing "— having sex", and the untranslated "Exeunt".

## Act 1, Scene 3: Venice. A council chamber

*Context note: Scene 2 is not in this packet, but Scene 3 stands on its own for a listener. 3.31 explains that the Moor was summoned by the state, 3.39 places Desdemona at the Sagittary, and Iago's presence beside Othello matches his plan in 1.40.*

### 1. Beats
- At a night council, the Duke and senators compare conflicting reports: 107, 140 or 200 war-galleys. All agree that a Turkish fleet is heading for Cyprus.
- A sailor reports that the Turks are now heading for Rhodes. The First Senator argues it is a decoy, because Cyprus matters more to the Turk and is less defended. The Duke agrees.
- A messenger confirms it. The Ottoman fleet met a second fleet at Rhodes, about thirty ships, and has turned openly for Cyprus; Montano vouches for the report. The Duke sends urgently for one Marcus Luccicos, who is in Florence.
- Brabantio, Othello, Iago and Roderigo arrive. The Duke gives Othello an immediate commission against the Turks and welcomes Brabantio. Brabantio says a private grief is swallowing everything else.
- Brabantio says his daughter is "dead" to him, stolen and bewitched with drugs and spells. The Duke promises the harshest law, even if the culprit were his own son. Brabantio names the Moor, the very man the state has summoned, and the council is embarrassed.
- Othello admits he has married her; that is the whole of his offence. He is a plain soldier, fighting since the age of seven, but he offers to tell the plain story of his "magic".
- Brabantio insists his shy daughter could never love a man she feared to look at without drugs. The Duke says assertion is not proof. A senator asks Othello directly: force, or fair courtship?
- Othello asks the council to fetch Desdemona from the Sagittary and let her testify, and he stakes his life on her account. He sends Iago to guide the attendants.
- Othello's story: Brabantio loved him, invited him often and asked for his life story. Desdemona slipped away from chores to devour it and asked to hear it all. She wept, sighed, wished she hadn't heard it, wished for such a man, and hinted that a man who told that story could win her. "She loved me for the dangers I had passed, and I loved her because she pitied them." That, he says, is his only witchcraft.
- The Duke says the story would win his own daughter too, and urges Brabantio to make the best of it.
- Brabantio asks Desdemona whom she owes obedience. She answers that her duty is divided. She owes her father respect, but now she owes her husband what her mother owed Brabantio.
- Brabantio gives up bitterly. He hands her to the Moor and says he is glad he has no other child. The Duke offers consoling rhymed proverbs. Brabantio mocks them ("let the Turk cheat us out of Cyprus") and asks to move on to state business.
- The Duke assigns Othello to Cyprus. Othello accepts readily and asks for proper provision for his wife. The Duke suggests she stay with her father, and Brabantio, Othello and Desdemona all refuse.
- Desdemona asks to go to the war with Othello, because she married his whole soldier's life. Othello backs her. He is not driven by lust, and if love ever makes him neglect his duty, he may be disgraced.
- The Duke leaves the decision to them and stresses haste; Othello must leave tonight. He names his "honest" ensign, Iago, to bring on his commission and his wife.
- Parting shots follow. The Duke tells Brabantio that his son-in-law is "more fair … than black". Brabantio warns Othello that she deceived her father and may deceive him. Othello stakes his life on her faith, asks Iago's wife to attend her, and leaves with Desdemona.
- Roderigo threatens to drown himself. Iago mocks him and argues that will and reason govern desire. He hammers home "put money in your purse": follow the army in disguise, because the marriage will not last and Iago's wits will get Desdemona into Roderigo's bed.
- Iago restates his hatred of the Moor, and they agree to take revenge together. Roderigo, "a changed man", will sell all his land.
- Alone, Iago reveals that he milks Roderigo for money. He hates the Moor, partly because of a rumour that the Moor slept with his wife. His plan is to make Othello believe Cassio is too intimate with Desdemona, which would win Iago Cassio's job and hurt the Moor. "It's conceived."

### 2. Meaning and intent
- **3.1**: The Duke means the reports conflict too much to trust. He wants firmer information.
- **3.4**: The Second Senator means the counts differ but every report agrees on a Turkish fleet bound for Cyprus. He wants the council to act on what the reports agree on.
- **3.5**: The Duke means the differences are understandable and the core threat is real. *(Turn: he is alarmed.)*
- **3.6**: The Sailor calls from outside, I assume, to be let in.
- **3.10**: The Sailor means the Turks are heading for Rhodes instead, on Signior Angelo's report. He wants to deliver his message. *(Turn.)*
- **3.11**: The Duke asks the council to interpret the change of course.
- **3.12**: The First Senator means the Rhodes move is a feint. Cyprus is both more valuable to the Turk and weaker, so the Turk would not leave it for last. He wants the Duke not to be fooled.
- **3.13**: The Duke agrees that the target is not Rhodes.
- **3.16**: The Messenger means the Turkish fleet has joined a second fleet at Rhodes. *(Turn.)*
- **3.18**: The Messenger means the combined fleet has turned openly for Cyprus, and that Montano vouches for this. He wants the report believed.
- **3.19**: The Duke means Cyprus is certainly the target. He asks for Marcus Luccicos, and I can't tell what he wants from that man.
- **3.22**: The First Senator announces Brabantio and "the brave Moor". *(Turn.)*
- **3.24**: The Duke means Othello must be sent against the Turks at once. Then, with no cue, he welcomes Brabantio. He wants Othello's service and Brabantio's counsel.
- **3.25**: Brabantio means he needed the Duke's help too. No state business brought him here; a private grief swamps everything else. He wants the Duke to hear his case.
- **3.27**: Brabantio: "My daughter!" *(Turn.)*
- **3.29**: Brabantio means she is dead to him, stolen and bewitched with drugs, since a sane girl could not do this naturally. He wants the council to treat it as a crime of witchcraft.
- **3.30**: The Duke means whoever did it will face the harshest law, at Brabantio's discretion, even if it were the Duke's own son. He wants to reassure a powerful senator, not knowing the culprit is Othello.
- **3.31**: Brabantio means the culprit is the Moor the state has just summoned. He wants the Duke to keep his promise. *(Turn.)*
- **3.32**: The whole council is sorry to hear it. *(Comic dismay, because they need the Moor.)*
- **3.33**: The Duke asks, presumably of Othello, what his defence is.
- **3.34**: Brabantio means, I think, that Othello can say nothing except that the charge is true. He wants to pre-empt him.
- **3.35**: Othello means yes, he married her, and that is the whole offence. He has been a soldier since he was seven and is no speaker, but he will tell the plain story of his "magic". He wants a fair hearing and uses irony about the witchcraft charge.
- **3.36**: Brabantio means his modest, blushing daughter could never, against her nature, age, country and reputation, love a man she feared to look at, so only drugs or spells explain it. He wants the charge to stick.
- **3.37**: The Duke means assertion is not proof and Brabantio needs real evidence. He wants due process. *(Turn against Brabantio.)*
- **3.38**: The First Senator asks Othello whether he used force and poison or honest courtship. He wants a direct answer.
- **3.39**: Othello means they should send for her, and if she condemns him they may take his office and his life. He wants her testimony. *(Turn.)*
- **3.40**: The Duke orders Desdemona brought. This is the first time the daughter is named.
- **3.41**: Othello tells his ensign, Iago, to guide them.
- **3.43**: This bracketed line has no speaker label; it must be Othello. He means that while they wait, he will tell, as honestly as a confession, how they won each other's love. He wants to use the time.
- **3.45**: Othello means Brabantio loved him, hosted him and asked for his life story. Desdemona devoured it and asked to hear it whole. She wept, sighed, and hinted that such a story would win her. She loved him for his dangers and he loved her for her pity, and that was the only witchcraft. He wants to prove that the love was natural and mutual.
- **3.47**: The Duke means that story would win his own daughter too, and that a damaged remedy beats none. He wants Brabantio to let it go.
- **3.48**: Brabantio means that if she admits to half the wooing, he will drop the blame. He asks her whom she owes obedience. He wants her to side with him.
- **3.49**: Desdemona means her duty is divided. She owes her father respect for her life and upbringing, but now she owes her husband what her mother owed Brabantio. She wants to affirm the marriage without disowning her father.
- **3.50**: Brabantio means he gives up. He hands her to the Moor only because he can't prevent it, and he is glad he has no other child, since he would chain it. He wants to end the matter and move on.
- **3.51**: The Duke means, in rhyming proverbs, that grieving over what can't be changed only brings new grief, and that smiling at a loss robs the thief. He wants to reconcile Brabantio to the marriage.
- **3.52**: Brabantio means, sarcastically, that by that logic the Turk may as well take Cyprus. Proverbs comfort only people with no real grief, and words can't heal a broken heart. He wants the Duke to stop moralising.
- **3.53**: The Duke means the Turk is coming for Cyprus and public opinion trusts Othello more than the current deputy. He wants Othello to take the command despite his wedding.
- **3.54**: Othello means habit has made war's hardships comfortable, so he accepts. He asks for proper lodging, rank and allowance for his wife, because he wants her provided for.
- **3.55**: The Duke suggests she stay at her father's. *(Turn.)*
- **3.56–3.57**: Brabantio refuses, and so does Othello.
- **3.58**: Desdemona means she won't stay and inflame her father. She asks the Duke's favour to speak, because she wants support as an inexperienced speaker.
- **3.60**: Desdemona means her open defiance proves her love. She loves Othello's mind and his soldier's life, and left behind she would lose her married life. She wants permission to go to Cyprus.
- **3.61**: Othello means he supports her not from lust, since he is past youthful passion, but from generosity. Love will never make him neglect his duty, and if it does, let him be disgraced. He wants the senate to agree without doubting him.
- **3.62**: The Duke leaves it to them and stresses haste.
- **3.63**: The First Senator says Othello must leave tonight. *(Turn.)*
- **3.65**: The Duke sets a meeting at nine and asks Othello to leave an officer to bring the commission.
- **3.66**: Othello means his ensign is honest and trustworthy, and he entrusts his wife to him. *(Dramatic irony: the listener knows Iago hates him.)*
- **3.67**: The Duke means, to Brabantio, that if virtue is beauty, Othello is fair rather than black. He wants to console Brabantio, with a backhanded compliment.
- **3.69**: Brabantio means she deceived her father and may deceive her husband. He wants to wound Othello and plant doubt. *(Turn: the first seed of suspicion.)*
- **3.71**: Othello means he would stake his life on her fidelity. He asks "honest Iago" to have his wife attend Desdemona and bring them after him; he has only an hour with her. He wants to settle arrangements and be with her.
- **3.77**: Roderigo means he will drown himself at once. He wants sympathy and help. *(Turn.)*
- **3.78**: Iago mocks him. He wants to jolt him out of it.
- **3.79**: Roderigo means life is torture and death is the cure. He wants his despair taken seriously.
- **3.80**: Iago means that in 28 years he has never met a man who knew how to love himself, and that drowning for a woman is beneath a baboon. He wants to shame Roderigo out of despair.
- **3.81**: Roderigo means he is ashamed but powerless. He wants Iago to tell him what to do.
- **3.82**: Iago means we tend ourselves like gardeners, reason balances lust, and love is only an offshoot of lust. He wants Roderigo to believe desire can be managed, which also makes him pliable.
- **3.84**: Iago means Roderigo should be a man: raise money and follow the army in disguise. The marriage began violently and will end the same way. Moors are fickle, and Desdemona will tire of him and want someone young. With Iago's cunning, Roderigo will have her. Iago wants Roderigo's money.
- **3.85**: Roderigo asks whether Iago will stand by him. He wants a promise. *(Turn.)*
- **3.86**: Iago means he hates the Moor, so they should take revenge together, and cuckolding the Moor will please them both. He wants Roderigo committed, and paying.
- **3.90–3.92**: Iago means no more talk of drowning. He wants to lock in the change of heart.
- **3.93**: Roderigo means he has changed his mind and will sell his land. *(Turn: the money is secured.)*
- **3.95**: Iago, alone, means he uses fools like Roderigo for money and sport. He hates the Moor, partly because of a rumour that the Moor slept with his wife, which he will treat as true. His plan is to make Othello believe Cassio is too intimate with Desdemona, winning Cassio's job and harming the Moor, whose trusting nature makes this easy. He wants the audience to know the plot has been born.

### 3. Unclear points
- **3.4**: "[SECOND SENATOR And mine say two hundred. But though they don't agree … still they all confirm a Turkish fleet, and one heading for Cyprus.]" **H (format).** A spoken speech is formatted as a bracketed stage direction, with the speaker label inside the brackets and no full stop after it. A narrator or text-to-speech voice may read it as a direction or skip it. It is also the speech that first states the key fact: a *Turkish* fleet heading for *Cyprus*.
- **3.5**: "No, it makes sense enough when you think it through." **L.** "No" to what, and what makes sense? I took it as the Duke revising 3.1: the discrepancies are understandable and the core report is credible.
- **3.6 / 3.8**: "SAILOR. Hey there! Hey there! Hey there!" before "[Enter Sailor.]". **L.** The sailor speaks before his entrance, with no "offstage" cue. Read aloud, three "Hey there!"s sound like a casual greeting, which is comic in a war council.
- **3.7**: "A messenger from the war-galleys." **L.** The war-galleys just discussed are the Turkish ones, so for a moment this sounds like a messenger from the enemy fleet. I assumed a Venetian ship.
- **3.10 / 3.18 / 3.19**: "by Signior Angelo", "Signior Montano, your loyal and most courageous servant" and "Marcus Luccicos — isn't he in town?" **L.** None of these names is explained. I can't tell who Angelo or Montano are (perhaps Montano is the "deputy" of 3.53) or why the Duke wants Luccicos. They are harmless, but aloud they are noise.
- **3.12**: "since it matters more to the Turk than Rhodes does, he can also take it with an easier fight". **M.** "Since" makes Cyprus's importance sound like the *reason* it is easier to take. The argument is that it is both more important *and* easier. The rest of the speech is one long sentence. I got the gist (Rhodes is a feint) but not the steps.
- **3.16**: "The Ottomans". **L.** Until now the enemy has been "the Turk" and "Turkish". Here "the Ottomans … have joined up there with a second fleet". A listener who doesn't know that Ottoman means Turkish could think a second enemy has appeared.
- **3.21**: "send it off at top, top speed". **L (tone).** It sounds like a stutter or a typo.
- **3.24**: "I didn't see you; welcome, noble sir, we missed your advice and your help tonight." **M.** The address switches from Othello to Brabantio with no cue, so aloud "noble sir" still seems to be Othello. Brabantio's reply resolves it one line later.
- **3.25**: "And I missed yours." **L.** The meaning is compressed. I took it as "and I needed your help tonight", a pointed opening to his complaint, but it puzzles for a moment. In the same speech, "it wasn't my office" can be heard as a workplace rather than his official duty (L).
- **3.28**: "[DUKE and SENATORS. Dead?]" **M (format).** Another spoken line inside stage-direction brackets.
- **3.29**: "She's been abused". **L.** To a modern ear this means physical or sexual abuse. The sense here seems to be "deceived, misused", which becomes clear with "stolen … corrupted by spells".
- **3.30**: "you'll read the bloody law book yourself". **M (tone).** "Bloody" lands as a swear word ("the damn law book"), not as "the book of death sentences". It is jarring and misleading in the Duke's mouth.
- **3.33–3.34**: "What can you say to this in your own defense?" and then "Nothing, except that it's true." **M.** "In your own defense" implies the Duke is asking Othello, so Brabantio must be butting in on Othello's behalf ("he can say nothing but that it's true"). I got there only after a double take. A cue such as "to Othello" would fix it.
- **3.35**: "The very head and face of my offense — that's how far it goes, no further." **L.** "Head and face" is an unfamiliar phrase. The clause after it rescues the meaning: that is the whole of it.
- **3.36**: "It's crippled and badly flawed judgment that would admit such perfection could go so wrong against all the rules of nature; it must be driven to look for the schemes of cunning hell to explain how this happened." **M.** "It" seems to refer back to the crippled judgment, which garbles the argument. The intended sense, I think, is that any *sound* judgment is forced to look for hellish schemes.
- **3.37**: "Claiming this isn't proof". **M.** A garden-path sentence: aloud it first parses as "[he's] claiming 'this isn't proof'". "To claim it isn't to prove it" emerges only on a re-parse, and the long, abstract rest of the sentence doesn't help.
- **3.43**: "[And until she comes, as truthfully as to heaven I confess the sins of my flesh, just as honestly I'll lay out for your serious ears how I came to win this lovely lady's love, and she mine.]" **H (format).** Othello's speech is in brackets with no speaker label, so it looks like a stage direction. Read in a narrator voice, it becomes baffling first-person narration. I assigned it to Othello from its content.
- **3.45**: "in which, of vast caves and empty deserts, rough quarries, rocks, and hills whose peaks touch heaven, I had occasion to speak — that was how it went —". **L.** The word order is inverted and "that was how it went" is limp filler. The images come through; the grammar doesn't.
- **3.45**: "the Anthropophagi". **L.** An unknown word, but "the cannibals who eat each other" glosses it just before, so it works.
- **3.45**: "would lean in, completely serious". **L (tone).** "Lean in" is a modern buzzword. It is clear, but slightly anachronistic.
- **3.45**: "which she had heard only in bits and pieces, and never attentively". **M.** This contradicts the line just before ("with a hungry ear devour everything I said"): she was intensely attentive. I suspect the intended meaning is "never all the way through, never continuously", and I suspect that only from knowing the original. "Attentively" says the opposite.
- **3.45**: "yet she wished that heaven had made her a man like that". **M, PK.** The line is ambiguous. Did she wish she *were* such a man, or that heaven had *made such a man for her*? Aloud, the first reading comes first. The next sentence (the hint about "a friend who loved her") points to the second. I know the double meaning from the original; a first-time listener will probably hear only one reading.
- **3.45**: "At this hint, I spoke: She loved me for the dangers I had passed, and I loved her because she pitied them." **M, PK.** The colon after "I spoke" makes the next sentence sound like his words to her, oddly in the third person. It is really his summary to the council. "I spoke" also doesn't make clear that he declared his love or proposed. I got past it only because I know the line.
- **3.50**: "God be with you! I'm done." **L.** "God be with you" now sounds like a blessing, but here it is a curt "Goodbye, then!". The tone becomes clear only with "I'm done". In the same speech, "what — if you didn't already have it — with all my heart I'd keep from you" is clever but needs a second pass (L).
- **3.51**: "Let me speak as you would". **L.** It could mean "let me say what you ought to say" or "let me speak as you would if you were calm". I took the first.
- **3.51**: "When there's no cure left, our grief is at an end once we see the worst, when just now we still hung on hope." and "patience makes a joke of the injury she gave". **M.** The second "when" clause tangles the first proverb. In the later couplet, "she" (Fortune) can attach to the nearer noun, "patience". The gist is to stop grieving over what can't be undone. The sudden rhymes (saved/gave, thief/grief) stand out and work as the Duke's proverb register.
- **3.52**: "but he has to bear both the saying and the sorrow who, to pay off his grief, must borrow from his poor patience". **M.** Very hard to follow aloud. "The sorrow who" sounds as if the sorrow were a person, the relative clause arrives late, and the play on "bear" needs a second pass. The gist: proverbs are easy comfort for the untroubled, while a grieving man must bear both the proverb and the grief.
- **3.52**: "are strong both ways, so they cut both ways". **L.** The logic is circular. I understood that such sayings can sweeten or embitter, so they prove nothing.
- **3.54**: "the hard flint-and-steel bed of war into my softest feather bed, the down sifted three times over". **L.** "Flint-and-steel" suggests a fire-lighting kit more than a bed of stone and iron, and "sifted three times over" is obscure (why sifted?). The gist, that war's hardship now feels like comfort, comes through.
- **3.58**: "let your voice give me the permission to help my lack of skill". **L.** It sounds like permission to *help* her lack of skill. I took it as "let your approval support me, since I'm no speaker".
- **3.60**: "That I loved the Moor enough to live with him, my outright defiance and the way I've stormed my own future can trumpet to the world." **M.** As an answer to "What do you want?", the opening "That I loved the Moor …" first sounds like the start of a wish, and putting the object first forces a re-parse. "Stormed my own future" is opaque: did she take her future by storm, or wreck it?
- **3.60**: "a useless moth in peacetime". **L.** I got it only half: something idle and fluttering, perhaps eating away at comfort. The point, being left behind with nothing to do, is clear.
- **3.60**: "I'm robbed of the rites of love — the rights of a wife — I love him for". **M.** "Rites" and "rights" sound identical, so aloud the gloss is a stammered repeat, and the trailing "I love him for" is hard to attach to anything. I can't tell whether she means sex and married life, or sharing the soldier's life she has just said she loves him for.
- **3.61**: "if the light-winged trifles of feathered Cupid ever sew shut, like a hawk's eyes, with lazy lust, my sight and the faculties that serve my duties". **M.** The object ("my sight and the faculties") arrives only after two interruptions, and sewing a hawk's eyes shut is an unfamiliar practice, though the line spells it out. The punchline about the helmet turned into a cooking pot rescues the meaning.
- **3.67**: "And, noble sir, if goodness never lacks a beauty that delights, your son-in-law is far more fair — more beautiful, more bright — than black." **L.** Nothing shows that Brabantio is being addressed until "son-in-law", and the "if" clause is abstract. The gloss "— more beautiful, more bright —" explains the pun on "fair" instead of letting it land. The compliment and its racial edge are clear.
- **3.70**: "[Exeunt Duke, Senators, Officers, etc.]" **L.** "Exeunt" again (see 1.50). The direction also doesn't say whether Brabantio leaves here; I assume he is in the "etc.".
- **3.71**: "I have only an hour for love, for practical matters, and for giving orders to spend with you". **M.** Aloud, "giving orders to spend with you" misparses: orders to spend? The intended sense is "I have only an hour to spend with you, and it must cover love, business and orders."
- **3.74**: "What are you saying, noble heart?" **L.** Roderigo has said only "Iago —", so "What are you saying?" sounds like a reply to a statement. I understood it as "What is it?".
- **3.80**: "Oh, what villainy!" **L.** Nothing villainous has been said. I took it as disgust ("How contemptible!"), but it sounds like an accusation.
- **3.80**: "four times seven years". **L.** The listener has to do the arithmetic (28). It passes, but it is an odd way to give an age aloud.
- **3.81 / 3.86**: "it's not in my virtue — my power — to fix it" and "If you can cuckold him — sleep with his wife —". **L (tone).** Characters gloss their own words mid-line. Aloud this sounds like self-correction or a narrator's aside. It is the same pattern as 1.33, 3.60 and 3.67. In 3.81 the gloss at least sets up Iago's "Virtue!" retort.
- **3.84**: "It can't be that Desdemona will keep loving the Moor for long — put money in your purse — nor he her." **L.** "Nor he her" is stranded after the interjection and easy to miss.
- **3.84**: "The food that tastes as sweet to him now as honey-sweet carob pods will soon taste as bitter as bitter-apple." **L.** Carob pods and bitter-apple are unfamiliar plants, and the listener has to infer that "the food" is Desdemona, or his appetite for her. The contrast between sweet and bitter carries it.
- **3.85**: "if I count on how this turns out". **L.** Vague. I took it as "if I stake everything on the outcome, will you stick with me?"
- **3.95**: "to crown my ambition with feathers by a double piece of villainy". **L.** The image is mixed (a crown made of feathers). "Double" is explained only by what follows: Cassio's job plus the Moor's ruin.
- **3.95**: "to fill Othello's ear with lies that Cassio is too intimate with his wife". **L.** Because 1.4 gave Cassio "a pretty wife", "his wife" flickers for a moment as Cassio's own. Sense resolves it as Othello's.

### 5. Rating: **4 / 5**
The story is fully clear on one hearing: the Turkish threat, the witchcraft charge, Othello's love story, Desdemona's choice, the Cyprus posting and Iago's plot, and Othello's defence and the Iago–Roderigo scene are the clearest writing in the packet. The stumbles are local but more numerous than in Scene 1: three speeches are formatted as bracketed stage directions (3.4, 3.28, and 3.43, which has no speaker at all), several changes of addressee are unmarked (3.24, 3.33–3.34, 3.67), and some word choices mislead ("the bloody law book", "never attentively", "Claiming this isn't proof", "At this hint, I spoke:"). The densest stretches need a second hearing: the senator's Cyprus argument (3.12), the Duke and Brabantio's duel of proverbs (3.51–3.52), and the inverted sentences in Desdemona's plea and Othello's Cupid oath (3.60–3.61).

## Summary

**Five biggest comprehension obstacles**
1. **Speeches formatted as stage directions.** Three speeches sit in brackets: 3.4 (the Second Senator, which carries the key fact that a Turkish fleet is heading for Cyprus), 3.28 ("Dead?"), and 3.43 (Othello, with no speaker label at all). A narrator or text-to-speech voice will read them as directions or skip them.
2. **Pronouns with nothing to refer to, and unmarked addressees.** In 1.1 "this", 1.3 "him" and 1.10 "her father" (no woman has been mentioned yet), the listener cannot tell who or what is meant. 1.4 "his own eyes" could be Cassio or the Moor. 3.24 "noble sir" and 3.33–3.34 leave unclear who the Duke is addressing and why Brabantio answers. I resolved several of these only from prior knowledge.
3. **Riddles left unsolved.** 1.8 "if I were the Moor, I wouldn't want to be Iago"; 1.4 "a man half-damned already by a pretty wife"; 3.45 "At this hint, I spoke: She loved me…", which sounds like what he said to her; and 3.45 "heaven had made her a man like that".
4. **Misleading modern word choices.** 3.30 "the bloody law book" is heard as a swear. 3.45 "never attentively" contradicts "hungry ear". 3.37 "Claiming this isn't proof" is a garden-path sentence. 3.29 "abused" suggests assault. 1.37 "belongs here and everywhere" sounds like praise. 3.71 "giving orders to spend with you" misparses.
5. **Long sentences and dense proverbs.** 1.37, 1.40, 3.12, 3.36, 3.51–3.52 ("the sorrow who…"), 3.60 (object-first opening and "rites/rights") and 3.61 (the Cupid and hawk sentence) are hard to hold by ear.

**Patterns across both scenes**
- **Inline glosses.** Characters define their own words mid-speech: 1.33 "— having sex", 3.60 "— the rights of a wife —", 3.67 "— more beautiful, more bright —", 3.81 "— my power —", 3.86 "— sleep with his wife —". They help a reader but sound like an editor speaking, and "having sex" is flatly jarring.
- **"Exeunt" is never explained** (1.50, 3.42, 3.70, 3.72).
- **Names are withheld.** Neither the Moor nor the daughter is named in Scene 1; "Othello" and "Desdemona" are first heard at 3.23/3.24 and 3.40. That works, because the text links them clearly ("the brave Moor", then "Brave Othello"; "the lady", then "Bring Desdemona here").
