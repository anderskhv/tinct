## 1. Beats

- **9.1–9.7.** In the castle garden on Cyprus, Desdemona promises Cassio that she will plead for him with Othello and give Othello no rest until Cassio gets his post back. Cassio has lost the post and Othello's favour over some offence. Emilia says her husband is as upset as if the trouble were his own.
- **9.8–9.14.** Othello and Iago approach. Cassio is too uneasy to face his general, so he leaves at once, although Desdemona asks him to stay.
- **9.15–9.20.** Iago mutters "I don't like that." He then "denies" that Cassio would sneak off looking guilty, which plants exactly that idea.
- **9.21–9.33.** Desdemona pleads for Cassio and badgers Othello for a date: tonight, tomorrow, Tuesday or Wednesday, and no later than three days. She reminds him that Cassio was with him through the courtship and took his side when she criticised him.
- **9.34–9.40.** Othello gives in ("I won't refuse you anything") but asks to be left alone. She leaves with Emilia, saying she is obedient.
- **9.41.** Othello exclaims how much he loves her. If he ever stops, chaos will return.
- **9.42–9.65.** Iago asks whether Cassio knew about the courtship. He then answers each of Othello's questions with an echo ("Indeed?", "Honest?", "Thinking?") and will not say what he means. Othello is alarmed. He takes the hesitation as the sign of an honest man hiding something terrible.
- **9.66–9.76.** Iago refuses to share his "thoughts". He says they may be false and that a good name is precious. He warns Othello against jealousy, the "green-eyed monster", and Othello groans "Oh, misery!"
- **9.77.** Othello says he is not the jealous kind. He will see first, then doubt, then get proof, and then act at once.
- **9.78–9.83.** Iago tells him to watch Desdemona with Cassio. Venetian wives hide their affairs from their husbands, he says, and she deceived her own father to marry Othello. Othello concedes this and thanks him.
- **9.84–9.92.** Iago keeps telling Othello he looks shaken. Othello begins, "when nature strays…". Iago jumps in: her rejection of men of her own country, colour and rank shows something unnatural, and she may come to regret her choice.
- **9.93–9.99.** Othello dismisses Iago and asks him to have Emilia watch Desdemona. Iago lingers, or comes back, to advise delaying Cassio's reinstatement and watching whether Desdemona pleads hard for him. Then he leaves.
- **9.100.** Alone, Othello calls Iago honest and wise. He fears Desdemona has betrayed him, perhaps because he is Black, not smooth-talking and getting older. He swears he would release her like a wild hawk and would rather be a toad than share her. When she approaches, he cannot believe she is false.
- **9.101–9.111.** Desdemona calls him to dinner with the island nobles. He says his forehead hurts, and she tries to bind it with her handkerchief. He pushes it away, it falls, and they go in without it.
- **9.112–9.129.** Emilia picks up the handkerchief. It was Othello's first gift to Desdemona, and Iago has often asked Emilia to steal it. Iago mocks her, then takes it and refuses to say why he wants it. He sends her away.
- **9.130–9.132.** Alone, Iago plans to plant the handkerchief in Cassio's lodging, because to a jealous man trifles are proof. As Othello returns, Iago gloats that Othello will never sleep peacefully again.
- **9.133–9.145.** Othello is in agony. He says it was better not to know, and he bids farewell to his peace of mind and his life as a soldier. He threatens Iago with death unless Iago produces visible proof.
- **9.146–9.155.** Iago acts wounded and offers to resign, and Othello, torn, keeps him. Iago says catching the pair in the act is impossible, but he offers strong circumstantial evidence.
- **9.156–9.166.** Iago says that Cassio talked in his sleep about Desdemona and kissed and embraced Iago as if she were there. He then claims he saw Cassio wipe his beard today with her strawberry-embroidered handkerchief.
- **9.167–9.178.** Othello is convinced. He gives up love for vengeance and swears a solemn vow, and Iago joins it. Othello orders Cassio killed within three days, and Iago agrees but asks him to spare Desdemona. Othello resolves to kill her and makes Iago his lieutenant.

## 3. Unclear points

Severity: **H** = likely to derail a listener or lose a plot point. **M** = a listener will stumble or mishear the sense. **L** = a small snag that context resolves. **(PK)** = I understood it only through prior knowledge of the play, so it counts as unclear.

### 3a. Stage directions, and speech that looks like a direction

1. **9.109 [H]** — "[Leave it. Come, I'll go in with you.]" This is Othello's speech: it finishes 9.107, which ends on a semicolon. But it is printed in brackets with no speaker, exactly like the stage direction above it. Heard aloud, it could be taken for a direction ("they leave it") or skipped. I read it as Othello saying "leave the handkerchief / never mind, let's go in", but only by inference, helped by PK. "Leave it" is also ambiguous. It could mean leave the handkerchief on the ground, or forget the headache.
2. **9.130 [H]** — "[I'll drop this handkerchief in Cassio's lodging and let him find it. … Just as I said.]" Iago's soliloquy is in brackets with no speaker. It is the only place in this scene where Iago states his scheme outright. If it is heard as a direction or skipped, the listener loses the irony of 9.164, the beard lie, and may not realise that Iago is lying on purpose. I could tell Iago was speaking only because he is the one person on stage.
3. **9.132 [H]** — "[Look, here he comes. Not opium poppy, nor mandrake, nor all the sleep-inducing syrups in the world will ever dose you back into that sweet sleep that was yours yesterday.]" This has the same problem. It also speaks to Othello as "you" as he enters, and nothing marks it as an aside, so a listener may think Iago says it to Othello's face.
4. **9.171–9.173 [M-H] (PK)** — "with the proper reverence of a sacred vow I here pledge my word." / "Don't get up yet." / "[They rise.]" No direction says that Othello kneels, or that Iago kneels with him. "Don't get up yet" and "They rise" come out of nowhere. I know about the kneeling only from the play. As a result, the mock-sacred, almost marriage-like vow is half lost.
5. **9.94–9.98 [M]** — "My lord, I'll go now." … "Why did I marry? This honest fellow, no doubt, sees and knows more, much more, than he's letting on." … "My lord, I wish I could beg Your Honor not to dig into this any further." … "Once more, I'll take my leave." Iago says he is going but keeps talking. Nothing shows him starting to leave and coming back, and nothing marks 9.95 as an aside. By ear, Othello seems to say "this honest fellow knows more than he's letting on" where Iago can hear it. "Once more" makes sense only afterwards.
6. **9.111, 9.178 [L-M] (PK)** — "[Exeunt Othello and Desdemona.]", "[Exeunt.]" This is a Latin stage word that has not been modernised. It can be guessed from "Exit", but a first-time listener may not know it means "they go out".
7. **9.108 [L-M]** — "[He puts the handkerchief from him, and she drops it.]" The plot-critical action exists only in a direction. If directions are not read aloud, the drop is invisible until Emilia's speech. "Puts … from him" is old phrasing. It is also unclear whether either of them notices the handkerchief fall.
8. **9.40 [L]** — "[Exit with Emilia.]" The direction has no subject. Context shows it is Desdemona.
9. **9.126 [L]** — "Why, what's that to you?" Nothing shows Iago taking the handkerchief. Emilia's "give it back to me" (9.127) implies he has it.

### 3b. Opaque images and references

10. **9.6 [M]** — "or live on such a thin and watery diet, or keep breeding itself out of chance events". The diet image seems backwards: a thin diet should starve the "caution", yet Cassio fears it will last. "Breeding itself out of chance events" is abstract. I got the gist, "he'll forget me", only from the end of the sentence.
11. **9.74 [M]** — "which mocks the meat it feeds on". I can't unpack this by ear. Does jealousy mock the jealous man it feeds on, or the person he loves? "Green-eyed monster" is a familiar idiom, so that part lands.
12. **9.100 [M] (PK)** — "this forked plague — a cuckold's horns — is fated for us from the moment we're conceived." The line names "a cuckold's horns" but does not explain them. A listener who doesn't know the old joke that a betrayed husband grows horns won't know why the plague is "forked".
13. **9.105 [M] (PK)** — "I have a pain here, on my forehead." The subtext is that he feels the horns sprouting. That depends on knowing where horns grow, and the text never makes the link. Without it, this is just a headache.
14. **9.100 [M] (PK)** — "Yet that's the plague of great men: they have fewer privileges than the lowly." I couldn't connect "fewer privileges" with being betrayed, or say what privilege the lowly have. I take it to mean great men are less protected from betrayal than common men, but that comes from knowing the original, not from this line.
15. **9.82 [L-M] (PK)** — "to blindfold her father's eyes as tight as close-grained oak — he thought it was witchcraft." Oak is an odd thing to compare a blindfold to. And what did he think was witchcraft? I know from the play that her father accused Othello of bewitching her. This scene doesn't say so.
16. **9.70 [L-M]** — "Who steals my purse steals trash. It's something, nothing; it was mine, it's his, and has been slave to thousands." "Something, nothing" and "slave to thousands" (money that has served thousands of owners) need unpacking. By ear, "Who steals my purse…" can briefly sound like a question. I recognised the famous line (PK).
17. **9.130 [L-M] (PK)** — "burn like sulfur mines" and "Just as I said." Do sulfur mines burn? Hellfire and brimstone is probably the idea, but I got "burn fiercely" rather than a picture. "Just as I said" leaves open what he said and when. It comes *before* Othello enters, so it can't be a reaction to seeing him. I know that in the original it reacts to Othello's entrance.
18. **9.76 [L]** — "endless riches are as poor as winter to the man who's always afraid he'll become poor." The listener has to build the link from money to jealousy (the fear of losing what you have). "As poor as winter" is an odd simile. In "defend the souls of all my kind from jealousy!", "my kind" could mean his family or all people.
19. **9.100 [L]** — "If she's unfaithful, oh, then heaven is mocking itself!" I got this on reflection, not on first hearing: she looks heavenly, so her falseness would make a mockery of heaven.
20. **9.167 [L]** — "Swell, my chest, with your cargo, for it's made of the tongues of venomous asps!" "Cargo" is odd here. I got "my heart fills with poison".
21. **9.171 [L]** — "keeps rushing on into the Propontic and the Hellespont" and "by that marble sky up there". The two seas are not glossed, although "the Pontic Sea — the Black Sea" is. They work as "the seas further on". Why the sky is marble (hard? cold? unmoving?) I only half got.
22. **9.132 [L]** — "mandrake". This is an unfamiliar plant, but "sleep-inducing syrups" makes the sense clear.
23. **9.7 [L]** — "his dinner table like a confessional". Whose confession? I got "no peace anywhere" but not the exact picture. Perhaps she will preach at him like a priest.
24. **9.100 [L]** — "I'd whistle her away and release her downwind". It isn't explained why releasing a hawk downwind means letting it go for good, but "to hunt for herself and take her chances" carries the meaning.
25. **9.139 [L]** — "Othello's life's work is gone!" It isn't explained why losing faith in his wife means farewell to soldiering. The closing line helps. "Jove" is understood through "thunder".

### 3c. Tone, register and clashes with modern idiom

26. **9.133 [M]** — "Ha! Ha! Unfaithful to me?" To a modern ear, "Ha! Ha!" is laughter, but it has to be a cry of anguish. It risks sounding manic or comic. Similarly, "Ha?" at 9.73 sounds like "Huh?" [L].
27. **9.41 [M] (PK)** — "Wonderful little wretch!" "Wretch" sounds like an insult today. I know it is affectionate only from the play. A listener may wonder why he insults her while declaring his love.
28. **9.115–9.118 [M]** — "I have a thing for you." / "A thing for me? It's a common thing — one anybody can have —" / "Huh?" / "to have a foolish wife." "I have a thing for you" is a modern idiom for a crush, which muddies Emilia's plain "I've got something for you". Iago's bawdy dig, that her "thing" is common and anybody can have it, is only half audible. Because the sentence is split around "Huh?", the punchline is hard to reassemble by ear. I got "he calls her a foolish wife" at once, but the double meaning only on reflection. "Huh?" is very modern.
29. **9.167 [M]** — "Oh, if only the slave had forty thousand lives!" "The slave" means Cassio, used as an insult. To a modern ear "slave" is literal, and it is especially loaded in a play whose hero is Black. On first hearing it is not obvious who "the slave" is.
30. **9.39 [L-M]** — "Be however your whims tell you to be; whatever you are, I'm obedient." "Whims" sounds pointed. I can't tell whether she is teasing, hurt or sincerely deferential.
31. **9.156 [L-M] (PK)** — "I shared a bed with Cassio recently". Nothing tells a modern listener that soldiers routinely shared beds. The line may draw a laugh or be read another way.
32. **9.154 [L-M]** — "It would be a tiresome, hard job, I think, to set them up in that view." "Set them up" suggests "frame them", which is exactly what Iago is doing. "In that view" is clumsy.
33. **9.96 [L]** — "I wish I could beg Your Honor" / "I beg Your Honor." This sounds like addressing a judge, which is jarring for a general.
34. **9.141 [L]** — "Villain, make sure you prove my love's a whore". Today "villain" means the bad guy. For a moment it sounds as though Othello has seen through Iago, when he is only threatening him.
35. **9.176 [L]** — "the lewd tramp". "Tramp" means slut in the US but vagrant in the UK, so the sense depends on dialect.
36. **9.154 [L]** — "as horny as goats". This is slangy and jumps register, though it suits Iago's crudeness. "swipe it" (9.125) and "go crazy" (9.127) are similar but fine.
37. **9.77 [L]** — "my wife is beautiful, eats well, loves company". In a list of a wife's charms, "eats well" sounds odd or comic today (a good appetite?). "Trade me for a goat" came across as "I'd be a dumb beast", which is odd but workable.
38. **9.78 [L]** — "I know the character of our countrywomen well; in Venice they let heaven see the naughty tricks they don't dare show their husbands." "Naughty" sounds childish or cheeky for adultery. "Our countrywomen" implies that Othello is Venetian too. That blurs Iago's real point, which is that Othello, as an outsider, cannot know Venetian women.
39. **9.92 [L]** — "ugh!" and "something rank". "Ugh!" is modern but conveys disgust. "Rank" as in foul-smelling comes right after "color and rank" as in status. The echo could confuse a listener or be heard as a pun.

### 3d. Syntax, pronouns and reference that are hard to follow by ear

40. **9.68 [M]** — "I beg you — though maybe I'm wrong, even wicked, in my guess, since, I admit, it's the curse of my nature to pry into wrongdoing, and my suspicious mind invents faults that aren't there — that your wisdom would take no notice of someone whose guesses are so unreliable; and don't build yourself trouble out of his random, uncertain observations." The long interruption between the dashes loses the thread of "I beg you … that". "Someone" and "his" are Iago speaking of himself in the third person, and "his" can be heard as Cassio's. The sentence also shifts from a plea to an order. I got the gist, "ignore my guesses", only on rereading.
41. **9.67 [M] (PK)** — "You're conspiring against your friend, Iago, if you even think he's been wronged and keep his ear a stranger to your thoughts." "Your friend" is Othello speaking of himself, but after so much talk of Cassio it can sound like Cassio. "Keep his ear a stranger to your thoughts" is a mouthful.
42. **9.146 [M]** — "God be with you. Take back my post. — Oh, wretched fool, you've lived to make your honesty a vice!" By ear, "wretched fool" sounds aimed at Othello, but Iago is addressing himself. "God be with you", meaning goodbye, may be heard as a blessing. Iago's post is never named, though "take back my post" is clear as a resignation.
43. **9.154 [M]** — "Damn them, then, if mortal eyes ever see them share a pillow except their own eyes!" The conditional curse is hard to parse. I had to work out that it means "no one but the two of them will ever see them in bed".
44. **9.33 [M]** — "Michael Cassio, who came courting with you". This can be heard as Cassio courting her too, as a rival, rather than going along with Othello as a go-between. That is a risky ambiguity in a jealousy plot. 9.49 ("he carried messages between us") clears it up later.
45. **9.77 [M]** — "away at once with love — or with jealousy!" The dash sounds like a self-correction. On first hearing I couldn't tell whether he means both go, or whichever one the proof decides.
46. **9.112 [M]** — "I'll have the embroidery copied and give it to Iago." What is Emilia's plan: to give Iago a copy and return the original, or to give him the original? She hands over the original at 9.123 and asks for it back at 9.127. I couldn't tell, even on rereading.
47. **9.33 [L-M]** — "(except that, they say, in wartime they have to make examples of their best men)". This parenthesis sits mid-sentence. By ear, the main clause ("his offence, by ordinary reckoning … is hardly even the kind of fault that earns a private scolding") falls apart. The scene never says what the offence was.
48. **9.164 [L-M]** — "But a handkerchief like that (I'm sure it was your wife's) today I saw Cassio wipe his beard with." The object comes first, then a bracket, then a stranded "with". The listener has to hold "a handkerchief like that" through the whole sentence.
49. **9.7 [L-M]** — "I'll work into everything he does Cassio's request." This is a garden-path sentence: you hear "he does Cassio's request" as a clause.
50. **9.100 [L-M]** — "he understands every type, with an expert's mind, of human behavior." The split phrase is hard to follow by ear.
51. **9.100 [L-M]** — "the smooth, charming ways of talking that bedroom ladies' men have". This stacked compound can parse as "the men of bedroom ladies".
52. **9.112 [L-M]** — "since he made her swear she'd always keep it". "He" follows "My unpredictable husband", so it can sound as though Iago made her swear. It must be Othello.
53. **9.62 [L-M] (PK)** — "Men should be what they seem; or those who aren't — I wish they didn't even seem to be men!" This is knotty. I took it as "those who aren't what they seem shouldn't look like honest men", but only after thinking about it and knowing the original.
54. **9.91 [L-M] (PK)** — "And yet, when nature strays from its own course —". Whose nature? I read it as "even a good nature, hers, can go astray", which Iago twists into "an unnatural match". On its own the line is vague.
55. **9.92 [L]** — "Take this — to be blunt with you — turning down so many proposed matches with men of her own country, color and rank" and "though I may fear her desire, once it falls back on her better judgment, may start comparing you". "Take this" followed by an "-ing" phrase is clumsy, and "take this" can sound like handing something over. "Falls back on her better judgment" is abstract, and "may" appears twice.
56. **9.66 [L]** — "I'm not bound to do what even slaves are free to refuse." The next sentence ("Speak my thoughts?") is what explains "what". In "some unclean suspicions don't hold their own little courts there, and sit on the bench alongside honest thoughts", "courts" first sounds royal until "bench" makes it a law court.
57. **9.74 [L]** — "The cuckold lives in bliss who knows for sure he's been betrayed and doesn't love the one who wronged him". The "who" clause is split from "cuckold", so the listener first hears the paradox "the cuckold lives in bliss". "Cuckold" is not glossed, though the sentence half-explains it.
58. **9.96 [L]** — "since he certainly fills it with great ability" and "if you'd keep him at arm's length a while, you'll see from that what he's like and how he operates". The first uses the present tense for a post Cassio has lost. In the second, it is fuzzy how delaying his reinstatement reveals Cassio's character. The real test is watching her plead.
59. **9.103 [L]** — "It's my fault." This could be "sorry I'm late" or a guilty double meaning. I'm not sure which is intended.
60. **9.106 [L]** — "Just let me tie this tight around it". "This", the handkerchief, is named only in Othello's reply.
61. **9.149 [L]** — "As long as there are ropes or knives, poison or fire, or streams to drown in, I won't put up with this." Suicide or murder? This may be deliberately ambiguous.
62. **9.172 [L]** — "Iago hands over all the workings of his mind, hands and heart" and "I'll obey as an act of pity for him". "Hands over … hands" is a jingle. Obeying bloody orders "as an act of pity" is odd.
63. **9.155 [L]** — "Give me a real, living reason she's unfaithful." "Living" is odd here.
64. **9.137 [L]** — "A man who's been robbed, if he doesn't miss what was taken — don't let him know it, and he's not robbed at all." The sentence restarts midway, but it is followable.

### 3e. Context this scene assumes but does not state

65. **9.2–9.3 [L]** — "I'm sure it upsets my husband as much as if the trouble were his own." / "Oh, he's an honest fellow." That Emilia's husband is Iago is confirmed only at 9.93 and 9.112.
66. **9.9 [L]** — "Madam, here comes my lord." Othello and Emilia's husband Iago enter together. "My lord" means Othello, but for a moment it is ambiguous.
67. **9.23 [L]** — "Why, your lieutenant, Cassio." This comes after 9.6's "my post filled by someone else", so I took it as "former lieutenant". The scene never says what Cassio did wrong.
68. **9.112 onward [L] (PK)** — "her first keepsake from the Moor". "The Moor" is never glossed as Othello. It can be inferred from context, for example from "Maybe because I'm black" and "Cursed fate, that gave you to the Moor!"
69. **9.80 [L]** — "when she seemed to tremble and be afraid of your looks, she loved them most". This refers to a courtship we did not see. Othello's "So she did" confirms it.

**Glosses that worked well and should stay:** "the Pontic Sea — the Black Sea", "Diana, the chaste goddess", "honest — faithful to me", "a foregone conclusion — something already done", "midday dinner", "the torture rack", "the lowest ditch-diggers", "like a hawk being trained", "proof I can see with my own eyes", "embroidered with strawberries", "keep your eyes like this — neither jealous nor too trusting". The echo game at 9.50–9.57 is crisp and gripping.

## 5. Scene rating

**4 / 5** for comprehension by ear. It becomes **3 / 5** if the bracketed speeches at 9.109, 9.130 and 9.132 are voiced as stage directions or skipped.

The biggest obstacle is formatting, not language. Othello's "Leave it" (9.109) and Iago's two gloating speeches (9.130, 9.132) sit in square brackets with no speaker, so by ear they may be taken for directions or skipped, and the missing kneel before 9.172 and Iago's unmarked exit and return at 9.94–9.98 leave "Don't get up yet", "They rise" and "Once more, I'll take my leave" unexplained. Next, a few images still need knowledge the text doesn't supply (the cuckold's horns behind "forked plague" and the forehead pain, "mocks the meat it feeds on", Cassio's "thin and watery diet"), and some modern-English choices clash in tone ("Ha! Ha!", "Wonderful little wretch!", "I have a thing for you", "the slave", "Your Honor"). Finally, several long sentences are hard to parse by ear because of interruptions, objects placed first or characters speaking of themselves in the third person (9.33, 9.67, 9.68, 9.100, 9.146, 9.154, 9.164), although most of the scene is plain and conversational, and Iago's echo game and Othello's collapse are gripping and clear.
