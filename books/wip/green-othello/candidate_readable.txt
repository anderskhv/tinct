
=== Chapter 1: Act 1, Scene 1 — Venice. A street ===
[0] [Enter Roderigo and Iago.]
[1] RODERIGO. Stop, don't try to talk me down. I take it badly that you, Iago — who's handled my purse as if the strings were your own — knew about this and said nothing.
[2] IAGO. Damn it, you won't even hear me out. If I ever dreamed such a thing was coming, despise me.
[3] RODERIGO. You told me you hated him.
[4] IAGO. Hate him? Hold me in contempt if I don't. Three important men in the city personally petitioned him to make me his lieutenant. They took off their hats to him. And by God, I know my worth — I deserve a place no worse than that. But he, in love with his own pride and his own plans, brushed them off with pompous, evasive talk, stuffed with military jargon. And in the end he dismissed my supporters. He said, “Truly, I have already chosen my officer.” And who was it? A bookkeeper, if you can believe it — one Michael Cassio, a Florentine, a man almost ruined by a beautiful wife. He's never led a squadron in the field, knows no more about how to deploy a battle than an old maid, except for the textbook theory that the togaed politicians can quote as expertly as he does. Pure talk, no practice — that's the whole of his soldiering. But he was the one chosen, sir. And I, whom Othello's own eyes have seen tested at Rhodes, at Cyprus, and on other ground, Christian and pagan, must be left becalmed by this calculator, this bean-counter. He, in good time, must be his lieutenant — and I, God help me, must be his Moorship's ensign.
[5] RODERIGO. By heaven, I would rather have been his hangman.
[6] IAGO. Well, there's no fixing it. That's the curse of service — promotion goes by favor and personal connection, not by seniority, where each second-in-line inherits from the one above. So, sir, you judge for yourself whether by any fair measure I'm obliged to love the Moor.
[7] RODERIGO. Then I wouldn't follow him.
[8] IAGO. Oh, sir, calm down. I follow him only to use him for my own ends. We can't all be masters, and not all masters can truly be followed. You'll notice plenty of devoted, knee-bending servants who, in love with their own slavish obedience, wear out their lives — like their master's donkey — for nothing but their feed, and when they're old, they're dismissed. Whip those honest fools. But there are others who, dressed up in the forms and appearances of duty, keep their hearts attending to their own interests. They put on a show of service to their lords, and they do well by them, and once they've lined their pockets, they pay homage to themselves. Those fellows have some spirit, and I count myself one of them. For, sir, as surely as you are Roderigo, if I were the Moor I wouldn't want to be Iago. In following him, I follow only myself. Heaven is my judge — I act not out of love and duty, but for my own private purpose, only appearing dutiful. For if I ever showed in my outward behavior what was really in my heart, before long I'd wear my heart on my sleeve for crows to peck at. I am not what I am.
[9] RODERIGO. What incredible luck the thick-lipped one has, if he can pull this off!
[10] IAGO. Call her father. Wake him up. Go after Othello. Poison his happiness. Shout his name in the streets. Set her relatives against him. And though he lives in a fertile place, plague him with flies. Though his joy may be real, throw enough disturbances on it to dim its color.
[11] RODERIGO. This is her father's house. I'll shout.
[12] IAGO. Do it. Use the same panicked tone and dreadful yell as when, at night and through carelessness, fire is spotted in a crowded city.
[13] RODERIGO. Hey, Brabantio! Signior Brabantio, hey!
[14] IAGO. Wake up! Hey, Brabantio! Thieves, thieves! Look to your house, your daughter, and your money-bags! Thieves, thieves!
[15] [Brabantio appears above at a window.]
[16] BRABANTIO. What's the reason for this awful summons? What's the matter out there?
[17] RODERIGO. Signior, is all your family inside?
[18] IAGO. Are your doors locked?
[19] BRABANTIO. Why? Why do you ask?
[20] IAGO. Damn it, sir, you've been robbed. For shame, put on your gown. Your heart's been broken, you've lost half your soul. Right now, this very moment, an old black ram is mounting your white ewe. Get up, get up. Wake the snoring citizens with the bell, or else the devil will make a grandfather of you. Get up, I say.
[21] BRABANTIO. What, have you lost your minds?
[22] RODERIGO. Most reverend signior, do you know my voice?
[23] BRABANTIO. I don't. Who are you?
[24] RODERIGO. My name is Roderigo.
[25] BRABANTIO. The worse for you. I've ordered you not to hang around my doors. In plain honesty you've heard me say my daughter is not for you. And now, in your madness, full of supper and strong drink, out of malicious daring, you come to disturb my peace.
[26] RODERIGO. Sir, sir, sir—
[27] BRABANTIO. But you can be sure my position and influence have the power to make you pay for this.
[28] RODERIGO. Patience, good sir.
[29] BRABANTIO. What are you telling me about robbery? This is Venice. My house is not some isolated farm.
[30] RODERIGO. Most grave Brabantio, I come to you in pure and simple goodwill.
[31] IAGO. Damn it, sir, you're one of those people who won't serve God even if the devil orders you to. Because we come to do you a favor, and you take us for ruffians, you're going to have your daughter covered by a Barbary stallion. You'll have nephews who whinny at you. You'll have racehorses for cousins and small Spanish horses for in-laws.
[32] BRABANTIO. What blasphemous wretch are you?
[33] IAGO. I'm one, sir, who's come to tell you that your daughter and the Moor are at this very moment making the beast with two backs.
[34] BRABANTIO. You're a villain.
[35] IAGO. You — are a senator.
[36] BRABANTIO. You'll answer for this. I know you, Roderigo.
[37] RODERIGO. Sir, I'll answer for anything. But I beg you, if it's by your pleasure and wise consent — as I half suspect it is — that your beautiful daughter, at this odd hour of dead night, has been taken away with no better escort than a hired commoner, a gondolier, into the rough embrace of a lustful Moor: if you know about this and approve of it, then we've done you a bold and rude wrong. But if you don't know, then by my manners we've been unfairly scolded. Don't think that, out of any breach of civility, I would dare to mock or trifle with your honor. Your daughter — if you haven't given her leave — has, I tell you again, committed a gross rebellion, binding her duty, her beauty, wit, and fortunes to a roving, wandering stranger from nowhere and everywhere. Check for yourself right now. If she's in her room or in your house, turn the law of the state loose on me for deceiving you like this.
[38] BRABANTIO. Light the tinder, hey! Bring me a candle! Wake up all my people! This accident is not unlike my dream — the belief of it already weighs me down. Light, I say, light!
[39] [Exit from above.]
[40] IAGO. Farewell. I have to leave you. It's neither fitting nor safe for my position to be seen — as I would be if I stayed — testifying against the Moor. For I know the state, however much this may sting him with some rebuke, cannot safely dismiss him. He's so loudly committed to the Cyprus wars, which are happening right now, that for their lives the senators have no one of his caliber to lead their business. So even though I hate him like the pains of hell, for the necessity of present survival I must put on a flag and show of love — which is indeed only a show. You'll find him for certain — lead the search party to the Sagittary inn, and I'll be there with him. So, farewell.
[41] [Exit.]
[42] [Enter Brabantio with Servants and torches.]
[43] BRABANTIO. It's too true an evil. She's gone. And whatever comes of my despised, remaining time, it's nothing but bitterness now. Now, Roderigo, where did you see her? (Oh, unhappy girl!) With the Moor, you say? (Who would be a father!) How did you know it was her? (Oh, she deceives me beyond all thought.) What did she say to you? Bring more candles. Wake all my relatives. Are they married, do you think?
[44] RODERIGO. Truly, I think they are.
[45] BRABANTIO. Oh heaven! How did she get out? Oh, treason of the blood! Fathers, from now on, don't trust your daughters' minds by what you see them do. Aren't there charms by which the nature of youth and maidenhood can be abused? Haven't you read of such a thing, Roderigo?
[46] RODERIGO. Yes, sir, I have indeed.
[47] BRABANTIO. Call up my brother. Oh, I wish you had had her! Some go one way, some another. Do you know where we may catch her and the Moor?
[48] RODERIGO. I think I can find him, if you please to get a good guard and come with me.
[49] BRABANTIO. Please lead on. I'll call at every house — I have authority at most of them. Get weapons, hey! And rouse some special night-officers. On, good Roderigo. I'll repay you for your trouble.
[50] [Exeunt.]

=== Chapter 2: Act 1, Scene 2 — Venice. Another street ===
[0] [Enter Othello, Iago and Attendants with torches.]
[1] IAGO. Though in the trade of war I've killed men, I make it a point of conscience never to commit deliberate murder. I sometimes lack the wickedness to serve me well. Nine or ten times I felt like sticking him here, under the ribs.
[2] OTHELLO. It's better as it is.
[3] IAGO. No, but he babbled on, and spoke such filthy, provoking words against your honor that, with the little godliness I have, I had a hard time holding back. But please, sir, are you securely married? Be sure of this — the magnifico is much loved, and his voice in effect carries as much weight as the Duke's. He'll get you a divorce, or load you with whatever restraint and trouble the law (which he can pull on at full strength) will allow him.
[4] OTHELLO. Let him do his worst. The services I've done the Senate will speak louder than his complaints. It's not yet known — and when I find that boasting is honorable, I'll proclaim it — that I draw my life and being from men of royal lineage. And my own qualifications can stand bareheaded as an equal to as proud a fortune as the one I've reached. Because know this, Iago — if I didn't love the gentle Desdemona, I wouldn't give up my free, unsettled life for any treasure in the sea. But look — what lights are those coming over there?
[5] IAGO. That's the roused father and his friends. You'd better go inside.
[6] OTHELLO. Not me. I must be found. My qualities, my title, and my clear conscience will speak rightly for me. Is it them?
[7] IAGO. By Janus, I don't think so.
[8] [Enter Cassio and Officers with torches.]
[9] OTHELLO. The Duke's servants and my lieutenant. The goodness of the night be on you, friends! What's the news?
[10] CASSIO. The Duke sends his greetings, general, and requires you to appear in all haste — immediately.
[11] OTHELLO. What's the matter, do you think?
[12] CASSIO. Something from Cyprus, I'd guess. It's an urgent business. The galleys have sent a dozen messengers in a row this very night, hard on each other's heels. And many of the senators, woken and assembled, are at the Duke's already. You've been urgently called for, and when they couldn't find you at your lodgings, the Senate sent out three separate search parties to find you.
[13] OTHELLO. Good thing you found me. I'll just have a word in the house here, then come with you.
[14] [Exit.]
[15] CASSIO. Ensign, what's he doing here?
[16] IAGO. The truth is, tonight he's boarded a land-galleon. If she proves a lawful prize, he's made for life.
[17] CASSIO. I don't follow.
[18] IAGO. He's married.
[19] CASSIO. To whom?
[20] [Enter Othello.]
[21] IAGO. Married to — Come on, captain, ready to go?
[22] OTHELLO. I'm with you.
[23] CASSIO. Here comes another group looking for you.
[24] [Enter Brabantio, Roderigo and Officers with torches and weapons.]
[25] IAGO. It's Brabantio. General, be warned, he means trouble.
[26] OTHELLO. Hold it, stop right there!
[27] RODERIGO. Signior, it's the Moor.
[28] BRABANTIO. Down with him, thief!
[29] [They draw on both sides.]
[30] IAGO. You, Roderigo! Come on, sir, I'm ready for you.
[31] OTHELLO. Put up your shining swords, the dew will rust them. Good signior, you'll command more with your years than with your weapons.
[32] BRABANTIO. Oh, you foul thief — where have you stashed my daughter? Damned as you are, you've enchanted her. For I'll appeal to every reasonable mind: if she weren't bound in magic chains, would a girl so tender, fair, and happy — so against marriage that she avoided the wealthy curled-haired darlings of our nation — would she ever, to incur a general mockery, have run from her guardian to the sooty bosom of such a thing as you — running to fear, not delight? Let the world judge me whether it isn't grossly obvious that you've worked on her with foul spells, abused her delicate youth with drugs or minerals that weaken the will. I'll have it argued out. It's probable, and obvious to common sense. So I arrest you and detain you as a corrupter of the world, a practitioner of forbidden, unauthorized arts. Seize him. If he resists, subdue him at his peril.
[33] OTHELLO. Hold your hands, both you on my side and the rest. If my cue were to fight, I would have known it without a prompter. Where do you want me to go to answer this charge of yours?
[34] BRABANTIO. To prison, until the right time of law and the course of direct session calls you to answer.
[35] OTHELLO. What if I do obey? How will the Duke be satisfied with that, whose messengers are right here at my side, on some current state business, to bring me to him?
[36] OFFICER. It's true, most worthy signior. The Duke's in council, and I'm sure your noble self has been sent for.
[37] BRABANTIO. What? The Duke in council? At this time of night? Bring him along. Mine is no trivial matter. The Duke himself, or any of my fellow senators, must feel this wrong as if it were his own. For if such acts can pass freely, our statesmen will end up slaves and pagans.
[38] [Exeunt.]

=== Chapter 3: Act 1, Scene 3 — Venice. A council chamber ===
[0] [The Duke and Senators sitting at a table; Officers attending.]
[1] DUKE. There's no consistency in this news that makes it credible.
[2] FIRST SENATOR. Indeed, the reports don't match. My letters say a hundred and seven galleys.
[3] DUKE. And mine say a hundred and forty.
[4] [SECOND SENATOR. And mine, two hundred. But though the numbers don't agree exactly — as is common in these cases, where reports estimate and often differ — they all confirm a Turkish fleet, and one heading for Cyprus.]
[5] DUKE. Yes, that's reasonable enough to my judgment. I'm not so reassured by the discrepancy that I don't accept the main point with real fear.
[6] SAILOR. Hey, ho! Hey, ho! Hey, ho!
[7] OFFICER. A messenger from the galleys.
[8] [Enter Sailor.]
[9] DUKE. Now — what's the matter?
[10] SAILOR. The Turkish preparation is heading for Rhodes, so I was told to report here to the state by Signior Angelo.
[11] DUKE. What do you say to this change?
[12] FIRST SENATOR. It can't be the case, by any test of reason. It's a show, to fool our eyes. When we consider how important Cyprus is to the Turk — and when we recognize that, since it matters to him more than Rhodes does, he can take it more easily, because it's not in such a warlike state of defense, and altogether lacks the strength Rhodes is dressed in — when we think this through, we shouldn't think the Turk is so unskilled as to leave for last what concerns him most, neglecting an easy and profitable attempt to wake up a worthless danger.
[13] DUKE. No, with full confidence, he's not heading for Rhodes.
[14] OFFICER. Here's more news.
[15] [Enter a Messenger.]
[16] MESSENGER. The Ottomans, reverend and gracious lords, steering directly toward the isle of Rhodes, have there joined themselves with a second fleet.
[17] FIRST SENATOR. Yes, just as I thought. How many, by your estimate?
[18] MESSENGER. Of thirty sail, and now they're reversing their course, clearly heading toward Cyprus. Signior Montano, your trusty and most valiant servant, with his free devotion sends you this report and asks you to believe him.
[19] DUKE. Then it's certain — for Cyprus. Marcus Luccicos, isn't he in town?
[20] FIRST SENATOR. He's now in Florence.
[21] DUKE. Write to him from us — send it with the utmost haste.
[22] FIRST SENATOR. Here comes Brabantio and the valiant Moor.
[23] [Enter Brabantio, Othello, Iago, Roderigo and Officers.]
[24] DUKE. Valiant Othello, we must straightaway send you against our common enemy, the Ottoman. I didn't see you — welcome, gentle signior. We needed your counsel and help tonight.
[25] BRABANTIO. And I needed yours. Forgive me, your grace. Neither my office, nor anything I heard about state business, raised me from my bed. The general crisis doesn't grip me — my personal grief is of such a flood-gate, overpowering nature that it swallows up all other sorrows and is still itself.
[26] DUKE. Why, what's the matter?
[27] BRABANTIO. My daughter! Oh, my daughter!
[28] [DUKE and SENATORS. Dead?]
[29] BRABANTIO. Yes, to me. She's been abused, stolen from me, and corrupted by spells and potions bought from charlatans. For nature couldn't err so monstrously — not being deficient, blind, or dim of sense — without witchcraft.
[30] DUKE. Whoever he is who in this foul business has thus tricked your daughter out of herself, and you out of her, the bloody book of the law you shall yourself read in its harshest letter, according to your own interpretation — even if our own son were charged with it.
[31] BRABANTIO. Humbly I thank your grace. Here is the man — this Moor — whom it now seems your special order on state affairs has brought here.
[32] ALL. We're very sorry to hear it.
[33] DUKE. What can you say to this, for your own part?
[34] BRABANTIO. Nothing — except that this is so.
[35] OTHELLO. Most powerful, grave, and reverend signiors, my very noble and trusted good masters: that I've taken away this old man's daughter — it is most true. True, I have married her. The very head and front of my offense extends this far, no further. I am rough in my speech, and little blessed with the soft language of peace. For since these arms of mine were seven years strong, until now, just nine months past, they have done their best work in tented battlefields. And little of this great world can I speak of, except what relates to feats of violence and battle. So I shall do little to grace my cause by speaking for myself. Yet, by your gracious patience, I will give you a plain, unvarnished account of my whole course of love — what drugs, what charms, what conjuration, and what mighty magic (for that's what I'm charged with) I used to win his daughter.
[36] BRABANTIO. A maiden never bold — of a spirit so still and quiet that her every motion blushed at itself. And she, in defiance of her nature, her age, her country, her reputation, everything, to fall in love with what she feared to look at! It is a maimed and most imperfect judgment that would admit perfection could so err against all rules of nature, and one must be driven to find the workings of cunning hell as the reason. So I claim again: with some mixtures powerful over the blood, or with some potion conjured to this purpose, he worked on her.
[37] DUKE. To claim this is not proof. We need a wider and more open test than these thin assertions and weak likelihoods of modern-seeming charges press against him.
[38] FIRST SENATOR. But, Othello, speak — did you, by indirect and forced methods, subdue and poison this young maiden's affections? Or did it come about by request and the kind of fair conversation that soul to soul allows?
[39] OTHELLO. I beg you, send for the lady to the Sagittary, and let her speak about me in front of her father. If you find me foul in her report, the trust, the office I hold from you — not only take them away, but let your sentence even fall upon my life.
[40] DUKE. Bring Desdemona here.
[41] OTHELLO. Ensign, conduct them — you know the place best.
[42] [Exeunt Iago and Attendants.]
[43] [And until she comes, as truly as I confess my sins to heaven, I'll just as honestly present to your grave ears how I succeeded in winning this fair lady's love, and she in winning mine.]
[44] DUKE. Say it, Othello.
[45] OTHELLO. Her father loved me, often invited me, and constantly asked me the story of my life from year to year — the battles, sieges, fortunes I've been through. I ran through it all, even from my boyhood days to the very moment when he asked me to tell it. I spoke of the most disastrous events, of moving accidents by flood and field, of hair-breadth escapes through deadly breaches, of being captured by the insolent enemy and sold into slavery, of my redemption from it, and of my bearing in my traveler's history. There I spoke of vast caverns and idle deserts, rough quarries, rocks, and hills whose tops touch heaven — such was the process. And of the Cannibals who eat each other, the Anthropophagi, and men whose heads grow beneath their shoulders. Hearing all this, Desdemona was deeply attentive. But the housework would keep drawing her away. And whenever she could hurry through it, she'd come back, and with a hungry ear she devoured my talk. Noticing this, I once found a willing moment and good means to draw from her a heartfelt request that I would describe my whole pilgrimage in detail — of which, in fragments, she had heard something, but not attentively. I agreed, and often I made her weep when I spoke of some distressing blow my youth had suffered. When my story was done, she gave me a world of sighs for my pains. She swore, in faith, it was strange — it was passing strange. It was pitiful, it was wondrously pitiful. She wished she hadn't heard it, yet she wished that heaven had made her such a man. She thanked me, and told me that if I had a friend who loved her, I should just teach him how to tell my story, and that would woo her. On this hint I spoke. She loved me for the dangers I had been through, and I loved her because she pitied them. This is the only witchcraft I have used. Here comes the lady. Let her testify to it.
[46] [Enter Desdemona, Iago and Attendants.]
[47] DUKE. I think this tale would win my daughter too. Good Brabantio, take this damaged matter in the best possible way. Men use their broken weapons rather than their bare hands.
[48] BRABANTIO. Please hear her speak. If she confesses she was half the wooer, may destruction be on my head if my unfair blame falls on this man! Come here, gentle lady — do you perceive, in all this noble company, where you owe most obedience?
[49] DESDEMONA. My noble father, I perceive here a divided duty. To you I am bound for life and education. My life and education both teach me how to respect you. You are the lord of duty, I have until now been your daughter. But here is my husband. And as much duty as my mother showed to you, preferring you over her own father — that much duty I claim the right to declare I owe to the Moor, my lord.
[50] BRABANTIO. God be with you! I've finished. Please, your grace, go on with the state affairs. I would rather adopt a child than have one. — Come here, Moor. I here give you with all my heart what — except you already have it — I would, with all my heart, keep from you. — For your sake, jewel, I'm glad in my soul I have no other child. Your escape would teach me tyranny, to hang chains on them. — I've finished, my lord.
[51] DUKE. Let me speak in your place and give a verdict that, like a step, may help these lovers into your favor. When remedies are gone, griefs end with seeing the worst — that, until lately, depended on hopes. To mourn a misfortune that is past and gone is the surest way to draw on new misfortune. What can't be preserved when fortune takes it away — patience makes a mockery of fortune's injury. The robbed man who smiles steals something from the thief. He robs himself who spends himself in useless grief.
[52] BRABANTIO. So let the Turk deceive us of Cyprus — we don't lose it as long as we can smile. The man bears the verdict well who bears nothing but the free comfort that comes with it. But he bears both the verdict and the sorrow who, to pay grief, must borrow from poor patience. These sayings — turned to sugar or to gall, being strong on both sides — are ambiguous. But words are words. I never yet heard that a bruised heart was healed through the ear. I humbly beg you, proceed to the state's business.
[53] DUKE. The Turk, with most mighty preparation, is heading for Cyprus. Othello, the strength of that place is best known to you. And though we have there a deputy of fully acknowledged competence, opinion — a sovereign mistress of outcomes — gives a safer vote to you. So you must be content to mar the gloss of your new fortunes with this more stubborn and rough expedition.
[54] OTHELLO. The tyrant Custom, most grave senators, has made the flinty, steel couch of war my thrice-driven bed of down. I acknowledge a natural and prompt eagerness I find in hardship, and I undertake this present war against the Ottomans. So most humbly, bowing to your state, I ask suitable arrangements for my wife — proper accommodation, allowance, and such comfort and company as is fitting for her upbringing.
[55] DUKE. If you like, let her stay at her father's.
[56] BRABANTIO. I won't have it.
[57] OTHELLO. Nor I.
[58] DESDEMONA. Nor I. I wouldn't reside there, putting my father in impatient thoughts by being in his sight. Most gracious Duke, lend a favorable ear to what I'm about to say, and let me find a sanction in your voice to support my simple request.
[59] DUKE. What is it you wish, Desdemona?
[60] DESDEMONA. That I loved the Moor — to live with him — my outright violence and storm of fortunes may proclaim to the world. My heart's subdued to the very essence of my lord. I saw Othello's face in his mind, and to his honors and his valiant qualities I consecrated my soul and fortunes. So that, dear lords, if I'm left behind — a moth of peace — and he goes to the war, the rites for which I love him are taken from me, and I shall endure a heavy interim through his beloved absence. Let me go with him.
[61] OTHELLO. Give her your consent. I call heaven to witness — I'm not asking this to please the palate of my appetite, nor to satisfy lust (those young impulses in me have died down), nor for my own satisfaction, but to be free and generous to her mind. And heaven forbid that you should think I would neglect your serious and great business because she's with me. No — when the light-winged toys of feathered Cupid blind, with wanton dullness, my contemplative and dutiful faculties, so that my pleasures corrupt and stain my business, let housewives make a skillet of my helmet, and all unworthy and base disasters rise up against my reputation.
[62] DUKE. Let it be as you privately decide — whether for her staying or going. The matter calls for haste, and speed must match it.
[63] FIRST SENATOR. You must leave tonight.
[64] OTHELLO. With all my heart.
[65] DUKE. At nine in the morning we'll meet again here. Othello, leave some officer behind, and he shall bring our commission to you, with such other things of importance and rank as concern you.
[66] OTHELLO. If it please your grace, my ensign — he is a man of honesty and trust. To his keeping I assign my wife, with whatever else your good grace shall think necessary to be sent after me.
[67] DUKE. Let it be so. Good night to everyone. — And, noble signior, if virtue lacks no delighted beauty, your son-in-law is far more fair than black.
[68] FIRST SENATOR. Adieu, brave Moor. Treat Desdemona well.
[69] BRABANTIO. Watch her, Moor, if you have eyes to see — she has deceived her father, and may deceive you.
[70] [Exeunt Duke, Senators, Officers, etc.]
[71] OTHELLO. My life upon her faith! Honest Iago, my Desdemona must I leave to you. I beg you, let your wife attend on her, and bring them after at the best opportunity. — Come, Desdemona, I have only an hour of love, of worldly matters, and instructions to spend with you. We must obey the time.
[72] [Exeunt Othello and Desdemona.]
[73] RODERIGO. Iago—
[74] IAGO. What do you say, noble heart?
[75] RODERIGO. What do you think I'll do?
[76] IAGO. Why, go to bed and sleep.
[77] RODERIGO. I'll drown myself right now.
[78] IAGO. If you do, I'll never love you again. Why, you silly gentleman!
[79] RODERIGO. It's silliness to live, when to live is torment. And then we have a prescription to die when death is our physician.
[80] IAGO. Oh, villainous! I've looked upon the world for four times seven years, and since I could tell a benefit from an injury, I've never found a man who knew how to love himself. Before I would say I'd drown myself for the love of a hen, I'd trade my humanity with a baboon.
[81] RODERIGO. What should I do? I admit it's shameful to be so smitten, but it's not in my power of virtue to correct it.
[82] IAGO. Virtue! Nonsense! It's in ourselves that we are this or that. Our bodies are gardens, and our wills are gardeners. So that if we want to plant nettles or sow lettuce, set hyssop and weed up thyme, supply it with one kind of herb or distract it with many, either to leave it sterile through idleness or fertilize it with effort — well, the power and corrigible authority of all that is in our wills. If the balance of our lives didn't have one scale of reason to balance another of sensuality, the blood and baseness of our natures would lead us to the most absurd conclusions. But we have reason to cool our raging impulses, our carnal stings, our unbridled lusts — of which I take this, that you call love, to be an offshoot, or a graft.
[83] RODERIGO. It can't be.
[84] IAGO. It's merely a lust of the blood and a permission of the will. Come, be a man. Drown yourself? Drown cats and blind puppies. I've declared myself your friend, and I confess I'm bound to your interests with cables of unbreakable toughness. I could never serve you better than now. Put money in your purse. Follow the wars. Disguise your face with a fake beard. I say, put money in your purse. It can't be that Desdemona will long continue her love for the Moor — put money in your purse — nor he his for her. It was a violent start, and you'll see a corresponding break — just put money in your purse. These Moors are changeable in their wills. Fill your purse with money. The food that's now as luscious to him as locusts will soon be as bitter to him as colocynth. She must change, for youth's sake. When she's sated with his body, she'll see the error of her choice. She must have change, she must. So put money in your purse. If you must damn yourself, do it in a more delicate way than drowning. Get all the money you can. If holy ceremonies and a fragile vow between an erring barbarian and a hyper-clever Venetian aren't too much for my wits and all the tribe of hell, you'll enjoy her — so make money. A pox on drowning yourself! It's completely out of the question. Better to be hanged in pursuit of your joy than to be drowned and go without her.
[85] RODERIGO. Will you stand by my hopes if I commit to the outcome?
[86] IAGO. You're sure of me. Go, make money. I've told you often, and I tell you again and again — I hate the Moor. My motive is from the heart. Yours has no less reason. Let us be united in our revenge against him. If you can cuckold him, you give yourself a pleasure, me a sport. There are many events in the womb of time about to be delivered. Move, go, prepare your money. We'll have more of this tomorrow. Adieu.
[87] RODERIGO. Where shall we meet in the morning?
[88] IAGO. At my lodging.
[89] RODERIGO. I'll be there early.
[90] IAGO. Go now, farewell. Do you hear me, Roderigo?
[91] RODERIGO. What do you say?
[92] IAGO. No more talk of drowning, do you hear?
[93] RODERIGO. I'm changed. I'll sell all my land.
[94] [Exit.]
[95] IAGO. This is how I always make my fool my purse. For I would dishonor my own hard-won knowledge if I'd spend time with such a fool except for my sport and profit. I hate the Moor — and it's rumored about town that between my sheets he has done my office. I don't know if it's true, but I, on mere suspicion of that kind, will act as if it were certain. He thinks well of me — all the better my purpose shall work on him. Cassio's a fine-looking man. Let me see now — to take his place, and to feather up my will in double villainy. How? How? Let me see. After some time, to whisper in Othello's ear that Cassio is too familiar with his wife. Cassio has the looks and the smooth manner to be suspected — built to make women false. The Moor is of a free and open nature, who thinks men are honest who only seem so, and will as gently be led by the nose as donkeys are. I have it. It is conceived. Hell and night must bring this monstrous birth to the world's light.
[96] [Exit.]

=== Chapter 4: Act 2, Scene 1 — A seaport in Cyprus. A Platform ===
[0] [Enter Montano and two Gentlemen.]
[1] MONTANO. What can you see at sea from the cape?
[2] FIRST GENTLEMAN. Nothing at all — it's a high, wrought-up flood. I can't pick out a sail anywhere between heaven and the main.
[3] MONTANO. It seems to me the wind has spoken loudly on land. A fuller blast has never shaken our battlements. If it has raged like this upon the sea, what ribs of oak — when mountains of water melt down on them — can hold the joints together? What shall we hear about all this?
[4] SECOND GENTLEMAN. A breaking-up of the Turkish fleet. Just stand on the foaming shore — the chiding surge seems to pelt the clouds, the wind-shaken wave, with its high and monstrous bulk, seems to throw water on the burning Bear and put out the guards of the ever-fixed pole. I have never seen such an attack on the angered flood.
[5] MONTANO. If the Turkish fleet isn't sheltered and bayed in somewhere, they're drowned. It's impossible to ride it out.
[6] [Enter a third Gentleman.]
[7] THIRD GENTLEMAN. News, lads! Our wars are done. The desperate storm has so battered the Turks that their plan has stalled. A noble ship of Venice has seen a grievous wreck and damage upon most of their fleet.
[8] MONTANO. How? Is this true?
[9] THIRD GENTLEMAN. The ship has put in here, a Veronese one. Michael Cassio, lieutenant to the warlike Moor Othello, has come ashore. The Moor himself is at sea, and is in full command here for Cyprus.
[10] MONTANO. I'm glad of it. He's a worthy governor.
[11] THIRD GENTLEMAN. But this same Cassio, though he speaks of comfort about the Turkish loss, still looks sad and prays the Moor is safe — for they were parted by a foul and violent storm.
[12] MONTANO. May heaven keep him safe. For I have served under him, and the man commands like a full soldier. Let's go to the seaside, then! As much to see the ship that's come in as to strain our eyes for brave Othello, until we make the sea and the blue air a single indistinct view.
[13] THIRD GENTLEMAN. Come, let's do that. Every minute there's expectation of more arrivals.
[14] [Enter Cassio.]
[15] CASSIO. Thank you, valiant men of this warlike isle, who think so well of the Moor! Oh, let the heavens give him defense against the elements, for I have lost him on a dangerous sea.
[16] MONTANO. Is he well shipped?
[17] CASSIO. His ship is sturdily built, and his pilot is of very expert and approved skill. So my hopes, not overfed to death, stand in good chance of being cured.
[18] [Within.] A sail, a sail, a sail!
[19] [Enter a Messenger.]
[20] CASSIO. What's that noise?
[21] MESSENGER. The town is empty. On the brow of the sea, ranks of people are standing and they cry "A sail!"
[22] CASSIO. My hopes shape him for the governor.
[23] [A shot.]
[24] SECOND GENTLEMAN. They're firing their shots of courtesy. At least friends of ours.
[25] CASSIO. Please, sir, go out and find out for us who has arrived.
[26] SECOND GENTLEMAN. I shall.
[27] [Exit.]
[28] MONTANO. But, good lieutenant, is your general married?
[29] CASSIO. Most fortunately. He has won a maiden who outdoes any description and any wild rumor — one who exceeds the cleverness of flattering pens, and in the essential garment of creation tires out the imagination itself.
[30] [Enter second Gentleman.]
[31] [How now? Who has come in?]
[32] SECOND GENTLEMAN. It's one Iago, ensign to the general.
[33] CASSIO. He has had a most favorable and lucky passage. The storms themselves, high seas, and howling winds, the gutted rocks and gathered sands — traitors lying in wait to snag the innocent keel — as if they had a sense of beauty, set aside their deadly natures and let the divine Desdemona pass safely.
[34] MONTANO. Who is she?
[35] CASSIO. The woman I spoke of — our great captain's captain, left in the care of bold Iago. His arrival here outruns our expectations by a week. Great Jove, guard Othello, and fill his sail with your own powerful breath, so he may bless this bay with his tall ship, make love's quick gasps in Desdemona's arms, give renewed fire to our extinguished spirits, and bring all of Cyprus comfort!
[36] [Enter Desdemona, Iago, Roderigo, and Emilia.]
[37] [Oh, behold — the riches of the ship have come ashore! You men of Cyprus, kneel to her. Hail to you, lady! And the grace of heaven, before, behind, and on every side of you, surround you completely!]
[38] DESDEMONA. I thank you, valiant Cassio. What news can you tell me of my lord?
[39] CASSIO. He hasn't arrived yet, nor do I know anything except that he's well, and will be here shortly.
[40] DESDEMONA. Oh, but I'm afraid — how did you lose company with him?
[41] [Within.] A sail, a sail!
[42] CASSIO. The great struggle of sea and sky parted our company. But — listen! A sail.
[43] [Guns within.]
[44] SECOND GENTLEMAN. They're greeting the citadel. This one is a friend too.
[45] CASSIO. Go see what the news is.
[46] [Exit Gentleman.]
[47] [Good ensign, you are welcome. [To Emilia.] Welcome, mistress. Don't let it offend your patience, good Iago, that I take this liberty — it's my breeding that gives me this bold show of courtesy.]
[48] [Kissing her.]
[49] IAGO. Sir, if she'd give you as much of her lips as she often gives me of her tongue, you'd have had enough.
[50] DESDEMONA. Sadly, she has no speech.
[51] IAGO. Truly, too much. I still find it when I want to sleep. Of course, I admit, in your ladyship's presence, she keeps her tongue a little inside her heart and only scolds in her thinking.
[52] EMILIA. You have little reason to say that.
[53] IAGO. Come now, come now — you're pictures out in public, bells in your parlors, wild cats in your kitchens, saints when you've been wronged, devils when you've been offended, players in your housework, and housewives in your beds.
[54] DESDEMONA. Oh, shame on you, slanderer!
[55] IAGO. No, it's true, or else I'm a Turk. You get up to play, and go to bed to work.
[56] EMILIA. You shall not write my praise.
[57] IAGO. No, let me not.
[58] DESDEMONA. What would you write about me, if you had to praise me?
[59] IAGO. Oh, gentle lady, don't push me to it — for I am nothing if not critical.
[60] DESDEMONA. Come on, try. — Has someone gone to the harbor?
[61] IAGO. Yes, madam.
[62] DESDEMONA. I'm not cheerful, but I distract myself from what I am by seeming otherwise. — Come, how would you praise me?
[63] IAGO. I'm working on it, but really, my invention comes out of my head as birdlime does from cloth — it plucks out brains and all. But my Muse is laboring, and here she's delivered. If she's fair and wise, fairness and wit — the one's for use, the other uses it.
[64] DESDEMONA. Well praised! What if she's dark and witty?
[65] IAGO. If she's dark, and also has wit, she'll find a white man who'll fit her darkness.
[66] DESDEMONA. Worse and worse.
[67] EMILIA. What if she's fair and foolish?
[68] IAGO. She was never yet foolish who was fair, for even her folly helped her to an heir.
[69] DESDEMONA. These are old fond paradoxes to make fools laugh in the alehouse. What miserable praise have you for the woman who's ugly and foolish?
[70] IAGO. There's no one so ugly and foolish that she doesn't pull foul tricks that fair and wise ones do.
[71] DESDEMONA. Oh, heavy ignorance! You praise the worst best. But what praise could you give to a truly deserving woman — one who, by the authority of her merit, could rightly claim the endorsement of malice itself?
[72] IAGO. She who was always fair and never proud, had tongue at will and yet was never loud, never lacked gold and yet never went showy, fled from her wish, and yet said, "Now I may"; she who, when angered, with her revenge near, told her wrong to stay and her displeasure to fly; she who in wisdom was never so weak as to trade the cod's head for the salmon's tail; she who could think and never reveal her mind, see suitors following and not look behind — she was a creature, if ever such creature existed —
[73] DESDEMONA. To do what?
[74] IAGO. To nurse fools and keep household accounts.
[75] DESDEMONA. Oh, what a lame and impotent conclusion! — Don't learn from him, Emilia, though he is your husband. — What do you say, Cassio? Isn't he a most profane and free-tongued counselor?
[76] CASSIO. He speaks plainly, madam. You may relish him more in the soldier than in the scholar.
[77] IAGO. He takes her by the palm. Yes, well said, whisper. With as small a web as this I will trap as great a fly as Cassio. Yes, smile at her, do. I will shackle you with your own courtship. You speak true — that's so, indeed. If tricks like these strip you of your lieutenancy, it would have been better if you hadn't kissed your three fingers so often, as you're about to do again, playing the gentleman. Very good — well kissed, an excellent courtesy! That's so, indeed. Your fingers to your lips again? I wish they were enema-pipes for your sake!
[78] [Trumpets within.]
[79] [The Moor! I know his trumpet.]
[80] CASSIO. It is, indeed.
[81] DESDEMONA. Let's meet him and welcome him.
[82] CASSIO. Look, here he comes!
[83] [Enter Othello and Attendants.]
[84] OTHELLO. Oh, my fair warrior!
[85] DESDEMONA. My dear Othello!
[86] OTHELLO. It gives me wonder as great as my contentment to see you here before me. Oh, my soul's joy! If after every storm such calms come, may the winds blow till they've woken death! And let the laboring ship climb hills of seas Olympus-high, and dip again as low as hell is from heaven! If I were to die now, it would be the happiest moment to die — for I fear my soul has such absolute contentment that no other comfort like this is to come in unknown fate.
[87] DESDEMONA. The heavens forbid that our love and comforts shouldn't increase as our days do!
[88] OTHELLO. Amen to that, sweet powers! I can't speak enough of this content. It stops me here — it's too much joy. And this, and this, may be the greatest discords our hearts ever make!
[89] IAGO. Oh, you are well tuned now — but I'll loosen the pegs that make this music, as honest as I am.
[90] OTHELLO. Come, let's go to the castle. — News, friends — our wars are done, the Turks are drowned. How does my old acquaintance on this isle? — Honey, you'll be very welcome in Cyprus. I've found great love among them. Oh, my sweet, I'm chattering out of fashion, and I dote in my own comforts. — Please, good Iago, go to the bay and unload my chests. Bring the captain to the citadel — he's a good one, and his worthiness deserves much respect. — Come, Desdemona, once more, well met at Cyprus.
[91] [Exeunt Othello, Desdemona and Attendants.]
[92] IAGO. Meet me presently at the harbor. Come here. If you're brave — as they say, base men in love have a nobility in their natures more than is native to them — listen to me. The lieutenant tonight is on watch in the court of guard. First, I must tell you this: Desdemona is directly in love with him.
[93] RODERIGO. With him? Why, that's not possible.
[94] IAGO. Put your finger here, like this, and let your soul be instructed. Notice with what violence she first loved the Moor, only for bragging and telling her fantastical lies. And will she keep loving him for prattle? Don't let your sensible heart think it. Her eye must be fed. And what delight could she take in looking on the devil? When the blood is made dull by the act of sport, there should be — to inflame it again and give satiety a fresh appetite — loveliness in looks, similarity in age, manners, and beauty. All of which the Moor is deficient in. Now, for lack of these required pleasures, her delicate tenderness will find itself abused, begin to feel sick, lose taste for and loathe the Moor — nature itself will instruct her in it, and force her to some second choice. Now, sir, granting this — and it's a most obvious and unforced position — who stands so eminently positioned to take this fortune as Cassio does? A very fluent knave; no further conscientious than in putting on the mere appearance of civil and humane seeming, the better to encompass his lecherous and most hidden loose affection. Why, no one — no one! A slippery and subtle knave, a finder of opportunities; one whose eye can stamp and counterfeit advantages, even when no true advantage shows itself — a devilish knave! Besides, the knave is handsome, young, and has all those qualities that folly and green minds look after. A pestilent complete knave, and the woman has found him out already.
[95] RODERIGO. I can't believe that about her — she is full of the most blessed condition.
[96] IAGO. Blessed fig's end! The wine she drinks is made of grapes. If she had been blessed, she would never have loved the Moor. Blessed pudding! Didn't you see her paddle with the palm of his hand? Didn't you notice that?
[97] RODERIGO. Yes, I did. But that was just courtesy.
[98] IAGO. Lechery, by this hand. An index and obscure prologue to the history of lust and foul thoughts. They met so close with their lips that their breaths embraced together. Villainous thoughts, Roderigo! When such mutualities so lead the way, the master and main act follows hard at hand, the bodily conclusion. Pish! But, sir, be ruled by me. I have brought you from Venice. Stand watch tonight. As for the command, I'll arrange it for you. Cassio doesn't know you. I'll be close to you. Find some occasion to anger Cassio, either by speaking too loud, or undermining his discipline, or from whatever other course you please, which the time shall more favorably provide.
[99] RODERIGO. Very well.
[100] IAGO. Sir, he is rash, and very quick to anger, and may strike at you with his truncheon. Provoke him to do it — for from that very thing I'll cause these Cypriots to mutiny, whose calm shall not come back into true taste again except by the unseating of Cassio. So you'll have a shorter path to your desires by the means I shall then have to advance them, and the obstacle most profitably removed — without which there'd be no expectation of our success.
[101] RODERIGO. I will do this, if I can bring it to any opportunity.
[102] IAGO. I guarantee you. Meet me shortly at the citadel — I must fetch his necessities ashore. Farewell.
[103] RODERIGO. Adieu.
[104] [Exit.]
[105] IAGO. That Cassio loves her — I do believe it. That she loves him is plausible, and quite credible. The Moor — though I can't endure him — is of a constant, loving, noble nature, and, I dare say, will prove a most dear husband to Desdemona. Now, I love her too — not out of absolute lust (though perhaps I'm accountable for as great a sin), but partly led on to feed my revenge. For I do suspect the lusty Moor has leapt into my seat. The thought of which gnaws my insides like a poisonous mineral, and nothing can or shall content my soul until I'm even with him, wife for wife — or, failing that, that I at least put the Moor into a jealousy so strong that judgment cannot cure it. To do this — if this poor trash of Venice, whom I bait for his quick hunting, takes the bait — I'll have our Michael Cassio on the hip, slander him to the Moor in the foul fashion (for I fear Cassio with my nightcap too), make the Moor thank me, love me, and reward me for making him egregiously an ass and working on his peace and quiet even to madness. It's here, but still confused. Knavery's plain face is never seen till it's used.
[106] [Exit.]

[76] [Nay, good lieutenant — God's will, gentlemen. Help, ho! — Lieutenant — sir — Montano — sir — help, masters! Here's a fine watch indeed!]
[77] [A bell rings.]

[79] [Enter Othello and Attendants.]
[80] OTHELLO. What is the matter here?
[81] MONTANO. Zounds, I'm still bleeding — I'm hurt to the death.
[82] OTHELLO. Stop, for your lives!

He that stirs next to cut for his own rage holds his soul cheaply — he dies the moment he moves. Silence that dreadful bell, it frightens the isle out of its proper bearings. What is the matter, masters? Honest Iago, who looks dead with grief, speak — who began this? On your love, I charge you.

I can't speak of any beginning to this peevish quarrel — and I wish I had lost in some glorious action the legs that brought me to take part in this!

[89] MONTANO. Worthy Othello, I'm dangerously hurt. Your officer, Iago, can tell you — while I spare speech, which somewhat pains me now — all that I know. Nor do I know anything said or done amiss by me this night, unless self-care is sometimes a vice, and to defend ourselves is a sin when violence assails us.

Zounds, if I stir, or even lift this arm, the best of you shall sink in my rebuke. In a town of war, still wild, the people's hearts brimful of fear, to handle private and domestic quarrels at night, on the court and guard of safety? It's monstrous. Iago, who began it?
[91] MONTANO. If, partially attached or leagued in office, you deliver more or less than the truth, you are no soldier.

[95] [Look — if my gentle love hasn't been raised up! I'll make you an example.]
[96] DESDEMONA. What's the matter?

What, man — there are ways to recover the general again. You're just now cast off in his mood, a punishment more in policy than in malice — even as one would beat his innocent dog to frighten an imperious lion. Plead to him again, and he's yours.
[106] CASSIO. I would rather plead to be despised than to deceive so good a commander with so worthless, so drunken, and so indiscreet an officer. Drunk? And speaking parrot? And squabbling? [107] IAGO. Who was he that you were chasing with your sword? What had he done to you?
[108] CASSIO. I don't know.
[109] IAGO. Is it possible?
[110] CASSIO. I remember a mass of things, but nothing clearly — a quarrel, but nothing about what. Oh God, that men should put an enemy in their mouths to steal away their brains! That we should, with joy, pleasure, revel, and applause, transform ourselves into beasts!
[111] IAGO. Why, but you're sober enough now — how did you recover like this?
[112] CASSIO. It has pleased the devil drunkenness to give way to the devil wrath. One imperfection shows me another, to make me frankly despise myself.
[113] IAGO. Come, you're too severe a moralizer. Given the time, the place, and the state of this country, I could heartily wish this hadn't happened — but since it is what it is, mend it for your own good.
[114] CASSIO. I'll ask him for my place again — he'll tell me I'm a drunkard! If I had as many mouths as Hydra, such an answer would stop them all. To be a sensible man one moment, a fool the next, and presently a beast! Oh, strange! Every excessive cup is unblessed, and the ingredient is a devil.
[115] IAGO. Come, come, good wine is a good familiar creature, if it's used well. Don't exclaim against it anymore. And, good lieutenant, I think you think I love you.
[116] CASSIO. I have well approved it, sir. — I, drunk!
[117] IAGO. You, or any man living, may be drunk at a time, man. I'll tell you what you shall do. Our general's wife is now the general — I may say so in this respect, that he has devoted and given himself up to the contemplation, observation, and marking of her qualities and graces. Confess yourself freely to her. Importune her help to put you back in your place. She is of so free, so kind, so apt, so blessed a disposition that she considers it a vice in her goodness not to do more than she's asked. Entreat her to splint this broken joint between you and her husband, and I'll bet my fortunes against any wager worth naming that this crack in your love shall grow stronger than it was before.
[118] CASSIO. You advise me well.
[119] IAGO. I protest, in the sincerity of love and honest kindness.
[120] CASSIO. I take it freely, and in the early morning I will beseech the virtuous Desdemona to undertake for me. I'm desperate about my fortunes if they check me here.
[121] IAGO. You're in the right. Good night, lieutenant — I must go to the watch.
[122] CASSIO. Good night, honest Iago.
[123] [Exit.]
[124] IAGO. And who's he then, that says I play the villain? When this advice I give is free and honest, sensible to reason, and indeed the way to win the Moor again? For it's most easy to subdue the inclining Desdemona in any honest suit. She's made as fruitful as the free elements. And then, for her, to win the Moor — even if it meant renouncing his baptism, all seals and symbols of redeemed sin — his soul is so enfettered to her love that she may make, unmake, do whatever she lists, even as her appetite shall play the god with his weak nature. How am I then a villain, to counsel Cassio on this parallel course, directly toward his good? Divinity of hell! When devils want to put on the blackest sins, they suggest them at first with heavenly shows, as I do now. For while this honest fool plies Desdemona to repair his fortune, and she pleads strongly for him to the Moor, I'll pour this pestilence into the Moor's ear — that she calls Cassio back for her body's lust. And the more she strives to do him good, the more she shall undo her credit with the Moor. So I will turn her virtue into pitch, and out of her own goodness make the net that shall entangle them all.
[125] [Enter Roderigo.]
[126] [How now, Roderigo?]
[127] RODERIGO. I follow here in the chase, not like a hound that hunts but one that fills out the cry. My money is almost spent, I have been tonight exceedingly well beaten, and I think the outcome will be that I shall have only this much experience for my pains, and so, with no money at all and a little more wit, return again to Venice.
[128] IAGO. How poor are they who have no patience! What wound has ever healed except by degrees? You know we work by wit, and not by witchcraft, and wit depends on slow-passing time. Doesn't it go well? Cassio has beaten you, and you, by that small hurt, have gotten Cassio dismissed. Though other things grow fair against the sun, yet fruits that blossom first will ripen first. Content yourself awhile. By the mass, it's morning — pleasure and action make the hours seem short. Withdraw — go where you're billeted. Away, I say — you shall know more hereafter. Now, get going.
[129] [Exit Roderigo.]
[130] [Two things are to be done: my wife must move for Cassio with her mistress. I'll set her on. And I myself, meanwhile, will draw the Moor aside and bring him just at the moment when he may find Cassio soliciting his wife. Yes, that's the way. Don't dull the device with coldness and delay.]
[131] [Exit.]

[1] DESDEMONA. Be assured, good Cassio, I will do all I can on your behalf.
[2] EMILIA. Good madam, do. I warrant it grieves my husband as if the cause were his.
[3] DESDEMONA. Oh, he's an honest fellow. Don't doubt, Cassio, that I'll have my lord and you as friendly again as you were.

[11] DESDEMONA. Why, stay, and hear me speak.

[13] DESDEMONA. Well, use your judgment.
[14] [Exit Cassio.]
[15] IAGO. Ha, I don't like that.
[16] OTHELLO. What did you say?
[17] IAGO. Nothing, my lord — or if I did, I don't know what.
[18] OTHELLO. Wasn't that Cassio just parting from my wife?
[19] IAGO. Cassio, my lord? No, surely, I can't think he would steal away so guilty-like, seeing you coming.
[20] OTHELLO. I do believe it was him.
[21] DESDEMONA. How now, my lord? I've been talking with a suitor here — a man who languishes in your displeasure.
[22] OTHELLO. Who do you mean?
[23] DESDEMONA. Why, your lieutenant, Cassio. Good my lord, if I have any grace or power to move you, accept his present reconciliation. For if he isn't one who truly loves you, who errs in ignorance and not in cunning, I have no judgment in an honest face. Please call him back.
[24] OTHELLO. Did he leave just now?
[25] DESDEMONA. Yes, truly — so humbled that he has left part of his grief with me to suffer along with him. Good love, call him back.
[26] OTHELLO. Not now, sweet Desdemon — some other time.
[27] DESDEMONA. But will it be soon?
[28] OTHELLO. The sooner, sweet, for your sake.
[29] DESDEMONA. Will it be tonight at supper?
[30] OTHELLO. No, not tonight.
[31] DESDEMONA. Tomorrow at dinner then?
[32] OTHELLO. I shall not dine at home — I meet the captains at the citadel.

And yet his offense, by our common reason — unless they say the wars must make examples of their best men — is hardly a fault to incur a private rebuke. When shall he come? Tell me, Othello: I wonder in my soul what you could ask me that I should deny or hesitate so on. What? Michael Cassio, who came courting with you, and so many times, when I have spoken disparagingly of you, has taken your part — to have so much trouble bringing him back in! Trust me, I could do much.
[34] OTHELLO. Please, no more. Let him come when he will — I will deny you nothing.
[35] DESDEMONA. Why, this is not even a favor — it's as if I should beg you to wear your gloves, or feed on nourishing dishes, or keep yourself warm, or plead with you to do a particular benefit to your own person. No, when I have a real suit in which I mean to truly touch your love, it shall be full of weight and difficult substance, and frightening to grant.

[37] DESDEMONA. Shall I deny you? No, farewell, my lord.
[38] OTHELLO. Farewell, my Desdemona. I'll come to you right away.
[39] DESDEMONA. Emilia, come. Be as your fancies teach you. Whatever you are, I am obedient.
[40] [Exit with Emilia.]
[41] OTHELLO. Excellent creature! Damnation catch my soul, but I do love you! And when I love you not, chaos has come again.
[42] IAGO. My noble lord —
[43] OTHELLO. What do you say, Iago?
[44] IAGO. When you wooed my lady, did Michael Cassio know of your love?
[45] OTHELLO. He did, from first to last. Why do you ask?
[46] IAGO. Just for the satisfaction of my thought. No further harm.
[47] OTHELLO. Why your thought, Iago?
[48] IAGO. I didn't think he had been acquainted with her.
[49] OTHELLO. Oh yes, and went between us very often.
[50] IAGO. Indeed?
[51] OTHELLO. Indeed? Yes, indeed. Do you see anything in that? Isn't he honest?
[52] IAGO. Honest, my lord?
[53] OTHELLO. Honest? Yes, honest.
[54] IAGO. My lord, as far as I know.
[55] OTHELLO. What do you think?
[56] IAGO. Think, my lord?

I heard you say just now, you didn't like that — when Cassio left my wife. What didn't you like? And when I told you he was in my confidence throughout my whole wooing, you cried, "Indeed?" And contracted and pursed your brow, as if you had then locked up in your brain some horrible conceit. If you do love me, show me your thought.

For such things in a false, disloyal knave are tricks of habit; but in a man who's just, they are tight expansions, working from the heart, that passion cannot rule.

[65] OTHELLO. No, yet there's more in this. Please, speak to me as you do to your own thoughts, as you ruminate, and give your worst thoughts the worst words.
[66] IAGO. Good my lord, pardon me. Though I am bound to every act of duty, I am not bound to that which all slaves are free from. Utter my thoughts? Why, suppose they are vile and false: where is that palace into which foul things sometimes do not intrude? Who has a breast so pure but some unclean apprehensions hold court and law-days, sitting in session with lawful meditations?
[67] OTHELLO. You do conspire against your friend, Iago, if you think him wronged and make his ear a stranger to your thoughts.
[68] IAGO. I do beseech you — though perhaps I'm vicious in my guess, as, I confess, it is my nature's plague to spy into abuses, and my jealousy shapes faults that aren't there — let your wisdom take no notice from one who so imperfectly conceives, nor build yourself a trouble out of his scattered and unsure observance. It would not be for your quiet or your good, nor for my manhood, honesty, or wisdom, to let you know my thoughts.
[69] OTHELLO. What do you mean?
[70] IAGO. Good name in man and woman, dear my lord, is the immediate jewel of their souls. Whoever steals my purse steals trash — it's something, nothing; it was mine, it's his, and has been slave to thousands. But he that filches from me my good name robs me of that which does not enrich him, and makes me poor indeed.
[71] OTHELLO. By heaven, I'll know your thoughts.
[72] IAGO. You cannot — even if my heart were in your hand. Nor shall you, while it's in my custody.
[73] OTHELLO. Ha?
[74] IAGO. Oh, beware, my lord, of jealousy — it is the green-eyed monster that mocks the meat it feeds on. That cuckold lives in bliss who, certain of his fate, doesn't love the woman who wronged him. But oh, what damned minutes does he count over who dotes, yet doubts, suspects, yet strongly loves!
[75] OTHELLO. Oh, misery!
[76] IAGO. Poor and content is rich, and rich enough — but riches without limit are as poor as winter to the man who ever fears he shall be poor. Good heaven, defend the souls of all my tribe from jealousy!
[77] OTHELLO. Why, why is this? Do you think I'd make a life of jealousy, to follow constantly the changes of the moon with fresh suspicions? No. To be once in doubt is once to be resolved. Exchange me for a goat when I shall turn the business of my soul to such bloated and blown-out surmises, matching your inference. It does not make me jealous to say my wife is fair, eats well, loves company, is free in speech, sings, plays, and dances well. Where virtue is, these are more virtuous. Nor from my own weak merits will I draw the smallest fear or doubt of her revolt — for she had eyes, and chose me. No, Iago — I'll see before I doubt; when I doubt, I'll prove; and on the proof, there's nothing more but this: away at once with love or with jealousy!
[78] IAGO. I'm glad of that — for now I shall have reason to show the love and duty I bear you with a franker spirit. Therefore, as I am bound, receive it from me. I do not yet speak of proof. Watch your wife. Observe her well with Cassio. Wear your eye like this — not jealous, nor secure. I would not have your free and noble nature, out of your own generosity, be abused. Watch for it. I know our country's disposition well. In Venice they let heaven see the pranks they dare not show their husbands. Their best conscience is not to leave undone, but to keep unknown.
[79] OTHELLO. Do you say so?
[80] IAGO. She did deceive her father, marrying you. And when she seemed to shake and fear your looks, she loved them most.
[81] OTHELLO. And so she did.
[82] IAGO. Why, go to then. She that so young could give out such a pretense — to seal up her father's eyes as tight as oak, so that he thought it was witchcraft. But I'm much to blame. I humbly beg your pardon, for loving you too much.
[83] OTHELLO. I am bound to you forever.
[84] IAGO. I see this has somewhat dashed your spirits.
[85] OTHELLO. Not a jot, not a jot.
[86] IAGO. Trust me, I fear it has. I hope you'll consider that what is spoken comes from my love. But I do see you're moved. I must ask you not to strain my speech to grosser issues or larger reach than mere suspicion.
[87] OTHELLO. I will not.
[88] IAGO. Should you do so, my lord, my speech would fall into such vile consequence as my thoughts never aimed at. Cassio is my worthy friend. My lord, I see you're moved.
[89] OTHELLO. No, not much moved. I cannot but think Desdemona's honest.
[90] IAGO. Long may she live so! And long may you live to think so!
[91] OTHELLO. And yet, how nature erring from itself —

One may smell in such a will the rankest, foul disproportion, thoughts unnatural. But pardon me. I do not in setting out distinctly speak of her, though I may fear her will, recoiling to her better judgment, may fall to comparing you with her country forms, and happily repent.

[94] IAGO. My lord, I take my leave.
[95] OTHELLO. Why did I marry? This honest creature doubtless sees and knows more, much more, than he reveals.

Though it's fit that Cassio have his place — for he fills it with great ability — yet if you please to hold him off awhile, you shall by that perceive him and his means. Note if your lady strain his entertainment with any strong or vehement importunity — much will be seen in that. In the meantime, let me be thought too busy in my fears (as I have worthy cause to fear I am) and hold her free of blame, I do beseech your honor.
[97] OTHELLO. Don't fear my self-government.
[98] IAGO. I once more take my leave.
[99] [Exit.]
[100] OTHELLO. This fellow's of exceeding honesty, and knows all qualities, with a learned spirit, of human dealings. If I prove her wild, though her jesses were my dear heartstrings, I'd whistle her off and let her down the wind to prey at fortune. Perhaps because I am black, and don't have those soft parts of conversation that chamberers have — or because I'm declined into the vale of years (though that's not much) — she's gone, I'm abused, and my relief must be to loathe her. [188] CASSIO. There is besides, in Roderigo's letter, how he reproaches Iago for making him provoke me on watch, which led to my being dismissed. And even just now he spoke, after long seeming dead, Iago wounded him, Iago set him on.
[189] LODOVICO. You must leave this room, and come with us. Your power and command are taken from you, and Cassio rules in Cyprus. For this slave, if there is any cunning cruelty that can torment him much and hold him long, it shall be his. You shall remain a close prisoner, until the nature of your offense is known to the Venetian state. Come, bring him away.
[190] OTHELLO. Wait. A word or two before you go. I have done the state some service, and they know it. No more of that. I beg you, in your letters, when you relate these unhappy deeds, speak of me as I am. Do not minimise anything, nor record anything from malice. Then you must speak of one that loved not wisely, but too well; of one not easily made jealous, but, when worked upon, troubled in the extreme; of one whose hand, like the base Judean, threw a pearl away richer than all his tribe; of one whose hardened eyes, though unused to weeping, drop tears as fast as the Arabian trees their medicinal gum. Set this down. And say also that once in Aleppo, where a malicious and turban-wearing Turk beat a Venetian and slandered the state, I took the circumcised dog by the throat, and struck him, thus.
[191] [Stabs himself.]
[192] LODOVICO. Oh bloody ending!
[193] GRATIANO. All that has been said is undone.
[194] OTHELLO. I kissed you before I killed you. There was no other way but this—killing myself, to die upon a kiss.
[195] [Falling upon Desdemona.]
[196] CASSIO. This I feared, but I thought he had no weapon, for he was great of heart.
[197] LODOVICO. Oh Spartan dog, more cruel than anguish, hunger, or the sea, look on the tragic burden of this bed. This is your work. The sight is poisonous. Let it be hidden. Gratiano, keep the house, and seize the fortunes of the Moor, for they pass on to you. To you, lord governor, remains the sentencing of this hellish villain. The time, the place, the torture—oh, enforce it! I myself will go straight aboard, and to the state I shall this heavy act with heavy heart relate.
[198] [Exeunt.]
