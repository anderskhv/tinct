Obstacles:
- **1 (hard):** "a complicated struggle was being carried on with greater intensity than ever in the highest circles—between the parties of Rumyantsev, the French, Maria Fedorovna, the Tsarevich, and others" — a list of faction names dropped in with no gloss; a new reader can't tell what any of these parties stand for.
- **4 (hard):** "the enchanting countess's illness arose from an inconvenience caused by marrying two husbands at the same time, and that the Italian's cure consisted of removing that inconvenience" — euphemism whose literal referent (bigamy, and what "removing" it means medically) isn't spelled out.
- **14 (hard):** "a diplomatic note sent to Vienna with some Austrian banners captured from the French by Wittgenstein, \"the hero of Petropol\" as he was then called in Petersburg" — unexplained person and epithet, dropped in mid-sentence.
- **19 (unclear):** "\"The path to Warsaw, perhaps,\" Prince Hippolyte remarked loudly and unexpectedly. Everybody looked at him, understanding what he meant." — the text asserts the room understood the remark's point but never tells the reader what it was; genuinely can't say what it means.
- **20 (hard):** "Moscow, our ancient capital, the New Jerusalem, receives her Christ"—he placed a sudden emphasis on the word her—"as a mother receives her zealous sons into her arms" — dense, mixed devotional metaphor (city as mother/Jerusalem/receiving Christ) stacked in one breath.
- **25 (hard):** "humble Faith, the sling of the Russian David, shall suddenly strike his head in his bloodthirsty pride" — archaic syntax wrapped around a Biblical allusion; parseable but requires a re-read.

Rhythm/voice: Mostly natural modern English narration; the quoted letter and the reported dialogue at Anna Pavlovna's read as deliberately florid/archaic *within the story* (a read-aloud church letter, a courtier's joke), which is appropriate to what's being depicted rather than a translation problem. The connective narration around it is plain and contemporary — **natural modern English, with intentionally archaic material quoted inside it.**
Unexplained references: Rumyantsev, Maria Fedorovna, the Tsarevich, Wittgenstein/"hero of Petropol," the Goliath/David allusion — a cluster of period-political and Biblical names a new reader would have to look up.
Argument/story thread: A Petersburg salon evening on the day of Borodino, contrasted with the court's insulated unawareness of the war; loses no thread, but the political banter (para 19) leaves one joke opaque.
Anachronism/over-explanation: None noticed; no modern idiom jars.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE

- **7 (unclear):** "the Queen of Spain's private physician had prescribed small doses of a certain drug to produce a certain effect" — deliberately vague ("a certain drug," "a certain effect"); a new reader can't tell if this means medical treatment, an illicit procedure, or poisoning, and the paragraph later pivots to an overdose without clarifying which reading is meant.
- **9 (hard):** "Kutuzov was a traitor, and Prince Vasili, during the condolence visits paid to him on the occasion of his daughter's death" — the sentence swings into what reads as the court's opinion stated as fact ("was a traitor") without a clear signal it's reported/free-indirect speech, and "his daughter" (= Helene) is never stated as Vasili's daughter anywhere in the chapter — the reader has to infer it.

Rhythm/voice: Natural modern narration alternating with two formal official letters (Rostopchin's and the Emperor's), which are appropriately stiffer in register — that's a genre shift, not translated syntax. **Reads as natural modern English.**
Unexplained references: Tuchkov, Bagration, Kutaysov (named only, no role given); the Vasili/Helene family relation is assumed rather than stated.
Argument/story thread: Clear overall — false rumor of victory, then the real news of Moscow's fall reaches Petersburg via two escalating official dispatches. Easy to follow end to end.
Anachronism/over-explanation: None.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
