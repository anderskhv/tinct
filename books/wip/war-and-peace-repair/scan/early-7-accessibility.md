## Chapter 67 — Book Three (1805) — Chapter 18 (45 paragraphs)

Counts: clear 42 / hard 3 / unclear 0

| Index | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1–18, 20, 22–35, 37–45 | clear |
| 19, 21, 36 | hard |

Obstacles:
- §19: "...rode up to the ditch, dug in his spurs, and leapt lightly over it... He turned the horse sharply, jumped back over the ditch, and respectfully addressed the rider with the white plumes... The rider — whose figure seemed familiar to Rostov and held his gaze — made a gesture of refusal..." Two riders are tracked only as "one"/"the other"/"the rider" across one very long sentence; the reader has to hold both figures in mind to work out which one turns out to be the Emperor.
- §21: "But just as a young man in love trembles, loses his nerve, and can't bring himself to say the words he's rehearsed through sleepless nights... so Rostov, now that he had achieved what he wanted more than anything in the world, had no idea how to approach the Emperor." An extended simile whose payoff clause ("so Rostov...") arrives after a long delay, requiring a reread to reconnect it to the opening "just as."
- §36: "...on that narrow dam, among the wagons and the cannons, under the horses' hooves and between the wagon wheels, men disfigured by the terror of death now crushed together, trampling one another, dying, stepping over the dying, and killing each other..." Three parallel "on that dam"/"where" clauses stack up before the main verb finally arrives, delaying sense.

Rhythm/voice: Reads as natural modern prose with vivid, controlled battle imagery; the few obstacles are very long, clause-stacked sentences that retain 19th-century syntax rather than being broken up.

Unexplained references: Pratzen Heights, Hosjeradek, Augesd Dam, Przhebyshevsky, Langeron, Dokhturov are all dropped in as already-known places/commanders — fine for a continuing reader, a gap for a cold start.

Argument/story thread: Rostov searches for the wounded Emperor and Kutuzov amid the rout at Austerlitz, glimpses the Tsar but loses his nerve and rides away in shame; the narrative then pulls back to the broader collapse of the Russian lines and the catastrophic crush and drowning at the Augesd Dam. Clear two-part structure, easy to summarize.

Anachronism/over-explanation: None noticed.

**Accessibility verdict: PASS**

Abstract philosophical alternatives stacked with no signposting; takes real effort to see this is Prince Andrew weighing two rival conceptions of God.

Argument/story thread: The wounded Prince Andrew has his "vast sky" epiphany on the battlefield, is noticed and admired by Napoleon, is carried off among the prisoners, and undergoes a private religious/philosophical crisis in which Napoleon shrinks to insignificance beside the sky he has seen.
