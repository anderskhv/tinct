**Before:** "I'm not joking," Bilibin went on. "Nothing could be more true or more tragic. These gentlemen ride onto the bridge alone, waving white handkerchiefs. They assure the officer on duty that they, the marshals, are on their way to negotiate with Prince Auersperg. He lets them into the tête-de-pont — the bridgehead. They spin a thousand tall tales, saying the war is over, that Emperor Francis is arranging a meeting with Bonaparte, that they'd like to see Prince Auersperg, and so on. The officer sends for Auersperg. The gentlemen embrace the Austrian officers, crack jokes, sit on the cannons, and meanwhile a French battalion slips onto the bridge unnoticed, throws the detonation charges into the water, and reaches the fortifications. At last the lieutenant general himself appears—our dear Prince Auersperg von Mautern. 'Dearest enemy! Flower of the Austrian army! Hero of the Turkish wars! Hostilities are over, we can shake hands... Emperor Napoleon burns with impatience to make Prince Auersperg's acquaintance.' In short, these gentlemen—true Gascons—so bewildered him with their fine words, and he was so flattered by his sudden intimacy with the French marshals, and so dazzled by the sight of Murat's cloak and ostrich plumes, that he couldn't see what was right in front of him—and forgot he was supposed to be firing on the enemy!" Despite his animated delivery, Bilibin didn't forget to pause after this bon mot to let it land. "The French battalion rushes the bridgehead, spikes the guns, and the bridge is taken! But the best part," he continued, his excitement subsiding under the pleasant momentum of his own story, "is that the sergeant in charge of the cannon—the one meant to give the signal to detonate the mines—seeing the French troops swarming onto the bridge, was about to fire. But Lannes grabbed his hand. The sergeant, who was clearly smarter than his general, went up to Auersperg and said: 'Prince, you're being deceived—these are the French!' Murat, seeing that all was lost if the sergeant was allowed to speak, turned to Auersperg with a look of feigned astonishment—a true Gascon—and said: 'I hardly recognize the world-famous Austrian discipline when you allow a subordinate to address you like that!' It was a stroke of genius. Prince Auersperg, feeling his authority challenged, ordered the sergeant arrested. Come on, you have to admit this Thabor Bridge business is magnificent! It's not exactly stupidity, and it's not exactly treachery either..."
