# Chapter 255 — French pass log

## Paragraph 21

**Before:** The French attributed the Fire of Moscow to the ferocious patriotism * of Rostopchin; the Russians blamed the barbarity of the French. In reality, however, the burning of Moscow could not be attributed to any individual or any group of people. Moscow burned because it found itself in a position where any town built of wood was bound to burn, quite apart from whether it had a hundred and thirty inferior fire engines or not. Deserted Moscow had to burn as inevitably as a heap of shavings must burn when sparks continuously fall on it for several days. A town built of wood, where fires break out almost daily even when homeowners are present and a police force is maintained, cannot help burning when its inhabitants have left and it is occupied by soldiers who smoke pipes, make campfires of Senate chairs in the Senate Square, and cook themselves meals twice a day. In peacetime, merely billeting troops in the villages of any district immediately increases the number of fires. How much more must the probability of fire increase in an abandoned, wooden town occupied by foreign troops? "The ferocious patriotism of Rostopchin" and the barbarity of the French were not to blame. Moscow was set on fire by soldiers' pipes, kitchens, and campfires, and by the carelessness of enemy soldiers occupying houses they did not own. Even if there was any arson (which is very doubtful, since no one had any reason to burn the houses—in any case a troublesome and dangerous thing to do), arson cannot be regarded as the cause, for the same thing would have happened without it.
