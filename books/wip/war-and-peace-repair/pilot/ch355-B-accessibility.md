## Hard paragraphs — obstacles

**Paragraph 4.** "Gervinus, Schlosser, and others, for instance, at one point prove that Napoleon was a product of the Revolution, of the ideas of 1789 and so on." These names are dropped in with no gloss at all (contrast paragraph 2, where Thiers and Lanfrey are each given a one-word label — "a Bonapartist," "a Republican"). A new reader has no way to know these are historians, let alone what school they represent, so the example partly floats free of its argument.

**Paragraph 6.** "To break a composite, or resultant, force down into the component forces that equal it, those components must add up to the resultant." This sentence introduces abstract force/resultant vocabulary very tersely, with no worked example yet attached, and has to be re-read to parse which noun is doing what.

**Paragraph 7.** "The historian, in effect, breaks Alexander's power down into its components: Talleyrand, Chateaubriand, and the rest... how, that is, component forces adding up to one A could give a resultant equal to a thousand times A." Two stacked obstacles: a run of six named figures (Stein, Metternich, Madame de Staël, Talleyrand, Fichte, Chateaubriand) introduced with no identifying tag, and then an abstract algebra-style formulation ("one A," "a thousand times A") that a general reader has to work to translate back into plain meaning.

**Paragraph 11.** "But how a book, Le Contrat Social, had the effect of making Frenchmen begin to drown one another cannot be understood without an explanation..." Two problems compound here: the book title is left in French with no author or gloss (so a reader may not even register it as Rousseau's Social Contract), and "making Frenchmen begin to drown one another" is a specific, violent historical image (mass drownings during the Terror) presented as if the reader already knows the event — nothing in the chapter has prepared this reference. This is the single worst obstacle in the chapter: it's not just an unfamiliar name but an unglossed atrocity used as a load-bearing example.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as one consistent voice throughout — a formal, argumentative essay register with long cumulative sentences building toward a thesis, not choppy or fragmented. It doesn't sound like a modern paraphrase; it holds a 19th-century essayistic tone (e.g., "history's essential question") without lapsing into either textbook flatness or artificial archaism.

**Unexplained references.** Thiers, Lanfrey, Gervinus, Schlosser, Stein, Metternich, Madame de Staël, Talleyrand, Fichte, Chateaubriand, and "Le Contrat Social" are all named with no identification beyond (in two cases) a political label. A reader would need to look up most of these to know who they were or why they're cited as examples.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter surveys several classes of historians (biographical, universal, "culture") and shows each one implicitly relying on the idea of "power" as a cause even while officially rejecting it — building toward the claim that no existing school of history has actually answered what force moves nations. The thread is traceable end to end; nothing derails it, though the accumulation of unexplained proper nouns in paragraphs 4, 7, and 11 slows the read.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. No modern idiom intrudes, and the prose does not over-explain — if anything it under-explains its historical examples (see above), never the opposite problem.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
