## Hard/unclear obstacles

**Paragraph 10** — *"The fifth prisoner, the one next to Pierre, was led away—alone. Pierre did not understand that he was saved, that he and the rest had been brought there only to witness the execution."*
The pronoun "he" in "he was saved" most naturally points back to its nearest antecedent, "the fifth prisoner," just named. But the fifth prisoner is not saved — he is the one about to be shot (as the rest of the paragraph makes clear). The actual referent is Pierre. On first reading this invites the wrong picture (the fifth prisoner being spared) and only resolves a sentence or two later, once the boy is shown being dragged to the post.

**Paragraph 13** — *"followed by the reports of eight muskets"*
Two paragraphs earlier the text establishes "Twelve sharpshooters with muskets stepped out of the ranks... and halted eight paces from the post" (para 7). Here only eight muskets fire, with no explanation of why the squad size changed or only part of it fired. A reader trying to track the mechanics of the execution hits a contradiction between the stated squad size and the number of shots.

**Paragraph 16** — *"The twenty-four sharpshooters with discharged muskets, standing in the center of the circle, ran back to their places..."*
A third, different number for the firing squad (after twelve in para 7 and eight in para 13), introduced without explanation of how it relates to the earlier counts. The overall action (soldiers returning to their companies) is still followable, but anyone trying to hold a consistent mental picture of the firing squad's size across the chapter cannot reconcile twelve, eight, and twenty-four.

**Paragraph 18** — *"'That will teach them to start fires,' said one of the Frenchmen."*
Nothing earlier in the chapter explains that the prisoners were accused of arson. This is the first and only mention of "fires" as the reason for the executions, dropped in without setup — a new reader has no basis in the chapter itself for what it refers to.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as continuous, single-author literary prose rather than choppy paraphrase — sentences vary in length, several are compound/complex but resolve cleanly. Repetition (Pierre "again" turning away, "again" hearing the volley) reads as deliberate stylistic patterning, not translation artifact. A few phrasings are mildly formal/old-fashioned ("at the officer's command," "a certain order, according to the list") but nothing archaic enough to stop a reader.

**Unexplained references.** Prince Shcherbatov's house, the Virgin's Field, and "the nunnery" are named with no orientation (which city, whose house) — a new reader can still follow the scene spatially but doesn't know where it is. "Sharpshooters of the 86th" (an unexplained regiment number) and the "start fires" accusation (para 18, see above) are the two references a new reader would most want explained.

**Argument or story thread.** Clear across the whole chapter: Pierre, a prisoner, is marched out with four others and made to watch the French shoot them one and two at a time by firing squad; only near the end does he realize he himself is not slated to die, and the chapter closes with the shaken crowd dispersing after the pit is filled in.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None found — no modern idiom jars, and the one expository aside ("shakos — the tall, stiff military caps of the day," para 1) is a reasonable gloss on a period term rather than over-explaining what the scene should simply show.
