## Hard-paragraph obstacles

**P2** — "As soon as historians of different nationalities and different leanings start describing the same event, their answers immediately lose all meaning... Thiers, a Bonapartist, says Napoleon's power rested on his virtue and his genius. Lanfrey, a Republican, says it rested on his trickery." Thiers and Lanfrey are named with only a political label, no indication of who they are or when they wrote; combined with the paragraph's length, this asks extra work to track the claim.

**P4** — "the power of historical figures—presented as the product of many forces—can no longer, it would seem, be treated as a force that itself produces events." This introduces "resultant"/"product of many forces" as the chapter's key mechanism without ever defining it in plain terms; Gervinus and Schlosser are named with no gloss at all.

**P6** — "To find component forces equal to a composite or resultant force, the sum of the components must equal the resultant." A bare vector-addition statement dropped in with no illustration yet attached; a reader with no physics/math background has nothing to hang it on until the next paragraph.

**P7** — "the activity of the statesmen and writers Stein, Metternich, Madame de Staël, Talleyrand, Fichte, Chateaubriand and others" and "component forces adding up to some quantity A produced a resultant a thousand times greater than A." Six proper names arrive in one clause with no individual context, and the algebra metaphor ("quantity A") asks the reader to hold an abstract symbol in mind through a long sentence.

**P11** — "how a book, Rousseau's Le Contrat Social (The Social Contract), had the effect of making Frenchmen start drowning one another." The book is glossed, but "drowning one another" alludes to a specific historical episode (the Terror's mass drownings) with no context; the rhetorical point still lands, but the image itself is opaque.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as a single, consistent argumentative voice — formal, essayistic, reasoning in stages — not as choppy fragments or visibly translated syntax. Several sentences (P2, P7) are long and multi-clause and need a second pass, but the prose holds together as one author's sustained line of reasoning throughout.

**Unexplained references.** Historian names (Thiers, Lanfrey, Gervinus, Schlosser) and a cluster of statesmen/writers (Stein, Metternich, Madame de Staël, Talleyrand, Fichte, Chateaubriand) are dropped in with minimal or no context. Historical shorthand — "ideas of 1789," "campaign of 1812/1813," "restoration of the Bourbons," Frenchmen "drowning one another" — assumes the reader already knows the Napoleonic-era timeline and the Terror.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter surveys three kinds of historians (single-event, universal, and "of culture") and shows that each secretly reintroduces an unexplained notion of "power" to explain what causes historical events, after officially rejecting it. This thread stays intact end to end — it's a stepwise argument, not a narrative, and it doesn't get lost.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom jars, and nothing is over-explained; if anything the opposite — the essay under-explains, leaning on period and technical (physics/math) vocabulary it never unpacks for a lay reader.
