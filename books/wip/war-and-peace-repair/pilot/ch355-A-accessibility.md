## Obstacles in hard/unclear paragraphs

**¶2** — "Thiers, a Bonapartist, says Napoleon's power rested on his virtue and his genius. Lanfrey, a Republican, says it rested on his trickery and his deception of the people." The two names get only a one-word political tag each, and the paragraph asks the reader to hold their opposed claims in mind through several more sentences of abstract argument about "this force" before the point (historians disagree) lands. Followable, but it takes real effort to track who believes what and why it matters.

**¶4** — "Gervinus, Schlosser, and others, for instance, at one point prove that Napoleon was a product of the Revolution..." These two names arrive with zero identification — no dates, nationality, or role — unlike Thiers/Lanfrey one paragraph earlier. A new reader has nothing to anchor them to.

**¶6** — "To find component forces equal to a composite or resultant force, the sum of the components must equal the resultant." This is a physics/vector-addition concept (parallelogram of forces) dropped in as a bare abstract rule, with no signal that it's an analogy or where it comes from. It reads as a technical axiom a reader is assumed to already know.

**¶7** — "the activity of Stein, Metternich, Madame de Staël, Talleyrand, Fichte, Chateaubriand, and others." Six names in one clause, none identified, on top of "the campaign of 1813" and "the restoration of the Bourbons," both assumed familiar. The paragraph's own logic (components vs. resultant) is otherwise followable, but the name-list is a wall.

**¶11** — "how a book, Le Contrat Social, had the effect of making Frenchmen start drowning one another." The title is left in French, untranslated and unglossed (it's Rousseau's *The Social Contract*), and "Frenchmen start drowning one another" alludes to a specific historical episode (the Terror's noyades) without any framing — a reader can guess it means something violent but not what or why.

## Chapter-level notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as a single, consistent argumentative voice throughout — sustained philosophical-essay register, not choppy, not obviously "translated." Sentences are long and clause-heavy by nature of the argument (chains of qualification, "and yet," "but"), which is demanding but coherent rather than garbled.

**Unexplained references.** A cluster of named 19th-century historians and statesmen (Thiers, Lanfrey, Gervinus, Schlosser, Stein, Metternich, Madame de Staël, Talleyrand, Fichte, Chateaubriand) appear with no identification beyond two political labels. "Le Contrat Social" is left in French. "Restoration of the Bourbons" and "the campaign of 1812/1813" are assumed known. A new reader would need to look all of these up.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that no school of history — biographical, "universal," or "cultural" — has actually explained what force drives historical events; each one, having ruled out simple explanations, ends up smuggling in an unexamined "power" to patch its own theory. It proceeds by taking each school in turn and showing the same contradiction. The thread is traceable start to finish; nothing caused me to lose it.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. No modern idiom jars, and if anything the chapter under-explains (bare proper nouns, untranslated title) rather than over-explaining.

## Verdict

Five of thirteen paragraphs are hard, all for the same reason: dense clusters of unglossed historical proper nouns (plus one untranslated French title and one uncontextualized technical metaphor). No paragraph is unclear and the argument itself is traceable, but the volume of unexplained names is more than "a few... with minor obstacles."
