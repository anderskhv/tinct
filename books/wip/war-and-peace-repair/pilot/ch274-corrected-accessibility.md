## Obstacles

**Paragraph 13 (hard):** "followed by the reports of eight muskets" — paragraph 7 said "Twelve sharpshooters ... stepped out of the ranks" for the first shooting. Here the count shifts to "eight muskets" for a single man, with no explanation of how many shooters are assigned to whom. A reader trying to track the mechanics of the executions has to re-read to see whether this is a new detail or a contradiction.

**Paragraph 14 (unclear):** "They all plainly and unmistakably knew that they were criminals who must hide the traces of their guilt as quickly as possible." This stands alone as its own one-sentence paragraph, right after a paragraph about soldiers dragging the body into the pit. "They" has no clear antecedent — it could mean the soldiers at the pit, the French generally, or everyone present. It reads as a narrator's aside rather than continuous action, and I can't say with confidence whom it's describing.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as natural, flowing prose with varied sentence length — not choppy, not obviously "translated" syntax. The restrained, grim tone is consistent throughout and sounds like one narrating voice.

**Unexplained references.** "Prince Shcherbatov's house," "Virgin's Field," and "the nunnery" are place names given with no orientation, but they don't block comprehension since they function as generic geography. More significant: the reason for the executions is never stated in the chapter itself — the reader only gets a hint at the very end ("That will teach them to start fires"), so for most of the scene the charge against the prisoners is unknown.

**Argument or story thread.** Pierre, a prisoner sixth on the execution list, watches as the men ahead of him are shot two at a time and buried; when the fifth is killed he realizes — only after the fact — that he and the rest were brought only to witness, not to die, and the chapter ends with a French soldier's bitter, unfinished remark about fire-setting. The thread holds together; I didn't lose it.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. No modern idiom jars against the period setting, and the prose generally shows rather than explains.
