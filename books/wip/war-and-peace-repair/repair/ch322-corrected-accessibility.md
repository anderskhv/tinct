## Obstacles

**Paragraph 2** — "(History of the Year 1812. The character of Kutuzov and reflections on the unsatisfactory results of the battles at Krasnoe, by the historian Bogdanovich.)" This citation is dropped in as its own paragraph with no framing sentence (e.g., "as the historian Bogdanovich later wrote"); a reader has to infer that it's a source note for the claim just made, rather than narrative text.

**Paragraph 4** — "Napoleon—that most insignificant tool of history, who never anywhere, even in exile, showed human dignity—Napoleon is the object of adulation... But Kutuzov—the man who, from the beginning to the end of his activity in 1812, never once wavered by word or deed from Borodino to Vilna, gave an example of self-sacrifice unmatched in history, along with a living awareness of the future significance of what was happening—Kutuzov seems to them something indefinite and pitiful..." Two long appositives, each repeating the subject's name after a lengthy interruption, take real effort to parse even though the repeated naming does help track them.

**Paragraph 6** — "Kutuzov never spoke of 'forty centuries looking down from the Pyramids'..." This is an unglossed allusion (apparently a Napoleon quotation) dropped in with no context for why it's being invoked as a contrast. The same paragraph also packs in several unexplained proper nouns in quick succession (Madame de Stael, Rostopchin, the Yauza bridge, Arakcheev, Ermolov) that a new reader would need to look up.

**Paragraph 8** — "In reply to Lauriston's proposal of peace, he said: There can be no peace, for such is the people's will." "Lauriston" is named with no identification, and this reported line is given without quotation marks while every other quoted remark in the chapter uses them — a reader can't be sure whether it's a direct quote or a paraphrase.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Essayistic and argumentative — rhetorical triads, repeated constructions, and direct questions to the reader ("But how did that old man... so accurately perceive..."). It reads as one consistent authorial voice throughout, not as flattened paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** A dense cluster: Bogdanovich, the "forty centuries" quotation, Madame de Stael, Rostopchin, the Yauza bridge, Arakcheev, Ermolov, Lauriston, Austerlitz, and the place names Tarutino/Vyazma/Krasnoe/Vilna — all used with no identification, assuming a reader who already knows the 1812 campaign and its cast.

**Argument or story thread.** Clear: the chapter argues that history and Kutuzov's contemporaries unfairly glorify Napoleon while dismissing Kutuzov, when Kutuzov alone consistently grasped and acted on the true national aim of the 1812 campaign — driving out the French while sparing further bloodshed — even as everyone around him disagreed. It closes on the aphorism that a "lackey" cannot recognize true greatness.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom. One internal inconsistency stands out: the unquoted reported speech in paragraph 8 breaks the pattern set by every other quoted line in the chapter.

Accessibility verdict: PASS
