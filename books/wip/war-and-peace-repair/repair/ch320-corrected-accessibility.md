## Obstacles

**Paragraph 3** — "just as a deep wound may heal until its edges join, physical and spiritual wounds alike can only heal completely through a vital force from within." The simile's object ("a deep wound") gets folded into the very category it's illustrating ("physical and spiritual wounds alike"), so the sentence has to be re-read to see which wound is the example and which is the point being made.

**Paragraph 16** — "Natasha, who had previously turned away from that life of devotion, submission, and the poetry of Christian self-sacrifice with passive incomprehension, now—feeling bound to Princess Mary by affection—learned to love her past too and to understand a side of life that had been entirely foreign to her." A stack of abstract nouns (devotion, submission, "the poetry of Christian self-sacrifice") sits between the subject and its verb, so the main claim ("Natasha... learned to love") is easy to lose track of on first pass.

**Paragraph 17** — "this silence gradually—without their realizing it—caused them to begin forgetting him." The referent of "him" is not restated here; it depends on recalling Prince Andrew from paragraph 5, several paragraphs earlier, with no name to anchor it.

**Paragraph 20** — "she called again—though she could hear Dunyasha coming—this time using the deep chest tones she used to sing with, and listened to herself attentively." The parenthetical aside interrupts the action mid-sentence, so it takes a re-read to realize Natasha called a second time on purpose, to test her own voice, rather than because the first call went unanswered.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as coherent, warm literary prose in one consistent voice — reflective, psychological narration. A few long sentences with stacked subordinate clauses slow the pace, but it never collapses into choppy fragments or flattened paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** Prince Andrew, Petya, Sonya, "the count," and Dunyasha are all named with no introduction; a reader coming to this chapter cold would need to already know who each is and how they relate to Natasha and her mother (this chapter assumes that groundwork was laid earlier in the book).

**Argument or story thread.** Clear. After Petya's death devastates her mother, Natasha finds renewed purpose caring for her, and a close, healing friendship grows between Natasha and Princess Mary as they share their grief — the emotional wound in both women begins, slowly, to heal.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. No modern idiom intrudes, and the prose doesn't over-explain what it shows (e.g., Natasha's private tests of her strength and voice in paragraphs 19–20 are shown, not narrated as symbolic).
