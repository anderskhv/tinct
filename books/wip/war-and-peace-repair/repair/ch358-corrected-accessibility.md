## Obstacles

**Paragraph 7 — hard.**
> "(With this method of observation, it often happens that the observer, influenced by whichever direction he himself prefers, regards those as leaders who, owing to the people's change of direction, are no longer in front—they may be off to one side, or even at the back.)"

The parenthetical stacks three levels of qualification (the observer's bias, the change of direction, the leader no longer being in front) into one sentence, and "regards those as leaders who ... are no longer in front" requires a re-read to see who is doing what.

**Paragraph 11 — hard.**
> "If the realm of human knowledge were limited to abstract reasoning, then, having subjected to criticism the explanation of \"power\" that juridical science gives us, mankind would conclude that power is merely a word with no real existence behind it."

A long conditional with an interposed participial clause ("having subjected to criticism...") between subject and verb — the reader has to hold the "if...then" open across the whole insertion.

**Paragraph 12 — hard.**
> "Not to speak of the fact that no description of the collective activity of men can do without the idea of power, the existence of power is proven both by history and by observing events happening around us now."

"Not to speak of the fact that" is an archaic way of saying "not to mention," with no cue for a reader unfamiliar with the construction — it reads at first like the start of a list rather than an aside.

**Paragraph 15 — hard.**
> "On one hand, reflection shows that the expression of a man's will—his words—is only part of the general activity expressed in an event, as in a war or a revolution, and so, without assuming some incomprehensible, supernatural force—a miracle—one cannot accept that words could be the immediate cause of the movements of millions of men."

A double negative buried in a long sentence ("without assuming ... one cannot accept that words could be...") — a reader has to untangle the negatives to get the actual claim (words alone can't cause mass movements).

**Paragraph 19 — hard.**
> "But when we speak of commands that express the will of men acting in time and in relation to one another, to explain the connection between commands and events we must restore two things: (1) the condition underlying everything that happens—the continuity of movement in time, both of the events themselves and of the person giving the command; and (2) the inevitability of the connection between the person giving the command and those who carry it out."

Dense abstract terminology ("continuity of movement in time," "the inevitability of the connection") is introduced here without illustration — the paragraph sets up ideas the chapter doesn't yet demonstrate, so the reader has to take the phrasing on faith.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** One consistent argumentative voice throughout. The extended "herd of cattle" analogy (paragraphs 5–8) carries the chapter well and keeps it from feeling like a dry list of claims — it reads as sustained prose, not choppy fragments, though a few sentences show an old-fashioned syntactic habit (interposed clauses, "not to speak of the fact that").

**Unexplained references.** Napoleon III's decree sending the French to Mexico, and Bismarck's decree sending an army into Bohemia, are both used as illustrative examples without any framing of what those events were — a new reader can still follow the point (a ruler's word precedes an army's movement) without knowing the specific history, but the events themselves would need looking up. Bismarck and the Bourbons are both usefully glossed in-line.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that the "transfer of the people's will to a ruler" theory is just a restatement of the question, not an answer (shown through the cattle-herd analogy and a Q&A passage), then pivots to argue that "power" is nevertheless a real, experience-verified phenomenon — just not one where a ruler's words are the direct cause of events. It ends by setting up the idea that explaining power requires looking at the continuity of the relationship between commander and followers over time. The thread holds together across the chapter, though the final paragraph introduces its key terms abstractly, without yet showing them at work.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom intrudes. If anything the chapter under-explains rather than over-explains: the Mexico and Bohemia references are asserted rather than shown.
