## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "...a complicated struggle was being carried on with greater heat than ever in the highest circles—between the parties of Rumyantsev, the French, Marya Fedorovna, the Tsarevich (heir to the throne), and others—drowned as usual by the buzzing of the court drones."
A list of named factions with no indication of who these people are or what each "party" stands for (why is "the French" a court party rather than a nationality?). Only the Tsarevich gets a gloss.

**Paragraph 4** — "Everyone knew perfectly well that the enchanting countess's illness arose from an inconvenience resulting from having married two husbands at the same time, and that the Italian's cure consisted of removing that inconvenience..."
The euphemism "an inconvenience... from removing that inconvenience" never specifies what the actual predicament or treatment is. A reader cannot tell whether this means a physical condition, a legal one, or something else — the sentence works only if you already infer the euphemism's target.

**Paragraph 14** — "...the Russian general Wittgenstein, \"the hero of Petropol\" as he was then called in Petersburg."
"Petropol" is left unglossed. Nothing tells the reader this is a poetic/ceremonial name (apparently for St. Petersburg or an associated feat), so the epithet's meaning is inaccessible.

**Paragraph 19** — "\"The path to Warsaw, perhaps,\" Prince Hippolyte remarked loudly and unexpectedly. Everybody looked at him, understanding what he meant."
The text explicitly says the other characters grasped the remark's meaning but never tells the reader what that meaning is. There is no way to reconstruct the joke or its political point from the chapter alone — this is a genuine blank, not just an effortful inference.

**Paragraph 20** — "Moscow, our ancient capital, the New Jerusalem, receives her Christ... and through the gathering mists, foreseeing the brilliant glory of thy rule, sings in exultation, 'Hosanna, blessed is he that cometh!'"
Dense, archaic ceremonial rhetoric (deliberately performed in-scene as a formal address) that takes real effort to parse on first read, and "New Jerusalem" applied to Moscow is not explained.

**Paragraph 24** — "Let the bold and insolent Goliath from the borders of France encompass the realms of Russia with death-bearing terrors; humble Faith, the sling of the Russian David, shall suddenly smite his head in his bloodthirsty pride."
Extended Biblical metaphor (David and Goliath, applied to Napoleon/Russia) delivered in ornate, archaic phrasing. Readable only if the reader already knows the Biblical story; the syntax itself ("death-bearing terrors," "bloodthirsty pride") also requires rereading.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** The narration is natural, ironic modern prose in one consistent voice. The two set-piece declamations (paragraphs 20 and 24) deliberately shift into ornate, archaic rhetoric as an in-scene performance (a letter being read aloud) — an intentional register change, not an inconsistency, though it is a sharp jump from the surrounding prose.

**Unexplained references.** Rumyantsev, Marya Fedorovna, and "the French" as named court parties (paragraph 1); "Petropol" as an epithet; the meaning of "the path to Warsaw" is withheld from the reader outright; "New Jerusalem" applied to Moscow; the David-and-Goliath allusion assumed familiar.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable: while the battle of Borodino is fought, Petersburg high society continues its gossip (centered on Countess Bezukhova's scandalous illness) and political theater, culminating in a stagey reading of an obsequious patriotic letter to the Tsar, with Anna Pavlovna predicting good news the next day.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None noted; register stays consistent with the period throughout.
