## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "a plan (again one of Pfuel's) had been devised to catch Napoleon in a strategic trap at the Berezina River." "Pfuel" is named with no identification, and "again" signals this refers back to something outside this chapter that isn't available to the reader here.

**Paragraph 2** — "unarmed soldiers, civilians from Moscow, and women with children who were traveling with the French—all carried forward by vis inertiæ—pressed forward into boats..." The Latin phrase is left untranslated and unglossed (it means roughly "the force of inertia"), which a reader with no Latin has no way to resolve from context alone.

**Paragraph 4** — "Certain destruction lay behind the French, but ahead lay hope. Their ships had been burned; there was no salvation except in collective flight..." This can be read two ways: as the idiom for committing to a course with no way back, or — since actual boats at the Berezina bridges were just described two paragraphs earlier — as a literal claim about boats destroyed during the crossing. Nothing in the sentence forces one reading over the other.

**Paragraph 5** — "Anticipation that the failure of the Petersburg Berezina plan would be attributed to Kutuzov led to growing dissatisfaction, contempt, and ridicule." The abstract nominalized subject ("Anticipation that X would be attributed to Y") takes a re-read to untangle who is anticipating what, and why it produces dissatisfaction, and in whom.

**Paragraph 12** — "In undress naval uniform, with a dirk, and holding his cap under his arm, he handed Kutuzov a garrison report..." "Undress uniform" is period military terminology for an everyday (non-formal) uniform; without that cue, a modern reader could easily misread it literally.

**Paragraph 22** — "This embrace too, because of a long-standing impression related to his innermost feelings, had its usual effect on Kutuzov, and he gave a sob." The phrase "a long-standing impression related to his innermost feelings" is vague enough that a reader can't tell what specific history or feeling is being pointed to, or why an embrace from the Emperor customarily moves Kutuzov to tears.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Mostly clear narrative prose — scene-setting, dialogue, and description of the Emperor's arrival read naturally and consistently in one voice. The harder passages are concentrated in the opening analytical paragraphs (1–5) rather than spread through the chapter; once the narrative turns to the Vilna arrival scene (paragraph 9 onward) it reads smoothly.

**Unexplained references.** Pfuel, Bennigsen, Wittgenstein, Chichagov's earlier postings (Greece, Warsaw), Borisov, Konovnitsyn, Count Tolstoy, Prince Volkonsky, and the Austerlitz reference are all named with no identification — a reader needs outside knowledge of the campaign's cast to place them.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable: the French army's destruction at the Berezina is put in perspective (less costly to them than Krasnoe), Kutuzov is increasingly undermined and mocked by his own staff and superiors even as he privately senses his role is ending, and the chapter closes with the Emperor's arrival at Vilna, where Kutuzov is coldly received in private but publicly given the Order of St. George.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom intrudes. The chapter shows rather than explains Kutuzov's fall from favor (the frost, the waiting officers, the Emperor's frown) — nothing is over-narrated.
