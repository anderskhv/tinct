## Obstacles

**Paragraph 5 — unclear**
> "For three days, separate portions of the French army—first Murat's (the Viceroy's), then Davout's, then Ney's—ran, as it were, the gauntlet of the Russian army."

This can be read two ways: either "the Viceroy" is a title being applied to Murat, or "(the Viceroy's)" names a separate corps that has been folded into the same list item by mistake. Nowhere else in the list is a commander given a parenthetical title, so it's not clear which corps is meant, or how many corps are actually being listed.

**Paragraph 6 — unclear**
> "Ney, who came last, had been busy blowing up the walls of Smolensk, which were in nobody's way—because despite their unfortunate plight, or because of it, the French wanted to punish the floor that had hurt them."

"Punish the floor that had hurt them" does not connect logically to blowing up a city's walls — a floor is not a wall, and nothing established beforehand explains what "the floor" refers to or how it "hurt" them. A reader cannot say with confidence what this image means.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** One sustained narrative voice built around the extended "blindman's buff" metaphor, which is picked up and returned to across the chapter. Reads as natural, continuous prose rather than choppy or visibly translated syntax.

**Unexplained references.** Niemen, Kaluga road, Smolensk road, Dnieper, Krasnoe, Orsha, Vilna, and Berezina all appear as bare place/road/river names with no orientation. Murat, Davout, and Ney are named as commanders with no stated rank or role (compounded by the paragraph-5 ambiguity above). "Supreme chief" near the end is unnamed but inferable from context as the army's top commander.

**Argument or story thread.** In two sentences: the chapter tracks the chaotic French retreat from Moscow to the Niemen using the blindman's-buff analogy, following how successive French corps were caught and mauled by the pursuing Russians near Krasnoe and further battered at the Berezina crossing. The thread holds together except at the two unclear points above, where it's momentarily hard to tell who is being described or what image is meant.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None found. No modern idiom jars, and the blindman's-buff metaphor is built up and explained rather than assumed.
