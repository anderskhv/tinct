## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "Yet in reality those personal interests of the moment so much transcend the general interests that they always prevent the public interest from being felt or even noticed."
A dense, abstract historical-philosophy claim, phrased with layered qualifiers, opening the chapter before any concrete example has been given. It takes a re-read to be sure what is being claimed.

**Paragraph 2** — "In historic events, the rule forbidding us to eat of the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge is especially applicable. Only unconscious action bears fruit, and the person who plays a part in a historic event never understands its significance. If he tries to understand it, his efforts are fruitless."
An extended Biblical metaphor (Eden's forbidden fruit) applied to a historical-philosophical claim, with its connection to the surrounding argument left for the reader to work out. Also mentions "Pierre's and Mamonov's regiments" with no context for who Pierre is or what those regiments were.

**Paragraph 3** — "...they thought about their next pay, their next quarters, about Matreshka the vivandière, and similar matters."
"Matreshka the vivandière" is a specific named figure dropped into an otherwise general statement, and "vivandière" (a period military-camp term) is never glossed, unlike similar foreign/period terms later in the chapter.

**Paragraph 19** — "...there were no men who could at all compete with the cavalier of St. George, the hussar remount officer, the good-natured and well-bred Count Rostov."
"Cavalier of St. George" (a military honor) is used with no gloss, unlike other foreign or period-specific terms elsewhere in the chapter (e.g. "ecossaise," "clavichord") which do get a parenthetical explanation. A reader may not register that this phrase is describing Nicholas's own decoration.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Opens in an abstract, essayistic register (paragraphs 1–3) arguing a historical-philosophy point, then shifts into brisk, warm narrative prose following Nicholas Rostov for the rest of the chapter. Both registers read as one consistent authorial voice; the gear change between them is real but not confusing once the reader is through it.

**Unexplained references.** Pierre and "Mamonov's regiments" (assumes earlier books' context); "vivandière"; "cavalier of St. George"; the Tree-of-Knowledge metaphor assumed familiar.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable: the narrator argues that ordinary people pursuing their own private interests, not self-conscious patriots, were the era's genuinely useful actors; the chapter then follows Nicholas Rostov, who avoids reflecting on the war and instead enjoys a horse-buying trip to Voronezh, where provincial society makes much of him. The opening philosophical argument (paragraphs 1–2) is the hardest part to state back in two sentences — it needs a re-read before the Rostov story proper begins.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** The idiom "in for a penny, in for a pound—who cares?" (paragraph 17) reads as a distinctly modern/English colloquialism sitting oddly in 1812 Russia — a mild tonal jar rather than a comprehension problem. Conversely, glosses like "(a Scottish dance)" and "(an early keyboard instrument)" (paragraph 16) are helpful but read as inserted dictionary notes rather than prose, and are applied inconsistently — other unfamiliar terms in the same chapter ("vivandière," "cavalier of St. George") get no such gloss.

Accessibility verdict: PASS
