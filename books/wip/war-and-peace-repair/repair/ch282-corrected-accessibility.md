## Obstacles

**Paragraph 2 — hard.**
> "...whether it would be better to put officer A in B's place and B in D's, or on the contrary to put D in A's place, and so on..."

The letters used are A, B, D — skipping C — with no explanation. A reader expecting a simple alphabetical placeholder sequence may read this as a typo or wonder what happened to "C," which is a small but real snag before the point (the arbitrariness of the reshuffling) lands.

**Paragraph 3 — hard.**
> "Among the officers, A was undermining B, D was undermining C, and so on in all possible combinations and permutations."

Same issue recurring: C reappears here after being skipped in the previous paragraph, so the letter scheme is inconsistent across the two paragraphs. Minor, but it asks the reader to shrug off an apparent inconsistency rather than resolve it.

**Paragraph 4 — hard.**
> "From General Wintzingerode's reports, I see that an enemy corps of ten thousand is moving on the Petersburg road. Another of several thousand moves on Dmitrov. A third has advanced along the Vladimir road, and a fourth, quite sizable detachment, is stationed between Ruza and Mozhaysk."

This is one long formal letter compressed into a single paragraph, listing five place names (Serpukhov, Tula, Petersburg road, Dmitrov, Vladimir road, Ruza, Mozhaysk) in quick succession with no geographic orientation. Followable as a document, but a reader has to work to keep the separate enemy detachments and roads distinct.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Consistent ironic narratorial voice across registers — plain narration, an inset formal Imperial letter, and two lines of spoken dialogue — all read as one authorial hand rather than a patchwork. Not choppy.

**Unexplained references.** Bagration, Barclay, Ermolov, Bennigsen, Wintzingerode, Shapovalov, Murat, plus a cluster of place names (Serpukhov, Tula, Dmitrov, Ruza, Mozhaysk, the Vladimir/Petersburg roads). None are glossed; a new reader needs outside knowledge of the personnel and geography of the 1812 campaign.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter shows a split command (Kutuzov in the field, the Emperor directing from a distance), petty infighting over staff appointments, an angry Imperial letter demanding an offensive, and a chance discovery by a Cossack scout that exposes Murat's flank — which forces the battle regardless of what Kutuzov wanted. That is recoverable as a two-sentence summary; the thread is not lost.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. The formal letter and proclamation-style prose stay in period register throughout; no modern idiom intrudes, and nothing is over-explained.
