## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "Helene understood that the question was very simple and easy from the Church's point of view, and that her spiritual directors were creating difficulties only because they feared how the secular authorities would react."
The chapter opens on "the question" with no antecedent. A new reader does not yet know what question is at stake (remarriage while her husband lives); it only becomes inferable two paragraphs later. Read in isolation, the opening sentence is a dangling reference.

**Paragraph 11** — "...if you marry the prince... you lose forever the chance of marrying the other... But if you marry the old count, you'll make his last days happy, and as widow of the Grand... the prince would no longer be making a mésalliance by marrying you."
"the Grand..." is cut off mid-title with no indication of what rank or title is meant, so the logic of the sentence (why widowhood from the old count would later let "the prince" marry her without a mésalliance) cannot be followed with confidence. "Mésalliance" is also left untranslated/unglossed.

**Paragraph 15** — "* Une maîtresse-femme!"
A standalone line of untranslated French preceded only by an asterisk, with no note explaining that it is a footnote reproducing, in the original language, a line already given in English in the paragraph above. Without that convention being established anywhere in the chapter, a reader has to guess why isolated French is appearing as its own paragraph.

**Paragraph 26** — "* Ah, Maman, ne dites pas de bêtises. Vous ne comprenez rien. Dans ma position j'ai des devoirs."
Same unexplained French-footnote pattern as paragraph 15.

**Paragraph 31** — "* Non, dites-lui que je ne veux pas le voir, que je suis furieuse contre lui, parce qu'il m'a manqué parole."
Same unexplained French-footnote pattern.

**Paragraph 33** — "* Comtesse, à tout péché miséricorde."
Same unexplained French-footnote pattern.

**Paragraph 37** — "And so I pray God to have you, my friend, in His holy and powerful keeping — Your friend Helene."
The previous paragraph describes, in indirect summary, the letter Helene sent. This paragraph then switches without warning or quotation marks into what reads like the letter's own closing line and signature. Nothing marks the shift from narration to quoted text, so a reader has to work out that this is the letter's sign-off rather than a new narrative statement.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as consistent, ironic literary prose throughout — one authorial voice, keeping its satirical distance from Helene and Petersburg society. Dialogue exchanges are brisk and natural.

**Unexplained references.** "The question" at the opening; Marya Dmitrievna Akhrosimova is introduced with no context for who she is or why her opinion matters; "His Highness," who never receives a name, arrives to end the mother-daughter scene; "N.N." stands in as a placeholder name for the suitor Helene intends to marry; the truncated "Grand..." title in paragraph 11; "mésalliance" is used without a gloss.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable: Helene manages her image and manipulates two suitors and society opinion to make a scandalous remarriage, while she is alive to her first husband, seem acceptable; her mother's religious objection is waved off; the chapter ends with her letter reaching Pierre's household while he is at Borodino.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. The repeated French-footnote fragments (paragraphs 15, 26, 31, 33) are a period-appropriate device (quoting the original beneath an English rendering) but are never signaled as such within the chapter, so they read as unexplained interruptions rather than anachronisms.
