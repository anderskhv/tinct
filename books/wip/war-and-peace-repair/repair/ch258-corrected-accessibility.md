## Obstacles

**Paragraph 4 — hard.**
> "There was so much good nature and nobility (in the French sense of the word) in the officer's voice..."

The parenthetical "(in the French sense of the word)" gestures at a cultural distinction — that French "nobility"/bonhomie means something narrower or different from the English sense — but gives no cue what that different sense actually is. A reader is left to guess what's being flagged.

**Paragraph 57 — unclear.**
> "The conflict of magnanimity between mother and daughter, ending in the mother sacrificing herself and offering her daughter in marriage to her lover, still moved the captain..."
> "...an episode in which the husband played the part of the lover and he—the lover—played the part of the husband..."

Both clauses have pronouns with no secure antecedent. In the first, "her lover" could belong to the mother or read as an odd match with "her daughter" — it isn't clear whose lover is marrying whom. In the second, the "husband"/"lover" role-swap is described so tersely ("the husband played the part of the lover and he—the lover—played the part of the husband") that I could not confidently reconstruct who did what. This is a genuine two-readings case, not just a hard sentence.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads naturally and holds two distinct voices well: Ramballe's garrulous, self-satisfied chatter versus Pierre's quieter, more reflective interior passages. It reads as one author's prose throughout, not a flattened modern paraphrase — the captain's speech patterns (interruptions, self-interrupted boasts, repeated exclamations) come through consistently.

**Unexplained references.** "The affair on the seventh of September" (Borodino, never named as such here); the battle names "Wagram," "Smolensk," "la Moskowa"; the Paris names "Talma, la Duchenois, Potier"; why Pierre saved the officer's life in the first place (assumed from an earlier chapter); "his best friend" whose betrayal of Pierre is mentioned but never identified by name; and "that bright comet which was connected in Pierre's heart with his love," which leans on an earlier scene not present in this chapter. None of these block comprehension of their sentences — they read as backstory a new reader simply doesn't have yet — except paragraph 57, addressed above.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable throughout: the French officer Ramballe, grateful to Pierre for saving his life, draws him into a long, wine-soaked dinner and conversation; over its course Pierre's secret resolve to assassinate Napoleon quietly collapses, and by the end, loosened by drink and goodwill, he ends up confessing the whole story of his life and his love for Natasha to a man who was a stranger hours earlier.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None noticed. No modern idiom jars, and nothing is over-explained.
