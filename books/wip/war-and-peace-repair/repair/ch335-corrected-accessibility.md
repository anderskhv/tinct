## Obstacles

**Paragraph 16 — hard.**
> "At breakfast Pierre told his cousin, the princess, that he had visited Princess Mary the day before and had met there—'Guess who? Natasha Rostova!'"

Two different women are called "princess" within one sentence: Pierre's unnamed cousin ("his cousin, the princess") and "Princess Mary." Nothing here flags them as separate people, so a reader has to work out that "the princess" who follows for the next several paragraphs is the cousin, not Princess Mary.

**Paragraph 19 — unclear.**
> "'I've seen the princess,' she replied. 'I heard they were arranging a match for the princess with young Rostov. It would be a very good thing for the Rostovs—they say the family's utterly ruined.'"

Pierre has just asked "Do you know her?" about Natasha. The cousin's reply talks about "the princess" being matched with "young Rostov" — which, given the paragraph-16 ambiguity above, could be misread as Natasha being matched with a Rostov (nonsensical, since she is a Rostov) or as a reference to a third, unnamed princess. Read on its own, I could not confidently say who "the princess" is here or whom she's being matched with.

**Paragraph 21 — hard.**
> "'I heard about that business of hers at the time. It was a great pity.'"

"That business of hers" is a vague pointer to some past scandal involving Natasha, with no content given — a new reader has no way to know what happened.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Natural, warm close-third-person prose throughout, consistent in tone. The fragmented interior monologue (Pierre's half-sentences to himself) reads as a deliberate device for his agitated state, not as choppiness — it stays legible.

**Unexplained references.** The reuse of "the princess" for two different characters (Pierre's cousin and Princess Mary) is the chapter's main friction point, discussed above; "that business of hers" (Natasha's past) is never specified; "freedom" offered to Savelich assumes the reader infers serfdom/manumission from context (workable, but uncued); "the Faceted Palace" and "vespers" are named without gloss but don't block the sentences they appear in.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable as a whole: a sleepless, elated Pierre resolves he wants to marry Natasha, postpones a planned trip to Petersburg, has a slightly crossed-wires conversation with his cousin about her, then visits Princess Mary, confesses his hopes, receives her encouragement to write to Natasha's parents, and shares a charged goodbye with Natasha before finally leaving for Petersburg. The only place the thread genuinely blurred was paragraph 19's "princess" confusion.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None noticed.
