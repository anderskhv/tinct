## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 — hard**
> "He had been firmly convinced of the truths revealed to him by his benefactor, Joseph Alexeevich, and happy in perfecting his inner self, to which he had devoted himself with real passion — all the meaning of that life vanished after Andrew's engagement and Joseph Alexeevich's death, the news of which reached him almost simultaneously."

The sentence sets up "He had been convinced... and happy..." as if building toward a predicate, then pivots after the dash to a new subject ("all the meaning of that life vanished") that isn't grammatically stitched to what came before. Requires a re-read to find the main clause.

> "he... avoided the Brothers"

"the Brothers" is dropped in with no gloss at first mention — a new reader can't yet tell this means the Freemasons (only becomes retroactively clear via "Masonic dinners" three paragraphs later).

**Paragraph 9 — hard**
> "a retired gentleman-in-waiting, fond of eating and drinking and of — as he unbuttoned his waistcoat — grumbling about the government"

The parenthetical action ("as he unbuttoned his waistcoat") is dropped into the middle of a two-item list ("fond of eating and drinking and of ... grumbling"), forcing the reader to hold "fond of ... and of" open across the interruption to find what it's paired with.

**Paragraph 10 — hard**
> "he was startled by the realization that how many others, like him, had entered this life and this club \"temporarily\""

"the realization that how many others... had entered" mixes two constructions ("the realization that X had entered" vs. "the realization of how many others had entered") into one ungrammatical hybrid, which trips the reader on first pass.

**Paragraph 11 — hard**
> "it seemed to him that he was quite different from and distinct from those other retired gentlemen-in-waiting he had formerly looked down on: they were empty, stupid, self-satisfied fellows, content with their lot, \"while I am still dissatisfied and want to do something for humanity.\""

The passage slides from third-person narration ("they were empty, stupid...") into a quoted first-person thought ("while I am still dissatisfied...") without any framing cue (no "he thought," no clear open-quote at the switch point). A reader has to double back to realize whose voice the quoted clause is in.

**Paragraph 13 — unclear**
> "Napoleon Bonaparte was despised by everyone as long as he was great, but now that he has become a pathetic comedian, Emperor Francis wants to offer him his daughter in an illegal marriage."

This reads as internally backwards: marriage offers from a fellow monarch are the kind of thing that accompanies power, not disgrace, so "despised... while great" paired with "offered a daughter... now that he's a pathetic comedian" doesn't parse as the irony the surrounding examples (Spaniards/French both claiming the same victory, Masons preaching charity while hoarding money) are clearly building toward. A reader can't tell with confidence whether this is intentional irony about the arbitrariness of court favor or a reversed/garbled statement of fact.

> "He had the unfortunate capacity... of seeing and believing in the possibility of goodness and truth, but of seeing the evil and falsehood of life too clearly to be able to take any serious part in it."

A secondary obstacle in the same paragraph: the parallel "of seeing X... but of seeing Y too clearly to be able to Z" construction is dense enough to require a second pass to see how the two halves connect to "the unfortunate capacity."

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** The chapter reads as one sustained authorial voice — reflective, ironic, willing to run a sentence long when tracing a thought — rather than as choppy modern prose. Most of the chapter moves cleanly (short scene-setting paragraphs, a clear chain-of-habit paragraph at #14), but a handful of sentences (1, 9, 10, 11, and the Napoleon line in 13) show seams: dashes doing the work of missing connectives, or a clause folded in mid-sentence, that a smoother modern rendering would resolve.

**Unexplained references.** "the Brothers" (¶1) for the Freemasons, unglossed at first use; "Joseph Alexeevich" named only as "benefactor" with no further identification; "Astraea Lodge" and "Manna Seekers" (¶13), given a partial gloss ("rival Masonic lodges") but the names themselves carry no meaning to a new reader; "forty times forty churches" (¶13), an idiom for Moscow's traditional church count, left unglossed; "Sivtsev Vrazhok" (¶2), a Moscow district name dropped in with no cue that it's a place.

**Argument or story thread.** Easy to state: after Andrew's engagement to Natasha and the death of his mentor Joseph Alexeevich, Pierre loses his sense of purpose and retreats into idle, self-indulgent life among Moscow high society, oscillating between self-contempt and self-justifying rationalization, and numbing his despair at the world's hypocrisy with books and wine. The thread holds throughout; it only strains briefly in the dense interior monologue of ¶13.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None found — diction stays period-appropriate (waistcoat, gentleman-in-waiting, club, Masonic dinners) and no modern idiom intrudes. If anything the chapter under-explains rather than over-explains (see references above); it never pauses to show what it could simply state.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
