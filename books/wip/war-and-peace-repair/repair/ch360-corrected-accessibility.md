## Obstacles

**Paragraph 4 — hard.**
> "When a man works alone, he always has a certain set of reflections which, as it seems to him, guided his past activity, justify his present activity, and steer his plans for the future. A group of people does exactly the same thing, letting those who don't take a direct part in the activity work out considerations, justifications, and guesses about their collective activity."

The second sentence's "does exactly the same thing" and the "letting..." clause require a re-read to work out that "those who don't take a direct part" are the group's stand-in for the lone man's private reflections.

**Paragraph 5 — hard.**
> "For reasons known or unknown to us, the French began drowning and killing one another. ... Men marched from west to east killing their fellow men, and the event was accompanied by phrases about the glory of France, the baseness of England, and so on. History shows us that these justifications make no sense at all and contradict one another—as when killing a man is the result of recognizing his rights..."

The events referred to (mass killing in France, a march "from west to east") are never named, so a reader without prior knowledge of the French Revolution and Napoleon's invasion of Russia cannot place what's being discussed. "Killing a man is the result of recognizing his rights" is also a compressed paradox left unexplained.

**Paragraph 14 — hard.**
> "But as soon as an event does happen, whatever it may be, some will always be found, out of all the continually expressed wishes of different people, which, by their meaning and their timing, are related as commands to the events."

The final clause is long and inverted; "some" (referring back to "wishes") is separated from its verb by a heavy parenthetical, forcing a re-read to see what the sentence is claiming.

**Paragraph 22 — hard.**
> "In the last analysis, we arrive at the circle of infinity—the final boundary that human reason, in every field of thought, reaches whenever it is not merely toying with its subject."

"Circle of infinity" is introduced as a load-bearing term but never defined — a reader can guess it means something like an endless loop of cause and effect, but the phrase itself is opaque on first encounter.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Consistent essayistic voice throughout, carried by two strong extended analogies (men hauling a log; a ship's bow wave) that keep the abstract argument grounded and readable. It builds cleanly to two crisp, numbered definitional answers at the end. Reads as natural sustained prose, not choppy or paraphrase-flat.

**Unexplained references.** Paragraph 5's historical allusions (mass killing in France, a west-to-east march, "humiliating England") are never named as specific events — a reader needs outside knowledge to recognize the Terror and Napoleon's 1812 campaign. "Circle of infinity" (paragraph 22) is used without a definition. Otherwise the chapter is largely self-contained, needing no outside names or places to follow the log and ship analogies.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that historical "commands" are matched to events after the fact rather than causing them (shown via the log-hauling and ship-wake analogies), then answers two questions directly: power is a relation in which the more a person is seen to direct, the less they actually do; and the movement of nations comes from the combined activity of everyone involved, not from power or ideas alone. It closes by comparing this to a scientific law (like electricity or atomic attraction) rather than a discoverable "cause." Two sentences suffice to summarize it, and the thread stays followable throughout.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** The locomotive-snowplow and electricity/atom analogies are period-appropriate for a mid-nineteenth-century argument and don't feel anachronistic. No modern idiom intrudes. If anything, paragraph 5 under-explains its historical allusions rather than over-explaining.

Accessibility verdict: PASS
