## Obstacles

**Paragraph 3** — "He has no convictions, no habits, no traditions, not even a name, and not even a Frenchman." Taken literally, "not even a name" is confusing — the man obviously has a name — and it isn't clear on first read whether this means he's unknown/obscure or that the text is deliberately withholding his name as a device (which later paragraphs bear out, but nothing here signals that).

**Paragraph 4** — "this man's dazzling, self-assured limitations raise him to the head of the army." "Dazzling limitations" is a paradoxical pairing that stops the eye — limitations described as dazzling reads as a contradiction until re-read as ironic. The paragraph also stacks unexplained biography (refused a place in the Russian service, a post in Turkey, a near-destruction in Italy) with no cues for a reader unfamiliar with the events.

**Paragraph 11** — "Chance shapes the character of Paul I of Russia... Chance puts the royalist Duc d'Enghien in his hands... but instead unexpectedly falls upon the Austrian general Mack and the Austrians." Three named figures (Paul I, the Duc d'Enghien, General Mack) arrive in one long sentence with no roles or context beyond their bare names, on top of an already long "chance... chance... chance" structure — a reader has to hold a lot open at once.

**Paragraph 12** — "The Emperor of Austria counts it a favor that this man receives a daughter of the Caesars into his bed." This phrase has no cue at all for what it refers to (a Habsburg marriage tied to claims of Roman imperial descent); read cold, "a daughter of the Caesars" is opaque rather than merely unfamiliar. "The most suitable celebration the Germans can think up in his honor is a commemoration of the battles of Jena and Auerstädt" carries a buried irony (Jena and Auerstädt were German defeats) that isn't recoverable without outside knowledge.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as one sustained narrative-essayistic voice — long cumulative sentences building an ironic portrait of an unnamed "he" through relentless repetition of "chance." Natural prose, not choppy; the density comes from stacked clauses and named figures rather than translated syntax.

**Unexplained references.** The chapter's central figure (clearly Napoleon) is never named — a deliberate device once recognized, but disorienting at first. Malta, the Africa expedition, Paul I of Russia, the Duc d'Enghien, General Mack, Austerlitz, Wagram, Borodino, Jena, and Auerstädt are all named with no explanation; a reader without prior European-history knowledge would need to look most of these up.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter narrates, ironically, how an anonymous, plan-less man is carried by a relentless string of "chances" from obscurity through Italy and Egypt to seizing power in France, then through a decade of conquest to Moscow, and finally into a reversal of fortune that exiles him to an island. In two sentences: the chapter traces how "chance" after "chance" hands this man ever-greater power, with everyone around him dressing up his crimes as greatness. It ends with the same chance abruptly reversing after Moscow, stripping him of everything except, mysteriously, his freedom and an island kingdom.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom appears. The repeated withholding of the man's name is a deliberate rhetorical device (showing rather than naming), consistent with the chapter's ironic tone rather than an accessibility failure by itself — but it does compound the difficulty of paragraphs 3–4 for a reader with no prior context.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
