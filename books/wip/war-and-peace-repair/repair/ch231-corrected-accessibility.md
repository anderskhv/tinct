## Obstacles

**Paragraph 6 — hard.** "It was impossible not to retreat one day's march, and then in the same way it was impossible not to retreat another day's march, and a third..." The double-negative construction ("impossible not to retreat") requires a re-read to confirm it means retreat was unavoidable, not that retreat was somehow blocked. The paragraph also compares the French army's advancing momentum to growth "in inverse proportion to the square of the distance from its goal" — a physics-style figure whose exact sense (momentum builds as the goal nears) takes a moment to extract from the technical phrasing.

**Paragraph 9 — hard.** "...on the twenty-eighth it is suggested to him that he cross to the Kaluga road..." — the date is given with no month attached; a reader has to infer from an earlier paragraph (which names August 26) that this is late August, and even then it isn't stated outright. The same paragraph also introduces "a letter from the sovereign," where the rest of the chapter has used "the Emperor" (paragraph 4) for the same figure — the shift in designation gives a moment's doubt about whether the same person is meant.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Flowing narrative-essayistic prose that mixes historical narration with physics-derived metaphors (a falling body's increasing speed, a recoiling billiard ball, inverse-square growth). One consistent voice throughout — not choppy, not flattened into paraphrase — even where individual sentences run long.

**Unexplained references.** Place names (the Kaluga road, Malo-Yaroslavets, the Berezina, Vilna, Fili, the Drissa camp, the Shevardino Redoubt) and figures (Kutuzov, Miloradovich, "the Emperor"/"the sovereign") are used without introduction. A reader unfamiliar with the 1812 campaign would need outside knowledge to place them precisely, though the narrative sense — a long, forced retreat, and a commander besieged by conflicting demands — still comes through without it.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter narrates the French army's advance to Moscow and its subsequent retreat as a matter of physical momentum outside any one man's control, then turns to defend Kutuzov against armchair critics by describing the flood of simultaneous, contradictory demands any commander in chief actually faces. In two sentences: it traces the campaign from Smolensk through Borodino to the occupation and abandonment of Moscow, presenting the retreat as inevitable rather than chosen; it then argues that a commander's real situation — bombarded moment to moment by conflicting reports and requests — makes retrospective "why didn't he just..." criticism unfair.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. The physics analogies (falling body, billiard-ball recoil, inverse-square growth) fit the essayistic register and are used as illustration rather than belabored explanation.
