## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 — hard.** "If history dealt only with external phenomena, establishing this simple, obvious law would be enough, and we could consider the argument finished." The referent of "this simple, obvious law" is never stated in this chapter — it depends on argument from an earlier, unseen chapter, so a reader starting here has nothing to attach it to.

**Paragraph 18 — hard.** "However often experiment and reasoning may show a man that he will do the same thing as before, under the same conditions and with the same character, yet when he approaches, under those same conditions and with that same character, an action that always ends the same way — even for the thousandth time — he feels just as certain as he did before the experiment that he can act however he pleases." The near-duplicate clause ("under the same conditions and with the same character" / "under those same conditions and with that same character") is repeated with a long gap between subject and main verb, forcing a re-read to find where the sentence actually lands.

**Paragraph 23 — unclear.** "Man is the creation of an all-powerful, all-good, all-seeing God. What is sin — the idea that arises from man's consciousness of his own freedom? That is a question for theology." Stated flatly, with no "if" or other conditional marker, this reads as the author asserting theism as fact — which sits oddly against the surrounding argument's neutral, analytical stance. Only in hindsight (after paragraphs 24–26 repeat the same flat-premise-then-question pattern for jurisprudence, ethics, and history) does it become likely that these are meant as parallel illustrations rather than sequential claims. On first encounter the paragraph supports both readings.

**Paragraph 24 — hard.** "The actions of men are subject to general, unchanging laws expressed in statistics. What is man's responsibility to society — the idea that follows from the idea of freedom? That is a question for jurisprudence." Same unmarked premise-then-question structure as paragraph 23; the connective logic between the first sentence and the question has to be inferred.

**Paragraph 25 — hard.** "A man's actions arise from his innate character and the motives acting on him. What is conscience, and the perception of right and wrong in actions, which follows from the consciousness of freedom? That is a question for ethics." Same structural gap as 23–24.

**Paragraph 26 — hard.** "Man, seen in connection with the general life of humanity, appears subject to the laws that determine that life. But the same man, apart from that connection, appears to be free." Continues the same series; readable once the pattern is recognized, but still requires supplying the missing "if" that would make the logic explicit.

**Paragraph 27 — hard.** "Only in our own self-confident age of popularized knowledge — thanks to that most powerful engine of ignorance, the diffusion of printed matter — has the question of free will been put on a level on which the question itself cannot exist." The final clause ("put on a level on which the question itself cannot exist") is compressed enough that its meaning — the question has been trivialized into nonexistence — takes a second pass to recover.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Formal philosophical-essay register throughout, reading as one consistent authorial voice working through an argument rather than as choppy fragments or a flattened paraphrase. Many sentences are long and clause-heavy by nature of the argument, but the prose is not translation-stiff.

**Unexplained references.** "This simple, obvious law" (para 1) depends on material outside the chapter. The four-paragraph theology/jurisprudence/ethics/history sequence (23–26) assumes the reader will recognize an unmarked parallel-illustration device without a signal word introducing it.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that the free will of the individual and the law of necessity governing history are both undeniable from their own vantage points — reason finds man subject to law, consciousness finds him free — and that this contradiction underlies every field that studies human beings. It closes by attacking contemporary popularizers who mistake partial, physiological/evolutionary findings for a full resolution, comparing them to plasterers who paper over unfinished work.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. No modern idiom intrudes, and the prose does not over-explain what it could show.
