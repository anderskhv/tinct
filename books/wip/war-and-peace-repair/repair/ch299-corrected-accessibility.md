## Obstacles

**Paragraph 2 — hard**
> "All historians agree that the external activity of states and nations in their conflicts with one another is expressed through wars, and that military success or failure directly causes the political strength of states and nations to rise or fall."

The clause "the external activity of states and nations in their conflicts with one another is expressed through wars" is abstract and has to be unpacked before the sentence's claim (wars are how national power struggles play out) becomes clear. Followable, but takes a re-read.

**Paragraph 9 — hard**
> "The victory that was won brought none of the usual results, because two peasants, Karp and Vlas—who after the French evacuated Moscow drove their carts in to loot the city, and who in general personally displayed no heroic feelings—along with the innumerable multitude of similar peasants, did not bring their hay to Moscow for the high prices offered them."

A long parenthetical about Karp and Vlas's looting is inserted between the subject ("two peasants... along with the innumerable multitude of similar peasants") and its verb ("did not bring"), forcing a re-read to reconnect subject and predicate.

**Paragraph 13 — hard**
> "Some highly placed Russians felt it was rather disgraceful to fight with a cudgel; they wanted to assume the fencing positions—en quarte or en tierce, according to all the rules—and deliver an adroit thrust en prime."

The French fencing terms ("en quarte," "en tierce," "en prime") are given no gloss beyond "fencing positions... according to all the rules." A reader can guess they are technical terms for stances/thrusts but not what they specifically mean. "Kutuzov" is also introduced by name only, with no role given.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as one continuous, consistent essayistic voice — an argumentative historical digression with varied sentence lengths, some quite long and clause-stacked, but never choppy or mechanically "translated." It sounds like a single author working through an argument, not a paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** "Jena and Auerstadt" (battles, named without context), "Kutuzov" and "Emperor Alexander" (named without role/context), "Smolensk" and "Borodino" (place names assumed familiar), the fencing terms "en quarte / en tierce / en prime," and the date "1813." None of these block the sentence-level meaning, but a new reader would need to look them up to place them historically.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that the normal historical "rule" — that a winning army's nation gains power and a losing army's nation submits — breaks down in the 1812 campaign, because after Borodino and the fall of Moscow the French army was destroyed without further set-piece battles. It illustrates this with an extended duel metaphor: the French fought "by the rules" (rapier), the Russian people abandoned the rules (cudgel), and historians who insist on describing it by the rules of formal warfare misrepresent what happened. The thread is continuous and never lost.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None found. No modern idiom jars, and the extended duel/cudgel metaphor is deliberately built up and explained rather than left implicit — appropriate to the essay's argumentative purpose, not padding.
