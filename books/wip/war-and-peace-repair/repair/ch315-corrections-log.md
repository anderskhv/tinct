# Chapter 315 — corrections log

## ¶0
Before: "The movements of the Russian and French armies during the retreat from Moscow to the Niemen were like a game of Russian blindman's buff, where two blindfolded players stumble about and one occasionally rings a little bell to let the catcher know where he is. At first, the one with the bell rings it fearlessly, but when he gets cornered, he runs away as quietly as he can—and often, thinking he's escaping, runs straight into his opponent's arms."
After: "The movements of the Russian and French armies during the campaign from Moscow back to the Niemen were like a game of Russian blindman's buff, in which two players are blindfolded and one of them occasionally rings a little bell to let the catcher know where he is. At first, the one with the bell rings it fearlessly, but when he gets cornered, he runs away as quietly as he can—and often, thinking he's escaping, runs straight into his opponent's arms."
Findings: MINOR ("retreat" applies only to the French, but the sentence covers both armies; "back" dropped — restored "campaign... back"); MINOR ("stumble about" is an added action the source does not describe — removed).

## ¶2
Before: "Because of the speed of the French flight and the Russian pursuit, and the resulting exhaustion of the horses, the main means of determining the enemy's position—cavalry scouting—was unavailable. Besides, the frequent and rapid changes of position by both armies meant that even information received couldn't be delivered in time. If news came one day that the enemy had been at a certain position the day before, by the third day when anything could have been done, that army was already two days' march farther on and in a completely different spot."
After: "Because of the speed of the French flight and the Russian pursuit, and the resulting exhaustion of the horses, the main means of approximately determining the enemy's position—cavalry scouting—was unavailable. Besides, the frequent and rapid changes of position by both armies meant that even information received couldn't be delivered in time. If news came one day that the enemy had been at a certain position the day before, by the third day when anything could have been done, that army was already two days' march farther on and in quite another position."
Findings: MINOR (hedge "approximately" dropped — restored; cavalry scouting never gave more than an approximation, the paragraph's premise); MINOR ("position", the paragraph's running term, broken by "spot" — restored).

## ¶3
Before: "One army fled and the other pursued. Beyond Smolensk there were several different roads available to the French, and one would have thought that during their four-day halt there they might have learned where the enemy was, devised a better plan, and tried something new. But after a four-day rest, the mob—with no strategy or plans—simply started running again along the beaten track, neither turning right nor left but taking the old, worst road through Krasnoe and Orsha."
After: "One army fled and the other pursued. Beyond Smolensk there were several different roads available to the French, and one would have thought that during their four-day halt there they might have learned where the enemy was, devised a better plan, and tried something new. But after a four-day halt, the mob—with no maneuvers or plans—started running again along the beaten track, neither turning right nor left but taking the old, worst road through Krasnoe and Orsha."
Findings: MINOR ("rest" implies recuperation the source does not claim, having just said they did not use the time — restored "halt"; concrete "maneuvers" abstracted to "strategy" — restored); added intensifier "simply" removed.

## ¶4
Before: "Expecting the enemy from behind rather than ahead, the French spread out over a distance of twenty-four hours' march. At the very front fled the Emperor, then the kings, then the dukes. The Russian army, expecting Napoleon to take the road to the right beyond the Dnieper—which was the only sensible thing for him to do—turned right themselves and came out on the highway at Krasnoe. And here, as in a game of blindman's buff, the French ran into the Russian vanguard. Spotting their enemy unexpectedly, the French fell into confusion and froze with sudden fright, then resumed their flight, abandoning comrades who were farther behind. For three days, separate portions of the French army—first Murat's (the Viceroy's) corps, then Davout's, then Ney's—ran, as it were, the gauntlet of the Russian army. They abandoned one another, abandoned all their heavy baggage, artillery, and half their men, fleeing past the Russians at night by making wide detours to the right."
After: "Expecting the enemy from behind rather than ahead, the French separated in their flight and spread out over a distance of twenty-four hours' march. At the very front fled the Emperor, then the kings, then the dukes. The Russian army, expecting Napoleon to take the road to the right beyond the Dnieper—which was the only sensible thing for him to do—turned right themselves and came out on the highway at Krasnoe. And here, as in a game of blindman's buff, the French ran into our vanguard. Spotting their enemy unexpectedly, the French fell into confusion and froze with sudden fright, then resumed their flight, abandoning comrades who were farther behind. For three days, separate portions of the French army—first Murat's (the Viceroy's), then Davout's, then Ney's—ran, as it were, the gauntlet of the Russian army. They abandoned one another, abandoned all their heavy baggage, artillery, and half their men, fleeing past the Russians at night by making semicircles to the right."
Findings: MINOR ("separated in their flight" dropped, reducing two facts to one — restored); MINOR (added "corps" makes the candidate more definite than the source at the point Tolstoy calls this "the mob, with no maneuvers or plans" — cut per editor ruling); MINOR (narrator's "our vanguard" neutralised to "the Russian vanguard" — restored); MINOR (precise "semicircles" replaced by vague "wide detours" — restored).

## ¶5
Before: "Ney, who came last, had been busy blowing up the walls of Smolensk, which were in nobody's way—because despite their desperate plight, or perhaps because of it, the French wanted to punish the floor that had hurt them, the way a child who has fallen strikes the ground for tripping it. Ney, who had started with a corps of ten thousand men, reached Napoleon at Orsha with only a thousand left, having abandoned all the rest along with his cannon, and having crossed the Dnieper by stealth at night at a wooded spot."
After: "Ney, who came last, had been busy blowing up the walls of Smolensk, which were in nobody's way—because despite their unfortunate plight, or because of it, the French wanted to punish the floor that had hurt them. Ney, who had started with a corps of ten thousand men, reached Napoleon at Orsha with only a thousand left, having abandoned all the rest and all his cannon, and having crossed the Dnieper by stealth at night at a wooded spot."
Findings: MAJOR (added child simile — the source states the floor image bare and leaves the child unstated — removed entirely per editor ruling); the agent ("the floor that had hurt them") kept exactly as the editor ruled, not changed to a reflexive; MINOR ("unfortunate" sharpened to "desperate", hedge "perhaps" added to an alternative the source states flatly — restored); MINOR (second "all" dropped from "all his cannon" — restored).

## ¶6
Before: "From Orsha they fled farther along the road to Vilna, still playing blindman's buff with the pursuing army. At the Berezina they fell into disarray again—many drowned, many surrendered—but those who got across the river fled on. Their supreme commander put on a fur coat and, climbing into a sleigh, galloped off alone, abandoning his companions. The others who could manage it drove away too, leaving those who could not to surrender or die."
After: "From Orsha they fled farther along the road to Vilna, still playing blindman's buff with the pursuing army. At the Berezina they fell into disarray again—many drowned, many surrendered—but those who got across the river fled on. Their supreme chief put on a fur coat and, climbing into a sleigh, galloped off alone, abandoning his companions.
