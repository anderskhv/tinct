## Obstacles

**Paragraph 7 — hard.**
> "But to achieve this, you must add your own efforts: forget, if possible, the misfortunes you've suffered; should entertain the hope of a less cruel fate; be certain that inevitable, shameful death awaits anyone who threatens your persons or your remaining property..."

This is a list of instructions that starts as bare imperatives ("forget," "be certain") but the second item switches to "should entertain," breaking the parallel grammar. The reader has to stop and reparse whether "should entertain" is still governed by "you must" or is a separate clause.

Also in the same paragraph:
> "Its members will be marked by a red ribbon worn across the shoulder, and the mayor will also wear a white belt. But when not on duty, they will wear only a red ribbon on the left arm."

It's unclear whether "they" in the second sentence refers to all municipal members, the mayor, or both, and how the on-duty/off-duty insignia relate to each other — followable but requires re-reading to reconcile.

**Paragraph 14 — hard.**
> "(3) Sundays and Wednesdays are the main market days, with sufficient troops stationed along the highways on Tuesdays and Saturdays at such distances from the town as to protect the carts."

Troops are posted on the days before the market days, presumably to escort travelers heading in, but that connection is never stated — the reader has to infer why the protection schedule doesn't match the market schedule.

**Paragraph 17 — hard.**
> "Then, as Thiers eloquently recounts, he ordered his soldiers to be paid in forged Russian currency he had prepared: 'Raising the use of these means by an act worthy of himself and of the French army, he let relief be distributed to those who had been burned out. ... Napoleon preferred to supply them with money to buy food from outside, and had paper rubles distributed to them.'"

The passage opens on soldiers being paid in forged currency, then the quoted material shifts to relief for Moscow's fire victims, without a clear transition marking the change of subject. A reader has to re-read to work out that the forged rubles are now being given to civilians, not soldiers.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** The chapter alternates flat catalogue-style narration ("With regard to X...") with inset official proclamations in a stiff, formal register, and closes with an ironic authorial aside quoting the historian Thiers. The shifts are deliberate and consistent — it reads as one satirical voice cataloguing Napoleon's performative administration, not as a flattened paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** Sebastiani, Murat, Yakovlev, Rostopchin (identified as Moscow's governor), Tutolmin (never identified beyond "that old man," though paragraph 17 implies he runs the Foundling Hospital), Thiers, Mokhovaya Street, the Provision Market. A new reader would need outside knowledge to place several of these figures precisely.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter catalogues, category by category (military, diplomatic, legal, administrative, supply, religious, commercial, philanthropic, disciplinary), the orders Napoleon issued while occupying Moscow — presented as evidence of grand administration but undercut by the narrator's asides (paying with forged currency, ordering a "scoundrel's" houses burned). That two-sentence summary holds; the thread is not lost, though the sheer number of proclamations and categories makes the chapter feel list-like rather than narrative.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. The bureaucratic/proclamation register throughout is period-consistent; no modern idiom intrudes. The footnote glossing "à la maraude" ("As looters.") is appropriately brief rather than over-explaining.
