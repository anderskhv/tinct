## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 (hard).** "Millions of men committed against one another such innumerable crimes, frauds, betrayals, thefts, forgeries, counterfeiting, burglaries, arson, and murders as in whole centuries are not recorded in the annals of all the law courts of the world, but which those who committed them did not at the time regard as crimes." One long catalog sentence stacked onto the opening scene-setting sentence; the meaning holds but it takes real effort to hold the whole list in mind before the final clause lands.

**Paragraph 4 (hard).** "It seemed to the generals and old soldiers that the main reason for the war was the necessity of giving them employment; to the legitimists of that day, that it was the need of restoring les bons principes (good principles). And it seemed to the diplomats that it all resulted from the fact that the alliance between Russia and Austria in 1809 had not been sufficiently well concealed from Napoleon, and from the clumsy wording of Memorandum No. 178..." A long semicolon-chained list of seven or eight different groups' opinions, each requiring a small mental reset; followable only with careful re-reading.

**Paragraph 5 (hard).** "...each separate cause or whole series of causes appears to us equally valid in itself and equally false in its insignificance compared to the magnitude of the events, and in its powerlessness — apart from the cooperation of all the other coinciding causes — to produce the event." The double "equally valid... equally false" construction plus a parenthetical aside in the middle of the clause makes this sentence's logic hard to hold on a first pass.

**Paragraph 12 (hard).** "\"The king's heart is in the hands of the Lord.\"" Presented as a bare, unattributed quotation (it is Proverbs 21:1) with no signal that it is scripture or why it is being invoked here. A reader who doesn't recognize the source is left to guess whether this is the author's own aphorism or a citation.

**Paragraph 15 (unclear).** "...as Alexander expressed it in the last letter he wrote him — he had never been so much in the grip of inevitable laws..." The pronouns "he" and "him" have no clear antecedents — it's not stated whether Alexander wrote to Napoleon or the reverse, so a reader can't tell whose letter is being quoted. The parenthetical "(in French)" dropped mid-clause right after "peoples" is also disconnected from anything it's modifying on first read; only the following footnote paragraph clarifies (retroactively) that it flags the quoted phrase's original language.

**Paragraph 16 (hard).** "* Verser (ou ne pas verser) le sang de ses peuples." A French footnote left untranslated and presented as its own standalone paragraph. The English sense was given earlier, in-line, in paragraph 15 ("shed (or not to shed) the blood of his peoples"), but this footnote is disconnected from that translation and offers a reader nothing on its own.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as sustained, consistent essayistic argument — long cumulative sentences, rhetorical questions, and anaphora ("Nor could there have been a war... Nor could there have been a war..."). It sounds like one authorial voice throughout, not a flattened modern paraphrase, though the density of the sentences (especially paragraphs 1, 4, 5) asks a lot of a first-time reader.

**Unexplained references.** Duke of Oldenburg and the Continental System are helpfully glossed in-line. Metternich, Rumyantsev, and Talleyrand are not glossed, though they function as interchangeable examples of "a diplomat," so the argument survives without knowing them individually. The Vistula (river) and Dresden are mentioned with no geographic context. The Proverbs quotation in paragraph 12 is the chapter's one significant unglossed reference that actually carries argumentative weight.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that no single cause — diplomatic slights, Napoleon's ambition, Alexander's firmness — explains the 1812 war; instead millions of individual choices coincided by necessity, and even the two emperors were not free agents but instruments of "history," illustrated by the closing apple-falling metaphor. The thread holds together end to end.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom intrudes. Glossing is inconsistent rather than over-explained: some period terms (Continental System, Duke of Oldenburg) get parenthetical help, others (Metternich, Talleyrand, the Proverbs quotation) get none.
