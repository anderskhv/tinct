## Obstacles

**Paragraph 3** — "General events involuntarily group themselves around some particular incident. So now the courtiers' pleasure was based as much on the fact that the news arrived on the Emperor's birthday as on the fact of the victory itself... Mention was made in Kutuzov's report of the Russian losses, among which figured the names of Tuchkov, Bagration, and Kutaysov."
An abstract generalization is stated first and only connected to the concrete example a sentence later, requiring a re-read to see how the claim and the example fit together. The three names are listed with no indication of who they are or why their inclusion matters, beyond what follows about Kutaysov.

**Paragraph 7** — "...in intimate circles details were mentioned of how the Queen of Spain's private physician had prescribed small doses of a certain drug to produce a certain effect; but Helene, tormented by the fact that the old count suspected her... had suddenly taken a very large dose of the drug and had died in agony..."
"A certain drug to produce a certain effect" is never specified. It's impossible to say with confidence whether this refers to a medical treatment, an abortifacient, or something else, or whether her fatal overdose was accidental or deliberate — the passage supports more than one reading. "The old count" is also never identified within this chapter.

**Paragraph 9** — "This was terrible! What a position for the Emperor to be in! Kutuzov was a traitor..."
This shifts from narration into what appears to be a paraphrase of public/society opinion (free indirect discourse) with no quotation marks or attribution, so it's not clear whether "Kutuzov was a traitor" is the narrator's own claim or a rendering of what gossiping Petersburg was saying — especially since the same sentence goes on to describe Prince Vasili's opinion ironically.

**Paragraph 10** — "\"I only wonder that the fate of Russia could have been entrusted to such a man.\""
An unattributed quoted line immediately following a description of Prince Vasili's remarks. It reads as if it could be Prince Vasili speaking or a further generic quotation from "they" (society), and the paragraph gives no speaker tag to resolve it.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Mostly natural, plain narration; two passages (paragraph 3's essayistic aside, paragraph 9's unmarked free indirect discourse) blur narrator and crowd/opinion in ways that ask more of the reader. The two quoted official letters (paragraphs 12 and 14) are appropriately formal and easy to follow as letters.

**Unexplained references.** Tuchkov, Bagration, and Kutaysov are named as casualties with no indication of who they were or why they mattered (beyond Kutaysov, whose importance the following paragraphs make clear); "the Queen of Spain's private physician," introduced with no context if this chapter is read alone; "the old count," never identified here; "Prince Michael Ilarionovich" in the Emperor's letter is Kutuzov by his name and patronymic, but the chapter never says so directly.

**Argument or story thread.** Mostly followable: Petersburg's mood swings from celebration over apparent victory news, to anxiety at the ensuing silence, to grief at Helene's sudden death, to alarm at Moscow's fall — closing with Rostopchin's letter and the Tsar's stern reply to Kutuzov. The one place the thread genuinely breaks down is the cause of Helene's death in paragraph 7, which stays deliberately murky.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None; register is consistent with the period throughout.
