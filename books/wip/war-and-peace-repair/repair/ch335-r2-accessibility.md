## Hard paragraphs — quoted obstacles

**Paragraph 13:** "He has no idea how frightening it is, how dangerous. Too soon, or too late... it's terrible!" — The elliptical fragment "Too soon, or too late" has no stated referent (too soon or too late for what?). A new reader can guess it relates to the timing of marriage/remarriage but has to pause and infer rather than follow directly.

**Paragraph 19:** "I've seen Princess Mary," she replied. "I heard they were arranging a match for her with young Rostov." — Pierre had just asked "Do you know her?" about Natasha, but the princess answers about a different person and a different match. It is unclear on first read whether "her" refers to Natasha or to Princess Mary, and "young Rostov" is not identified (a new reader could easily conflate him with the Rostov family Natasha belongs to). The paragraph is followable but requires re-reading to sort out who is being discussed.

**Paragraph 21:** "I heard about that business of hers at the time. It was a great pity." — "That business of hers" refers to some earlier scandal involving Natasha, but nothing in the chapter says what it was. A new reader understands only that something negative happened, not what.

**Paragraph 25:** "inviting him to send someone to the Faceted Palace to collect property that was to be returned to its owners that day." — "The Faceted Palace" is named with no explanation of what or where it is, or why looted property would be stored there. A reader unfamiliar with 1812 Moscow has no cue to place it.

**Paragraph 29:** "he felt her presence with his whole being, in the loss of his sense of freedom." — The phrase "in the loss of his sense of freedom" is compressed and abstract; it takes a moment to work out that it means being overwhelmed/captivated by her presence rather than literally losing freedom.

**Paragraph 31:** "but Princess Mary was going to vespers, so Pierre left the house along with her." — "Vespers" is used without any explanatory cue (an evening church service). A reader unfamiliar with the term has no way to infer it from context.

**Paragraph 42:** "because she had seen from the sudden change in Natasha two days earlier that far from being hurt if Pierre spoke of his love, it was exactly what she wanted." — "The sudden change in Natasha two days earlier" refers to an event not shown in this chapter. The reader is told a change happened and what it implies, but not what it was.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as natural, flowing prose with a consistent introspective voice — long sentences carrying Pierre's tumbling internal monologue alternate with short, clipped dialogue exchanges (especially with Savelich), which reads as an intentional contrast rather than choppiness. Narration slides into free indirect thought (character reflections embedded in the narrative voice) fluidly; this is a stylistic choice, not a translation artifact, and it holds together as one voice throughout.

**Unexplained references.** "Young Rostov" (¶19), "that business of hers" regarding Natasha (¶21), "the Faceted Palace" (¶25), "vespers" (¶31), and "the sudden change in Natasha two days earlier" (¶42) all assume knowledge from outside this chapter — prior chapters' events, Moscow geography, or period religious practice — with no in-text cue.

**Argument or story thread.** Pierre, now certain of his love for Natasha, cancels his trip to Petersburg and spends his days at Princess Mary's house to be near her; he confides in Princess Mary, who promises to prepare Natasha and encourages him to hope. The chapter closes with Pierre finally leaving for Petersburg after all, buoyed by joyful disbelief and a parting word from Natasha. The thread is easy to follow start to finish.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idioms or jarring vocabulary. The prose generally shows rather than explains — for instance the Savelich exchange lets the reader infer the master/serf relationship from dialogue rather than stating it outright, which works well; the handful of unexplained proper nouns/terms above are the exception rather than a pattern of over-compression.
