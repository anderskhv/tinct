## Obstacles

**Paragraph 6** — "I don't know why some particular event happens; I think I can't know it; so I do not try to know it, and I call it chance." The three-step chain ("don't know" / "think I can't know" / "do not try to know") repeats the same idea with small shifts that require a re-read to see they're building one definition rather than three separate claims.

**Paragraph 7** — "that this particular ram—the one that, instead of going into the general fold every evening, goes into a special pen full of oats—that this very ram, now bursting with fat, ends up slaughtered for meat." The doubled "that this... that this..." construction, with a long parenthetical dropped in the middle, forces a re-read to find where the main clause resumes, even though the ram allegory itself is vivid and easy to picture.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** One consistent, confident essayistic voice, built around the extended ram-and-shepherd allegory. Reads as natural argumentative prose rather than choppy fragments; the two hard spots are syntax knots rather than tone breaks.

**Unexplained references.** Napoleon and Alexander are named without introduction, and "the European convulsions" / "movements from west to east and east to west" are referred to as if already familiar. The allegory carries the core argument well enough that these references don't block the chapter's meaning.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that "chance" and "genius" are not real explanatory forces but labels historians reach for when they don't understand a cause, illustrated by a ram that looks blessed by fortune right up until it's fattened for slaughter. It concludes that once we give up demanding an immediately graspable purpose, Napoleon and Alexander stop looking exceptional and instead look like men perfectly, inevitably fitted to a process beyond human understanding.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. No modern idiom, and the allegory is left to do its own work rather than being over-glossed.
