## Obstacles

**Paragraph 6** — "'...How like he is growing, Mary!' he added, turning to Countess Mary." Taken on its own this line is not natural English word order and its meaning ("how much he's coming to resemble [someone]") only resolves retroactively once the next paragraph's "Like my father?" supplies the missing referent — until then the sentence is genuinely puzzling.

**Paragraph 10** — "'One used to have to be German—now one has to dance with Tatáwinova and Madame Kwüdener, and wead Ecka'tshausen and the bwethwen. Oh, they should let that fine fellow Bonaparte loose... Fancy giving command of the Semenov wegiment to a fellow like that Schwa'tz!' he cried." This is the first appearance of a phonetic r→w spelling representing a speech impediment, applied here to a string of unfamiliar Russian/German proper nouns. Without the convention established yet, "wead" is genuinely ambiguous — it could be misread as another proper name (parallel to "Tatáwinova," "Kwüdener") rather than the verb "read," and the garbled names ("Ecka'tshausen," "Schwa'tz") cannot be confidently identified at all. Two different readings of the sentence are possible on first encounter.

**Paragraph 11** — "...it seemed to him a matter of real importance that A had been appointed Minister of This, and B Governor General of That..." "A" and "B" read as placeholder letters rather than people; it is not evident on first reading whether this is a deliberate device (mocking the interchangeability of political gossip) or a leftover editorial stand-in.

**Paragraph 12** — "But Natasha, who knew all her husband's ways and ideas, could see that he had long been wanting, without managing it, to steer the conversation onto another track and give voice to the idea he felt so deeply, the very idea for whose sake he had gone to Petersburg to consult with his new friend Prince Theodore..." A single sentence carries a relative clause, an infinitive chain, and a second relative clause before it resolves; "Prince Theodore" is also introduced here with no explanation of who he is.

**Paragraph 21** — "'...He's given himself over entirely to this mysticism'—Pierre could no longer stand mysticism in anyone—'he only wants peace...'" The narrator's aside is inserted in the middle of Pierre's own quoted speech, breaking the sentence he is speaking; it takes a re-read to see that the middle clause is the narrator commenting, not part of what Pierre says aloud.

**Paragraph 34** — "'Why? Did the Tugendbund, which saved Europe' (they did not yet dare suggest that it was Russia that had saved Europe) 'do any harm?...'" Same pattern as paragraph 21: a narratorial parenthetical is dropped inside a character's spoken sentence, splitting it in two.

**Paragraph 41** — "'...But you also say our oath of allegiance is a conditional matter, and to that I reply: "You are my best friend, as you well know, but if you formed a secret society and started working against the government... I know it's my duty to obey the government. And if Arakcheev ordered me to lead a squadron against you and cut you down, I wouldn't hesitate an instant, and I'd do it." Argue about that all you like!'" A quotation nested inside Nicholas's own quoted speech, addressed to a hypothetical "you" that is not the same "you" as his actual listener (Pierre); tracking who is being addressed at each layer takes deliberate effort.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Largely natural dialogue-driven prose that reads as one voice; the narrated passages occasionally interrupt a character's own quoted sentence with a narratorial aside (paragraphs 21, 34), which briefly breaks the illusion of continuous speech. Denisov's lisp, once established, is handled consistently.

**Unexplained references.** Dessalles (identified as tutor by context), Denisov and Sonya (named with no introduction), the samovar (usable from context as a tea vessel), Prince Theodore (named with no explanation of who he is or why Pierre consulted him), Magnitsky and Arakcheev (named as figures Pierre disapproves of, no gloss), "Military Settlements" and "Pugachev" (usable from context but unglossed), the Tugendbund (partly glossed as "an alliance of virtue").

**Argument or story thread.** Clear in two sentences: the family's evening conversation drifts from government gossip into Pierre laying out, to a skeptical Nicholas and a sympathetic Denisov, his idea for a society of "active virtue" to counter growing corruption — while young Nicholas Bolkonski watches from a corner, absorbing every word as near-religious devotion to Pierre and grief for the father he never knew. The thread is followable throughout; only paragraph 10's untranslatable proper nouns momentarily obscure specific content, not the overall movement.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None jarring. Foreign phrases (sans foi ni loi, tutti quanti, Je suis vot're homme) are footnoted with translations, which is helpful rather than intrusive.
