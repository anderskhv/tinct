## Obstacles

**Paragraph 24** — "'I say, is it true that we have been beaten?' 'And what did you think? Look what folks are saying.'" Two quoted lines are run together in one paragraph with no speaker tags and no line break between them. It reads as one person asking and answering themselves until the next paragraph ("Questions and answers were heard") retroactively clarifies that this represents overlapping crowd chatter.

**Paragraph 32** — "'Early tomorrow I shall go to his Serene Highness,' he read. ('Sirin Highness,' said the tall fellow with a triumphant smile on his lips and a frown on his brow.) '— to consult with him, to act, and to aid the army to exterminate these scoundrels. We too shall take part...' the reader continued, then paused. ('See?' the young man shouted victoriously. 'He's going to sort the whole thing out for you!') '...in destroying them, and will send these visitors to the devil...'" Three voices are interleaved in one paragraph — the decree text, and two separate parenthetical interjections from the tall young man — without any typographic separation beyond parentheses. "Sirin Highness" is never explained: it isn't clear whether the tall fellow is mishearing "Serene Highness," mocking it, or repeating it accurately in a form the reader doesn't recognize. It's possible to read the passage two different ways (a garbled echo versus a deliberate jeer), and nothing in the text settles which.

**Paragraph 35** — "'We should ask him... that's the man himself?' ... 'Yes, ask him! Why not? He'll explain...' voices at the back of the crowd were suddenly heard saying." The trailing ellipses and the mid-quote "..." break make it hard to tell whether one voice is speaking in fragments or several different voices are overlapping — the collective attribution at the end ("voices... were heard saying") only partly resolves this.

**Paragraph 36** — "The superintendent of police — who had gone that morning, on Rostopchin's orders, to burn the barges and had in connection with that errand acquired a large sum of money now sitting in his pocket — on seeing the crowd bearing down on him, told his coachman to stop." The grammatical subject ("The superintendent of police") and its verb ("told his coachman to stop") are separated by a 30-word aside enclosed in dashes, forcing a re-read to reconnect subject and action.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Vivid, scene-driven narration that moves naturally between description and overlapping street dialogue; it reads as one consistent narrative voice rather than short choppy sentences or flattened paraphrase, and it successfully conveys a chaotic crowd scene.

**Unexplained references.** Varvarka Street, Moroseyka Street, and Lubyanka Street are given with no orientation beyond being places in Moscow (acceptable as setting detail). "His Serene Highness" is never named — presumably a specific commander the contemporary reader would have recognized, but a new reader can't identify who is meant. "Rostopchin" is named once as the source of the superintendent's orders and, separately, "the count" is referred to with no explicit link stated between the two — a reader has to infer they're the same person. The "broadsheet of August 31" is referenced as if its significance is already known.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable in two sentences: a drunken crowd led by a belligerent young man moves through Moscow's streets — from a tavern brawl, to the public reading of a government proclamation that fails to reassure anyone, to a confrontation with the fleeing police superintendent — with the crowd's mood curdling from bravado into the sense that the city's authorities are abandoning them.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None found. The aside "China-Town, a district of Moscow" is a helpful, unobtrusive clarification rather than over-explaining.
