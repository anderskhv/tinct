## Ruling on ¶27 — "meaning the imaginary rival of their old joke"

**The source does not determine this reading. It is an added explanation that closes an ambiguity Tolstoy leaves open, and it goes further than closing it: it invents supporting facts. I grade it MAJOR.**

What the source gives, in order: ""Always about the same thing," said Pierre with a smile. "Jealo…"" — ""Don't say it! I can't bear it!" Natásha cried, and her eyes glittered coldly and vindictively. "Did you see her?" she added, after a pause." — ""No, and if I had I shouldn't have recognized her.""

That establishes exactly one thing: the recurring subject of their quarrels is jealousy. It does not establish who "her" is. The pronoun has no antecedent anywhere in the chapter or in the preceding chapters of the Epilogue, and Tolstoy never supplies one — the point of the exchange is that after years of marriage the two of them need no antecedent, and the reader is left outside the shorthand that ¶1–2 have just described at length. The candidate's gloss destroys the one effect the passage exists to produce.

The gloss is also not merely a resolution but two new claims:
1. **"imaginary"** — the source does not say the rival is imaginary, and Pierre's own answer cuts against it: "if I had I shouldn't have recognized her" presupposes a woman he could in principle have met and failed to recognise. You do not fail to recognise someone who does not exist.
2. **"their old joke"** — nothing in the source makes this a joke. Natasha's eyes "glittered coldly and vindictively" and she says "I can't bear it"; Pierre smiles, but she does not. Calling it a joke recharacterises her reaction.

So even the minimum grade the caller allows — MODERATE, for an added connective/explanation that closes an ambiguity — understates it here, because the explanation carries invented plot facts and misreads the tone of the paragraph it annotates. *Proposal (corrector decides):* delete the clause entirely and let "her" stand unglossed, exactly as the source does.

- **Sismondi** (¶12) — "the historian Sismondi". **BORDERLINE, FLAGGED — MINOR.** It is a bare category tag on a name, which is the mildest admissible kind of gloss, and it adds no claim about Nicholas or his library. - **Plutarch** (¶41) — "a book of ancient Greek and Roman generals". **NOT ALLOWED — MINOR.** Not source-supported, and inaccurate as a description of the *Lives*. - **Mucius Scaevola** (¶43) — "The legendary Roman". **BORDERLINE, FLAGGED — MINOR.** A category tag, adding no plot claim, so admissible in kind; but "legendary" is an editorial judgement on the story's truth, inside a boy's silent vow that depends on his taking it as true. *Proposal:* "the Roman Mucius Scaevola" if a tag is kept at all.
- **les fils de la Vierge** (¶41) — "the ones Dessalles called les fils de la Vierge, **the Virgin's threads**". **ALLOWED.** This is exactly the conventions file's rule 3: the French wording is itself the point (it is Dessalles's name for the cobwebs, and the boy's dream is built out of it), so the French is retained inline and immediately followed by the English. The gloss is a translation, not a claim. No finding on the gloss; the *Vièrge* → *Vierge* respelling is logged separately as cosmetic.

## Whole-chapter notes

- The chapter's governing idea is set out in ¶1–3 — the illogical, simultaneous, dream-like talk of a married couple in which the feeling, not the words, carries the sense — and the rest of the chapter is that idea performed. The candidate keeps the performance intact: the topic-switching (brother, Mary, Petersburg ladies, Mitya, Nicholas's library, the society, Karataev, jealousy, the baby) is in the source's order, nothing is merged, and the non-sequiturs are not smoothed into sequence. That is the hardest thing in the chapter and it is done well.
- Against that, the additions all pull the other way. ¶17's Karataev gloss, and above all ¶27's, are the narrator stepping in to make sure the reader keeps up — in a chapter whose subject is a private language that leaves outsiders behind. ¶27 is the sharpest instance because "her" is the single most emphatically unexplained word in the chapter.
- Natasha's "awfully" is a cross-chapter thread (¶21 twice, ¶40 once) and the flattening in all three places is a single correctable decision, not three separate slips. Likewise "special" at ¶22 and ¶24 is one repetition broken in two places.
- Little Nicholas's dream (¶41–43) is faithful in its sequence and its objects: the lamp, Dessalles's snoring, the Plutarch helmets, the cobweb threads, Glory thicker than the rest, the threads slackening, Uncle Nicholas with Arakcheev's orders, Pierre replaced by the formless father, the waking in terror, the sobbing. The broken sealing wax and pens correctly carry over from chapter 351 into both the dream (¶42) and the uncle's accusation — the callback is intact.
- Interior monologue is handled three different ways in one chapter (unquoted third person at ¶17, unquoted first person at ¶43 and ¶46, against quoted first person in the source throughout). Whatever convention is chosen, it should be one convention.
- Em-dash spacing in this chapter does not match the conventions file or the other two chapters reviewed in this batch; it looks like a whole-file setting rather than a per-paragraph slip and should be normalised in one pass.
