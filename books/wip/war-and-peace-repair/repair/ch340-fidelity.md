## Gloss rulings (every gloss the candidate adds)

- "the royalist Duc d'Enghien" (¶10) — **acceptable**: category only. D'Enghien was a Bourbon prince and émigré; "royalist" states his affiliation and makes no claim about the plot named in the previous clause.
- "the Austrian general Mack" (¶10) — **acceptable as a gloss**: category and fact, no claim added. The MINOR finding above is about "the Austrians" being absorbed into "his forces", not about the tag itself.
- "the battles of Jena and Auerstädt" (¶11) — **acceptable**: category only, correct, no claim.
- "the battles of Austerlitz all the way to Wagram" (¶12) — **acceptable as a category** ("the battles of"); "all the way" is a separate added intensifier, logged MINOR.
- "Borodino, the battle fought just outside Moscow" (¶12) — **not acceptable, MAJOR**: adds a claim, not a category, and the claim is wrong. Logged above.
- "a guard of soldiers" for "guards" (¶15) — **MINOR**: a small added specification rather than a gloss proper.

## Whole-chapter notes

- Nothing is dropped and nothing is summarised: every sentence of the source has a counterpart, the two long catalogue paragraphs (¶10, ¶11) are complete, and every name, date, place and battle survives — 1805, 1806, 1807, 1809 (¶11), 1805, 1807, 1809 (¶14, correctly without 1806), the ten-year period (¶11, ¶3), "two days' sail", "ten years before and a year later" (¶15); Moscow, Paris, Italy, Egypt, Africa, Malta, England, Turkey, Central Europe, Jena, Auerstädt, Austerlitz, Wagram, Borodino; Napoleon, Paul I, the Duc d'Enghien, Mack, Caesar, Alexander the Great, the King of Prussia, the Emperor of Austria, the Pope; and the four kin-groups at ¶11 (ancestors, brothers, stepsons, brothers-in-law). The three numbered conditions at ¶0 are complete and in order.
- Two running threads are damaged across packet boundaries, and both matter more than any single-paragraph wording choice:
  1. **movement / march.** The chapter opens by defining the subject as "the movement of the mass of the European peoples", and ¶14 depends on the reader hearing "movement" and "countermovement" as the same word. The candidate keeps "movement" in those two frame paragraphs but switches to "march" at ¶1 and ¶14, so the frame no longer closes on itself.
  2. **destiny.** "Destined" (¶5) is dropped and "would stand" (¶1) is weakened to "could stand", while "predestined path" (¶3) and "predestined goal" (¶12) are kept. The determinist vocabulary is what holds this chapter to ch339's argument that chance and genius are names for ignorance; thinning it in two places weakens the join.
- A third, smaller thread: "crowned with success" appears twice in the source (¶4, ¶8) and is rendered differently both times; "an outlawed brigand" appears twice in ¶15 and is rendered two ways. Neither changes meaning, but the source's repetitions are doing rhetorical work.
- The anaphora of ¶10 — "Chance … Chance … Chance … Chance … Chance and genius" — is preserved, including the sentence splits, and is the strongest passage in the candidate.
- Directional drift is the same as in ch338 and ch339: "sheer", "actually", "own" (nine times), "possible", "single", "instead", "very", "still", "together", "suddenly", "all the way", "not once but twice", "unharmed", "brilliantly". None is individually serious; together they make a deliberately dry catalogue of ironies read as an emphatic one.
- Typography and diacritics follow the conventions file: straight double quotes, unspaced em dashes, Borodinó → Borodino (stress accent correctly stripped), Auerstädt's genuine diacritic correctly kept, "rôle" → "role". No name in this chapter falls under the character-name table.
