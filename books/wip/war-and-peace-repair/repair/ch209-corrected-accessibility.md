## Obstacles

**Paragraph 3** — "For Kutuzov this was as mathematically clear as a simple rule in checkers: if I have one piece fewer than my opponent and keep trading pieces evenly, I will certainly lose, and so I should stop trading."
The paragraph opens in the third person about Napoleon and Kutuzov, then drops into first person ("if I have," "my opponent," "I should stop trading") for the checkers analogy with no quotation marks or signal that this is Kutuzov's own reasoning being voiced. A reader has to re-read to work out whose "I" is speaking.

**Paragraph 4** — "for at Smolensk he had seen what condition the Russians were leaving their own towns in—left to him—"
The em-dash aside "left to him" is compressed; a reader has to infer, without being told, that this refers to a scorched-earth withdrawal (towns destroyed before being abandoned to the enemy). The phrase alone doesn't carry that meaning.

**Paragraph 13** — "Only much later, when reports on the battle of Borodino were written at leisure, was the incorrect and extraordinary claim invented—probably to justify the mistakes of a commander in chief who had to be portrayed as infallible—that the Shevardino Redoubt had been an advanced post (whereas in reality it was simply a fortified point on the left flank), and that the battle of Borodino was fought by us on a pre-selected, entrenched position (whereas it was fought on a quite unexpected and almost unfortified spot)."
One sentence carrying two parallel "that X (whereas Y)" claims plus an inserted em-dash clause before either claim arrives. Following which parenthetical corrects which claim takes a second pass.

**Paragraph 17** — "Had Napoleon not ridden out on the evening of the twenty-fourth to the Kolocha, and had he not then ordered an immediate attack on the redoubt, but instead begun the attack the next morning, then no one would have doubted that the Shevardino Redoubt was the left flank of our position. The battle would have taken place where we expected it. In that case, we would probably have defended the Shevardino Redoubt—our left flank—even more stubbornly."
A multi-sentence counterfactual chain (what would have happened if Napoleon had acted differently) stacked against the actual sequence of events described just after. Keeping track of hypothetical vs. actual requires re-reading.

**Paragraph 19** — "So throughout the entire battle, the Russians opposed the entire French army launched against our left flank with only half as many men... Under those conditions, it was not merely unthinkable to fight for ten hours and achieve an indecisive result—it was unthinkable to keep the army even from complete collapse and rout."
Two problems: the opening sentence delays its main verb ("maintained") behind a long em-dash clause ("the Russian commanders—not having fully grasped what had happened, namely...—maintained..."), and the closing sentence uses "unthinkable" in a way that reads as "impossible" but is meant as "remarkable/astonishing" — as worded, it can be misread as saying the mere ten-hour fight was itself something that shouldn't have happened, rather than praising the army's near-miraculous survival.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as a single, consistent essayistic voice — Tolstoy's historiographic argument, not narrative. Long, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences are part of the argumentative register rather than translation noise; it doesn't read as choppy or as a modern paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** Miloradovich, Barclay de Tolly, Bagration, Poniatowski, Uvarov, and "the fight at Gridneva" are all named with no introduction — a new reader gets that they're Russian commanders/engagements but not who they specifically are.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues two things: (1) the battle of Borodino made no rational sense for either side, yet both commanders accepted it; (2) the standard historical account of a deliberately chosen, fortified Russian position is false — the position was accidental and weak, a result of losing the Shevardino Redoubt on the 24th.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. The checkers analogy is the author's own device, not translator-added explanation, and no modern idiom intrudes.
