## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "in their eagerness to overwhelm and cut off the enemy, and so on—the farther movement..." The trailing "and so on" mid-sentence reads as if part of a longer list has been cut, without signaling what it stands in for; a new reader can't tell what's being abbreviated.

**Paragraph 6** — "The French route was unknown, so the closer our troops trod on their heels, the greater the distance they had to cover. Only by keeping some distance back could they cut across the French zigzag path." The logic is counterintuitive and compressed — it takes a re-read to see why pursuing more closely would mean covering *more* ground (because you'd follow every zigzag of the enemy's path instead of cutting across it).

**Paragraph 12** — "Miloradovich, who said he did not want to know anything about the commissary affairs of his detachment and could never be found when needed—this man who styled himself chevalier sans peur et sans reproche, 'the knight without fear and without reproach,' and who loved to parley with the French—sent envoys demanding their surrender..." The subject ("Miloradovich") and its main verb ("sent envoys") are separated by a long parenthetical description, so the sentence has to be held in mind across a lot of intervening material.

**Paragraph 13** — "(Knight without fear and without reproach.)" This stands alone as its own paragraph, simply repeating the phrase just translated in the paragraph before it, with no marker (footnote number, "Ed.," italics convention, etc.) to say what it is or why it's set apart. A reader can't confidently say whether this is an authorial aside, a translator's/editor's note, or a formatting artifact.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Expository, analytical narration — Tolstoy's essayistic register, describing the campaign and passing ironic judgment on the officers. Long sentences with embedded clauses are frequent, and in a couple of places (paragraph 12) the syntax separates subject from verb enough to feel like translated prose, but the voice is otherwise consistent throughout.

**Unexplained references.** Place names (Vyazma, Krasnoe, Tarutino) are used with no geography given; generals are introduced with no identification (Toll, Prince Eugene of Wurttemberg, Miloradovich); "the Linen Factories" is named as a place with no explanation. A new reader would need outside knowledge to place any of these.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable: after Vyazma, the Russians pursue the fleeing French toward Krasnoe, wearing out their own army almost as much as the French are destroyed by their retreat. Kutuzov deliberately avoids reckless engagement, to the frustration of glory-seeking generals, and though the Russians win a large battle at Krasnoe, the chapter closes by noting history unfairly credits Napoleon's "grandeur" over Kutuzov's actual judgment.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom. The chapter's one real structural jar is not tonal but formatting: the standalone parenthetical in paragraph 13 breaks the reading flow rather than integrating into the prose.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
