## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 — hard.**
> "the truths revealed to him by his benefactor" ... "he stopped keeping a diary, avoided the Brothers, began going to the club again"
"His benefactor" is never identified in this paragraph — a reader has to guess it means Joseph Alexeevich, mentioned two sentences later almost in passing ("the death of Joseph Alexeevich"), but nothing states the connection. "The Brothers," capitalized, is dropped in with no gloss at all; a new reader has no way to know this means a Masonic fraternity.

**Paragraph 4 — hard.**
> "if it hadn't been for two friends who had borrowed large sums and taken him under their wing, he would have given everything away"
The logic is inverted from what a reader expects: the friends who restrained his spending are themselves described as having borrowed large sums from him, with no explanation of why people who owe him money would be the ones reining him in. Requires a re-read to accept rather than resolve.

**Paragraph 8 — hard.**
> "Hadn't he once longed with all his heart to establish a republic in Russia; then to be a Napoleon; then a philosopher; then a strategist and the conqueror of Napoleon?"
Four different self-images are stacked in one semicolon chain with no signal of time passing between them, and the last two are directly contradictory (wanting to *be* a Napoleon, then wanting to *conquer* Napoleon) with nothing marking the reversal.

**Paragraph 11 — hard.**
> "it seemed to him that he was quite different from and distinct from those other retired gentlemen-in-waiting he had formerly despised: they were empty, stupid, contented fellows, content with their lot: 'while I am still dissatisfied and want to do something for humanity,' he thought."
Two colons and an embedded direct quotation in a single sentence blur where the narrator's description ends and Pierre's own thought begins; the quote reads as if it continues the list of the other men's traits until "he thought" retroactively reassigns it to Pierre.

**Paragraph 13 — hard.**
> "the Astraea Lodge against the Manna Seekers ... an authentic Scottish carpet and a charter that nobody needs"
> "Emperor Francis wants to offer him his daughter in an illegal marriage"
> "offer thanks to God for their victory over the French on the fourteenth of June"
Several specialist references land back-to-back with no cues: "Scottish carpet" (a Masonic ritual object) is given no gloss at all; "Emperor Francis" and why an "illegal marriage" offer to a disgraced Napoleon would follow is asserted with no context; "the fourteenth of June" assumes knowledge of a specific battle date. Individually followable, but stacked together they slow a first reading considerably.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as continuous 19th-century narrative prose, not choppy modern paraphrase — long compound and semicolon-linked sentences carry real weight, and the interior-monologue passages (8, 11–13) shift naturally into Pierre's own voice via short rhetorical questions and quoted thought. One consistent narratorial tone throughout; nothing here reads as flattened or over-modernized.

**Unexplained references.** "The Brothers" / Freemasons and Pierre's "benefactor" (para 1); "Joseph Alexeevich" (named once, role unexplained); the Astraea Lodge, the Manna Seekers, and the "Scottish carpet" (para 13, Masonic lodge politics); "the fourteenth of June" as a battle date; "Emperor Francis" offering his daughter to Napoleon; "forty times forty churches" as a count of Moscow's churches; "Apollon Nikolaevich" (named once, no further identification).

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter traces Pierre's collapse into apathy after Andrew and Natasha's engagement: his Masonic self-improvement project loses its meaning, he retreats into idle, well-liked Moscow aristocratic life, and settles into a private, unresolved disgust at the world's hypocrisy that he anesthetizes with drink and reading rather than acting on. The thread holds together end to end; no paragraph derails it.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom breaks the period voice. The prose does not over-explain — Pierre's contradictions are shown through his own unresolved reasoning (paras 10–13) rather than narrated at him, consistent with the source's interior style.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
