## Obstacles

**Paragraph 32** — "* Der Krieg muss in Raum verlegt werden. Der Ansicht kann ich nicht genug Preis geben."
Left entirely in German with no translation and no marker explaining what it is (a footnote reproducing the original of the line just given in English above it). A reader with no German cannot read this paragraph at all, and nothing signals that it's safe to skip.

**Paragraph 34** — "* der Zweck ist nur den Feind zu schwächen, so kann man gewiss nicht den Verlust der Privat-Personen in Achtung nehmen."
Same problem as paragraph 32 — untranslated German footnote text with no framing.

**Paragraph 36** — "In that 'extend' were my father, son, and sister at Bald Hills. That's all the same to him!" ... "They've surrendered all of Europe to him, and now they've come to teach us."
Two issues. First, "In that 'extend' were my father, son, and sister at Bald Hills" is an unusual, compressed construction — a reader has to work out that it means "that policy of 'extending' the war put my family, at Bald Hills, in danger." Second, "him" is used twice in the paragraph for two different people: the first "him" refers to the German officer who just spoke, the second "him" (in "surrendered all of Europe to him") refers to Napoleon — with no noun reintroduced between the two to signal the switch.

**Paragraph 53** — "and suddenly he remembered how his love had ended. \"He didn't need anything like that. He neither saw nor understood anything of the kind. He only saw in her a pretty, fresh young girl with whom he didn't deign to join his fate. And I?... and he is still alive and happy!\""
"He" here has no antecedent noun anywhere in the paragraph or chapter — the only men established in the scene are Prince Andrew and Pierre, and neither fits (Andrew is doing the remembering; the passage is clearly about a third man who wronged Natasha). A new reader cannot tell who this "he" is.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Dialogue-driven and natural; each speaker has a distinct, consistent voice (Timokhin's soldierly dialect, Andrew's escalating, embittered rhetoric, Pierre's earnest naivety). Reads as one author's prose, not a choppy paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** Barclay de Tolly, Austerlitz, Tilsit, "the Club," Wolzogen and Clausewitz, Bald Hills, Gorki, "the Mozhaysk hill," Smolensk, and — most consequentially — Natasha, who is introduced by name with no context for who she is or was to Prince Andrew, plus the untranslated German footnote lines (see above).

**Argument or story thread.** The night before Borodino, Pierre visits Prince Andrew; their conversation moves from tactics and commanders to Andrew's furious indictment of the "chivalry" of war, ending in his wish to take no prisoners. Left alone, Andrew relives a happy memory of Natasha before recalling, in a confusing final line, how that relationship ended.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None — vocabulary and register stay period-appropriate; no modern idiom intrudes.
