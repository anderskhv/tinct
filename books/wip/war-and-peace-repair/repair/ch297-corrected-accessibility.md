## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "From the moment he received this news until the end of the campaign..." — "this news" has no antecedent anywhere in the chapter; a reader starting here cannot know what news is meant.

**Paragraph 2** — "Everywhere Kutuzov retreated, but the enemy, without waiting for his retreat, fled in the opposite direction." — the parallel "retreated... fled in the opposite direction" is ambiguous about what "opposite" is measured against, and the apparent paradox (both sides seem to be retreating from each other) takes a re-read to resolve.

**Paragraph 4** — "How could that army—which had found abundant supplies in Moscow and had trampled them underfoot instead of preserving them, and on reaching Smolensk had looted provisions instead of storing them—how could that army recover in Kaluga province..." — a long sentence whose subject is repeated after a heavy parenthetical interruption; holding the structure together requires re-reading.

**Paragraph 6** — "...each concerned only with escaping as quickly as possible from this position, of the hopelessness of which they were all more or less vaguely conscious." — inverted, Latinate syntax ("of the hopelessness of which") that has to be unscrambled on a second pass.

**Paragraph 10** — "Napoleon, with his forty-year-old stomach, understood the hint." — an unglossed idiom for physical decline; its meaning only becomes clear from the sentence that follows.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** An argumentative, essayistic voice throughout (addressing "the historians," building rhetorical questions), consistent in tone but denser than ordinary narrative — several long, clause-stacked sentences (paras 4 and 6 especially) slow the reader down. Not choppy, but effortful in places.

**Unexplained references.** The chapter opens on "this news" with no antecedent; it also assumes the reader already knows what happened at Tarutino and Malo-Yaroslavets (both are referenced, not narrated), and introduces "the simple-minded soldier Mouton" with no further identification.

**Argument or story thread.** Kutuzov spends the rest of the campaign restraining his own army from attacking a French force he alone recognizes is already collapsing from within; a near-capture by looting Cossacks and the shame of an open retreat finally push Napoleon to order the withdrawal down the Smolensk road. The thread holds together, but the argumentative digressions (paras 3–6) require patience.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** "le hourra de l'Empereur" is helpfully glossed in-line — good practice, no complaint there. The paragraph on the Cossacks and the plunder (para 9) restates the same point — that looting, not fighting, let Napoleon escape — three times in close succession, which reads as over-explanation rather than anachronism.
