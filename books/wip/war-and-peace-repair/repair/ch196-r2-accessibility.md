## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 — hard.**
> "but the salons of Anna Pavlovna and Helene remained just as they had been—the one seven and the other five years before"

The "the one... and the other... before" construction forces a re-read to work out which number attaches to which salon. Combined with an abstract opening sentence about "categories... where substance matters most and those where form does," this paragraph demands more effort than it needs to establish its point.

**Paragraph 2 — hard.**
> "The view that prevailed there was Bilibin's... that matters would be settled not by gunpowder, but by those who had invented it."

This aphorism is left to do its own work: a new reader must infer that "those who invented gunpowder" means diplomats/statesmen rather than soldiers, with no cue that this is a clever society-salon witticism being reported at one remove.

**Paragraph 3 — hard.**
> "spoke of it as Plutarch speaks of the deeds of the ancients"

The simile depends on knowing who Plutarch was and what tone his writing has (heroic, moralizing biography of ancient figures). Without that, the comparison reads as an empty name-drop rather than the intended irony about inflated enthusiasm.

**Paragraph 7 — hard.**
> "He'll get nothing for his trouble!" ... "A fine reputation he made at Bucharest!"

"He'll get nothing for his trouble" has no clear referent — trouble doing what, nothing from whom — and reads as a non sequitur dropped into the rant. "A fine reputation he made at Bucharest!" alludes to some specific event or scandal that is never named, so the accusation lands as noise rather than a concrete charge.

**Paragraph 9 — unclear.**
> "This might indicate a wish to get rid of him, and so Prince Vasili's opinion remained valid, though he was not now in any hurry to express it."

The logic here is opaque on a first read: it's not stated whose wish this is, why being given the title of Prince would signal a wish to sideline Kutuzov rather than honor him, or how that connects to Prince Vasili's opinion "remaining valid." A reader could plausibly take the sentence either as ironic (a token honor used to remove him from real power) or literally (an honest promotion) with no way to settle which.

**Paragraph 18 — hard.**
> "they say he blushed like a girl to whom La Fontaine's Joconde is read"

"La Fontaine's Joconde" is named with no gloss. The general sense (extreme embarrassment) is guessable from "blushed like a girl," but the specific point of the comparison — what kind of text would make a girl blush — is unavailable to a reader who doesn't already know the reference.

**Paragraph 26 — hard.**
> "that his excellency made it an express condition that the sovereign himself should not be with the army"

Understanding why this line causes the reaction in the next paragraph requires holding onto a fine distinction from several paragraphs earlier — that Kutuzov's stated condition concerned the Tsarevich, not the Emperor — and recognizing that extending it to "the sovereign himself" is a much more serious, faux-pas-level claim. The chapter doesn't flag this distinction at the point it matters, so the reader has to reconstruct it retroactively.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as a single, controlled narrating voice with a formal, mildly ironic register — not chopped into short modern sentences and not flattened into paraphrase. The long analytical opening paragraph and the dialogue-driven scene that follows both sound like one author. A little translated-syntax residue survives in constructions like "the one seven and the other five years before."

**Unexplained references.** A heavy load of proper names arrives with no introduction or context: Rumyantsev, Bilibin, Barclay de Tolly, Kutuzov, the Tsarevich, the Dowager Empress, the committee members (Saltykov, Arakcheev, Vyazmitinov, Lopukhin, Kochubey), "Bucharest," "La Fontaine's Joconde," and the Plutarch comparison. Most of the scene-level action stays followable despite this, but several specific claims (Kutuzov's reputation, the Joconde simile) depend on knowledge the text doesn't supply.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter first contrasts two rival Petersburg salons — Anna Pavlovna's patriotic circle and Helene's pro-French one — then follows the specific political maneuvering, over several named dates, that leads to Kutuzov being named commander-in-chief, ending on a scene where Prince Vasili publicly reverses his earlier criticism of Kutuzov and a naive remark from another guest causes an awkward moment. The thread holds together, but the shift from general survey to dated blow-by-blow narrative (July 24, July 29, August 8, August 9) asks the reader to track time markers and names closely.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom jars against the period setting; vocabulary and register stay consistent throughout. If anything the paragraph errs toward under-explanation — several allusions and one causal claim (paragraph 9) are left for the reader to supply rather than shown.
