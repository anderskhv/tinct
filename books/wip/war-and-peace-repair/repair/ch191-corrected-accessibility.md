## Obstacles

**Paragraph 5 (hard).** "It was the only way an army of eight hundred thousand men — the best in the world and led by the best general — could be destroyed: in conflict with a raw army of half its numerical strength, led by inexperienced commanders, as the Russian army was." The logic runs backward on first read — it sounds like it's claiming the elite army's destruction is explained by facing a *weaker* opponent, when the actual point (late-season advance, burned towns, aroused hatred) is made in the sentence before. Needs a second pass to see it's summarizing the historical mismatch, not offering a new cause.

**Paragraph 6 (hard).** "...Russian authors are even more fond of telling us that from the very start a Scythian war plan was adopted to lure Napoleon into the depths of Russia..." "Scythian war plan" is never explained, yet the whole paragraph's claim (and the one it's refuting) depends on knowing what that strategy is — a scorched-earth retreat drawing an invader into vast territory.

**Paragraph 7 (hard).** "Conjectures as to Napoleon's awareness of the danger of extending his line, and on the Russian side about luring the enemy into the depths of Russia, are clearly of this kind, and only by great stretching can historians attribute such conceptions to Napoleon and his marshals, or such plans to the Russian commanders." One long, clause-stacked sentence that requires re-reading to see which "conjectures" it's dismissing and why.

**Paragraph 11 (hard).** "...by this effort to unite them while avoiding battle with a much stronger enemy—necessarily retreating at an acute angle—we led the French on to Smolensk. But we withdrew at an acute angle not only because the French advanced between our two armies; the angle became still more acute and we withdrew still farther, because Barclay de Tolly was an unpopular foreigner disliked by Bagration..." The "retreating at an acute angle" geometry is repeated twice with no picture to anchor it, and the causal chain tying Barclay's unpopularity to the widening angle is compressed into one dense clause.

**Paragraph 13 (hard).** "...but Paulucci, aiming to become commander in chief, suddenly turned his energy to influencing Alexander, and Pfuel's entire plan was abandoned and the command given to Barclay." Introduces three new names (Paulucci, Drissa camp, Pfuel) in quick succession, and the causal link — how exactly Paulucci's maneuvering caused Pfuel's plan to be scrapped — is asserted rather than shown.

**Paragraph 14 (unclear).** "And by this visit of the Emperor to Moscow, the strength of the Russian army was tripled." It's not clear whether "tripled" is meant literally (a surge of new recruits following the Emperor's appeal) or ironically, given the surrounding sentence frames the visit as a face-saving pretext for his departure rather than a real strategic act. Both readings are plausible and the paragraph doesn't settle it.

**Paragraph 18 (hard).** "Barclay put on his sash and came out to meet and report to his senior officer, Bagration." This establishes Bagration as the senior officer Barclay reports to — which directly sets up the confusion in the next paragraph.

**Paragraph 19 (unclear).** "Despite his higher rank, Bagration — in this contest of magnanimity — took orders from Barclay." This reverses paragraph 18: there Barclay "report[s] to his senior officer, Bagration"; here Bagration, despite outranking Barclay, "took orders from" him. A reader cannot tell from the chapter alone who is actually in command of whom.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Opens in the same essayistic determinism register as the previous chapter, then shifts into a brisk, list-heavy narrative of command disputes. The voice stays consistent throughout — ironic and aphoristic in the opening, procedural in the middle — but the middle section becomes a rapid procession of names and maneuvers that strains a first-time reader's ability to track who answers to whom.

**Unexplained references.** Kurakin, Balashev, Barclay de Tolly, Rostov, Pfuel, Toll, Paulucci, the Drissa camp, Bennigsen, "the Tsarevich" (unnamed), Lubomirsky/Bronnitsky/Wlocki/Wintzingerode, Neverovsky, Arakcheev — a dense cast introduced with no titles, ranks, or roles glossed. Most function as decorative examples and don't block comprehension individually, but the command hierarchy itself (who outranks whom, what "reporting to" versus "taking orders from" means here) is never explained and is exactly what paragraphs 18–19 depend on.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that the great commanders were as unfree as any conscript, then narrates the actual chaos of the war's first weeks: divided Russian armies, the Barclay–Bagration rivalry, Alexander's departure, and the retreat to Smolensk, where the armies finally unite just before the city is abandoned and burned. The overall arc is followable; the rank/command dispute in paragraphs 18–19 is where the thread genuinely breaks.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom intrudes. The chapter under-explains rather than over-explains — particularly the military-protocol context that would resolve the Barclay/Bagration rank contradiction.
