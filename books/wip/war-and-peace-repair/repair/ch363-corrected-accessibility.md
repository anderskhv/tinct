## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 — hard.** "In this way, our conception of free will and inevitability gradually shrinks or grows according to how closely or loosely a man is connected to the external world..." Opens on a connective ("In this way") that points back to reasoning not present in this chapter, so the opening sentence has no anchor for a reader starting here — though the rest of the paragraph is self-contained enough to recover from.

**Paragraph 5 — hard.** "But I am not now refraining from doing so at that first moment when I asked the question. Time has gone by, time I could not hold back; the arm I lifted then is not the same arm I am now refraining from lifting, nor is the air I lifted it in the same air that surrounds me now." The argument turns on a fine distinction between "not lifting now" and "not having lifted then," and the pronoun/tense shifts across the sentence require more than one pass to keep straight which moment is being discussed.

**Paragraph 15 — hard.** "In the first case, if inevitability were possible without freedom, we would have arrived at a definition of inevitability purely by the laws of inevitability itself — that is, a mere form without content." The form/content distinction is introduced here without definition; it only becomes retrievable once paragraphs 16–17 and 22 supply more context, so on first encounter the phrase "mere form without content" is opaque.

**Paragraph 16 — hard.** "...which, precisely because it was unconditioned and unlimited, would amount to nothing — mere content without form." Mirrors the same undefined form/content jargon as paragraph 15; clearer as a pair, but still requires the reader to infer the term's meaning rather than being given it.

**Paragraph 19 — unclear.** "Consciousness says: (1) I alone exist, and everything that exists is nothing but me; therefore I contain space within myself." This can be read two ways: as a literal solipsistic claim (nothing exists but me), or as a phenomenological one (everything I am aware of comes to me only through my own consciousness, so space is only ever known "within" my perspective). The chapter gives no signal for which is meant, and the two readings point to different arguments.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as a sustained, symmetrical philosophical argument built on parallel construction — repeated "if... we arrive at..." framings and numbered (1)(2)(3) lists give it an almost mathematical cadence rather than novelistic rhythm. It holds one consistent voice (an essayist working a proof through) rather than reading as choppy or as a flattened paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** The opening "In this way" depends on argument from outside this chapter. The form/content distinction (paras 15–17, 22) is used before it is defined. "Vital force" (para 33) is deployed as a specific period-scientific term without gloss.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that neither pure freedom nor pure necessity can ever be fully imagined: every act is inescapably conditioned by space, time, and cause, yet reducing an act to zero freedom also destroys the concept of "a man" doing it. It concludes that freedom (known through consciousness) and inevitability (known through reason) are two irreducible, complementary standpoints that together, not separately, produce a coherent idea of human life — with "free will," in history, simply naming whatever remains unexplained by known causal law.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None jarring. "An idiot" (para 12) is dated period-clinical usage rather than anachronism, but a modern reader may notice it. No modern idiom intrudes elsewhere.
