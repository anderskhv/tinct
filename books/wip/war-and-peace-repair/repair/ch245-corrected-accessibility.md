## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "Berg... still held his quiet, agreeable post as assistant to the head of staff of the assistant commander of the First Division of the Second Army." A title stacked four levels deep ("assistant to the head of staff of the assistant commander of..."). It's likely intentional satire on Berg's inflated, do-nothing rank, but on first read it just reads as a hard-to-parse chain of "of"s.

**Paragraph 7** — "such a heroic spirit, the truly ancient valor of the Russian army, which they — which it" — he corrected himself — "has shown or displayed in the battle of the twenty-sixth — there are no words worthy of it!" Berg's self-correction is folded directly into the middle of his own quoted sentence, with the narrator's aside ("he corrected himself") interrupting the quotation itself. This is almost certainly a deliberate character touch (Berg fumbling his own pompous speech), but it makes the sentence itself hard to parse on a first read — the reader has to work out that "which they — which it" is a slip being corrected in real time, not a typo.

**Paragraph 34** — "'I think,' Natasha nearly shouted, turning her furious face toward Petya, 'I think it's so horrid, so vile, so... I don't know what! Are we despicable Germans?'" The jump to "Germans" is unexplained — nothing earlier in this chapter establishes why stinginess or coldness would specifically be coded as German. (Chapter 176, not part of this candidate set, sets up a "Germans vs. Russians" thread in the army — but a reader of this chapter alone has no way to connect the two.)

**Paragraph 47** — "'The eggs... the eggs are teaching the hen,' the count muttered through tears of joy." An unglossed proverb (meaning roughly "the young are instructing their elder"). The emotional beat around it is clear, but the saying itself will puzzle a reader who doesn't already know it.

**Paragraph 59** — "The phaeton was packed with people, and there was some question about where Count Peter would sit." "Count Peter" appears with no signal that this is the boy "Petya" named throughout the rest of the chapter — the very next line ("won't you, Petya?") resolves it, but for a sentence the reader may wonder if a new, older character has been introduced.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as natural, flowing prose — dialogue carries most of the chapter and moves briskly without feeling clipped. Berg's speech is intentionally overwrought and self-interrupting (a character trait, not a translation flaw), and it stands out against the plainer, more urgent dialogue of Natasha and her parents. One consistent authorial voice throughout, not a flattened paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** The Order of Vladimir and Order of Anna (medals, named but not explained), Mitenka the steward (mentioned once, in passing), Yusupov's house, the "Russia is not in Moscow — she lives in the hearts of her sons" quotation, the "eggs teaching the hen" proverb, and the "despicable Germans" line all assume outside context or common knowledge the chapter itself doesn't supply. None block the main thread of the scene, though.

**Argument or story thread.** Easy to state in two sentences: Berg visits the Rostovs amid Moscow's evacuation, more interested in wheedling a cart out of his father-in-law for furniture than in the war, while Natasha is horrified to learn the family is loading its belongings onto carts instead of the wounded. She confronts her parents and gets them to give the carts to the wounded instead.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. The one in-line gloss ("a chiffonier — a chest of drawers") is a light, useful clarification rather than over-explaining.
