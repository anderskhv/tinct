## Obstacles

**Paragraph 2** — "It would seem that no great deliberation was needed to reach this conclusion, nor any particular care or trouble on the part of the Emperor and his marshals, nor was there any need of that special and supreme quality called genius which people are so ready to attribute to Napoleon; yet the historians who described the event later, and the men who surrounded Napoleon at the time, and Napoleon himself, all thought otherwise."
A single long sentence stacking three parallel "nor" clauses before its "yet" turn; followable but requires holding the whole structure in mind to see where the contrast lands.

**Paragraph 3** — "General Compans (who was to attack the flèches) proposed leading his division through the woods, and Napoleon agreed."
"Flèches" is used with no gloss or cue — a reader with no French or military-history background has no way to know it names a type of fieldwork/earthwork rather than, say, a place or a weapon.

**Paragraph 18** — "The Viceroy will occupy the village and cross by its three bridges, advancing to the same heights as Morand's and Gérard's divisions, which under his command will be directed against the redoubt and fall into line with the rest of the forces."
It's unclear what "which" and "his command" attach to — whose command, and which divisions are meant to fall in line. (The chapter itself later calls this exact sentence "unintelligible" in paragraph 27, which confirms the difficulty rather than resolving it at the point it first appears.)

**Paragraph 26** — "The fourth order was: The Viceroy will occupy the village (Borodino) and cross by its three bridges, advancing to the same heights as Morand's and Gérard's divisions (for whose movements no directions are given), which under his command will be directed against the redoubt and fall into line with the rest of the forces."
Same convoluted "which... his command" clause as paragraph 18, repeated here for analysis; the added parenthetical flags the gap but doesn't resolve the ambiguity of what's being commanded by whom.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Two registers alternate deliberately: ironic authorial narration ("without religious awe of Napoleon's genius") and a formal, bureaucratic military order reproduced as a document. Both read coherently in a consistent authorial tone; not choppy.

**Unexplained references.** "Flèches" (never glossed); a run of named officers with no introduction — Davout/Prince of Eckmühl, Duke of Elchingen/Ney, Compans, Pernetti, Fouché, Sorbier, Poniatowski, Tuchkov, Morand, Gérard — and "the Viceroy," never named as a person (historically Eugène de Beauharnais). The "(New Style)" calendar note is, by contrast, helpfully glossed.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter reproduces Napoleon's written battle order for Borodino, then goes through its four main clauses one by one to show none was, or could be, carried out as written — undercutting the reputation for genius the historians assign him.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None; period military vocabulary throughout, no modern idiom intrudes.
