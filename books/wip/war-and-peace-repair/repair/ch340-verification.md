## 3. New reader pass

Read end to end cold. Two things are materially better. The destiny vocabulary now runs unbroken —
"would stand" (¶1), "predestined path" (¶3), "destined to guide" (¶5), "predestined goal" (¶12) —
so a reader arriving from ch339's argument that chance and genius are names for ignorance sees the
join. And the movement/countermovement frame closes: ¶0 names the subject, ¶14 answers it in the
same word. The catalogue of ironies (¶4, ¶10, ¶11, ¶15) reads dry now rather than emphatic, which is
the register the source is written in.

One sentence stops the reader cold, and it is a sentence the correction created. ¶2 reads:

> He has no convictions, no habits, no traditions, not even a name, and not even a Frenchman.

"He has … not even a Frenchman" does not parse. The list is governed by "He has", so the final item
lands as something he possesses. The source does not have this problem because its whole list is an
appositive on the subject — "A man without convictions, without habits, without traditions, without
a name, and not even a Frenchman, emerges" — where "not even a Frenchman" predicates of the man. The
candidate had restructured the sentence into "He has…", and the correction then dropped the source's
phrase into a frame that cannot carry it, replacing a working clause ("not even the distinction of
being French") with a broken one. A reader either stumbles or silently loses the fact that Napoleon
was not French — which is one of the chapter's set-piece ironies and is never restated.

## 5. New findings

**BLOCKING — ¶2, new, introduced by the correction (MODERATE).**
"He has no convictions, no habits, no traditions, not even a name, and not even a Frenchman." The
final conjunct is ungrammatical under "He has", and the fact it carries (he is not French) is
garbled rather than restored. The finding it answers was MINOR — "a flat negation becomes 'the
distinction of being French'" — and the fix traded a MINOR stylistic addition for a MODERATE
grammatical break, which the correction brief forbids ("does not risk new meaning drift", "must not
make the paragraph harder to read than it needs to be").

The fix is local and does not require revisiting any other change. Either restore the source's own
construction —

> A man without convictions, without habits, without traditions, without a name, and not even a
> Frenchman, emerges—by what look like the strangest chances—from all the seething French factions.

— or keep the candidate's "He has…" frame and let the last item predicate properly:

> He has no convictions, no habits, no traditions, not even a name; he is not even a Frenchman.

Either wording keeps the flat negation the review asked for. The first is closer to the source.

**Non-blocking observations.**

- MINOR (readability): ¶5 "he is quite ready to step into his new role". The restore is correct
  against Maude, where "quite ready" means fully ready, but in present-day usage "quite ready" reads
  as *fairly* ready, which is close to the opposite of the source's sense and undercuts the contrast
  the sentence is built on. "Fully ready" would carry Maude's meaning without the eagerness that
  "more than ready" wrongly added. Worth considering while ¶2 is being fixed; not blocking on its
  own.
