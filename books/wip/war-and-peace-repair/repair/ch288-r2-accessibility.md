## Obstacles

**§2 — hard.** "he felt duty-bound to inform his Friend and Brother that Rostopchin, the governor of Moscow, had badly mismanaged things in Moscow" — "his Friend and Brother" reads, on first pass, like a reference to some other person the letter is *about*, rather than the formal way one monarch addresses another (i.e., Alexander himself, the letter's addressee). Requires a re-read to place who is being informed of what.

**§4 — hard.** "the scoundrel Rostopchin was punished by an order to burn down his houses" — ambiguous agency: it can read as Rostopchin being ordered to burn down houses (as a punishment imposed on him to carry out), or as an order being given (by Napoleon/the French) to burn down Rostopchin's own houses as punishment. The intended sense is recoverable but not on first pass.

**§7 — hard.** "Its members will be marked by a red ribbon worn across the shoulder, and the mayor will also wear a white belt. But when not on duty, they will wear only a red ribbon on the left arm." Two different markers (shoulder ribbon/belt vs. arm ribbon) are given for what seems like the same group without clearly flagging that the second is an off-duty alternative; a reader has to work out the on-duty/off-duty split rather than being shown it.

**§8 — hard.** "You will know them by the white ribbon on their left arm." This white left-arm ribbon (for police commissaries) sits close enough to the red left-arm ribbon described for municipal officials in the previous paragraph that the two groups' markers are easy to conflate on a single read. Also: "you must add your own efforts: forget, if possible, the misfortunes you've suffered; hope for a less cruel fate; be certain that inevitable, shameful death awaits anyone who threatens your persons or your remaining property; and you should not doubt that these will be safeguarded" — the long semicolon chain shifts between imperative ("forget," "hope") and declarative ("be certain," "you should not doubt") registers, and "these" in the final clause requires backtracking to confirm it means "your persons or your remaining property."

**§14 — hard.** "(3) Sundays and Wednesdays are the main market days, with sufficient troops stationed along the highways on Tuesdays and Saturdays at such distances from the town as to protect the carts." The logic connecting the troop days (Tue/Sat) to the market days (Sun/Wed) — troops guard the roads on the travel day before each market — is never stated, only implied; a reader has to infer it to see why the days differ.

**§16 — hard.** "reviews were constantly held and rewards distributed." "Reviews" is used in its period military sense (formal troop inspections/parades), with no cue distinguishing it from the modern sense of "review" (an evaluation or critique), which is the more common present-day reading of the word.

**§17 — unclear.** "he ordered his soldiers to be paid in forged Russian currency he had prepared" is directly followed by a quotation describing that money being given as "relief... to those who had been burned out" — i.e., to Moscow's civilian residents, not to soldiers. The setup sentence and the quoted explanation describe two different recipients for the same forged money, and it isn't possible to tell from the paragraph which is correct: were French soldiers paid in fake rubles, or were fake rubles handed out to burned-out civilians as "relief," or both? This is the chapter's worst obstacle — a factual contradiction, not just an awkward sentence.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** The chapter is organized as a catalogue ("With regard to military matters...", "With regard to diplomatic questions...", "With regard to legal matters...") and that structural repetition reads as a deliberate device, not a translation tic — it carries Tolstoy's ironic listing of Napoleon's busywork. Within each entry, sentences are plain, declarative modern English, not choppy or visibly translated. The two quoted proclamations shift into a distinct, more formal/rhetorical register (imperatives, numbered clauses, exalted address) appropriate to in-story official documents, and that register shift reads as intentional rather than inconsistent. The authorial irony ("the scoundrel Rostopchin," "the greatest virtue of crowned heads") comes through clearly in the surrounding narration.

**Unexplained references.** Sebastiani, Murat, Kutuzov (§1); Yakovlev (§2); Tutolmin (§3, recurring in §17); Rostopchin (§2, §4); Thiers (§17, cited as a historian). None are glossed. Most don't block comprehension of their sentence's action, but a reader with no background in the 1812 campaign would not know who is a general, who is the Russian commander, or why Tutolmin is significant until (if ever) it's clarified later.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter is a catalogue of Napoleon's administrative measures after taking Moscow: military orders, diplomatic overtures to Tsar Alexander, punishment of arsonists and Rostopchin, a new municipal government (quoted in full), army foraging, restored church services, a commerce proclamation, troop reviews and theater-going, charitable gestures (including the forged-currency episode), and army discipline. It moves topic by topic rather than as a scene, and that's followable in two sentences — except that §17's contradiction about who received the counterfeit money leaves one concrete fact in the chapter genuinely unresolved.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom jars against the period setting. No paragraph over-explains; the one archaic/foreign term ("à la maraude," §9) is handled well, with an unobtrusive footnote gloss immediately after rather than an inserted explanation in the text itself.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
