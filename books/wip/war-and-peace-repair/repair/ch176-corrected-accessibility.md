## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "The first army, with which was the Emperor, occupied the fortified camp at Drissa." The inverted clause "with which was the Emperor" reverses normal English word order and has to be unscrambled on a re-read to get "the Emperor was with the first army."

**Paragraph 2** — "He received Bolkonski stiffly and coldly..." The paragraph refers to the same person as both "Prince Andrew" and "Bolkonski" without signaling they are the same man; a reader meeting this cold could take "Bolkonski" for someone new. Also: "Already from his military experience and from what he had seen in the Austrian campaign, he had reached the conclusion that in war the most carefully considered plans have no significance and that everything depends on how unexpected movements of the enemy — which cannot be foreseen — are met, and on how and by whom the whole matter is handled." A single sentence carrying three stacked clauses plus a parenthetical dash; followable but requires a second pass.

**Paragraph 3** — "First, the army under Barclay de Tolly; second, the army under Bagration; and third, the one commanded by Tormasov... In attendance on him was the head of the imperial staff, Quartermaster General Prince Volkonsky, as well as generals, imperial aides-de-camp, diplomatic officials, and a large number of foreigners... Besides these, there were in attendance on the Emperor without any definite assignments: Arakcheev... Count Bennigsen... the Grand Duke Tsarevich Constantine Pavlovich; and Count Rumyantsev... There were also Stein... Armfeldt... Pfuel... Paulucci... Wolzogen — and many others." Thirteen names introduced in one paragraph, each with a title clause, none given room to register before the next arrives. The information itself is orderly, but the density is a genuine speed bump for a new reader.

**Paragraph 7** — "as a great favor he had petitioned the Emperor to make him a German." The joke depends on knowing that foreign (largely German) advisers were seen as more trusted or more theoretically credentialed than Russian officers — a point made two paragraphs earlier about faction one, but not reconnected here. A reader has to hold that thread across paragraphs to see why it's funny.

**Paragraph 17** — "These men, though delighted with the sovereign's refusal to command the army, nevertheless blamed him for such excessive modesty and only desired and insisted that their adored sovereign should abandon his diffidence and openly announce that he would take personal command of the army, gather a commander in chief's staff around him, and — consulting experienced theoreticians and practical men as needed — lead the troops himself, whose spirits would thereby be raised to the highest pitch." One sentence, five successive demands strung on "and," with a dash-set aside in the middle and a dangling "whose" at the end. Followable, but it takes real effort to keep the thread.

**Paragraph 18** — Describes five different self-interested behaviors (the man who agrees with everyone, the one who picks fights to look loyal, the one who asks for a bonus, the one who angles to be noticed, the one who argues just to get a dinner invitation) all in one unbroken paragraph with no numbering or paragraph breaks to separate the cases. Each sentence is clear on its own, but tracking which trait belongs to which "man" takes concentration.

**Paragraph 19** — "they watched only the weathercock of imperial favor; as soon as they noticed it turning in any direction, this whole drone population of the army began blowing hard that way." Two images are fused in one action — a weathercock (which is turned by wind) is also described as being "blown" toward by "drones" (insects, or idle men), which momentarily reverses the logic of a weathervane. The point lands but the metaphor briefly works against itself.

**Paragraph 23** — "This stirring of the people by their sovereign, and his call to them to defend their country—the very incitement which was the chief cause of Russia's triumph, insofar as it was produced by the Tsar's personal presence in Moscow—was suggested to the Emperor, and accepted by him, as a pretext for leaving the army." The grammatical subject ("This stirring... and his call") is separated from its verb ("was suggested") by a 25-word parenthetical, and that parenthetical itself makes a large claim (this act "was the chief cause of Russia's triumph") that isn't clearly the narrator speaking in his own voice versus reporting a view. On first reading it isn't possible to say with confidence whether the paragraph is asserting that this "stirring" caused Russia's victory, or only that the letter-writer claimed it would — the two readings diverge on what the sentence is actually claiming.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as translated 19th-century analytical prose — measured, ironic, fond of long compound sentences and enumerated factions — rather than choppy modern paraphrase. The voice is consistent throughout; it doesn't flatten into short declaratives, but the long sentences occasionally ask more of the reader than the plain narrative passages do.

**Unexplained references.** Drissa, Vilna, the Austrian campaign, Austerlitz, the war in Finland, and Bennigsen's conduct "in 1807" are all invoked as settled background the reader is assumed to already carry. Anatole Kuragin is named as someone Prince Andrew has personal history with, but that history isn't given here. "Bolkonski" as an alternate name for Prince Andrew is used without a cue that it's the same person.

**Argument or story thread.** Followable: Prince Andrew arrives at army headquarters and maps out nine competing factions arguing over how to fight Napoleon, ending with the faction that ultimately talks the Emperor into leaving the army. The throughline survives the density of names.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None found — no modern idiom intrudes, and the chapter doesn't over-explain things the reader could infer.
