## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1** — "the soldier who told stories called it Femgalka" — this character is identified only by role ("the soldier who told stories"), never by name, and nothing earlier in the chapter introduces him. A new reader has no way to place who this is.

**Paragraph 14** — "'You see, St. Thomas,' he said to me the other day" — "St. Thomas" reads as if it could be the religious figure; nothing signals it's a person's name (a fellow soldier or acquaintance), so the reference momentarily misdirects.

**Paragraph 19** — "'A promise is own brother to performance!'" — an archaic folk idiom given with no gloss; the reader has to work out on their own that it means a promise should be as good as its fulfillment.

**Paragraph 26** — "He took out an assignation ruble note and gave it to Karataev." — "assignation ruble" is a specific historical Russian paper-currency term dropped in with no explanation; the reader can tell it's money but nothing more.

**Paragraph 31** — "'A sweating hand is an open hand, a dry hand is a tight one.'" — an unglossed proverb; the reader must infer that "sweating" signals generosity/activity and "dry" stinginess.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as natural, unbroken prose in a single consistent narrative voice — warm and observational in the Pierre/dog/weather passages, plainspoken and colloquial in the dialogue with the corporal and Karataev. Not choppy; the one long opening paragraph (the dog's several names) is the only place syntax gets dense, and it stays followable.

**Unexplained references.** "the soldier who told stories" (para 1), "St. Thomas" (para 14), "assignation ruble note" (para 26), and the "sweating hand" proverb (para 31) — see obstacles above. None are glossed within the chapter.

**Argument or story thread.** Pierre, now a prisoner living roughly with fellow captives, savors a calm autumn morning and notices how much his body and outlook have changed. The chapter then turns to a small scene of shared humanity across the enemy line: Karataev finishes sewing a shirt for a French soldier, and their haggling over leftover scraps of cloth ends in an unexpected act of generosity from the Frenchman. The thread is easy to follow start to finish.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom jars against the period setting. The prose stays in a plain, contemporary register throughout without over-explaining what it shows — the closing exchange over the fabric scraps is left to speak for itself.
