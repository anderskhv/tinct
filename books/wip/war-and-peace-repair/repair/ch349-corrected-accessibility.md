## Obstacles

**Paragraph 3** — "The servants—the most reliable judges of their masters, since they judge not by what they say or the feelings they express but by their actions and their way of living—were glad of Pierre's return, because..." The 26-word aside between subject ("The servants") and verb ("were glad") has to be held in memory before the sentence resolves; on first reading it reads like the sentence has stalled.

**Paragraph 5** — "Pierre's past life, and his unhappiness before 1812—of which young Nicholas had formed a vague, poetic picture from a few words he had overheard—his adventures in Moscow, his captivity, Platon Karataev, of whom he had heard from Pierre, his love for Natasha, of whom the boy was also especially fond, and above all Pierre's friendship with the father Nicholas could not remember—all of it made Pierre, in his eyes, a hero and a saint." Six list items pile up before the verb ("made Pierre... a hero") ever arrives; a reader has to hold the whole list in suspension to see what it's the subject of.

**Paragraph 6** — "From broken remarks about Natasha and his father, from the emotion with which Pierre spoke of that dead father, and from the careful, reverent tenderness with which Natasha spoke of him, the boy... had formed the idea that..." Three parallel "from X" phrases precede the subject and verb, the same stacking pattern as paragraph 5.

**Paragraph 11** — "If Natasha found fault with him now, it was only for buying too much... To her other supposed faults—untidiness and neglect of herself... —she had now added stinginess." The leap from "faults" clearly attributed to Pierre (overspending) to "stinginess" attributed to Natasha herself requires inferring, unstated, that Natasha's own complaint about overspending is what earns her the "stinginess" label — the logic isn't shown, only implied.

**Paragraph 25** — Two sentences cause friction: "Her life had no outward aims left—only a need to keep exercising her various functions and inclinations remained visible," where the true subject ("a need to keep exercising...") is separated from its verb ("remained visible") by a long intervening list; and "She did none of these things under any outward pressure, the way people in the full vigor of life do, for whom the purpose behind exercising their own functions goes unnoticed, hidden behind whatever goal they're chasing," a nested abstraction (purpose hidden behind a goal, itself compared to something un-hidden) that takes real effort to unpack. A third line, "What is a goal, for people in full vigor, was for her evidently merely a pretext," inverts normal word order and has to be re-read to see it is a comparison, not a question.

**Paragraph 32** — "...they said that she had already played her part in life, that what they now saw before them was not the whole of her, that we must all become like her, and that they were glad to give way to her..." The clause "that we must all become like her" shifts abruptly into first-person-plural ("we") inside a chain of third-person reported thought, momentarily unclear whose voice "we" belongs to.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as one consistent, warm, literary voice for most of the chapter — the dialogue and the domestic scenes (present-giving, the countess's card game) move naturally. But several description-heavy paragraphs (5, 6, 25) stack multiple prepositional or appositive phrases ahead of the main verb, which reads as translated syntax rather than native modern English rhythm.

**Unexplained references.** "Bald Hills" (place name, never placed or described); "Dessalles" (named with no stated role in this chapter); "hussar" and "Knight of St. George" (period military/honorific terms, no gloss); "arshin" (unit for measuring cloth, left unglossed though usable from context); "Platon Karataev" (named only as someone "he had heard from Pierre," never explained); "Adèle" (relationship to the household unstated); "1812" (assumes the reader already knows its significance).

**Argument or story thread.** Easy to state in two sentences: the chapter shows how every layer of the Bald Hills household — servants, children, young Nicholas, guests, family — responds to Pierre's return, then follows Pierre and Natasha handing out presents before visiting the aging, fading Countess Rostova, closing on a meditation on old age and mortality. The thread never gets lost, even across the harder paragraphs.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None jarring. The écossaise is glossed inline ("a Scottish country dance") and "Memento mori" is translated in the text itself ("remember that you must die") — both helpful rather than intrusive. No modern idiom breaks the period voice.
