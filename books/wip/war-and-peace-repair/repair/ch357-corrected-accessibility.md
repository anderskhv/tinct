## Obstacles

**Paragraph 5 — hard.**
> "It cannot be the direct physical power of a strong man over a weak one—a domination based on applying or threatening physical force, like the strength of Hercules; nor can it rest on moral force, as some historians naively suppose when they say that the great figures of history are heroes, meaning men gifted with a special strength of soul and mind called genius. This power cannot rest on a predominance of moral strength, because—setting aside heroes like Napoleon, whose moral qualities are a matter of sharp disagreement—history shows us that neither a Louis XI, the French king, nor a Metternich, the Austrian statesman—both of whom ruled over millions of people—possessed any particular moral qualities..."

One sprawling sentence stacking two rebuttals (physical force, moral force) with nested asides and two named rulers. A new reader has to hold several clauses open at once to see where the sentence lands.

**Paragraph 7 — unclear.**
> "And that is exactly how power is understood by the science of jurisprudence—that exchange bank of history, offering to trade history's understanding of power for real gold."

The metaphor doesn't resolve. It's not clear which party is offering "real gold" for which — whether jurisprudence gives history something solid in exchange for its vague notion of power, or the reverse. Two readings are possible and neither is signaled as correct.

**Paragraph 12 — hard.**
> "Why was Napoleon III a criminal when he was captured at Boulogne, and why, later, were the people he himself arrested the criminals?"

"Captured at Boulogne" is dropped in with no context — a new reader has no way to know this refers to Napoleon III's failed 1840 coup attempt, so the rhetorical point (he was first a criminal, then later the one calling others criminals) is hard to follow.

**Paragraph 15 — hard.**
> "(2) We may assume that the will of the people is transferred to rulers conditionally, under definite and known conditions, and show that every limitation on power, every conflict, even every destruction of power, results from the rulers' failure to observe the conditions under which their power was entrusted to them. (3) We may assume that the will of the people is delegated to rulers conditionally, but that the conditions are unknown and undefined, and that the rise of rival authorities, their struggles, and their falls, result simply from how well or badly the rulers fulfilled these unknown conditions..."

Three near-identical, long, subordinate-clause-heavy definitions laid end to end. The differences between (1), (2), and (3) hinge on small phrases ("known" vs. "unknown" conditions) that are easy to lose track of across such long sentences.

**Paragraph 20 — hard.**
> "...how is it that Louis XIV and Ivan the Terrible end their reigns peacefully, while Louis XVI and Charles I are executed by their own people? ... the collective will is never withdrawn from a given line of rulers and their heirs, and then, suddenly, over the course of fifty years, passes to the Convention, to the Directory, to Napoleon, to Alexander, to Louis XVIII, to Napoleon again, to Charles X, to Louis Philippe, to a Republican government, and to Napoleon III."

A dense chain of names and regimes (French and Russian) with no glosses, requiring outside historical knowledge just to follow which rulers are being compared, let alone the argument being made about them.

**Paragraph 26 — hard.**
> "The most common generalizations adopted by nearly all historians are: freedom, equality, enlightenment, progress, civilization, culture. Having set up some such generalization as the goal of humanity's movement, historians go on to study the men who left behind the greatest number of monuments—kings, ministers, generals, authors, reformers, popes, journalists—according to how far, in the historian's opinion, these people advanced or hindered that abstraction."

Six sentences of abstract argument in a row, each dependent on the last, before the point (that this whole approach is unproven) lands. Followable, but demands sustained effort.

**Paragraph 27 — hard.**
> "Is the ferment among the peoples of the West at the end of the eighteenth century, and their surge eastward, explained by the activity of Louis XIV, XV, and XVI, their mistresses and their ministers, and by the lives of Napoleon, Rousseau, the philosopher Diderot, the playwright Beaumarchais, and the rest?"

A rapid list of names (three unglossed kings, "their mistresses," several public figures) packed into one rhetorical question — a lot to place even with two of the names glossed.

**Paragraph 29 — hard.**
> "Is the movement of the peoples at the time of the Crusades explained by the lives and activities of the Godfreys—men like the Crusader Godfrey—and the Louis-es, the various kings named Louis, and their ladies? ... The history of the Godfreys and the Minnesingers, the medieval poet-singers, plainly cannot cover the life of the peoples."

Despite the in-line glosses (Godfrey the Crusader, Peter the Hermit "a preacher," Minnesingers "medieval poet-singers"), the paragraph still stacks several unfamiliar proper nouns and a long argument about the Crusades' start and stop in one breath.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as one sustained, essayistic argumentative voice — long compound sentences linked by "But," "And so," "So that." It does not read as choppy or as a modern paraphrase; it holds a consistent, deliberate lecturing tone throughout, closer to a treatise than a narrative.

**Unexplained references.** Most named figures get a helpful in-line gloss (Pugachev "the Cossack who led a peasant uprising," Metternich "the Austrian statesman," Confederation of the Rhine "the alliance of German states under Napoleon," Diderot, Beaumarchais, Kurbsky, Peter the Hermit, Minnesingers). But several are left bare: Napoleon III's capture "at Boulogne," and the long roll call of French/Russian rulers in paragraph 20 (Louis XIV/XV/XVI, Charles I, Ivan the Terrible, the Convention, the Directory, Charles X, Louis Philippe) assumes a reader already has this history in hand.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter asks what "power" actually means once you no longer believe God hands it to a ruler, then tests three possible theories of how the people's will is "transferred" to rulers (unconditional, conditionally-known, conditionally-unknown) and shows each one breaks down against actual historical examples. The thread is followable start to finish, though it takes real concentration through the middle stretch of named rulers and regimes.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom intrudes, and the chapter does not over-explain — if anything it under-explains several proper nouns (Boulogne, the string of French/Russian rulers) that a modern general reader would not immediately place.

Accessibility verdict: REVISE
