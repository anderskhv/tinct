### Index 5 (¶6)
- Before: "Kutuzov never spoke of \"forty centuries looking down from the Pyramids,\" of the sacrifices he was making for the fatherland, or of what he intended or had accomplished. In general he said nothing about himself, struck no poses, always appeared to be the simplest and most ordinary of men, and said the simplest and most ordinary things. He wrote letters to his daughters and to the writer Madame de Stael, read novels, enjoyed the company of pretty women, joked with generals, officers, and soldiers, and never contradicted anyone who tried to prove anything to him. When Count Rostopchin, the governor of Moscow, galloped up to Kutuzov at the Yauza bridge and personally accused him of causing Moscow's destruction, saying: \"How could you promise not to abandon Moscow without a battle?\" Kutuzov replied: \"And I shall not abandon Moscow without a battle\"—though Moscow was already abandoned. When Arakcheev, coming from the Emperor, told him that Ermolov ought to be appointed chief of artillery, Kutuzov replied: \"Yes, I was just saying so myself\"—though a moment before he had said the exact opposite. What did it matter to him—the only man amid a senseless crowd who understood the tremendous significance of what was happening—whether Rostopchin blamed him or himself for Moscow's destruction? Still less could it matter who was named chief of artillery."
- After: "Kutuzov never spoke of \"forty centuries looking down from the Pyramids,\" of the sacrifices he was making for the fatherland, or of what he intended or had accomplished. ### Index 7 (¶8)
