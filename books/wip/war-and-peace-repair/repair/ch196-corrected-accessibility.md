## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 (hard).** "...but the salons of Anna Pavlovna and Helene remained just as they had been—the one seven and the other five years before." The "the one... the other" construction requires the reader to work out, without restatement, that "the one" is Anna Pavlovna's salon (mentioned first) and "the other" is Helene's — on top of an already long paragraph juggling a form-vs-substance framing, a run of political history ("made peace and quarreled again... made constitutions and unmade them"), and two separate salons' politics.

**Paragraph 2 (hard).** "The view that prevailed there was Bilibin's—he was now quite at home in Helene's house, which every clever man was obliged to visit—that matters would be settled not by gunpowder, but by those who had invented it." Bilibin is introduced with no context, and the paradox that closes the sentence (peace decided by "those who had invented" gunpowder, i.e. clever negotiators rather than force) takes real work to unpack.

**Paragraph 9 (hard).** "This might indicate a wish to get rid of him, and so Prince Vasili's opinion remained valid..." The reasoning — that granting Kutuzov the title "Prince" could be read as a way of sidelining him rather than honoring him — is asserted without the political logic (a decorative promotion used to remove someone from real power) being made explicit, so the connection between "given the title of Prince" and "wish to get rid of him" isn't self-evident.

**Paragraph 18 (unclear).** "They say he blushed like a girl to whom Joconde is read..." "Joconde" is never explained (a risqué verse tale by La Fontaine) and the simile's whole point — a blush of embarrassment at something faintly scandalous — depends entirely on recognizing the allusion. Without it, the image is opaque rather than merely unfamiliar.

## Whole-chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Fluent social narrative-satire: an ironic authorial aside opens the chapter, then it moves into brisk, naturalistic dialogue. One consistent voice throughout — observant, dry — not choppy and not flattened into generic modern paraphrase. The dialogue in particular reads idiomatically without feeling anachronistic.

**Unexplained references.** Anna Pavlovna, Helene, Prince Vasili, Bilibin, Rumyantsev, Kutuzov, Barclay de Tolly, and "the Tsarevich" are all assumed known from earlier in the novel — ordinary for a chapter this deep into the book, but genuinely opaque to a reader arriving without that context. "Legitimists" is used without gloss but its meaning (partisans of restored monarchy) is recoverable from context. The "Joconde" allusion in paragraph 18 is the one reference that is load-bearing rather than decorative.

**Argument or story thread.** Two rival Petersburg salons react differently to the war — one patriotic, one pro-French and skeptical — and the chapter then follows gossip around Kutuzov's appointment as commander in chief: Prince Vasili first dismisses him as unfit (even mocking his blindness), then does a complete about-face and reveres him the moment the appointment becomes official. The thread is easy to state and follow throughout, including its closing beat, where a minor character's faux pas (suggesting Kutuzov excluded the Emperor himself) draws a pointed, silent reaction.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** No modern idiom intrudes anywhere. Nothing is over-explained; if anything the chapter under-explains offhand allusions (Joconde) and assumes familiarity with characters established earlier in the novel.
