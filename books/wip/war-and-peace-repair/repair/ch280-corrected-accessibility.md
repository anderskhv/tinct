## Obstacles

**Paragraph 1 — hard.**
> "Yet there is the same difference between someone who says 'the peoples of the West moved eastward because Napoleon wished it' and someone who says 'this happened because it had to happen,' as between those who believed the earth was stationary and the planets revolved around it, and those who admitted they didn't know what held the earth in place but knew there were laws directing its movement and that of the other planets."

A single sentence carrying two parallel comparisons (historical-cause vs. historical-cause, astronomical-belief vs. astronomical-belief) stacked into one clause. The reader has to hold four positions in mind at once before the analogy resolves; it is followable but requires a re-read to sort which pair maps to which.

**Paragraph 5 — hard.**
> "Later, the French inactivity (they even lost sight of the Russian army), concern for the safety of the arsenal at Tula, and especially the advantage of moving closer to provisions caused the army to turn still further south onto the Tula road. After crossing to the Tula road beyond the Pakhra by a forced march, the Russian commanders planned to stay at Podolsk and had no thought of the Tarutino position. But countless circumstances forced our army to turn even further south..."

The paragraph tracks the army's route through five differently named roads/places (Nizhni, Ryazan, Tula, Podolsk, Kaluga/Tarutino) in sequence, each turn justified by a different cause, with no map or recap. A reader without a mental map of the 1812 campaign is likely to lose track of which turn came from which cause.

## Chapter notes

**Rhythm and voice.** Reads as sustained essayistic argument in one consistent voice — long qualifying clauses, "on the one hand / but" structures — rather than choppy sentences. It sounds like a single author reasoning aloud, not a flattened paraphrase.

**Unexplained references.** Battle of Borodino, occupation/burning of Moscow, the Ryazan–Kaluga flank march, Krasnaya Pakhra River, Tarutino camp, the council at Fili, Lanskoy, the Oka River, Tula, Nizhni-Novgorod, Podolsk. None are glossed; a new reader needs outside knowledge of the 1812 campaign geography to place them.

**Argument or story thread.** The chapter argues that historians wrongly credit a single genius (or single decision) for the Russian army's "flank march" retreat toward Kaluga; in fact the move emerged step by step from supply pressures and chance, and was only seen as a deliberate plan after the fact. That two-sentence summary holds up — the thread is not lost, though the geographic detail in paragraph 5 taxes attention.

**Anachronism or over-explanation.** None. "Even a dull thirteen-year-old could have guessed" reads as a natural period aside, not a modern intrusion. No over-explaining — the piece trusts the reader to follow the logic.
