# Accessibility Review — Oedipus at Colonus (modern-en), Reviewer A

**Coverage:** Read `candidate.json` in full, blind (no source, no drafter notes) — all
11 chapters, all 566 paragraphs, start to finish, then re-read paragraph by
paragraph. No sampling.

Chapters reviewed: 1 Prologue, 2 Parodos, 3 First Episode, 4 First Stasimon,
5 Second Episode, 6 Second Stasimon, 7 Third Episode, 8 Third Stasimon,
9 Fourth Episode, 10 Fourth Stasimon, 11 Exodos.

---

## Issues found (by chapter / paragraph index, 0-based within each chapter's array)

### Chapter 2 — Parodos

- **Paragraph indices 12, 13, 14** (`ANTIGONE. [Text lost in manuscript.]` /
  `OEDIPUS. [Text lost in manuscript.]` / `ANTIGONE. [Text lost in manuscript.]`,
  followed by the orphaned line "Follow with your blind steps, father, as I lead
  you."): a general reader with no footnote or introduction will not know this
  bracketed tag means the ancient manuscript is physically damaged at this
  point — it reads like a stray production note or an error in the file. This
  is inherent to the source (a real textual lacuna) but as presented with zero
  framing it is a comprehension stumble, not a translation-difficulty one.
  Recommend a one-time contextual note (in onboarding or a footnote) rather
  than a text fix.
- **Paragraph 15** ("CHORUS. You are a stranger in a strange land — bend your
  will to hers."): "hers" has no antecedent in the immediately preceding
  candidate text once the lacuna lines are skipped past mentally — a reader
  has to infer it means "the state's/city's will." Minor, but a reader could
  stumble on the pronoun.

### Chapter 6 — Second Stasimon (the "Colonus ode")

This is the densest chapter for a new reader — expected for a Sophoclean
choral ode, but worth flagging fully:

- **Paragraph 0**: "the pleasure-ground kept for the Bacchic god, where every
  night he holds his wild revels with the nymphs who raised the lusty child" —
  "the Bacchic god" and "the lusty child" assume the reader already knows this
  is Dionysus and that he was raised by nymphs; nothing in the sentence
  glosses this. A reader unfamiliar with the myth will parse the grammar fine
  but miss who is being described.
- **Paragraph 1**: "a wreath is twined with the crocus's golden hue for the
  Mother and the Daughter" — "the Mother and the Daughter" (Demeter and
  Persephone) is unglossed and easy to misread as generic figures rather than
  specific goddesses.
- **Paragraph 1**: "nor will the Cyprian queen with her golden reins" — "the
  Cyprian queen" (Aphrodite) is an epithet with zero contextual support; a
  reader could plausibly mistake this for a place-name reference rather than
  a goddess.
- **Paragraph 2**: "the plant that the Olive Planter tends, and the Grey-eyed
  Goddess herself protects" — "the Olive Planter" and "the Grey-eyed Goddess"
  (Zeus Morios and Athena) are both unglossed epithets stacked in one
  sentence.
- **Paragraph 3**: "son of Kronos, our divine king" and "swift as the hundred
  feet of the Nereids as they dance along the sea" — "Kronos" and "the
  Nereids" are proper nouns with no supporting context; a reader will
  register them as names but likely not know who/what they are.

Overall this chapter's *sentences* are grammatically smooth and read well
line to line — the difficulty is entirely in unglossed mythological epithets
and proper nouns stacking up (4–5 per short ode), which is the single
sharpest accessibility friction point in the whole book.

### Chapter 7 — Third Episode

- **Last paragraph (index 5, the choral ode after Theseus and Creon exit)**:
  "the dread Queen and Maid cherish the mystic rites... the priestly
  Eumolpidae" — "the dread Queen and Maid" (Demeter and Persephone again, in
  connection with the Eleusinian Mysteries) and especially "Eumolpidae" (a
  specific hereditary priestly clan) are the hardest single phrase in the
  book for a general reader — a proper noun with no gloss, referring to an
  institution most contemporary readers have never encountered.
- Same paragraph: "West of white Oea's cliff" — "Oea" is an unglossed
  place-name; reads fine grammatically but conveys no information to an
  unprepared reader.

### Chapter 8 — Third Stasimon

- **Paragraph 1** (Creon's self-defense): "For on her native hill of Ares
  here — I knew your far-famed Areopagus — Justice sits, and does not allow
  vagrants to stay within your borders." This one is actually well-handled —
  "Areopagus" is glossed in-line ("her native hill of Ares here... your
  far-famed Areopagus") so the reader gets both the term and its meaning
  without needing outside knowledge. Flagging as a *positive* example, not an
  issue.

### Chapter 9 — Fourth Episode

- **Paragraph 48** (Polyneices' long speech, the catalog of the Seven Against
  Thebes): "First comes the peerless warrior and peerless seer, Amphiaraus,
  with his lightning spear. Next an Aetolian, Tydeus, son of Oeneus. Third,
  Eteoclus of Argive birth. The fourth is Hippomedon, sent to war by his
  father Talaos. Fifth, Capaneus... Sixth, Parthenopaeus, an Arcadian, named
  after the maid long a maid and late-wed, Atalanta, whose true-born son he
  is." This is the second sharpest friction point in the book: seven proper
  names in rapid succession, most with a patronymic or epithet attached, none
  glossed, with no indication of why the list matters beyond "these are
  allies." The sentence-level grammar is clear and readable — the difficulty
  is pure information density of unfamiliar names. A reader can follow the
  scene's emotional stakes (Polyneices vs. his father) without this list, but
  the list itself will not land as anything but a wall of names.
- Same paragraph: "named after the maid long a maid and late-wed, Atalanta"
  is a genuinely clever, readable rendering of a wordplay-heavy original line
  (Parthenopaeus's name literally invokes "maiden") — flagging as
  particularly well done, not a problem.

### Chapter 11 — Exodos

- **Paragraph 15** (the Messenger's account of Oedipus's disappearance):
  "close to the rocky basin that records the pact of Theseus and Peirithous"
  — "the pact of Theseus and Peirithous" is a specific mythological reference
  (their oath of blood-brotherhood/descent into the underworld) presented
  with no support; likely to be read past without registering, rather than
  actively confusing.
- Same paragraph: "for Hermes the guide leads this way, and Persephassa,
  queen of the dead" — "Persephassa" is an alternate/archaic form of
  Persephone, kept unmodernized as a name (correctly, since it's a name, not
  archaic syntax) but a reader who knows "Persephone" may momentarily wonder
  if this is a different figure. Minor.
- **Paragraphs 566–end (the Antigone/Ismene/Chorus kommos, e.g. indices
  around "Longs?" / "To see the earthy bed." / "You mean?" / "Where our
  father lies.")**: this stretch of very short, clipped exchanges reads as
  genuinely choppy in isolation — short fragments trading one or two words
  at a time. This is a deliberate feature of the Greek stichomythic lament
  form, not a translation flaw, but flagging per the instruction to note
  natural-flow issues regardless of cause: a reader unfamiliar with the
  convention may find this passage disorienting rather than moving on first
  read.

## What reads unusually well

- The dialogue throughout (Prologue, the Theseus/Oedipus exchanges in
  Chapters 3 and 5, the Creon confrontation in Chapter 7, the Polyneices
  scene in Chapter 9) is clear, natural, contemporary prose — pronouns
  resolve easily, sentence lengths vary naturally, and the emotional beats
  land without archaism getting in the way. This is a marked strength
  compared to the choral odes.
- The Messenger's final speech in the Exodos (Ch. 11) is genuinely gripping
  and reads almost like plain narrative prose despite covering a mystical,
  emotionally heavy event — a strong piece of modernization.
- The in-line glossing of "Areopagus" (Ch. 8) and the Seven-Against-Thebes
  wordplay on Parthenopaeus's name (Ch. 9) show the drafter handling
  difficult allusive material well when the source itself gives room to do
  so.

## Overall verdict

**Needs targeted fixes** — not a broad rewrite. The dialogue-heavy episodes
(roughly 8 of the 11 chapters) are already highly accessible. The friction is
concentrated and predictable: the choral odes (Chapters 2, 6, 7-tailpiece, 8,
10) carry a dense, mostly unglossed load of mythological proper nouns and
epithets, and Chapter 9's catalog of the Seven Against Thebes is a similar
concentrated block of unfamiliar names. This matches the profile already
seen in the sibling play (Oedipus Rex): dialogue reads very well, odes carry
the difficulty. None of the flagged passages are grammatically broken or
misleading — they are dense with names a general reader has no way to
resolve on their own. A targeted pass (glosses, either in-text or via
onboarding/footnote apparatus) on the identified ode and catalog passages
would meaningfully raise accessibility without touching the strong dialogue
prose.
