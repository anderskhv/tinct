# Accessibility Review — Antigone (candidate modern-en), Round 1

**Reviewer stance:** Blind — source text and any drafter notes were not consulted. Judged purely as English prose a general adult reader would encounter today.

**Coverage:** All 11 chapters, all 318 paragraphs read in full, start to finish, then re-passed paragraph by paragraph: Ch1 Prologue (paras 0-29), Ch2 Parodos (0-4), Ch3 First Episode (0-33), Ch4 First Stasimon (0-4), Ch5 Second Episode (0-71), Ch6 Second Stasimon (0-11), Ch7 Third Episode (0-39), Ch8 Third Stasimon (0-5), Ch9 Fourth Episode (0-52), Ch10 Fourth Stasimon (0-17), Ch11 Exodos (0-42). No sampling — every paragraph was read.

---

## What's already working

The four dialogue-driven episodes (First, Second, Third, Fourth) and the Exodos are, on the whole, genuinely accessible. Sentence structure is largely modern subject-verb-object, stichomythic exchanges (the rapid Creon/Antigone and Creon/Haemon back-and-forth in Ch5 and Ch7) read cleanly and land their point-counterpoint rhythm without archaic scaffolding, and the Guard's and Messengers' narrative speeches (Ch3 paras 11/19, Ch11 paras 1/13) are vivid, plainly told, and easy to follow start to finish. The Prologue (Ch1) is close to fully clear prose throughout.

## Issues found

### The choral odes (Ch2, Ch4, Ch6, Ch9 lyric sections, Ch10) are the concentrated difficulty

This is where a new reader will struggle, and it's a cluster, not scattered instances:

- **Ch2 [0]** "how fair your light shone over **Dirce's fountain**" — unglossed place name; reader has no way to know this is a Theban spring.
- **Ch2 [0]** "the aspiring lord of **Argos**" — refers to Polyneices without naming him; a reader who doesn't already know the myth will lose track of who is attacking Thebes.
- **Ch2 [1]** "forced back by **the Dragon**" — capitalized with no explanation. This reads as a random monster reference; it's actually an allusion to the serpent from Thebes' founding myth (armed men sprang from its sown teeth), but nothing in the text signals that.
- **Ch2 [4]** "Bacchus, shaker of the ground!" — "shaker of the ground" is normally Poseidon's epithet; applying it to Bacchus/Dionysus with no gloss will read as either a mistake or an obscure allusion a general reader can't resolve.
- **Ch6 [6]** "On the house of **Labdacus** I see descending woe" — unglossed proper noun; this is Antigone's family line (Laius, Oedipus), but a first-time reader has no anchor for the name.
- **Ch6 [5]** "the **Etesian** winds from Thrace" — obscure meteorological/geographic term with no context clue to its meaning (seasonal Mediterranean winds).
- **Ch9 [5]** "**Tantalus' doomed child**, chained upon the high rocky crags of **Sipylus**" — this is Niobe, but she is never named; a reader is left trying to identify "Tantalus' doomed child" from description alone, with an unfamiliar place name (Sipylus) layered on top.
- **Ch9 [21]** "So too was **Dryas' child**, the rash Edonian king" — this is Lycurgus of Thrace, again never named. Combined with "Edonian" (an obscure Thracian tribe name), this sentence is very hard for a general reader to parse even after several readings.
- **Ch9 [22]-[23]** "By the **Iron Rocks**... on the lonely strand of **Bosporus**, where **Salmydessus'** plain stretches out... a daughter of **Boreas**" — a dense cluster of unglossed place names and mythological figures (Phineus' sons and Cleopatra, daughter of Boreas, though none of these names appear) describing a third exemplum. By this point a reader has been asked to identify three different mythological analogues to Antigone's fate (Danae, Niobe/"Tantalus' child," and the sons of Phineus/"Dryas' child") with almost none of them named outright. This is the single densest accessibility problem in the play.
- **Ch10 [11]-[15]** — the closing hymn to Bacchus stacks unglossed place names in quick succession: "Ismenus," "the Eleusinian Queen," "Corycian maidens," "the springs of Castaly," "Nysa's bastion." None are explained, and a reader with no classical background will not be able to visualize what's being invoked.

### Other specific phrases

- **Ch1 [5]** "a feast for **kites** to scent from far away" — "kite" here means a bird of prey, but a modern reader's first association is the toy; the sentence doesn't disambiguate.
- **Ch4 [2]** "the civic wit to live by counsel" — "civic wit" is an odd, compressed phrase; it isn't clear on first read that it means something like "the political intelligence to live within a community." Reads as choppy rather than dense.
- **Ch7 [5]** "I could not **arraign** your wisdom even if I tried" — "arraign" is now almost exclusively a legal-charging term; used here to mean "challenge/indict," it will likely stop a general reader.
- **Ch7 [5]** "The sailor who keeps his **mainsheet** taut and won't slacken in a gale is liable to find himself sailing keel-up" — nautical jargon ("mainsheet") without support; the general image (over-rigidity capsizes you) comes through from context, but the specific term will puzzle non-sailors.
- **Ch9 [33]** "these were the **frustrate signs** of oracles grown dumb" — "frustrate" is used here as an archaic adjective (meaning "thwarted/frustrated"), a grammatical form that has fallen out of modern use entirely. It reads as a typo or an odd word choice rather than an intelligible phrase; it's an archaic island in an otherwise modern speech.
- **Ch10 [2]-[3]** Two consecutive lines are both tagged **CHORUS**: "Son of Menoeceus, listen to good advice." followed immediately by "What should I do? Advise me — I will heed you." The second line reads exactly like Creon's expected reply (and Creon does reply to it at [4] as if he'd said it), but it's attributed to the Chorus. A blind reader will very likely misread this as a speaker-tag error and get confused about who is speaking to whom for the rest of this short exchange.
- **Ch3 [11]** "Conscience plied the spur and the bridle by turns" — a vivid but slightly compressed metaphor (Conscience urging him on, then holding him back); most readers will work it out from context but it costs a re-read.
- **Ch5 [19]** "as the hardest iron, **over-heated** in the fire to brittleness, flies apart first into fragments, shivered through" — long embedded simile; parseable but dense, one of the few places outside the odes where a sentence needs a second pass.

## Verdict

**Needs targeted fixes.** The dialogue scenes (Prologue, all four Episodes, Exodos) are substantially accessible as-is and need at most light touch-ups (the "frustrate," "arraign," "mainsheet," and "kites" points above). The real accessibility burden is concentrated in the choral odes — Ch2, Ch6, Ch9's lyric mythological exempla, and Ch10's closing hymn — where a cluster of unglossed proper nouns and un-named mythological figures will leave a general reader unable to follow who or what is being referred to. This matches the pattern already seen in Oedipus Rex and Oedipus at Colonus in this same batch: friction concentrated in the choral odes' mythological references, not in the spoken dialogue. The Ch10 [2]-[3] speaker-attribution point is a separate, narrower issue worth flagging to the merge owner even though (per the fidelity review) it originates in the source text itself.
