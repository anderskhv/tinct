# Blind tests of the first two drafts (both superseded by P3)

Fresh Sonnet readers were given only the packet. Answers are recorded verbatim.

## P1: "Perfectly well," (`04-blind-corrected-P1-packet.txt`)

1. Jane is disagreeing/refusing. She says: "I repeat I freely consent to go with you as your fellow-missionary, but not as your wife; I cannot marry you and become part of you." She's rejecting marriage while still agreeing to go along as a colleague/companion, not a wife.

2. Yes — one sentence read awkwardly: "How can I, a man not yet thirty, take out with me to India a girl of nineteen, unless she be married to me?" The archaic "take out with me" and "unless she be married" phrasing feels stilted/unnatural for a "modern reading edition." Also mildly awkward: "It is what I want," he said, speaking to himself — the "speaking to himself" tag is a bit clunky mid-dialogue.

*Lead note: this reader answered about 34.137, not the reply under test. The test is inconclusive for P1. The source-fidelity review rejected P1 on other grounds.*

## P2: "We could be together very well," (`04-blind-corrected-P2-packet.txt`)

1. Jane is disagreeing/refusing marriage. Her line "We could be together very well... quite as well as if I were either your real sister, or a man and a clergyman like yourself" argues that they don't need to be married to travel and work together — she's rejecting his claim that marriage is the only way, not agreeing to marry him.

2. Yes — this sentence is awkward/unnatural: "There would be recesses in my mind which would be only mine, …" (34.134, outside this review's scope).

*Lead note: P2 was understood correctly. It was rejected only because the fidelity review found that "could" softens Jane's assertion.*
