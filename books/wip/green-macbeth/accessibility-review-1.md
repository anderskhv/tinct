# Macbeth — Accessibility Review (Round 1)

**Reviewer stance:** Candidate-only, blind to source (per TRANSLATION_PROTOCOL.md
step A). Read `candidate.json` start to finish, all 28 chapters (Act/Scene
units), all paragraphs, no sampling.

**What I read:** Every paragraph of `candidate.json`, chapters 1–28
(Act 1 Scene 1 through Act 5 Scene 8), in full, in reading order. Indexing
below is 1-based chapter number as stored in the JSON (`chapter.number`),
0-based paragraph index within each chapter's `paragraphs` array.

## Overall impression

The modern-English rendering reads clearly and idiomatically throughout.
Sentence structure is rebuilt into ordinary contemporary syntax without
becoming choppy or list-like. Verse has been converted into readable prose
while retaining strong imagery. This is a genuinely strong draft on
accessibility grounds — no paragraph struck me as opaque, and I did not
need to consult the source to understand any passage as a first-time
reader.

## Specific observations (not blocking, mostly praise/notes)

- The Witches' incantatory language (ch1, ch3, ch18) is rendered with its
  ritual repetition intact ("I'll work my mischief, I'll work my mischief,
  I'll work my mischief"; the "three times... three times... three times
  more" counting-chant; "Double, double, toil and trouble" kept verbatim
  as the most famous line in the play). It reads as chant, not flattened
  into ordinary prose — this was a named risk for this book and it holds
  up.
- The Porter's scene (ch10) keeps its bawdy, rambling comic register and
  is easy to follow for a modern reader unfamiliar with "equivocator" or
  Jacobean tailor jokes — each joke is glossed just enough to land.
- The "out, damned spot" sleepwalking scene (ch21) reads with full
  emotional weight; nothing is softened or clinically flattened.
- Violence is rendered plainly and without euphemism throughout —
  "he split him open from the navel to the jaw," "his throat is cut,"
  the murder of Lady Macduff's son, Macbeth's beheading at the end. A
  new reader is not shielded from the play's brutality.
- Distinctive voices are differentiated: the Witches' riddling cadence,
  the Porter's comic patter, the Doctor's clinical caution, Macbeth's
  increasingly fragmented, imagery-heavy syntax in Act 5 versus his
  earlier more controlled rhetoric.
- A few passages that a first-time reader might still find dense purely
  because of the density of the underlying idea (not wording), e.g.
  Malcolm's self-slander catalogue in ch20 (Act 4 Sc 3) and Ross's "poor
  country... our grave" speech in the same chapter — but these read
  clearly at the sentence level; the difficulty is conceptual/political,
  inherent to the scene, not an accessibility defect in the prose.
