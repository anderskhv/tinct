# Style note: *The Awakening* (Kate Chopin), Tinct Modern English

- **Book:** `the-awakening`
- **Edition:** `modern-en`
- **Date:** 2026-10-06

This note governs every renderer, reviser and reviewer of this book.

**Fidelity anchor:** `served-original-en.json`, Chopin's English original (1899). The task is a modernization of that English, not a new text. No other edition was consulted.

## What this edition is

A paragraph-by-paragraph rendering of the 1899 novel into plain contemporary English. It keeps every event, every gesture, every line of dialogue, every image and Chopin's cool, ironic, sensuous voice. It removes avoidable barriers (period diction, stacked relative clauses, long semicolon chains, stock Victorian phrasing). It does not explain, soften or update the story.

## Voice and sentences

- **Narrator.** Third person, past tense, restrained and observing; irony is carried by plain statement ("It was Sunday; the papers were a day old"). Keep the irony, the understatement and the narrator's distance. Free indirect thought stays free indirect: do not add "she thought" or "she felt", and do not turn it into explicit explanation.
- **Interiority.** Edna's moods, dreams and half-understood impulses are written in images and indirection. Keep the image (the sea's "seductive voice", the "hopeless" sunlight, the bird with the broken wing) and its vagueness. Never name an emotion more clearly than Chopin does.
- **Sentences.** Expected depth: most sentences are restructured, not merely respelled. Split sentences that run past about 35 words or that hold two or more "which/who/that" clauses; turn "which was" and "who had" relatives into participles or short clauses; replace inversions ("Seating himself in a wicker rocker which was there") with natural order; trade period phrasings ("a trifle", "withal", "in a measure", "upon the instant", "bestir", "betake") for their ordinary equivalents. Keep a sentence as it is when it is already plain modern English; do not reword it just to lower the score. Short sentences stay short. Keep a few of Chopin's rhythmic lists and repetitions.
- **Target.** Across a chapter, about two thirds of the word sequences should differ from the source (the finished book should land around 0.60-0.72 similarity). A chapter that reads as the source with spelling fixes has failed; a chapter in which every clear sentence is gratuitously reworded has also failed. Output length is normally 85-105% of the source.
- **Connectives** (but, yet, for, though, and then, as if) stay where they carry a turn of feeling.

## Force

Hedges and judgments keep their strength ("perhaps", "almost", "seemed", "a little", "somewhat"). Keep Chopin's "seemed" and "as if"; do not turn a seeming into a fact or a fact into a seeming. Do not sharpen Edna's rebellion or soften Mr. Pontellier's, Madame Ratignolle's or the doctor's views.

## No additions

No commentary, notes, headings, stage directions, glosses or modern framing. A period reference may be clarified only inside the sentence, in a few words, where the text supports it (for example "panties" in the sense of the era's children's drawers may become "drawers"; the "cab" or "coupé" stays what it is).

## Structure 1:1

39 chapters, 1,044 paragraphs; chapter numbers and `title` fields ("Chapter 1" ... "Chapter 39") exactly as served. Output paragraph N renders source paragraph N and begins with the same content. Never merge, split, reorder, drop or invent a paragraph, including one-line paragraphs (41 paragraphs are under six words). The longest source paragraph has 255 words; keep it one paragraph.

## Dialogue and speech

- Dialogue stays in curly double quotes, spoken by the same speaker, in the same order, with the same tags ("he said", "she inquired"). Tags may be modernized ("inquired" to "asked" where it adds nothing, "exclaimed" kept when it is an exclamation).
- Chopin does **not** use eye-dialect spelling for the Creole, Spanish or French-speaking characters (the source has no "ze", "dat" and similar spellings). Never invent any. Speech is rendered as plain, natural modern English that keeps each speaker's register: Robert's playful gallantry, Madame Ratignolle's sweet candor, Mademoiselle Reisz's curtness, Mr. Pontellier's proprietary brusqueness, Victor's boyish swagger, the doctor's grave courtesy.
- Contractions are allowed in speech where the source has them or where the speaker's register is casual; narration stays uncontracted unless the source contracts.
- Period exclamations and forms ("Bon Dieu!", "Sapristi!", "Tiens!", "ma foi") stay. Mild English oaths and interjections ("My!", "Good heavens", "for pity's sake") may be modernized only if they would now mislead; keep their strength.

## Foreign words and phrases

French (and the few Spanish or Creole items) are **kept exactly as printed, with accents, and never glossed or translated**, unless the source itself glosses them inside the same sentence (keep that gloss). Keep: *Allez vous-en!*, *Sapristi!*, *Pauvre chérie*, *Tiens!*, *Voilà que...*, *Bon Dieu*, *ma reine*, *ma foi*, *chambres garnies*, *soirée(s)*, *coupé*, *chère*, *chérie*, *garçon*, *negligé*, *ménage*, *régime* and titles of music and songs. Do not italicize or add markup. Do not translate English words into French or the reverse.

## Quotations, citations and quoted matter

One decision: quoted speech and any sung, read or recited matter (songs, a letter, the guests' toasts, titles) is modernized only in the lead-in and tags; quoted prose speech itself is rendered in natural modern English with the speaker's meaning unchanged. Quoted verse or song lines and the titles of musical works (Zampa, Chopin's preludes, Impromptu, Liszt, Wagner, "Il était un petit navire") are kept in served wording. Citation data does not occur.

## Notes, markers and special elements

None in the source: no footnotes, no brackets, no asterisks, no speaker labels, no verse paragraphs, no numbered sections. Do not add any.

## Typography

Matches the served original and the live modern edition:
- Curly double quotes “ ” for speech and quoted titles; curly single quotes ‘ ’ only for a quotation nested inside. No straight quotes.
- Curly apostrophe ’.
- Dashes: unspaced em dash in the source becomes a spaced em dash " — ". Hyphens stay in compounds. "mocking-bird" becomes "mockingbird", "to-day" "today", "any one" "anyone", "eye-glasses" "glasses" or "eyeglasses".
- American spelling (honor, color, gray, toward).
- No ellipses beyond those in the source (the source has none). No newlines inside a paragraph.

## Things that must not happen

1. Paragraph count or chapter count changes.
2. Dialogue moved to the wrong speaker, merged with narration, or its quote marks dropped.
3. Invented dialect spellings, or French glossed or translated, or French accents lost.
4. "Madame", "Monsieur" or "Mademoiselle" turned into "Mrs.", "Mr." or "Miss", or a first name replaced by "Mrs. Pontellier".
5. Edna's interior states stated more plainly or analytically than the source ("she felt a sense of liberation" for an image).
6. The ironic understatement flattened into earnest commentary; modern slang or clichés ("went through the motions", "empowered").
7. Plot facts altered: ages, days, the Sunday setting, the dinner, the swim, Robert's departure, the suicide ending (keep its ambiguity, nothing added).
8. The source copied verbatim for long, clearly dated paragraphs, or the entire edition left at the live text's level (563 of 1,044 live paragraphs are changed, but the changes are mostly spelling and light phrasing; they must be deepened).
