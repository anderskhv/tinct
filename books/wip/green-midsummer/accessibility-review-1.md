# Accessibility Review 1 — A Midsummer Night's Dream (`midsummer-modern-en`)

**Reviewer:** blind pass, candidate.json only (source not consulted for this review).
**Coverage:** All 9 chapters, all 641 paragraphs read in full, start to finish, in
order (chapter paragraph counts verified by script: ch1=59, ch2=43, ch3=50, ch4=43,
ch5=85, ch6=141, ch7=68, ch8=18, ch9=134; sum=641). No paragraph skimmed or skipped.

## Overall verdict

**Substantially accessible.** This is already a genuinely modernized, idiomatic
rendering — not a light spelling-cleanup. Sentence structure is clear, archaic
grammar (thee/thou/doth/wherefore etc.) is fully rebuilt into ordinary contemporary
English, and long Shakespearean periodic sentences are broken into readable units
without becoming choppy. A general adult reader could read this straight through
with only a handful of genuine stumbling points, listed below. Most of what could
look "hard" at a glance (mythological names, fairy lore, Athenian law) is inherent
subject matter, not translation difficulty, and the candidate does not add glosses
that name anything the text itself leaves unstated.

## Specific accessibility issues

1. **Ch1 ¶39 (Egeus's complaint)** — one very long single-sentence paragraph
   listing gifts ("bracelets made of your hair, rings, trinkets, knickknacks,
   little gifts, flowers, and sweets") followed immediately by the legal threat.
   It is grammatically clear throughout, but a reader may need to re-read once to
   keep track that "all powerful tools of persuasion" refers back to the whole
   list. Non-blocking — flows better than it looks at first glance because each
   clause is short.

2. **Ch1 ¶87 (Helena's soliloquy)** — a dense, extended argument about love and
   perception ("Love doesn't see with the eyes but with the imagination...").
   The ideas are abstract but each sentence is short and declarative; a reader
   may want to slow down here, but nothing is actually unclear. Non-blocking.

3. **Ch2, Bottom's "Ercles" speech (¶12)** — "I could play Hercules brilliantly...
   the sun god's chariot shall shine from far" reads as perfectly clear plain
   English. (Flagging here only because a reader who somehow already knows this
   play may notice Bottom's comic mangled names — "Ercles," "Phibbus" — are gone;
   as pure prose on its own it is not confusing. See fidelity review for why this
   still needs a fix.)

4. **Ch2 ¶10 and later, French-crown joke ("Some of those French crowns have no
   hair at all")** — this pun relies on knowing "French crown" was period slang
   for baldness from disease; a modern reader won't get why baldness follows from
   the beard-color list. This is inherent to the joke's historical basis, not a
   translation defect — flagging as a genuine but non-blocking difficulty (the
   text plays fair; the reader just needs unavailable period context, and adding
   an explanatory gloss here would mean inventing/explaining a fact the source
   itself doesn't spell out).

5. **Ch5 ¶16 (Cobweb/Peaseblossom names joke, "if I cut my finger, I'll be sure to
   call on you for a bandage")** — relies on knowing cobwebs were a folk remedy
   for stopping bleeding. Same category as #4: inherent, not a wording problem.

6. **Ch8 ¶6–7 (Quince/Flute exchange)** — "QUINCE: ...he's an absolute dream when
   it comes to a sweet voice. FLUTE: You mean 'paragon.' A 'paramour' is — God
   help us — something indecent." **This is a genuine accessibility break**: Flute
   is visibly correcting a word Quince never actually said in the candidate text
   ("paramour" doesn't appear in Quince's line), so the joke reads as a non
   sequitur. A reader will be confused about what Flute is even responding to.
   Flagged here as a reader-facing problem; the fidelity review below traces the
   cause and required fix.

7. **Ch9, the mechanicals' play-within-the-play (Pyramus and Thisbe verses,
   roughly ¶40–121)** — intentionally terrible amateur verse in the source. The
   candidate's plainer, smoother wording is very easy to read, which is good for
   accessibility, but a reader with no other signal may not register that this
   section is supposed to sound bad on purpose (that's the joke — the audience
   on stage laughing at it). This is a judgment call rather than an accessibility
   defect per se; noting it because "natural flow" here arguably undersells the
   deliberate badness, though nothing is actually hard to read.

## What reads unusually well

- The four lovers' quarrel in Act 3, Scene 2 (ch6) is long (141 paragraphs) but
  moves briskly; insults ("you painted flagpole," "you bead, you acorn") land
  clearly without needing footnotes.
- Bottom's transformation and the fairies' names sequence (ch5, ch7) reads
  warmly and comically — "I'm such a sensitive donkey that if even one hair
  tickles me, I have to scratch" is genuinely funny in plain modern English.
- Theseus's "lunatic, the lover, and the poet" speech (ch9 ¶2) is one of the
  hardest passages in the source to modernize without flattening it, and this
  version keeps its shape and rhetorical build while being fully readable.
- Puck's closing epilogue (ch9, final paragraph) lands its rhythm well in prose
  form without needing rhyme to carry the meaning.

## Summary

The candidate is close to accessible-as-is. One clear defect (item 6, the
paramour/paragon exchange) actively confuses a reader and must be fixed. Items 4
and 5 are inherent-difficulty, non-blocking. No other paragraph in the 641
required a second read to parse.
