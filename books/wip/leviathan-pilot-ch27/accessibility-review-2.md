# Accessibility Review — Round 2 (Reviewer A)

**Chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 26 ("Of Civil Laws") — pilot chapter, `candidate-sonnet.json`

**Coverage:** Read all 48 paragraphs, in full, start to finish, in order (array
indices correspond to paragraphs 1–48 below; paragraph 1 = "By CIVIL LAWS I
mean...", paragraph 48 = "Likewise, laws and charters are interchangeably
taken..."). No source text, drafter's notes, or round-1 review were consulted,
per instructions.

This is a fresh read, not a diff against round 1 (I was not shown round 1's
findings). It reports the chapter's current state as encountered by a general
reader today.

---

## Issues found

**Paragraph 4** — "In this definition there is nothing that is not at first
sight evident... He sees also that laws are the rules of just and unjust,
since nothing is reputed unjust that is not contrary to some law." Two
stacked double-negative constructions in close succession ("nothing... not...
evident"; "nothing... not contrary to some law"). Logically fine, but a
reader has to mentally flip the negatives to parse them — a candidate for a
quick rephrase into positive form ("everything in this definition is
evident at first sight..."; "an act is unjust only when it's contrary to
some law").

**Paragraph 6** — "For he is free who can be free whenever he wills." This
is a compressed, aphoristic one-liner that a reader will likely have to
re-read to unpack (it's asserting that anyone with the power to release
himself from a rule was never really bound by it). Not wrong, just dense —
flagging per the prompt's instruction not to give a pass to short sentences
that read choppily.

**Paragraph 7** — "the length of time shall be no prejudice to his right."
"Prejudice" is used in its legal sense (harm/detriment to a legal claim), but
a modern general reader's first association with "prejudice" is bias/
discrimination, which would misdirect the reading of this sentence.

**Paragraph 12** — "Over natural fools, children, or madmen there is no
law." "Natural fools" is 17th-century legal/clinical terminology for people
with cognitive disabilities (paired here with "madmen" as the era's other
legal incapacity category). Left unglossed, a modern reader is likely to
read it as simply "foolish people," which changes the meaning of the
sentence — it's about legal incapacity to contract/covenant, not about
poor judgment.

**Paragraph 14** — "if a law obliges only some condition of men." "Condition
of men" (meaning class/rank/status) is an archaic sense of "condition" that
a modern reader will likely misread as a physical or medical condition.

**Paragraph 21** — "there is still another very important condition wanting
before they are binding." "Condition wanting" (= a condition that is
lacking/missing) is an inverted, archaic construction; a modern reader
expects "wanting" to mean "desiring," not "absent."

**Paragraph 25** — "The interpreter of the law is the judge, giving sentence
*viva voce* in every particular case." This is the one Latin term in the
whole chapter left completely unglossed. Every other Latin term in the
chapter (*civitas*, *Persona Civitatis*, *Rex In Parlamento*, *juris
prudentia*, *Responsa Prudentum*, *Lex Civilis*/*Jus Civile*, *jubeo,
injungo*, *dedi, concessi*) gets an immediate English gloss in the same
sentence. *Viva voce* does not, which makes it stand out as a gap in an
otherwise consistent policy — worth a quick "(by word of mouth)" or similar.

**Paragraph 28** (the long paragraph on judicial error and the fleeing-felon
example) — three separate issues:
- "no judge, subordinate or sovereign, **is incapable of erring** in a
  judgement of equity" — another stacked double negative ("no judge... is
  incapable of..." = every judge is capable of erring); worth flipping to
  a direct positive statement.
- "nay, heaven and earth shall pass, but not one **tittle** of the law of
  nature shall pass" — "tittle" (a tiny stroke/part, as in "jot or tittle")
  is unglossed and largely unknown to modern readers outside the King James
  idiom it echoes.
- "Put the case now that a man is accused of a capital crime, and, seeing
  the power and malice of some enemy, and the frequent corruption and
  partiality of judges, runs away for fear of the outcome — and is
  afterwards taken, brought to a legal trial, makes it sufficiently appear
  that he was not guilty of the crime, and being acquitted of it, is
  nevertheless condemned to lose his goods." One sentence carrying the
  entire hypothetical (accusation → flight → capture → trial → acquittal →
  forfeiture) strung together with commas and participial clauses. It's
  legal, not ungrammatical, but a reader has to hold five sequential events
  in mind before the payoff clause lands — a natural candidate to split into
  two sentences without losing anything.

**Paragraph 29** — "commentaries are commonly more open to **cavil** than
the text." "Cavil" (a trivial/carping objection) is an uncommon word for a
general reader today and isn't glossed by context as cleanly as most other
archaic terms in this chapter are.

**Paragraph 31** — "Second, contempt of unnecessary riches and
**preferments**." "Preferments" (promotions/advancements to office) is
archaic and not glossed; a modern reader may not recognize it as a noun
meaning "advancement" at all.

**Paragraph 22** (minor) — "as Alexander did with his sword in the Gordian
knot" compresses the familiar "cut the Gordian knot" idiom awkwardly — "did
... in the knot" reads slightly oddly even for a reader who knows the
reference. Low priority; most readers will still get the gist.

## What's working well

The chapter is, on the whole, in strong shape. Specific strengths worth
flagging so they're not disturbed in a later pass:

- **Paragraph 43** (divine positive law) and **paragraph 44** (the Abraham
  covenant example) are genuinely well done — dense theological-legal
  argument rendered as clear, flowing prose with rhetorical questions that
  read naturally rather than as translated legalese.
- **Paragraph 46**'s fundamental-law definition ("like a building whose
  foundation has been destroyed") is a clean, accessible analogy.
- The numbered "conclusions" structure (paragraphs 5–12) and the Institutes-
  of-Justinian list (paragraphs 33–39) are both easy to follow — each item
  is short, self-contained, and the historical comparisons ("Something like
  these laws are the Acts of Parliament in England") are handled
  consistently and helpfully throughout.
- Latin-term glossing is handled well everywhere except the one *viva voce*
  gap noted above — the pattern of Latin-term-then-immediate-English-
  gloss is otherwise applied consistently and is genuinely useful.
- Scripture citations use modern chapter:verse notation (Prov. 7:3, Deut.
  11:19, Deut. 31:12, Gen. 17:10) rather than leaving readers to guess at
  references — good accessibility choice.
- The long paragraphs (8, 16, 20, 28, 30, 31) are dense but for the most
  part genuinely well broken up internally with clear topic sentences; only
  paragraph 28's one hypothetical-case sentence and the double-negative
  constructions noted above actually cause avoidable friction.

## Verdict

**Needs targeted fixes** — not a broad pass.

**Explicit answer to the "remaining difficulty" question:** the great
majority of what remains hard in this chapter is unavoidable conceptual
complexity that's inherent to Hobbes's argument (the interlocking of civil
law and natural law in paragraph 8, the multi-step case analysis in
paragraph 28, the chain of reasoning about divine positive law in
paragraphs 43–44) and correctly preserved rather than simplified away. That
material is dense because the ideas are dense, not because the prose is
poorly rendered.

However, there is a real, avoidable residue of specific wording that a
targeted pass could fix without touching the argument at all:

1. Six unglossed or misleading archaic/legal terms: "prejudice" (¶7),
   "natural fools" (¶12), "condition of men" (¶14), "condition wanting"
   (¶21), "tittle" (¶28), "cavil" (¶29), "preferments" (¶31) — that's seven,
   listed above with exact wording and location.
2. Three double-negative or inverted constructions that force a re-read:
   ¶4 (two stacked), ¶6 ("free who can be free whenever he wills"), ¶28
   ("is incapable of erring").
3. One overloaded single-sentence hypothetical in ¶28 that could be split
   into two sentences with zero loss of meaning.
4. One minor idiom compression in ¶22.

None of these require rewriting the argument or softening Hobbes's content
— they're vocabulary swaps, gloss insertions, and sentence splits. Once
addressed, this chapter would be in "substantially accessible" territory;
as it stands now it's close but not quite there.
