# Accessibility Review — Reviewer A (fresh read, candidate only)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan pilot, Chapter 27 ("Chapter 26. Of Civill Lawes") — modern-English reading edition, general adult reader.

**Coverage:** I read all 48 paragraphs of `candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to finish, then went back through paragraph by paragraph. No sampling. Paragraph indices below are 1-based positions in the `paragraphs` array (1 = "By CIVIL LAWS I mean..." through 48 = "Likewise, laws and charters are commonly taken for the same thing...").

I was not shown the source text or any drafter's notes, and did not seek them out. This review judges the candidate purely as English prose.

---

## Issues found, by paragraph

**Paragraph 5** — "none but the sovereign can abrogate a law once made; because a law is not abrogated except by another law"
*Problem:* "abrogate" is a low-frequency legal word for a general reader. Not fatal (context makes the meaning inferable), but avoidable — the same chapter later uses "repeal" as a plain-English synonym (paragraph 6: "repealing the laws that trouble him"), so the vocabulary choice is inconsistent as well as unnecessarily formal.
*Avoidable wording*, not conceptual complexity.

**Paragraph 6** — "For he is free who can be free whenever he wills."
*Problem:* This sentence is circular on the page — it defines "free" using "free" — and a reader has to reconstruct the underlying logic (the sovereign counts as already free because he could free himself at any moment) rather than read it directly off the sentence.
*Mixed:* the underlying argument is Hobbes' own paradox-shaped point (somewhat unavoidable), but the sentence could be unpacked more plainly without losing the point — so the *phrasing* is avoidably terse even though the *idea* is inherently a little paradoxical.

**Paragraph 8** — "In the disputes of private men, to declare what is equity, what is justice, and what is moral virtue, and to make these declarations binding, requires the ordinances of the sovereign power and the punishments to be ordained for those who break them; and these ordinances are therefore part of the civil law."
*Problem:* One sentence carries an infinitive subject clause, two embedded lists, and a conclusion — grammatically legal but a lot to hold in working memory before the payoff clause arrives. A reader is likely to have to re-read it once.
*Avoidable sentence construction* — the content itself (law of nature = part of civil law) is not simple, but this specific sentence could be split into two without losing anything.

**Paragraph 10** — "the controller of laws is not Parlamentum but Rex In Parlamento."
*Problem:* Every other Latin term in this chapter gets an immediate English gloss (e.g., para 1 "civitas, which means a commonwealth"; para 47 "Lex Civilis and Jus Civile — that is, civil law and civil right"; para 48 "jubeo, injungo — 'I command,' 'I enjoin'"). Here, "Parlamentum" and "Rex In Parlamento" are left untranslated, breaking the chapter's own established pattern and forcing a reader with no Latin to guess ("King in Parliament").
*Avoidable* — an internal inconsistency, not an unavoidable difficulty, since the fix pattern already exists elsewhere in the same chapter.

**Paragraph 12** — "Over natural fools, children, or madmen there is no law, any more than there is over brute beasts"
*Problem:* "natural fools" is a period term (persons born without the capacity for reason/consent) that a modern reader may misread as "foolish people" rather than its intended narrower legal-category sense. It's also, by modern standards, a jarring phrase to hit without any signal that it's a historical term of art.
*Unavoidable-leaning:* this is a real historical legal category Hobbes is naming, so some archaic flavor is arguably appropriate; but a brief in-line cue (e.g., "the severely mentally incapacitated") would have removed the ambiguity — so I'd call the *concept* unavoidable but the bare unglossed phrase avoidably confusing.

**Paragraph 13** — "'Do not that to another which you would think it unreasonable to be done by another to yourself.'"
*Problem:* This is the Golden Rule, presented as a direct quotation, but left in archaic syntax ("Do not that to another" — missing "do," which a modern reader has to supply). Per the review instructions, quotation status doesn't excuse this: a reader will stumble on "Do not that."
*Avoidable* — the quotation could easily read "Do not do to another what you would think unreasonable if done to you" without changing the meaning at all.

**Paragraph 18** — "the sentence of the judge who, by commission, has authority to take cognisance of such cases"
*Problem:* "take cognisance of" is stiff, low-frequency legal phrasing. A reader will parse it but it slows the sentence down for no gain in precision over "authority to hear such cases."
*Avoidable wording.*

**Paragraph 28** — the whole paragraph is the chapter's longest and hardest stretch. Two distinct issues:
1. "For though a wrong sentence given by authority of the sovereign — if he knows and allows it, in laws that are mutable — be a constitution of a new law, in cases in which every little circumstance is the same; yet in immutable laws, such as are the laws of nature, such sentences are no laws to the same or to other judges in like cases ever after." Three nested parentheticals and a delayed main clause ("be a constitution of a new law... yet... such sentences are no laws") — a reader plausibly loses the thread and has to re-read.
   *Avoidable sentence construction* on top of *unavoidable* conceptual density (mutable vs. immutable law, precedent-binding vs. not).
2. The block quotation from "a great lawyer": "'If a man,' he says, 'that is innocent be accused of felony, and for fear flees for it; although he judicially acquits himself of the felony, yet if it be found that he fled for the felony, he shall, notwithstanding his innocency, forfeit all his goods, chattels, debts, and duties...'" This quoted passage is left essentially unmodernized — "notwithstanding his innocency" (archaic spelling/construction), "chattels," the whole clause structure — right in the middle of prose that has otherwise been thoroughly modernized. Per the review brief, quotation marks don't exempt this from being flagged.
   *Avoidable* — the surrounding prose proves modernization is possible; this quotation was left as an island of period English.

**Paragraph 29** — "In the same way, when the question is the meaning of written laws, the man who is not the interpreter of them is the one who writes a commentary on them."
*Problem:* This sentence is genuinely ambiguous on a first read. Read literally, "the man who is not the interpreter... is the one who writes a commentary" sounds like it's *defining* the commentary-writer as some third figure, when the actual point (confirmed by the next few sentences about commentaries needing further commentaries) is the opposite emphasis: a commentator is *not* the authoritative interpreter. The reversible "X who is not A is B" construction makes the logical relationship harder to extract than it needs to be.
*Avoidable* — a direct statement ("the person who writes a commentary on the laws is not their interpreter") would remove the ambiguity entirely without changing the meaning.

**Paragraph 43** — the long paragraph on divine positive law and how one can be assured of another's revelation.
*Problem:* This is the single most conceptually demanding argument in the chapter (an epistemological question about how anyone can verify a claimed revelation from God), and it is genuinely hard to follow on one pass — but the prose itself is clear, plainly punctuated, and not overloaded. E.g. "since we have no natural way of recognising that authority as coming from God — how can a man, without a revelation of his own, be assured of the revelation received by the one who declares it?" is a legitimately hard question, worded about as simply as the question allows.
*Unavoidable conceptual complexity* — I would not ask for a rewrite here; the difficulty is the idea, not the sentence.

**Chapter-wide (not paragraph-specific): "equity"**
The word "equity" is used repeatedly (paragraphs 7, 8, 19, 22, 24, 26, 28, 31, 43, 48 among others) as a load-bearing technical term — it's doing real argumentative work (natural fairness as distinct from written law) — but it is never once defined or glossed in plain language anywhere in the 48 paragraphs. A reader can infer roughly "fairness," but the chapter leans on a more precise sense ("the law of nature applied to a particular case") that's never spelled out.
*Avoidable* — a single one-line gloss at its first real use (paragraph 7 or 8) would fix this for the whole chapter; it doesn't need repeating each time.

## Where the chapter reads well

- **Paragraph 3** (the core definition of civil law) is clean and readable — a general reader can hold the whole definition in mind.
- **Paragraph 17** ("'Verified,' I say, not 'authorised'...") is a good example of the chapter's best mode: short, punchy, and precise.
- **Paragraph 20** (public officer's commission) is a clear, concrete worked example — exactly the kind of paragraph that makes the abstract argument land.
- **Paragraph 30** (letter vs. meaning of the law, with the "man thrust out of his house by force" and false-accusation examples) is the strongest stretch in the whole chapter — dense material made concrete and followable through worked examples, with none of the overload seen in paragraph 28.
- **Paragraph 31** (qualities of a good judge) reads naturally and briskly, with a clean four-item list at the end that doesn't feel mechanically chopped.
- The numbered taxonomy of Roman civil-law sources (paragraphs 33–39) consistently pairs each Latin term with an immediate plain-English gloss and an English analogue ("comparable to the Chief Justices in the courts of England") — this is the right pattern, and it's the one paragraph 10's unglossed Latin should have matched.

## Packet-boundary check (paragraphs ~15/16 and ~31/32)

I read both boundaries specifically for terminology drift, register shift, or argument discontinuity.

- **15/16 boundary:** Paragraph 15 ends on the Moses/Deuteronomy example of publishing law; paragraph 16 opens "Nor is it enough that the law be written and published; there must also be plain signs that it proceeds from the will of the sovereign." This is a clean continuation of the same argument thread, same vocabulary ("sovereign," "legislator," "sufficient signs"), same register. **No inconsistency found here.**
- **31/32 boundary:** Paragraph 31 closes the "interpretation of law" section (qualities of a good judge); paragraph 32 opens a new section ("The difference and division of the laws has been made in various ways... In the Institutes of Justinian, we find seven sorts of civil laws"). This is a real topic shift, but it's the source argument's own structural shift, not a drafting seam — register, sentence rhythm, and terminology carry over smoothly (both still say "sovereign," "commonwealth," etc. the same way). **No inconsistency found here either.**
- The one internal consistency problem I did find (unglossed "Parlamentum"/"Rex In Parlamento" in paragraph 10, versus the consistently-glossed Latin everywhere else including paragraphs 33–39 and 47–48) sits entirely inside the first packet, not at a boundary, so it reads as an isolated miss rather than a packet-to-packet drift.
- Overall, I did not detect a vocabulary, tone, or argument-continuity seam at either named boundary. The three packets read as one voice.

---

## Overall verdict: **needs targeted fixes**

This is not a broad-pass chapter — the great majority of the 48 paragraphs (the definitional core, the numbered points on sovereignty and interpretation, the Roman-law taxonomy, the fundamental-law and charter-vs-law sections) are clear, well-paced modern prose that a general reader can follow, and several passages (paragraphs 17, 20, 30, 31) are genuinely good examples of dense legal-philosophical material made accessible through concrete worked examples. The chapter's residual difficulty splits cleanly into two categories:

- **Unavoidable conceptual complexity** (should not be "fixed," just accepted as the cost of the material): paragraph 43's revelation-verification argument, and the general density of paragraph 8's natural-law/civil-law identity argument.
- **Avoidable wording issues** worth a targeted pass: the unmodernized quoted statute language in paragraph 28, the reversible/ambiguous sentence in paragraph 29, the unglossed Latin in paragraph 10 (inconsistent with the chapter's own pattern), the archaic "Do not that to another" in paragraph 13's Golden Rule quotation, a couple of overloaded single sentences (paragraphs 8 and 28), and the never-glossed recurring technical term "equity."

None of these require a structural rewrite or threaten paragraph alignment — they're all local, fixable at the sentence or phrase level, and my recommendation is a targeted fix pass over the ten items above rather than a broader rework of the chapter.
