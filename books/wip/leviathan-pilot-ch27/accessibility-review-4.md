# Accessibility Review — Round 4 (Reviewer A, fresh read)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan, Chapter 26 ("Of Civil Laws") — candidate modern-English edition, "pilot ch27" WIP slot.

**Coverage:** I read all 48 paragraphs of `candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to finish, then went back through paragraph by paragraph. I have not seen the source text, any prior review, or any drafter's notes.

---

## Issues found

### Paragraph 10
> "as if a commonwealth could subsist in which the force lay in any hand which justice had not the authority to command and to govern."

Two stacked relative clauses ("in which the force lay in any hand which justice had not the authority to command and to govern") force a re-read to work out what modifies what. This is the one sentence in the chapter I had to parse twice. Everything else around it (the Parliament/Common Law discussion) reads cleanly.

### Paragraph 13
> "'Do not that to another which you would think it unreasonable to be done by another to yourself.'"

This is presented as the plain-language "single sentence" statement of the law of nature — the one moment in the chapter meant to land with maximum clarity for a general reader — but "Do not that to another" is an archaic verb construction (missing "do": modern English would be "Do not do that to another"). A reader hits a stumble exactly where the text is promising ease. Worth smoothing since it's flagged in-text as the maximally accessible formulation.

### Paragraph 15
> "The law of nature excepted, it belongs to the essence of all other laws to be made known to every man..."

An absolute construction fronting the sentence ("X excepted, ...") is a slightly old-fashioned pattern a modern reader doesn't hit often; it takes a beat to realize "excepted" here means "aside from" rather than a verb acting on what follows. Minor, but it's the sentence that opens a paragraph, so it sets the reader's footing for what follows.

### Paragraph 28 (the flight/forfeiture case study — longest paragraph in the chapter)
- "Put the case now that a man is accused of a capital crime..." — "Put the case now that" is archaic for "Suppose" or "Say a man is accused..."; understandable from context but a genuine period construction, not just formal register.
- "nay, heaven and earth shall pass, but not one tittle..." — "nay" is an archaic interjection (here meaning roughly "indeed" / "more than that"); "tittle" is glossed immediately ("that is, not the smallest stroke"), which works well, but "nay" itself is left unglossed.
- "If the presumption were not of the fact, then for what should he lose his goods?" — "for what" as a stand-alone rhetorical "for what reason" reads stiffly; a modern reader gets there, but it takes a half-beat.

None of these break comprehension — the paragraph's legal argument (a man acquitted of a felony still forfeiting his goods on a presumption drawn from his having fled) comes through — but this is also simply an extremely long, single-paragraph case study with an embedded block quotation and a chain of conditional legal reasoning ("if... then... if not... then..."). Some of its density is structural and can't be fixed by word choice without violating the paragraph-alignment rule.

### Paragraph 31
> "twelve men of the common people are the judges, and give sentence, not only of the fact but of the right, pronouncing simply for the complainant or for the defendant — that is, they are judges not only of the fact but also of the right"

The clause "not only of the fact but of the right" is stated, then restated almost verbatim eight words later ("not only of the fact but also of the right"). It's not confusing, but it reads as an accidental double statement rather than a deliberate rhetorical repetition — the kind of thing a reader notices and re-reads to check they didn't misread the first instance.

### Paragraph 47
> "'Speak to us yourself, and we will listen; but do not let God speak to us, lest we die'"

"lest we die" is a construction general readers mostly know only from biblical or ceremonial English (meaning "so that we do not die" / "for fear that we will die"). Because this is a direct scriptural quotation, and "lest" is still passively recognizable to most adult readers, I'd call this the mildest item on this list — flagging it per the prompt's rule that quotations don't get an automatic pass, but it's a borderline call, not a clear stumble.

---

## What reads well

- The extended discussion of how laws are made known to subjects (paragraphs 16–21) — the "public registers, public counsels, public ministers, and public seals" passage in particular — has genuine rhythm and parallel structure that carries a reader through a long sentence without friction.
- The Roman-law taxonomy (paragraphs 33–39: Edicts, Decrees, Senatus consulta, Responsa Prudentum, etc.) handles its Latin terms well — every one is translated in the same breath it appears, so a reader never has to sit with an unglossed term.
- The worked example of the man locked out of his own house (paragraph 30) is a clean, concrete illustration that lands immediately — one of the most readable stretches in the chapter.
- Paragraph 48 (Lex Civilis vs. Jus Civile, and the law/charter distinction with "jubeo, injungo" vs. "dedi, concessi") is a strong closing passage — the Latin-then-gloss pattern is used consistently and effectively throughout the whole chapter, not just here.
- Paragraphs 43–46 (natural vs. positive, distributive vs. penal, divine positive law) carry a lot of nested conceptual distinctions but the sentence-level prose stays clean; the difficulty there is Hobbes's argument itself, not the wording.

---

## Overall verdict: **substantially accessible**

After three prior rounds of fixes, this chapter is in good shape. I found no vocabulary that's simply obscure, no dangling or ambiguous pronouns, and no sentence I'd call genuinely broken. What's left is:

1. A small number of specific, fixable wording spots (paragraphs 10, 13, 15, 28, 31, 47 above) — mostly single archaic constructions ("nay," "lest," "Put the case now that," "Do not that to another," a fronted absolute clause, one over-nested relative clause, one accidental near-repetition). These are targeted, not systemic — each is a phrase-level fix, not a rewrite.
2. Genuinely unavoidable difficulty that is conceptual/structural rather than wording: the legal-citation apparatus (Coke on Littleton with folio references, Roman law's seven categories, the chain of conditional reasoning in the flight/forfeiture case study), and the sheer length of a few source paragraphs (13 and 28 especially) that can't be shortened without violating paragraph alignment. A general reader will still have to slow down for these — that's Hobbes's legal-philosophical density, not a translation problem.

I would not call this "needs a broader pass." It's close to done; a targeted pass on the six spots above would likely close out the accessibility side of this chapter entirely.
