# Accessibility Review — Round 5 (Reviewer A, fresh/blind)

**Book/chapter:** Leviathan — Chapter 26, "Of Civil Laws" (candidate-sonnet.json)

**Coverage:** I read all 48 paragraphs of `candidate-sonnet.json` in full, start to
finish, then made a second pass paragraph by paragraph. I was not shown the
source text, any prior review, or drafter's notes. Paragraph indices below
are 1-48 in array order (1 = "By CIVIL LAWS I mean...", 48 = "Likewise, laws
and charters are interchangeably taken...").

## Issues found

**Paragraph 6** ("2. The sovereign of a commonwealth...")
> "he may when he pleases free himself from that subjection by repealing the
> laws that trouble him and making new ones; and consequently he was free
> before."
"...he was free before" lands oddly on first read — "before" what? The logic
(he can free himself at will, therefore he was never truly bound in the
first place) only resolves once the reader hits the *next* sentence ("For a
man who can free himself whenever he wills was never truly bound"). A
reader is likely to stop and re-read this clause. Minor but real stumble.

**Paragraph 7** ("3. When long use takes on the authority of a law...")
> "...and it is no longer law than the sovereign shall be silent on it."
Archaic "no longer X than Y" construction, meaning roughly "it remains law
only for as long as the sovereign stays silent about it." A contemporary
reader doesn't use this comparative pattern and will likely have to parse
it twice.

**Paragraph 10** ("6. Since then all laws, written and unwritten...")
> "...if it should assemble ever so many, or ever so wise, men from the
> countries subject to them..."
"ever so many, or ever so wise" is an archaic idiom (= "however many,
however wise"). Not glossed or restructured; will read as a typo or odd
phrasing to most readers today.

**Paragraph 11** ("7. That law can never be against reason...")
> "(as Sir Edward Coke makes it, upon Littleton, lib.2, ch.6, fol.97.b)"
The citation abbreviation "lib.2, ch.6, fol.97.b" is opaque to a general
reader (no gloss for "fol." = folio). This is a smaller issue since it
functions as a footnote-style citation rather than running prose, but it
does interrupt the sentence with unexplained notation.

**Paragraph 13** ("First, if a law obliges all the subjects without
exception...")
> "'Do not do that to another which you would think it unreasonable to have
> done to yourself by another.'"
This is the Golden Rule, but the phrasing is more convoluted than it needs
to be — "that to another which you would think it unreasonable to have
done to yourself by another" stacks an expletive "it," an infinitive, and
two separate uses of "another" in one clause. Readers will likely need a
second pass to parse it, even though they'll recognize the underlying idea
once they do. Worth flagging per the prompt's instruction not to give
quoted material a pass.

**Paragraph 28** (the long paragraph on judicial error / precedent /
the flight-and-forfeiture case)
> "For though a wrong sentence given by authority of the sovereign — if he
> knows and allows it, in laws that are mutable — be a constitution of a new
> law, in cases in which every little circumstance is the same; yet in
> immutable laws, such as are the laws of nature, such sentences are no laws
> to the same or to other judges in like cases ever after."
Two compounding problems: an archaic subjunctive ("though... be a
constitution" rather than "though... is/would be a constitution"), and a
heavily parenthetical structure (two inserted clauses before the main verb
arrives). This is the single densest sentence in the chapter and the most
likely to force a re-read — not because the idea is hard, but because the
grammar is doing more work than it needs to.

**Paragraph 30** ("In written laws, men commonly distinguish between the
letter and the meaning of the law...")
> "...he shall procure from the sovereign that another be made judge, and
> himself become witness."
Same archaic subjunctive pattern ("that another *be* made judge" instead of
"that another be appointed judge" / "have another judge appointed") — minor
on its own, but it's the third instance of this construction in the
chapter (see also paragraphs 28 and 43), suggesting a residual pattern
rather than one-off phrasing.

**Paragraph 31** ("The abilities required in a good interpreter of the
law...")
> "...are not the same as an advocate's, namely deep study of the laws."
Genuinely ambiguous on first read. "Namely" normally restates the phrase
right before it, so a reader can easily parse this as "the abilities
required... are... namely, deep study of the laws" — i.e., missing the
negation — when the intended meaning is "an advocate's abilities are,
namely, deep study of the laws, and a judge's abilities are *not* the
same." This is a real referent trap, not just density.

Same paragraph, later:
> "...is presumed to be most in those who have had the most leisure, and
> the most inclination, to meditate on it."
"is presumed to be most in those who..." is elliptical — "most" is missing
its completing noun (most *present*? most *developed*?). Reads as a small
grammatical gap that stalls the sentence.

Same paragraph, also:
> "...twelve men of the common people are the judges, and give sentence,
> not only of the fact but of the right, pronouncing simply for the
> complainant or for the defendant — that is, they are judges not only of
> the fact but also of the right..."
This isn't hard to understand, but it restates "judges not only of the fact
but of the right" twice in the same sentence with no new information added
in between — a flow issue (stitched-together, per the prompt's guidance on
judging flow and not just density) rather than a vocabulary one.

**Paragraph 43** ("Divine positive laws...")
> "...it belongs to the very essence of law that the man who is to be
> obliged be assured of the authority of the one declaring it..."
Fourth instance of the archaic "that X be Y" subjunctive pattern (see
paragraphs 28, 30 above). Individually mild, but as a recurring
construction across the chapter it's worth a single pass to modernize
("...that the man who is to be obliged is assured..." or similar) rather
than treating each instance separately.

## What reads well

- Paragraph 1 (the opening definition of civil law, with the Rome/civitas
  aside) is clear, well-paced, and a strong opening — no stumbles.
- Paragraph 30's two worked examples (the man locked out of his own house;
  the judge who witnessed the true culprit) are excellent — concrete,
  step-by-step, and genuinely clarify an abstract point about letter vs.
  intent of the law.
- Paragraphs 43-44 (the Abraham/Sinai narrative illustrating divine
  positive law) read almost like storytelling — the rhetorical questions
  ("how can a man... be assured of the revelation received by the one who
  declares it?") are effective and the Biblical quotations are integrated
  smoothly rather than dropped in as raw blocks.
- The seven-part Roman-law catalogue (paragraphs 33-39) handles inherently
  technical historical material about as well as it can — each item is
  glossed against a rough English-law equivalent, which gives the reader a
  handhold even where the underlying institutions (Senatus consulta,
  Responsa Prudentum) are unfamiliar by nature of the content, not the
  wording.
- The flight/forfeiture case discussion in paragraph 28, despite the one
  flagged sentence, is otherwise a genuinely gripping worked legal example
  — the reasoning is easy to follow step by step.

## Verdict

**Needs a small targeted pass — not yet fully closed out.** The chapter is
close to "substantially accessible": the great majority of its 48
paragraphs read cleanly, and most of what makes it demanding is legitimate
conceptual/structural density inherent to a 17th-century legal-philosophy
argument (nested legal distinctions, Roman-law taxonomy, chains of "if...
then" reasoning) rather than avoidable wording. But this pass did surface
concrete, fixable wording problems that a general reader would stumble on:
one genuinely ambiguous referent (paragraph 31's "namely deep study of the
laws"), one overloaded/archaic-subjunctive sentence that's the densest in
the chapter (paragraph 28), a recurring "that X be Y" subjunctive pattern
appearing three more times (paragraphs 30, 43, and the "be a constitution"
clause in 28), an archaic idiom left unglossed ("ever so many, or ever so
wise," paragraph 10), an archaic comparative construction ("no longer law
than...," paragraph 7), an odd temporal phrase ("he was free before,"
paragraph 6), a convoluted Golden Rule quotation (paragraph 13), and one
minor unglossed citation abbreviation (paragraph 11). None of these require
rethinking the chapter or touching content the reader needs for
comprehension — they're all local, sentence-level fixes. A single targeted
pass addressing this list (particularly the paragraph 28 and paragraph 31
items, which are the two most likely to actually stop a reader) would very
plausibly finish the job this time.
