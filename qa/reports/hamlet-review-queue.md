### 1. [Chapter 20, Paragraph 88] (reason: untranslated English word)
- **Original (EN):** "HAMLET. Give me your pardon, sir. I have done you wrong; But pardon't as you are a gentleman. This presence knows, and you must needs have heard, How I am punish'd with sore distraction."
- **Danish:** "HAMLET. Giv mig jeres tilgivelse, min herre. Jeg har gjort jer uret. Men tilgiv mig, som en gentleman ville. Alle her ved, og I ma have hort, hvordan jeg er blevet straffet med et forfaerdeligt vanvid."
- **Issue:** "gentleman" left untranslated in the Danish. This English loanword appears 6 times across the Hamlet DA edition (Ch8/p6, Ch16/p70, Ch20/p36, Ch20/p39, Ch20/p42, Ch20/p88). While "gentleman" is sometimes used in modern Danish as a loanword, in a Shakespeare translation it breaks the period register. Hamlet is addressing Laertes formally before the duel — the word carries weight about noble character, not just politeness.
- **Suggestion:** "adelsmand" (nobleman — matches the social rank implied), or "aedelbaaren herre" for a more archaic/elevated register. Alternatively "en mand af aere" (a man of honour) captures the moral dimension. Apply consistently across all 6 occurrences — or decide deliberately where "gentleman" as loanword is acceptable vs. where Danish is needed.

### 2. [Chapter 20, Paragraph 174] (reason: English conditional clause order in Danish)
- **Original (EN):** "FORTINBRAS. Let four captains Bear Hamlet like a soldier to the stage, For he was likely, had he been put on, To have prov'd most royally; and for his passage, The soldiers' music and the rites of war Speak loudly for him."
- **Danish:** "FORTINBRAS. Lad fire kaptajner baere Hamlet til scenen som en soldat, for han ville sandsynligvis, havde han faet chancen, have vist sig mest kongeligt. Og til hans sidste rejse, lad soldaternes musik og de militaere aeresbevisninger tale hojt for ham."
- **Issue:** "han ville sandsynligvis, havde han faet chancen, have vist sig mest kongeligt" — this mirrors the English conditional interpolation ("he was likely, had he been put on, to have prov'd"). In Danish, the conditional clause interrupting the main clause creates an awkward, hard-to-parse sentence. Danish prefers the conditional clause either before or after the main statement, not embedded within it.
- **Suggestion:** "for havde han faet chancen, ville han sandsynligvis have vist sig hoejst kongeligt" (conditional clause first, then main clause — standard Danish word order for conditionals). Or: "for han ville sandsynligvis have vist sig hoejst kongeligt, havde han faet chancen" (main clause first, conditional appended).
