### 2. [Chapter 13, Paragraph 26] (reason: article interference — "den mand")
- **Original (EN):** "Such was his story, but Minerva smiled and caressed him with her hand. Then she took the form of a woman, fair, stately, and wise, 'He must be indeed a shifty lying fellow,' said she, 'who could surpass you in all manner of craft...'"
- **Danish:** "Sadan lod hans historie, men Athene smilede og strog ham med handen. Sa antog hun en kvindes skikkelse — smuk, hoj og klog. 'Den mand, der kunne overga dig i alle former for list,' sagde hun, 'matte sandelig vaere en udspekuleret logner...'"
- **Issue:** "Den mand, der kunne overga dig" — the original says "He must be indeed a shifty lying fellow who could surpass you." The Danish uses "Den mand, der..." where the English uses a more colloquial "He must be... a fellow who." The Danish feels slightly formal/stiff for Athena's teasing, affectionate tone.
- **Suggestion:** "Den, der kunne overga dig i list, matte sandelig vaere en udspekuleret logner..." (dropping "mand" makes it lighter and more playful, matching Athena's tone).

### 3. [Chapter 4, Paragraph 28] (reason: article interference — "den mand")
- **Original (EN):** "'So,' he exclaimed, 'these cowards would usurp a brave man's bed!'"
- **Danish:** "'Sa,' udbrod han, 'disse kujoner vil overtage en tapper mands seng!'"
- **Issue:** "en tapper mands seng" — here "en... mands" (indefinite article) is grammatically correct and arguably natural. Menelaus is speaking about Odysseus but framing it generally. This is less problematic than the "den mand" pattern. However, "en tapper mands seng" could also be "en tappers seng" (more concise) or "helten seng" (the hero's bed) for more punch.
- **Suggestion:** Likely OK as-is. The indefinite article is correct for Menelaus's rhetorical framing. No change needed unless the reviewer finds it stilted in context.

### 4. [Chapter 17, Paragraph 54] (reason: "gilder" usage check)
- **Original (EN):** "'Call him here, then,' said Penelope, 'that I too may hear his story. As for the suitors, let them take their pleasure indoors or out as they will, for they have nothing to fret about. Their corn and wine remain unwasted in their houses...'"
- **Danish:** "'Kald ham herhen da,' sagde Penelopeia, 'sa ogsa jeg kan hore hans beretning. Hvad bejlerne angar, lad dem more sig inde eller ude, som det passer dem... mens de dag efter dag haenger om... og slagter vores kvaeg, far og fede geder til deres gilder og aldrig ofrer en tanke pa, hvor meget vin de drikker.'"
- **Issue:** "gilder" (feasts/banquets) — the original says "take their pleasure." Penelope is describing the suitors' ongoing feasting at her expense. "Gilder" is correct Danish for feasts/banquets and fits the context of slaughtering livestock for elaborate meals. However, "gilder" can also mean "castrations" in Danish (from "gilde" = to castrate). Context makes the meaning clear, but worth noting.
- **Suggestion:** OK. "Gilder" meaning feasts is the natural reading here. The banquet context (slaughtering cattle, drinking wine) removes any ambiguity.
