Gilgamesh comes from a world whose cities were already ancient when Homer’s poems took shape. Its survival depends on clay tablets, broken lines, and the patient work of people piecing together languages that had ceased to be spoken. Yet the feelings in it can seem almost indecently close: the pleasure of finding a friend who is an equal, the appetite to do something magnificent, and the refusal to accept that being alive must come to an end.

Its king begins with an excess of strength and very little understanding of what strength is for. The people who live under him experience his greatness as a burden. This gives the poem an unexpected starting point for a heroic adventure: a city needs protection from the very man who is supposed to embody its glory. Power and wisdom are visibly different accomplishments.

Friendship enlarges his world. In the company of Enkidu, ambition acquires the excitement of a shared undertaking, and danger becomes something two people can urge each other towards. There is affection in this partnership, but also the recklessness of being admired. The poem understands how a companion can make someone more fully human without necessarily making him prudent.

Beyond the city stand forests, gods, and creatures whose existence does not accommodate human wishes. The adventures are strange and physical, full of journeys, dreams, exhaustion, and things made by hand. Their strangeness matters. These people did not inhabit a modern world with different clothes, and their ideas about civilisation, sacred power, and fame can resist easy approval.

Through all of this runs a desire that requires very little explanation: to preserve what has become precious. The oldest surviving great epic gives that desire tremendous dignity without pretending that wanting something fiercely will secure it. Its broken tablets preserve a voice still arguing with the limits of a human life.
