Phileas Fogg lives as though uncertainty were a scheduling error. His habits are exact, his manner composed, and his life arranged to minimise surprise. Jules Verne sends this man around the world on a wager, placing a perfectly measured intention among railway lines, steamships, strangers, and events that have no obligation to respect it.

The pleasure of the journey comes from the encounter between calculation and contingency. A timetable turns distance into a promise, but a missed connection can restore the world's size in an instant. Fogg's composure is both comic and impressive. While others experience each interruption as a new emotional emergency, he continues considering what can be done.

Passepartout supplies a different appetite for the adventure. He notices, reacts, worries, and becomes involved. Through the contrast between master and servant, the world acquires more than the value of an obstacle course. There are people and experiences worth attending to even when attention consumes precious time.

The novel belongs to an age astonished by the speed with which transport and communication were changing geography. Its representations of other societies carry assumptions that deserve questioning, including the confidence of European travellers who regard the world as available for their passage. That confidence is part of the historical experience the book preserves.

Verne gives the contest a satisfying practical ingenuity, but the most interesting pressure falls on its purpose. A journey undertaken to demonstrate what is possible can become entangled with obligations no wager anticipated. The book makes punctuality exciting while leaving room for the thought that an encounter along the way might matter more than the plan that made it possible.
