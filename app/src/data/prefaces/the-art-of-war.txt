The Art of War has little patience with the kind of courage that needs an audience. Its ideal commander studies conditions, conserves strength, and prefers an advantage so decisive that a glorious struggle becomes unnecessary. A hard-won victory may reveal that something went wrong long before the fighting began.

This is an ancient military text, and its practical concerns give its thinking substance. Armies need food. Distances consume resources. Exhausted soldiers become vulnerable, and a prolonged campaign can impoverish the country that expects to benefit from it. The apparent simplicity of its instructions rests on an insistence that ambition must answer to these facts. A ruler's anger does not make a road shorter or restore a dead soldier.

The book is especially interested in perception. An opponent acts on what he believes is happening, so appearances themselves become part of the situation. At the same time, a commander must avoid being captured by his own hopes, habits, or reputation. Knowing the enemy requires an ability to imagine another point of view; knowing oneself requires something harder than confidence.

Its brief statements reward being considered together. Advice about deception sits beside advice about discipline, timing, information, and the costs of destruction. Taken alone, a maxim can sound like permission to be ruthless. In the larger argument, cleverness is repeatedly judged by what it preserves and what it needlessly spends.

The attraction of the book beyond warfare comes from this unsentimental attention to circumstances. It takes seriously the possibility that effort is being wasted because the wrong contest has been accepted. There is an austere pleasure in a mind so concerned with winning that it refuses to be impressed by the spectacle of a fight.
