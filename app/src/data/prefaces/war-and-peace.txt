War and Peace is about people trying to decide how to live while history makes other plans for them. They fall in love, worry about money, resent their parents, and imagine the impressive people they will become. Napoleon is changing the map of Europe, but an invitation, an embarrassing remark, or the possibility that someone’s affection has cooled can still occupy an entire mind.

Tolstoy gives these private concerns their full importance. The people living through a great historical event do not experience every hour as history. They are hungry, distracted, hoping to be noticed, trying to understand an order, or wondering whether they have made fools of themselves. The novel moves between drawing rooms and battlefields with an extraordinary awareness of how much remains the same: the desire for approval, the fear of humiliation, the difficulty of knowing what another person actually means.

His characters are especially convincing when they explain themselves. A man can sincerely believe he is acting from principle while also enjoying the prospect of being admired. Someone who longs to do good can make a mess of the lives nearest to him. Tolstoy follows the little adjustments by which people preserve a tolerable opinion of themselves, often with considerable humour. He also allows them moments of generosity that exceed anything their previous behaviour seemed to promise.

The length makes possible a particular kind of intimacy. Pierre, Andrei, and Natasha have time to change their minds, repeat mistakes, and become embarrassed by convictions that once seemed final. An early impression of someone survives alongside everything learned about them afterwards. The experience begins to resemble knowing people over many years, when affection includes memories of their foolishness and nobody can quite be reduced to the person they are today.

That patience with individual lives accompanies a deep suspicion of the stories told about nations. Tolstoy is fascinated by the distance between what happens and the explanation that eventually becomes accepted. A confused encounter can acquire a clear purpose in the retelling; an outcome produced by thousands of people can become the achievement of one celebrated man. His Napoleon belongs to a world in which authority and understanding are very different possessions. The novel keeps returning to all the things a commanding figure cannot see.

Yet its enormous reach leaves room for the pleasure of being alive. There are dances, friendships, family jokes, the excitement of being young among other young people, and moments when a familiar landscape suddenly seems beautiful again. These experiences matter in themselves, even while events of national consequence are taking place elsewhere.

Much of War and Peace concerns the search for a life that will finally feel right. Its people look for answers in love, achievement, service, and belief, sometimes with touching confidence that they have settled the matter. Tolstoy stays with them after that confidence falters. He gives their uncertainty enough time to become something more interesting than failure: the continuing experience of being a person whose life has not turned out as expected, and is still taking place.
