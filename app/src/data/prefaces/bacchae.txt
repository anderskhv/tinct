Pentheus believes that he understands what threatens his city. A new worship has drawn people beyond familiar restraints, and his answer is to restore order. The Bacchae places that confidence before Dionysus, a god whose power includes the forms of experience that the ruler most wants to dismiss or control.

Euripides makes the attraction of this worship palpable. Music, movement, the mountains, and release from ordinary roles offer a freedom that civic respectability cannot fully explain. The longing to step outside a regulated identity is given beauty, especially for people whose daily lives are tightly confined.

That release is also dangerous. Dionysus cannot be reduced to an amiable patron of pleasure, and the experience he brings does not remain safely within human measures. The play allows divine power to be compelling without making it reassuring. Recognition of a god's reality does not settle every question about the use of that power.

Pentheus's opposition has its own unstable motives. Disgust can conceal fascination, and a desire to suppress something can become an inability to stop thinking about it. His language of control grows increasingly involved with the very spectacle he condemns. Euripides is exact about the curiosity that can inhabit moral outrage.

Sober reason and liberating instinct each offer an incomplete account of what happens here. Order, ecstasy, authority, and desire have become too closely involved to permit a comfortable choice between them. A society may need limits, but it also needs to understand the experiences its limits are attempting to contain.
