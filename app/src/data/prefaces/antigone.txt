Antigone and Creon both believe that an obligation has become impossible to evade. For her, the dead make a claim that a ruler cannot abolish. For him, a city recovering from conflict needs an authority whose decisions cannot be set aside by private loyalty. Sophocles brings these convictions into a shared space where neither can remain merely a principle.

The conflict is sharpened by the people making it. Antigone's courage can be severe towards those who cannot match it. Creon's concern for order becomes entangled with the need to be obeyed, especially by someone whose defiance unsettles his idea of who is entitled to speak. A defensible concern can acquire a dangerous shape when it becomes inseparable from personal authority.

Around them stand people trying to preserve relationships, avoid punishment, or introduce a qualification before certainty makes further speech useless. Their hesitation has its own moral interest. Living together requires more capacities than a willingness to die for a position, though compromise is not always available without surrendering something essential.

The play's economy gives every exchange weight. Arguments about law are also arguments between relatives; a public command reaches into burial, marriage, and the household. The distinction between political life and private life cannot hold those consequences apart.

Antigone has remained a work through which later ages argue about resistance, but its dramatic strength exceeds any single application. It allows a commitment to become admirable and difficult at the same time. The question of what must be defended is accompanied by the question of what a defender can still hear from another person.
