Adam Smith notices that an ordinary object can depend on the work of people who will never meet. Their different skills, tools, materials, and exchanges come together without any one of them understanding the whole arrangement. The Wealth of Nations begins from this extraordinary social cooperation hidden inside everyday provision.

The division of labour makes enormous increases in productivity possible. Smith follows its consequences into wages, prices, trade, capital, and the institutions that shape economic life. He is interested in how a society becomes prosperous, and in the errors that arise when the wealth of a nation is confused with the stock of money it possesses or the privileges of its merchants.

His account of self-interest is more watchful than its familiar slogans suggest. Business interests can seek protection from competition, combine against customers, and influence policy in their own favour. A proposal advertised as good for trade may be good mainly for the people proposing it. Smith's confidence in exchange accompanies a persistent suspicion of concentrated advantage.

He also sees costs within productive achievement. Repetitive work can narrow the worker's capacities, and a commercial society has responsibilities that private bargains do not automatically discharge. Education, public works, justice, and defence belong to his inquiry into the conditions of prosperity.

The book is expansive because Smith follows an institution into its history and practical operation rather than leaving it as an abstraction. Some of its economics belongs to an earlier stage of the discipline, but its habits of attention remain valuable. It asks who benefits from an arrangement, what incentives sustain it, and how the prosperity of a whole society can be distinguished from the success of its most persuasive interests.
