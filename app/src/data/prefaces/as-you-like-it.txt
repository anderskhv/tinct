Rosalind is in love and still capable of finding the behaviour of lovers ridiculous. This gives As You Like It one of its great pleasures: a heroine whose wit does not protect her from feeling, but does help her explore what the feeling might mean. She can participate in a romance and conduct an experiment on it at the same time.

The Forest of Arden supplies room for this experiment. People displaced from the court acquire opportunities to speak, dress, work, and form attachments differently. The forest is no simple paradise; hunger, weather, and human vanity accompany them. Its freedom lies partly in making familiar social identities less compulsory.

Disguise allows Rosalind to try out forms of authority and candour that ordinary courtship would constrain. Love becomes a conversation in which conventions can be exaggerated, mocked, and revised. The play enjoys the uncertainty between a role someone adopts and a possibility that role reveals.

Other voices prevent any single philosophy of life from settling comfortably over the place. Jaques cultivates melancholy, Touchstone tests words against bodily interests, and the pastoral world has its own practical intelligence. Songs interrupt argument with a different kind of truth about pleasure, loss, and the seasons.

The comedy's generosity comes from letting these temperaments share a setting without requiring them to become alike. Its ease has substance: people need occasions on which they can imagine themselves otherwise, and companions who can answer an extravagant feeling without humiliating it. Shakespeare makes courtship an education in taking another person seriously while retaining the ability to laugh.
