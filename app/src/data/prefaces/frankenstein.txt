Frankenstein is unusually interested in what happens to an ambition after it has been achieved. Victor imagines the glory of creating life with far more energy than he imagines the needs of the life he might create. The discovery promises to make him exceptional. Responsibility would require a quieter, more sustained form of attention.

Mary Shelley gives the being at the centre of this ambition a voice, an education, and a desire to belong. The creature learns about human life from its edges, watching the affection and ordinary cooperation from which he is excluded. Language offers access to a world whose inhabitants may still refuse him recognition. Understanding other people does not guarantee that they will consent to understand him.

The novel's sympathy is powerful precisely because it does not settle every judgment. Suffering can explain a grievance without granting unlimited rights over others. Victor and the creature both tell persuasive stories about what has been done to them, and each can make his own responsibility harder to see. Their accounts arrive through other listeners, so the act of being moved by a story becomes part of the book's concern.

Its landscapes widen this human loneliness. Mountains, ice, and great distances offer beauty and apparent escape, but they do not remove the need for another person's response. The desire to venture beyond ordinary limits can be exhilarating and can leave ordinary obligations terribly neglected.

The book has become a shorthand for the dangers of invention, though its questions reach well beyond a laboratory. Creating something establishes a relationship that admiration for the achievement cannot fulfil. Shelley asks what is owed to a being whose existence has become inconvenient, and what happens when the wish to be extraordinary outruns the capacity to care.
