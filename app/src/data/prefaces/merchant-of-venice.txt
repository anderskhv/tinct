The Merchant of Venice joins love and friendship to contracts, credit, inheritance, and the risks of trade. Affection may speak generously, but someone has to provide the money, accept the obligation, or bear the cost. Shakespeare makes the language of giving and the language of debt continually overlap.

Venice depends on exchange while dividing the people who participate in it. Shylock is useful to a society that subjects him to contempt, and his response cannot be understood apart from that treatment. The play gives his injury an eloquence that exceeds the role other characters would prefer him to occupy.

Its antisemitism is nevertheless substantial. The humanity of a speech does not erase the humiliations the comedy can invite its audience to accept. Reading the work involves attending both to its capacity to expose prejudice and to the prejudices within its own dramatic arrangements. That unresolved difficulty is part of its history on the stage.

Portia's world offers a different relation between power and constraint. Wealth grants possibilities, but inherited conditions still govern intimate choices. Intelligence becomes a way of moving within rules whose authority cannot simply be wished away. Across the play, people interpret obligations with considerable ingenuity when their own happiness is at stake.

Mercy is the most elevated word in this network of transactions, and the most demanding to judge by conduct. Those who praise it may not distribute it as freely as they expect to receive it. The play's fascination comes from the distance between an attractive moral language and the terms on which people actually allow one another to belong.
