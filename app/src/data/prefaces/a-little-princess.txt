Sara Crewe likes imagining that she is a princess. The interesting part is what she thinks this should require of her: generosity, self-command, and an obligation to notice people who are easily overlooked. Frances Hodgson Burnett takes a child's private story seriously enough to test whether it can shape conduct when the surrounding world offers little encouragement.

The school Sara enters is finely attuned to money. Adults who speak about good character can become remarkably deferential to wealth, and children quickly learn which distinctions matter. The novel makes these changes in treatment legible without requiring a complicated explanation. A person remains the same while the attention granted to her changes.

Sara's imagination gives the book its warmth. A bare room can acquire a history, a small creature can become company, and an invented scene can help make an unpleasant hour bearable. The stories do not remove material need. Their value lies partly in preserving a capacity to respond to others while need and humiliation encourage withdrawal.

The friendships are especially affecting because they cross the school's own scale of importance. Intelligence, service, affection, and fear are distributed without regard to who wears the best clothes. A child who has been treated as insignificant can become central to someone else's life through the simple experience of being listened to.

The book has the extravagance of a fairy tale, including a wish for goodness to be recognised. Its emotional foundation is more ordinary and more durable: the importance of having an inward account of oneself that does not depend entirely on how powerful people behave. Burnett makes that resource feel both fragile and capable of being shared.
