Hamlet has the peculiar intimacy of hearing someone think before he has decided what he thinks. A sentence begins in confidence, finds an objection, turns bitter, and becomes a joke. The mind doing this is brilliant company, but its brilliance gives it no reliable protection from grief, cruelty, or mistakes. Shakespeare makes thought itself dramatic, with all the speed and danger of an encounter between people.

The prince lives in a court that would prefer him to recover from his father's death and behave agreeably. Around him, public ceremony continues. People offer advice, arrange marriages, attend to business, and explain what is reasonable. His refusal to join in can feel like fidelity to something everyone else is abandoning. It can also become an excuse to treat other people's feelings as less substantial than his own.

That uncertainty keeps the play alive. Hamlet can seem painfully honest in one exchange and evasive in the next; an interpretation that explains his hesitation may do little to explain his sudden violence. Other characters have their own claims on attention, though he is exceptionally good at occupying it. Ophelia, Gertrude, and the friends drawn into the court's schemes all have lives that exceed the parts he assigns them.

There is a surprising amount of comedy in this atmosphere. Official language, professional wisdom, theatrical vanity, and the social uses of flattery are all excellent material for Hamlet's impatience. His jokes can offer relief or make an encounter more dangerous. Even the play's most solemn concerns remain in contact with bodies, work, and ordinary conversation.

So many of its phrases have entered everyday speech that the whole work can seem familiar before it begins. In their setting, they become unsettled again. They belong to people who have something to gain by speaking and something to conceal. The pleasure of returning to Hamlet is partly this discovery that its famous thoughts have never quite stopped happening.
