Tocqueville notices that people can become more equal without becoming more independent. When inherited rank loses its authority, public opinion may acquire a new kind of power. Someone free to choose a life can still be acutely anxious about choosing differently from everyone else. Democracy in America explores the habits that grow around equality, including those that can quietly weaken freedom.

America gives him a place to observe a social transformation already under way. He studies institutions, but also associations, religion, family life, ambition, and the restless pursuit of comfort. Political arrangements depend on people who have learned, or failed to learn, how to act together. A town meeting or a voluntary association can matter because it gives citizens practice in being responsible for something beyond their own household.

His account of democratic desire is especially penetrating. Greater opportunity can make remaining differences harder to bear. Satisfaction keeps moving because someone nearby appears to possess a little more, and no inherited boundary supplies a convincing reason to stop. Equality can nourish hope and comparison at the same time.

The book also confronts the profound limits of America's own claims, particularly slavery and the treatment of Indigenous peoples. Tocqueville's observations do not escape every prejudice of his position, and his admiration is never a sufficient reason to suspend judgment. The value of the work lies partly in the tensions it is able to recognise inside a society's account of itself.

A population may welcome an authority that relieves it of trouble while gradually surrendering the habits needed to resist that authority. Tocqueville makes democracy interesting at the scale of daily life, where a political future can begin as a perfectly understandable preference for being left comfortable and undisturbed.
