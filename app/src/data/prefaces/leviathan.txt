Hobbes begins with people who have reasons to fear one another. They may prefer peace, but preference is insufficient when there is no dependable assurance that a neighbour will keep an agreement. Preparing for danger can itself look dangerous, and an attempt to become secure can make everyone less secure. Leviathan builds its account of political authority from this difficulty.

The argument reaches much further into human nature than its famous picture of conflict suggests. Hobbes examines sensation, imagination, language, desire, and the pursuit of power. Words can clarify an agreement or conceal a confusion; ambition can be driven as much by future vulnerability as by present greed. Politics rests on creatures who are thinking ahead without knowing what others intend.

The sovereign is his proposed answer to the problem of enforceable trust. A common power can make cooperation possible where private assurances remain fragile. That answer raises another formidable question: how much authority must be granted to escape insecurity, and what forms of liberty remain under it?

Religion occupies a large part of the book because competing claims to ultimate obedience can divide a commonwealth. Hobbes is writing in a world where disputes over spiritual authority have immediate political consequences. The theological argument belongs to his attempt to explain who may command and how public peace can survive disagreement.

His conclusions can be resisted without diminishing the force of the problem. A political order has to function among people who are afraid, proud, partial, and capable of reasoning from the same facts towards incompatible actions. Leviathan makes the existence of ordinary cooperation seem like an achievement that requires explanation, and asks what a society might be willing to authorise to keep it possible.
