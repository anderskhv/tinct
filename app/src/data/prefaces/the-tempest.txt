Prospero has an unusual opportunity: he can bring the people who wronged him into a world whose conditions he controls. He can arrange their meetings, interrupt their sleep, frighten them, and decide what they are allowed to know. The Tempest gives a wounded man the powers of a dramatist and watches what he wants to make other people do.

His island is never simply his own account of it. Ariel longs for freedom, Caliban contests his authority, and Miranda's first encounters with strangers exceed her father's careful arrangements. Each has reasons that his story of rightful rule cannot entirely contain. Even magic leaves him dependent on beings who have desires of their own.

Shakespeare makes this struggle for control extraordinarily beautiful. Music arrives without a visible source; the island seems to answer to moods and memories; familiar surroundings become strange enough to invite wonder. The beauty belongs to no single moral position. Caliban, whom other characters readily dismiss, can describe the place with a tenderness that complicates their judgment of him.

There is plenty of ridiculous human business here too. Rank, drink, greed, and the fantasy of becoming important survive a shipwreck remarkably well. People who barely understand where they have landed are soon imagining how they might govern it. The island gives their ambitions fresh scenery without necessarily improving them.

Prospero can produce astonishing effects, but an obedient performance is not the same thing as a freely changed heart. Among its songs, conspiracies, and spectacles, The Tempest considers what could satisfy an injured person who has finally acquired the means to insist on his version of events.
