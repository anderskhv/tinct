Medea has been betrayed by someone who can explain the betrayal as a sensible arrangement. Jason's account has reasons, advantages, and a reassuring view of the future. What it cannot accommodate is the full reality of the person expected to accept it. Euripides makes that failure of recognition the beginning of an encounter in which language becomes a weapon.

Medea is a foreign woman whose security depends on relationships that other people have greater power to redefine. Her intelligence does not remove that vulnerability. It makes her acutely aware of the terms on which she is being asked to live, and gives her the capacity to expose the self-interest inside apparently reasonable advice.

The play grants her grievance tremendous force without making grievance an unlimited moral entitlement. Sympathy is placed under pressure by what anger can demand. Euripides refuses the convenience of requiring an injured person to be harmless before the injury counts, while also refusing to let suffering settle every judgment about conduct.

Around the central struggle are people who foresee danger but possess limited power to prevent it. The household's insecurity extends beyond the two people conducting the argument. Private decisions have consequences for those whose dependence gives them no comparable voice.

Medea's enduring power lies in the experience of following a mind whose clarity and feeling cannot be comfortably separated. An argument may be devastatingly accurate and still lead towards something appalling. The play makes the need to be acknowledged fully intelligible, then examines how much of a life can become devoted to forcing that acknowledgment from someone who has withheld it.
