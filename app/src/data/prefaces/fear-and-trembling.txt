Fear and Trembling returns to the story of Abraham with a refusal to let familiarity make it easy. A father is commanded to sacrifice the son he loves. Religious tradition honours his faith, but an ordinary moral account of the same act seems to demand horror. Kierkegaard keeps both responses present long enough for their conflict to become difficult to evade.

He writes through the voice of Johannes de silentio, a narrator who admires Abraham while repeatedly acknowledging that he cannot understand him. That distance matters. The work explores the demands of faith through someone who cannot simply claim to possess it, and whose eloquence offers no shortcut across the difficulty.

The distinction between resignation and faith is central. Accepting the loss of what is most loved can require tremendous courage. Faith, as this book imagines it, involves a still stranger relation to possibility: the loved thing is not merely dismissed as unimportant because it cannot be secured. Earthly attachment remains fully serious.

The writing approaches its subject through variations, imagined lives, and movements of thought that circle rather than dispose of the problem. Abraham cannot become an ordinary example of public virtue without losing what makes his situation exceptional. Yet treating private conviction as sufficient justification would dissolve the very ethical claims the book is trying to take seriously.

Faith, sacrifice, love, and obedience are words that can become comfortable through repetition. Here they must bear the weight of Abraham's attachment to his son. Kierkegaard's narrator can describe the approach with extraordinary beauty while refusing to pretend that description has carried him all the way across.
