Ishmael goes to sea partly because remaining ashore has become bad for his spirits. He finds a world of dangerous work, extraordinary companions, and almost inexhaustible things to think about. Moby Dick has room for a captain's consuming purpose, but its narrator is interested in everything that purpose might overlook.

This abundance is one of the book's great pleasures. Whales invite anatomy, theology, commerce, folklore, and arguments about how anything so large could be adequately described. A practical task aboard ship can become a meditation, a joke, or a small piece of theatre. Melville is willing to interrupt the voyage because the world through which it passes deserves more than a supporting role.

The humour is often bodily and companionable. Ishmael can make fun of his own assumptions, discover affection where he expected strangeness, and follow a thought long past the point at which a more orderly narrator would bring it home. His curiosity makes a generous counterweight to Ahab's insistence that one object contains the explanation of his suffering.

The ship itself is a complicated human arrangement. Men from different countries and religions must work together under a hierarchy whose purposes they do not equally control. The sea demands skilled cooperation, while the captain's authority can give a private grievance collective force. What people owe to their work, their leader, and their own lives becomes difficult to separate.

The novel's size belongs to its argument with simplification. A whale is a creature with a body and a history, an object of commerce, and something imagination cannot leave alone. Melville permits all these ways of seeing to contend. The result is a book with an appetite large enough to make the search for meaning feel both magnificent and faintly ridiculous.
