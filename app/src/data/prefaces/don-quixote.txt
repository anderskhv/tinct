Don Quixote has read enough stories of chivalry to believe that the world is waiting for someone to act by their standards. Injustice needs a champion, beauty deserves devotion, and an unremarkable stretch of road may be the beginning of an adventure. Cervantes sends him into a Spain whose inhabitants have their own business to attend to, and whose objects show little willingness to become what his imagination requires.

The encounters are often gloriously funny. Don Quixote can explain away contrary evidence with an ingenuity that protects the belief responsible for the trouble. A defeat may confirm the existence of an enchantment; an ordinary person may be failing to understand his own role in a magnificent event. Conviction becomes capable of absorbing almost anything.

Sancho Panza accompanies him with a different vocabulary of desire. Food, sleep, money, and practical advantage remain persuasive concerns. Yet he is never merely common sense walking beside a dreamer. He has hopes of his own, and his master's world offers opportunities that an entirely sensible life might not. Their conversation gradually becomes one of the book's deepest pleasures: argument, irritation, dependence, and affection sharing the same journey.

The laughter can be rough. Good intentions do not prevent harm, and the people who recognise Don Quixote's delusions do not always behave more generously than he does. Someone can be mistaken without deserving humiliation; someone else can see clearly and use that advantage cruelly. Cervantes makes superiority an unreliable guide to goodness.

The novel also enjoys questioning how stories are made and who has authority to tell them. Accounts compete, narrators qualify their claims, and literary fame becomes another force acting on human behaviour. The book helped enlarge what a novel could contain by treating its own storytelling as part of the adventure.

Its humanity lies in allowing the absurdity of the quest and the attachment it creates to remain equally real. The road is full of reasons to abandon an extravagant understanding of life, but companionship develops along it nonetheless. Cervantes gives imagination all the room it needs to become troublesome, beautiful, and impossible to judge from a safe distance.
