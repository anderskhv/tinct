An old man arrives in a place where he hopes to be allowed to rest. His reputation arrives with him, and the people who hear his name must decide whether his presence is a danger, a responsibility, or something sacred. Oedipus at Colonus turns the apparently simple act of receiving a stranger into a test of a community's understanding of justice.

This Oedipus carries a past that other people believe they understand. Their account can reduce him to what happened, while his own account insists on intention, ignorance, and the difference between suffering an event and freely choosing it. Sophocles gives an old argument about responsibility the anger and exhaustion of someone who has had to live inside other people's judgment.

Age has changed his circumstances without removing his force. Dependence and authority coexist awkwardly; tenderness towards his daughters does not make every family feeling tender. The play allows suffering to confer neither automatic wisdom nor easy forgiveness. Injury can remain active long after physical power has diminished.

The landscape has an unusual presence. A sacred grove, the life of the countryside, and the hospitality of Athens create a setting in which the last part of a life may acquire a meaning different from the one its earlier history seemed to dictate. Political interests nevertheless follow the traveller, because even a rejected person can become useful to power.

Beneath the requests for shelter are unresolved claims about kinship, memory, and what a person is owed when usefulness has apparently ended. Sophocles gives the wish for a place to belong the full seriousness of a final human need.
