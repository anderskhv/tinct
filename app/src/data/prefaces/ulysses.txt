Ulysses gives an ordinary day the attention usually reserved for a lifetime. People go to work, find something to eat, attend a funeral, wander into a pub, and think about someone who is elsewhere. Underneath these activities their minds are busy with quite different things: a remembered embarrassment, a bodily discomfort, a scrap of music, a person they cannot stop missing. Joyce allows almost none of it to count as unimportant.

At the centre of this Dublin day is Leopold Bloom, an advertising canvasser whose curiosity extends to things that more imposing heroes would scarcely notice. He wonders how objects work, imagines other people's circumstances, and carries his private grief through streets where he is both at home and an outsider. His decency is often tentative and awkward. It exists alongside vanity, desire, and thoughts he would prefer to keep to himself, which makes it unusually convincing.

The novel's connection to Homer's Odyssey gives these errands and encounters another scale. An ancient voyage survives inside a modern city, though the correspondence is often funny, distant, or deliberately undignified. The grandeur belongs as much to the person buying breakfast as to the hero sailing home. A marriage, a lost child, the need for company: an epic can be made from things happening behind an unremarkable front door.

Joyce keeps finding new forms for this material. Prose follows the uneven movement of thought, borrows the manner of a newspaper or a catechism, becomes extravagant theatre, and turns language itself into part of the day's adventure. Some passages take patience. Others are immediately earthy, tender, or wonderfully ridiculous. The book has far more laughter in it than its forbidding reputation suggests.

Its abundance also makes it a book that changes with a life. An argument that once seemed central can recede behind a small gesture; a father's worry or a husband's loneliness can suddenly occupy a page that previously belonged to literary fireworks. There is always something happening outside the part that first caught the eye. Joyce built a city large enough for attention to keep discovering new ways through it.
