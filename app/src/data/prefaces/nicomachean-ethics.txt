Aristotle begins with the observation that people do things for the sake of other things. Work supplies money, money makes choices possible, and choices are expected to contribute to a good life. Eventually the chain needs an answer about what makes the life itself good. The Nicomachean Ethics asks for an account substantial enough to guide more than the next decision.

Happiness, in this inquiry, concerns the activity of a whole life. It cannot be secured simply by enjoying pleasant feelings or possessing admirable intentions. Character develops through what someone repeatedly does, until certain actions become easier and certain pleasures more attractive. Habits help create the person who will make tomorrow's choices.

Aristotle is unusually attentive to the judgment this requires. Courage depends on what is feared, why it is faced, and what the circumstances demand. Generosity is more complicated than giving as much as possible. A rule may orient conduct without replacing the perception needed to recognise the right action in a particular situation.

Friendship occupies a large place in the work because a good life is difficult to imagine in isolation. Other people offer pleasure and assistance, but they can also share activities valued for their own sake and make someone's character more visible to him. Affection becomes part of the inquiry into human excellence, rather than a distraction from it.

His account of flourishing leaves room for dependence on fortune: circumstances can injure even a well-lived life. The resources and leisure it presupposes also raise questions about who has been given a fair chance to live that way. Aristotle tries to describe living well at the scale on which it actually has to happen, through time, among other people, with imperfect control over what comes next.
