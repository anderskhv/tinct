Candide has been taught that the world is arranged for the best. Experience proves a remarkably energetic opponent of this education. Misfortune follows misfortune, while explanations remain available in almost unlimited supply. Voltaire finds a savage comedy in the distance between being able to account for suffering and having any intention of relieving it.

The story moves with extraordinary speed. A catastrophe that another novelist might spend a volume exploring can arrive and be left behind within a page. The lightness of the telling makes the brutality more conspicuous. Institutions continue their business, learned people continue explaining, and those with something to gain discover that events confirm their principles.

Voltaire's targets include religious cruelty, military glory, colonial exploitation, and the comfortable language in which these can be defended. His impatience is directed especially at a habit of thought: beginning with a conclusion about the goodness of the arrangement and fitting other people's pain into it afterwards.

Candide himself gives this attack its peculiar innocence. His readiness to hope is often absurd, yet a world entirely rid of hope would offer little reason to care what happens in it. The book can mock his education without making every form of trust contemptible. Its travelling company keeps the argument in contact with hunger, work, attachment, and the desire for somewhere to live.

The humour is dark enough to require no pretence that everything is harmless. Voltaire makes laughter an instrument for refusing explanations that have become too pleased with themselves. Beneath the extravagant adventures is a practical concern about how to live once an elegant theory has stopped being an adequate response to another person's misery.
