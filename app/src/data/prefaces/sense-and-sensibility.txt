When Henry Dashwood dies, his widow and three daughters lose the security of Norland, their family home. The property passes to his son by an earlier marriage. A promise of financial help is steadily reasoned away, and the women begin again in a cottage in Devonshire. There, Elinor and Marianne encounter new neighbours, new attachments, and new reasons to question what another person's behaviour can tell them.

Elinor feels deeply but guards what she reveals. Marianne regards unreserved feeling as a test of sincerity. Austen gives neither sister a simple monopoly on wisdom: restraint can conceal suffering, while emotional openness can mistake conviction for knowledge. Visits, music, letters, and apparently trivial conversations become tests of loyalty and judgment.

Published in 1811, the novel makes courtship inseparable from income, inheritance, family obligation, and reputation. Its comedy is often sharpest when a character finds a perfectly respectable explanation for doing exactly what suits them. Read for the distance between what people say, what they know, and what their actions cost somebody else.
