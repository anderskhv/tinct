Alice follows a White Rabbit with a watch down a hole and arrives in a world where the rules of ordinary conversation keep turning against her. A request for directions becomes an argument about destinations; a lesson becomes an accusation; a game of croquet has equipment that refuses to stay still. Alice tries to make sense of it all with the manners, schoolwork and confidence she has brought from home. Her own changing size makes even those resources uncertain.

Published in 1865, Lewis Carroll’s short novel moves through twelve chapters of encounters, interrupted stories and recitations that go wrong. Its nonsense has precise local rules. A word can change its meaning halfway through an exchange, while a creature can remain perfectly certain that only Alice has made a mistake. The narrator shares her curiosity but also lets us see the awkwardness of her assumptions, particularly when she praises her cat to animals the cat would eat.

The tea-party, the Caterpillar and the Queen belong to the same story, but they do not offer a single lesson to decode. Carroll lets playfulness, irritation, fear and tenderness sit close together. Reading the entire book gives its familiar scenes their peculiar rhythm: Alice keeps moving, questioning and trying again, even when a conversation has led nowhere.
