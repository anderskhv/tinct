Crito arrives at the prison with a practical proposal. Socrates can escape, his friends can arrange it, and there are excellent human reasons for doing so. He has children, people who love him, and a sentence that those friends regard as unjust. The urgency of the situation makes further discussion seem almost irresponsible.

Socrates nevertheless insists that the reasons must be examined. A principle held during a comfortable conversation cannot simply be discarded when it becomes expensive. The question is whether escaping would be right, and the fact that others have acted wrongly does not by itself provide an answer.

This gives Plato's brief dialogue an enduring difficulty. Loyalty to friends and family stands beside obligation to the city whose laws have made a shared life possible. The claims do not disappear because the institutions involved are fallible. Nor is gratitude an obviously sufficient reason to obey whatever a state demands. The argument asks how political membership can bind a person, and how far that binding reaches.

Crito's anxieties deserve attention too. He fears losing his friend and fears that others will think he failed to help. Affection, reputation, and moral conviction are mixed together in his appeal, as they often are in urgent advice. Socrates asks him to separate them without ceasing to be a friend.

Consistency sounds admirable until it competes with a readily available way to survive. In this small room, a lifetime of reasoning has to become a decision that someone else will find painfully hard to accept.
