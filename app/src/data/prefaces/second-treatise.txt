Locke asks what could give one person the right to govern another. Greater strength can compel obedience, but compulsion alone does not establish legitimate authority. The Second Treatise of Government attempts to explain political power from the position of people who are naturally free and equal, and who therefore require a reason to be ruled.

His account begins before government, with rights and obligations that do not depend on a ruler's permission. Political society is established to protect people more securely than they can protect themselves when each must judge and enforce his own case. Authority serves a purpose, and that purpose helps define its limits.

Consent carries much of the argument, though its meaning becomes difficult when applied to people born into an existing state. Property raises equally lasting problems. Labour, common resources, money, and accumulation enter an account whose influence has extended far beyond the circumstances of its composition. Locke supplies a vocabulary for liberty that also requires close attention to who is included and whose claims are overlooked.

Government, in this view, is a trust rather than the private possession of those who hold office. A breach of that trust can reopen the question of obedience. Resistance becomes a problem to reason about, not an offence ruled out by the mere existence of authority.

Inheritance, conquest, and custom may explain how power was obtained without proving a right to exercise it. Locke's arguments helped shape later declarations of rights, but reading them in full reveals unresolved questions inside that inheritance: what counts as consent, what deserves protection, and when a people may reclaim the authority exercised in its name.
