Falstaff thinks he understands what will appeal to two married women in Windsor. His confidence includes a useful belief that their affection might improve his finances. The Merry Wives of Windsor places this estimate before women who understand both the proposal and the proposer considerably better than he expects.

Shakespeare's comedy belongs to households, neighbours, gossip, and money. The scale is local, and that intimacy gives deception a practical difficulty: people compare notes. A plan that depends on each target remaining isolated can become excellent material for a community's entertainment.

The wives' friendship is one of the play's great pleasures. They have enough confidence in each other to turn an unwelcome approach into a shared undertaking. Their ingenuity gives domestic life an authority that romantic schemes often overlook. Being respectable does not prevent them from enjoying a thoroughly disreputable joke.

Jealousy supplies another kind of mistaken certainty. A husband can become so committed to discovering betrayal that reassurance merely invites a more elaborate suspicion. The comedy keeps showing how private fantasies meet a social world crowded with other people's intentions.

The language includes accents, verbal misunderstandings, and exuberant physical absurdity. Its warmth comes from the energy with which the inhabitants occupy their small world, each believing that a particular marriage, grievance, or opportunity deserves urgent attention. Shakespeare makes the neighbourhood capable of correcting an inflated sense of importance through laughter, while leaving its inhabitants plenty of room to remain foolish in ways of their own.
