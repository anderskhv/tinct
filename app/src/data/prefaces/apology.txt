Socrates stands before an Athenian jury accused of impiety and corrupting the young. A successful defence would seem to require reassuring the city that he is less troublesome than his reputation suggests. In Plato's Apology, he instead explains why the activity that has made him unwelcome is something Athens needs.

That activity is conversation pursued beyond the point at which most people would politely stop. A respected person claims to understand courage, virtue, or public affairs; Socrates asks what the claim means and discovers that confidence has outrun knowledge. The embarrassment is public, and being exposed is not always experienced as a service. The speech is acutely aware that resentment can accumulate around a person who has committed no straightforward offence.

His position combines an extraordinary modesty about knowledge with an equally extraordinary confidence about his vocation. He will not pretend to know what he does not know, including what death will be like. Yet he speaks as someone who believes that abandoning examination would damage the very thing that makes living worthwhile.

Plato gives this conviction a recognisable personality. Socrates is funny, exacting, proud, and capable of sounding infuriatingly unconcerned with what his listeners want. Admiration need not depend on imagining that he would have been easy company. The difficulty of tolerating him is part of the city's difficulty with philosophy.

Reputation, safety, religious duty, and intellectual honesty have all become immediate practical concerns within a single encounter. A person has to decide which concessions would preserve his life and which would leave him unable to recognise it as his own.
