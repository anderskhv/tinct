Raskolnikov has an idea about the kind of person he might be. History seems full of great men whose achievements excused the harm they caused, and he wonders whether the ordinary rules bind everyone equally. In his cramped room in St Petersburg, the question becomes intensely personal. A theory about humanity offers a young man a way to decide whether his own life is exceptional.

Crime and Punishment brings that theory into contact with a body that gets hungry, feverish, and frightened, and with people who refuse to remain examples in an argument. Dostoevsky keeps intelligence and self-knowledge at a considerable distance from each other. Raskolnikov can reason brilliantly while misunderstanding what is moving him, and an unexpected act of generosity can coexist with appalling contempt.

The city contributes to the pressure. There are crowded rooms, overheard conversations, debts, drink, and families trying to preserve a little dignity with almost nothing to live on. Poverty is a daily humiliation as well as a shortage of money. Everyone seems close enough to interfere, yet genuine contact can remain painfully difficult.

Much of the suspense is conversational. An apparently casual remark may be a test; an explanation can reveal more than silence would have done. People watch one another for the small discrepancy between what is being said and what it costs to say it. Dostoevsky makes a visit or a conversation across a table feel as hazardous as an escape.

The novel also gives serious attention to the people whom an ambitious theory would consider insignificant. Their suffering, loyalty, and demands cannot be settled by deciding whether they contribute to a larger good. They stand before Raskolnikov as particular people. His theory may explain what exceptional men are entitled to do. It cannot make the person asking for kindness cease to stand before him.
