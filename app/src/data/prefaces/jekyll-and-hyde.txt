The world of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde is full of men who know when not to ask a question. Friendship, professional standing, and ordinary discretion depend on allowing certain doors to remain closed. Stevenson makes this respectable restraint the setting for a mystery in which silence becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish from protection of something dangerous.

The story is approached through observers trying to make sense of incomplete evidence. A document, an unexplained association, or a disturbing encounter offers a fragment whose significance is not yet clear. The familiar names in the title can obscure how carefully the book arranges the experience of uncertainty.

Its London has an extraordinary compactness. Streets and interiors carry different moral atmospheres, while neighbouring spaces can seem to belong to separate lives. The city's arrangements give secrecy a physical form: a person may be well known from one entrance and scarcely recognised from another.

Beneath the suspense is an interest in the wish to separate conduct from consequence. Reputation encourages people to identify with the part of themselves that receives approval, while other desires are treated as though they belong to someone else. Stevenson takes that habit seriously enough to explore its imaginative possibilities.

The novella's brevity strengthens its effect. There is little room for the unease to disperse, and every attempt at explanation seems to expose another difficulty. Its enduring subject is the work required to preserve an agreeable account of oneself, especially when the private life has begun making demands that the public identity cannot acknowledge.
