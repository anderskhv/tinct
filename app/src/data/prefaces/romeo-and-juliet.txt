Romeo and Juliet belongs to a city where nearly everyone has something to say about love. Friends turn it into jokes, parents into arrangements, servants into worldly advice. The young lovers discover a language that makes these explanations seem inadequate, though the people offering them still control much of the world in which they must live.

Shakespeare gives their attachment the seriousness it has for them. Its speed does not make its beauty fraudulent. When they speak together, the verse becomes responsive and inventive: one person offers an image, the other changes it, and something takes shape that neither could have made alone. The intimacy is in this attention as much as in the declarations.

Outside it, Verona is noisy, sociable, and dangerously concerned with honour. An insult can become a public obligation because other people are watching. Young men inherit a quarrel that supplies them with loyalties before they have had much opportunity to choose their own. The adults can recognise youthful recklessness more readily than they recognise the habits that encourage it.

The play is also much funnier than its reputation as a monument to doomed love suggests. Mercutio's restless wit and the Nurse's talkative affection give it a crowded, bodily life. Their voices matter because love takes place among people who are interesting in their own right, with temperaments that will not politely withdraw when a romance begins.

The lovers may be inexperienced, but the society around them has no monopoly on wisdom. Shakespeare lets the astonishment of finding another person be fully present, with its awkwardness, delight, and sudden enlargement of what a life might contain.
