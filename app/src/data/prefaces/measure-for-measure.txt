Vienna has laws that have long been imperfectly enforced. When a new authority decides to make them effective, private conduct suddenly acquires public consequences. Measure for Measure begins with the gap between a society's declared standards and the arrangements through which people have actually been living.

Angelo's reputation for severity seems to qualify him to judge others. Shakespeare examines what happens when such a person discovers desires his account of himself has excluded. The problem reaches beyond hypocrisy. Someone who has treated temptation as proof of other people's weakness may be particularly ill prepared to recognise it in himself.

Isabella approaches authority with a plea that places mercy beside law. Her intelligence and conviction make the encounter powerful, but eloquence cannot guarantee safety when the listener controls the terms. The play is acute about the vulnerability of a person whose truthful account may be less credible than an official's reputation.

Around these struggles, the city's less respectable inhabitants conduct a lively commentary on the policies imposed above them. Their humour exposes the practical consequences of moral language. A ruler can announce reform; other people must discover what the announcement means for their bodies, work, and survival.

Arranging an outcome does not necessarily resolve the question of consent, and possessing the power to pardon is not the same as being free from responsibility. Shakespeare makes judgment a difficult activity for everyone involved, including those who are most confident that they stand outside the faults they punish.
