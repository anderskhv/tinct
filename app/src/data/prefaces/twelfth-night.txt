Twelfth Night inhabits a world where people are deeply attached to their own feelings. Orsino cultivates love, Olivia gives form to grief, and Malvolio finds considerable satisfaction in the thought of his own worth. Into this arrangement comes Viola, whose need to improvise a life disturbs the stories everyone else has settled into.

Disguise makes desire unpredictable. Someone can speak sincerely through an assumed identity and be loved for something she cannot openly acknowledge. Shakespeare gives these misunderstandings tenderness as well as comic ingenuity. The difficulty is often that a person is seeing something real without knowing quite whom it belongs to.

Music carries the play's mixed atmosphere. Pleasure and sadness remain close, and a song can recognise time passing while the surrounding company attempts to keep the party going. Feste's wit belongs to this awareness. He can make other people look foolish without offering the consoling idea that cleverness places anyone beyond loss.

The comedy is also capable of cruelty. A shared joke gives its participants a powerful sense of belonging, especially when someone else is assigned the role of outsider. Malvolio's vanity supplies material for laughter, but the uses made of it complicate the pleasure. Festivity does not distribute its generosity equally.

What gives the play its lasting warmth is the need for recognition beneath its elaborate confusion. People long to be answered by someone who sees them, while repeatedly presenting themselves in forms that make being seen difficult. Shakespeare lets the embarrassment, danger, and occasional delight of that predicament fill the same room.
