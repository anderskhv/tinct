Thoreau keeps accounts of what it costs to live, but money is only one of his measures. A possession also costs the time spent earning it, maintaining it, and organising life around it. Walden asks whether the things considered necessary are returning enough life to justify the life spent obtaining them.

His stay beside the pond is an experiment near society, with visitors, neighbours, and the railway still within reach. The book's interest does not depend on imagining a completely self-sufficient hermit. Thoreau changes some conditions of living so that habits accepted elsewhere as inevitable can become visible as choices.

He is a provocative companion. His exaggerations, jokes, and impatience with conformity can be bracing or irritating, sometimes within the same paragraph. The voice belongs to someone trying to wake attention, and it does not always observe the manners of a reassuring guide.

The close observation of the pond gives the argument another pace. Ice, animals, weather, and the changing seasons offer forms of eventfulness that a hurried life can miss. Thoreau's attention makes a small place abundant. The reduction in possessions accompanies an expansion in what there is to notice.

The practical question is therefore connected to an imaginative one. A life organised around acquiring more may become poor in the capacity to experience what is already present. Walden proposes that deliberate living requires examining both the work demanded by convention and the pleasures convention has taught people to overlook. Its challenge remains personal enough to resist being settled by an approving quotation: an account of expenses has to include what a particular way of life is costing its owner.
