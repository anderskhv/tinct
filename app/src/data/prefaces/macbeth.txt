Macbeth knows a good deal about why he should leave his life alone. He has honour, the confidence of his king, and a place in the world that other men would envy. He can describe the wrongness of what tempts him with extraordinary clarity. Shakespeare gives ambition a man who understands its objections and continues to want what it offers.

The witches are part of the fascination, but so is the work Macbeth does with what they tell him. A possibility begins to resemble a promise; waiting can feel like weakness; an obstacle can become an injustice. The future acquires such importance that the present starts to seem like something to be cleared away. His imagination, which might have warned him, becomes absorbed in making the desired life conceivable.

This is also a play about a marriage. Macbeth and his wife know where each other's courage and shame are vulnerable. Their intimacy gives their words unusual force: encouragement, reproach, and a claim about what love ought to prove can become almost impossible to separate. They share an ambition without necessarily understanding what living with it will demand of the other.

Shakespeare compresses the drama until its language seems to carry the strain. Sleep, darkness, blood, and the ordinary sounds of a house become intensely disturbing. Much of the horror comes from familiar things ceasing to offer reassurance. A person may still be able to give orders while losing the ability to rest quietly in a room.

Macbeth wants the security that he imagines success will bring, and every threat to that security makes another act seem necessary. He imagines success as an end to uncertainty, although his mind keeps finding reasons to be afraid. The future he wants would still have to be inhabited by the person doing the wanting.
