Richard tells the audience what he intends and then goes out to persuade other people of something quite different. The intimacy is immediately compromising. His wit makes the deception entertaining, and advance knowledge can make a spectator feel clever while watching someone else being used. Shakespeare turns the enjoyment of a brilliant performance into part of the play's political interest.

This Richard is an actor within the action. He adjusts his manner to the listener, borrows the language of religion or affection, and recognises the ambitions that make others available to him. People need not be entirely fooled to cooperate. Fear, convenience, and the prospect of advantage can do work that belief alone would not accomplish.

The surrounding world has been shaped by dynastic violence. Grief carries a memory that Richard's account of the future would prefer to bypass. The women whose losses interrupt political calculation give the play voices that cannot be absorbed into his performance as easily as flatterers and allies.

The historical Richard must be distinguished from Shakespeare's creation. The drama draws on hostile traditions and shapes history for theatrical purposes; its association of bodily difference with villainy also deserves scrutiny. Its power as a play does not establish its accuracy as a portrait.

What remains exceptionally alive is the study of charisma in the service of domination. Richard makes his purposes attractive through the sheer energy with which he pursues them. Shakespeare allows that attraction to operate while showing how a political world can become dependent on the next performance of a person whose main loyalty is to his own advancement.
