Elizabeth Bennet enjoys being a good judge of people. She notices absurdity quickly, can turn an awkward encounter into an excellent story, and has enough independence to resist the opinions of those around her. These are attractive qualities in a companion and potentially dangerous qualities in someone who is mistaken. Jane Austen gives the pleasure of being right a rival in the much less comfortable business of understanding another person.

Pride and Prejudice is wonderfully funny about the ways people advertise themselves without meaning to. A compliment reveals its speaker's condescension; a display of modesty takes a great deal of work; a person explaining why a marriage would be advantageous forgets that affection might require a different argument. The comedy depends on very precise attention to what someone thinks a conversation is accomplishing.

Underneath the manners lie practical anxieties. Marriage can determine a woman's home, income, and standing, and the Bennet sisters do not all possess the same resources for navigating that fact. Their mother's behaviour may be embarrassing, but the insecurity that drives her is real. Austen lets the ridiculous and the reasonable occupy the same person.

The romance draws much of its force from the difficulty of changing an opinion that has become part of one's self-respect. Intelligence offers no exemption from vanity. Nor does good breeding guarantee consideration, or an easy manner prove a generous character. People have to be encountered again after the first satisfying explanation of them has failed.

Austen makes this process pleasurable without making it effortless. Her world is small enough for a visit, a letter, or an invitation to alter its balance, and crowded enough that nobody can entirely control what others make of them. Within those limits she finds room for a large achievement: the possibility of becoming more truthful without losing the wit and energy that made life enjoyable in the first place.
