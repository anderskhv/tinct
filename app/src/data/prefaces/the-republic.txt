The Republic asks for something more demanding than a defence of respectable behaviour. A just person may be despised, while a successful cheat enjoys wealth, friendship, and public honour. If those are the circumstances, what remains to recommend justice? Plato allows the challenge to become strong enough that a comfortable answer will no longer do.

The conversation grows from this problem into an extraordinary examination of a human life. Education, desire, work, art, political power, and the possibility of knowledge all become relevant. To make justice easier to see, Socrates and his companions build a city in speech. Its institutions expose questions that are harder to notice inside a single person: who should govern, which appetites deserve satisfaction, and what happens when getting more becomes the organising purpose of everything.

Some proposals are deeply unsettling. The regulation of poetry, the authority of philosophers, and the control exercised over private life make the imagined city difficult to admire without qualification. That difficulty belongs to the reading. Plato's dialogue gives neither contemporary liberal assumptions nor ordinary admiration for authority a comfortable place to rest.

Its famous images have endured because they make an intellectual problem imaginable. A whole understanding of reality might depend on habits acquired before there was any opportunity to examine them. Education could require losing the approval of people who share those habits, rather than simply becoming more accomplished within them.

The voices matter as much as the architecture of the argument. Different speakers want different things from justice, and Socrates has to discover what sort of answer each will accept. The Republic preserves the excitement of a conversation whose participants cannot keep their original question safely contained. An inquiry into good conduct becomes an inquiry into what is worth wanting at all.
