Descartes decides to examine the foundations of what he believes. The senses have sometimes deceived him, dreams can feel convincing while they last, and habits acquired early may have survived without serious inspection. Meditations on First Philosophy follows an attempt to discover whether anything can remain certain when these familiar supports are deliberately withdrawn.

The form is unusually intimate for such an ambitious undertaking. The argument proceeds through the experience of a thinker trying to maintain attention, noticing the return of old assumptions, and beginning again. Doubt becomes an activity with a rhythm, rather than a fashionable refusal to believe anything.

His inquiry soon reaches questions about what the self is and how knowledge of a world outside thought could be justified. A familiar object changes its sensible qualities while remaining the same object; recognising it seems to require more than a collection of impressions. Descartes uses such ordinary experiences to expose difficulties beneath apparently effortless acts of knowing.

God is central to the structure he attempts to establish. The arguments about divine existence and the reliability of clear understanding belong to the work's philosophical burden, and have attracted objections from its earliest readers. Accepting the initial exercise in doubt does not commit anyone to accepting every step by which certainty is rebuilt.

Bodies, objects, and the thinking self cease to be items that can simply be assumed. Descartes tries to earn back a world that ordinary life takes for granted, and the effort makes visible how much is involved in saying that something is known.
