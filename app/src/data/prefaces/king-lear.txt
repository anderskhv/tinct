Lear wants to relinquish the work of kingship while preserving the experience of being king. He expects love to remain visible, deference to continue, and his presence to carry its accustomed weight. Shakespeare begins with a wish that age can make painfully understandable: to need other people without losing authority over the terms on which they respond.

The family cannot be separated from the kingdom. A father's preference distributes power, a daughter's speech becomes a public act, and an injury within the household reaches people far beyond it. The play makes love difficult to measure precisely where Lear most wants a measurement.

Its attention steadily extends towards forms of need that authority can usually keep out of sight. Bodies require warmth, shelter, food, and care regardless of the titles attached to them. A person who has commanded recognition can discover how much recognition depended on the command. Shakespeare brings the grandeur of human self-conception into contact with this physical vulnerability.

The language ranges from magnificent rage to the smallest offers of assistance. Humour persists in the company of suffering, often through the Fool's freedom to say what more prudent people avoid. Kindness can be improvised without any assurance that it will be rewarded or even effective.

King Lear gives little comfort to the belief that the world reliably distributes consequences according to desert. Its tenderness comes from the people who continue to attend to one another within that uncertainty. The play makes care feel substantial because so little else can be counted on, and because the person who needs it may be proud, difficult, and late in understanding what has been given.
