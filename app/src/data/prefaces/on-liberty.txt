A society can make an opinion difficult to express without passing a law against it. Disapproval, ridicule, and the risk of exclusion may do much of the work that censorship would otherwise perform. John Stuart Mill's On Liberty is concerned with this social power as well as the authority of government.

His defence of discussion asks more than tolerance for views that seem harmless. A suppressed opinion might be true; it might contain something the prevailing view has missed; even an error can force a sound belief to recover the reasons that support it. Agreement becomes intellectually thin when nobody has to understand what could be said against it.

Mill extends the argument from opinions to ways of living. A person's character develops through choosing, comparing, and sometimes making mistakes. A society that permits only familiar forms of conduct can lose the capacity to discover better ones. Eccentricity has value partly because convention is not a reliable measure of what a human life can become.

The limits matter. Mill distinguishes conduct that concerns the individual from harm to others, while leaving an enduring argument about where that boundary belongs. His own exclusions, including his treatment of colonised peoples, expose limits in the universality of the freedom he defends. The book's principles give later readers grounds for challenging parts of the book itself.

Holding a belief well requires more than surrounding it with agreement. Living freely requires room for people whose choices remain unattractive to their neighbours. Mill asks a society to make space for capacities it cannot develop by deciding everyone's life in advance.
