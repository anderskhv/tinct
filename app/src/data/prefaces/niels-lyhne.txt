Niels Lyhne is about a young man who expects a great deal from life and spends much of his time imagining how he will live it. He wants to be a poet, to love deeply, to think honestly. In his imagination these ambitions have already acquired the richness of experience. The difficulty comes when another person, or an ordinary day, asks something of him that he has not rehearsed.

J. P. Jacobsen understands the peculiar comfort of having an exceptional life still ahead. While it remains a possibility, it need not suffer comparison with anything actually achieved. A work of art can stay perfect as long as it is unwritten; a love can remain extraordinary while little is known about the person who inspires it. Niels’s sensitivity gives him access to pleasures other people might miss, but it also supplies him with endless ways of postponing an encounter with what is there.

The novel is particularly perceptive about the experience of being loved by someone with a powerful imagination. There can be something lonely about receiving devotion meant for a finer, more mysterious person than oneself. The women around Niels have desires of their own, and his reverence can leave surprisingly little room for them. Jacobsen notices how easily admiration becomes a demand that another person remain worthy of the story being told about her.

Published in 1880, Niels Lyhne also belongs to a generation’s struggle to live without religious belief. Jacobsen had translated Darwin into Danish, and he took seriously the possibility that human beings must make their lives without divine supervision or the promise of another world. He was equally interested in how much a conviction can ask of the person who holds it. Disbelief does not remove the longing to be protected, to see suffering answered, or to believe that someone loved cannot simply cease to exist. An idea that seems entirely convincing in conversation may be much harder to inhabit alone.

This gives the book an intimacy that arguments about religion often lack. Belief is entangled with temperament, memory, pride, and need. Niels wants to be honest, but he also wants an honesty he can bear. The same man who distrusts religious consolation is capable of expecting something very like salvation from love or art.

Jacobsen writes about these difficulties with an abundance of beauty. Weather, flowers, rooms, and the changing light receive an attention that makes the world seem worth lingering in. His prose shares the imaginative richness that makes Niels so susceptible to dreams; he understands their attraction too well to dismiss them.

The result is a novel about the longing for a profound life, including the vanity, tenderness, and fear that can accompany it. Niels wants his existence to amount to something, and that wish deserves sympathy. Jacobsen follows it far enough to ask how much of the life within reach can go unnoticed while someone waits to become the person he has imagined.
