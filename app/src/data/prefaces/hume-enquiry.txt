A person expects a flame to burn because flames have burned before. The expectation is indispensable, yet explaining what proves that the next case will resemble the earlier ones turns out to be surprisingly difficult. Hume's Enquiry takes this ordinary movement of thought and makes its foundations available for inspection.

His method is to ask where ideas come from and what experience actually supplies. Seeing one event repeatedly follow another is not the same as seeing a necessary connection between them. The mind contributes habits of expectation that feel so natural they can be mistaken for something directly observed in the world.

This does not leave Hume unable to act until philosophy repairs every uncertainty. Human nature continues its work. The interest is in recognising the difference between a useful, deeply rooted expectation and a demonstration that could compel assent through reason alone. Intellectual modesty can coexist with practical confidence.

The inquiry extends to freedom, necessity, miracles, and the limits of religious argument. Testimony has to be weighed against other experience; the strength of a belief is not established by the intensity with which it is held. Hume's scepticism is often most effective when it asks a claim to specify exactly what would support it.

His prose has a clarity that makes the difficulties harder to dismiss. He can begin with something apparently obvious and show how much has been supplied by custom, association, or an unnoticed assumption. The pleasure of the book is in following a mind that remains sociable and measured while steadily reducing the distance between a grand philosophical problem and the way an ordinary day is understood.
