Pip learns to be ashamed of his life before he knows what might deserve shame. His home, his manners, and the people who care for him begin to look inadequate beside a more splendid world. The wish to improve himself carries an increasingly difficult question about what, exactly, improvement would consist of.

Great Expectations understands the excitement of imagining a different future. Money seems capable of changing the meaning of the past, making an awkward boy admirable and an impossible attachment plausible. Dickens lets that hope become attractive enough for its errors to matter. Pip's aspirations contain real hunger as well as vanity.

Around him is a remarkable population of people who have made arrangements with disappointment. Some preserve an injury, some turn affection into obligation, and some conduct ordinary kindness without much vocabulary for explaining it. Dickens's eccentric characters can be funny at first sight and unexpectedly exact about a familiar habit once the laughter has settled.

The novel is told by a man looking back at the boy he was, and the distance between them gives it much of its tenderness. The younger Pip notices the wrong things; the older narrator cannot simply cancel those perceptions without losing the truth of how it felt to have them. Memory becomes a way of revisiting people who were not properly seen the first time.

The mystery surrounding Pip's prospects draws him onwards, while the life he hopes to leave behind keeps making claims upon him. A person can spend years acquiring the signs of distinction while becoming less able to recognise goodness when it appears in an unfashionable form. Dickens makes that confusion painfully understandable, and keeps the possibility of better judgment alive.
