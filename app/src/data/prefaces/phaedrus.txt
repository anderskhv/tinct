Phaedrus is delighted by a speech he has heard and eager to share it. Socrates is delighted to find someone willing to talk. They leave Athens for a place beside a stream, and a conversation about love gradually becomes an inquiry into what words can do to a soul.

The setting matters. Shade, summer sounds, and the attraction between speakers give the dialogue a sensuous life that a summary of its arguments can easily lose. Plato is examining persuasion in the company of people who are already being persuaded: by eloquence, by beauty, and by the pleasure of being attended to.

Love raises a problem that careful calculation cannot settle on its own. The lover's disturbance may be dangerous, but a life protected from every kind of disturbance could also be closed to something valuable. Socrates gives desire an imaginative grandeur while asking what sort of discipline would allow it to become more than possession. The famous image of the soul as a charioteer with mismatched horses makes inner conflict active and difficult to govern.

The discussion of rhetoric follows naturally. To speak well requires knowing something about the person being addressed and about the truth of the matter, rather than merely assembling effects that win agreement. A polished performance may succeed without producing understanding.

The same concern eventually reaches writing itself. Words can travel far from their author, repeat themselves with apparent authority, and remain unable to answer the question a particular reader needs to ask. Plato explores that limitation in a written work whose conversations have continued for centuries. Its invitation is to the kind of attention through which an impressive statement can become a thought that is actually understood.
