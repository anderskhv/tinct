Epictetus is interested in the ways a person can become dependent without noticing. A reputation must be maintained, an invitation secured, an insult answered. Soon strangers are deciding the conditions of a tolerable day. The Manual begins by separating what belongs to one's own judgment and choice from what remains subject to other people and circumstances.

The distinction is easy to repeat and difficult to practise. Epictetus takes it into ordinary situations: a crowded bathhouse, a dinner, a family relationship, the sight of someone enjoying an advantage. His examples expose the extra demand often concealed inside a reasonable intention. Going somewhere is one aim; requiring everyone there to behave well is another, and the second places contentment in unreliable hands.

Compiled by his pupil Arrian, this short work condenses a demanding philosophy. It asks for more than composure. A person must examine what deserves to be called good, what is worth fearing, and how social duties should be performed when praise is uncertain. Its language can be severe, especially about loss, because it attempts to change attachments that ordinary reassurance leaves intact.

There is a bracing suspicion of philosophical display. Knowing the terminology counts for little if the next provocation produces exactly the old response. The real evidence appears in conduct, often when nobody is impressed enough to notice it.

The freedom promised here is correspondingly specific. Life may remain difficult, and power over events may remain small. What might change is the ease with which every disappointment becomes a command to surrender judgment. Epictetus treats that capacity as something worth practising with the seriousness usually reserved for acquiring wealth or status.
