Coriolanus can perform extraordinary service for Rome and still find the citizens of Rome almost intolerable. He is prepared to risk his body for them, but asking for their approval feels degrading. Shakespeare builds a political drama around this difficult relation between deserving recognition and accepting the people who have the power to grant it.

Military distinction does not automatically become political judgment. The soldier's directness can appear honourable, especially beside the calculations of those who manage public opinion. It can also conceal a refusal to regard other people's needs as worthy of negotiation. Coriolanus experiences compromise as an injury to the self he has been taught to value.

His mother has helped form that self. Volumnia's pride gives courage an intimate audience, making public achievement inseparable from a son's relation to the person who first defined its worth. The play asks how independent an apparently uncompromising man can be when his identity depends so heavily on another person's expectations.

The citizens have hunger, memory, and political interests of their own. They can be influenced without becoming an undifferentiated crowd, and their representatives can defend genuine claims while enjoying the uses of conflict. Shakespeare distributes intelligence and self-interest across the argument rather than allowing either side to possess them exclusively.

A community needs exceptional capacities, but it also needs those capacities to become accountable. Coriolanus makes the desire to remain wholly oneself collide with the demands of belonging to a city whose other inhabitants cannot simply be treated as an inconvenience.
