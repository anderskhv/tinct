The Iliad gives extraordinary attention to people whose lives a war is about to make expendable. A warrior steps forward with a name, a home, and parents who once expected a future for him. The poem may follow him only briefly, but that brief attention keeps the battlefield from becoming an arrangement of anonymous losses.

Its heroes care intensely about honour. Public recognition helps give a short life meaning, and an insult can threaten more than pride: it can seem to invalidate everything danger and effort were supposed to secure. Achilles' anger belongs to this world. Homer allows its force to be felt while showing how far the consequences of one person's injury can travel.

Across the lines, the Trojans have their own affections and obligations. Hector's place in the poem is inseparable from the city and family that need him. The enemy is available to sympathy without the conflict becoming easy to resolve. Two people can have compelling reasons to live and still stand armed against one another.

The gods enlarge the spectacle and sharpen the difference between their existence and human life. They quarrel, intervene, and take sides, but the stakes are not the same for beings who cannot die. Mortal courage has a gravity that their power does not possess.

Homer repeatedly sets warfare beside another world: farming, weather, animals, craftsmen, children. The comparisons bring the ordinary abundance of life into the scene of its destruction. This is one reason the poem's beauty can be so painful. It knows how much exists beyond battle and how completely battle can command attention. Its ancient account of glory preserves, with equal care, the things for which glory is a poor exchange.
