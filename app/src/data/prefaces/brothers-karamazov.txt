The Brothers Karamazov gives questions about God and morality to people who are also worried about money, jealous of one another, and liable to say something unforgivable at a family gathering. Their arguments have consequences because they belong to lives already in disorder. A conviction can be a source of courage, a defence against shame, or a way of granting permission to an appetite.

The Karamazov family provides a crowded and often grotesquely funny setting for these problems. Its father seems determined to make dignity impossible, while his sons struggle with desires and loyalties that refuse to remain neatly separate. Intelligence, sensuality, and religious hope do not divide humanity into three convenient types. Each person contains more than his preferred explanation of himself.

Dostoevsky allows the objections to faith to become formidable. The suffering of the innocent cannot be waved away by an assurance that everything contributes to a higher purpose. At the same time, the novel gives religious life an emotional and practical substance that exceeds an argument for God's existence. Listening, forgiving, and accepting responsibility have to be attempted among actual people.

This is part of the book's generosity. It can expose a person's vanity without exhausting the interest of that person. Someone behaving disgracefully may also be capable of a perception that matters, while someone with admirable principles may fail badly at the next encounter. The writing keeps open the distance between an explanation and a human being.

Its scale allows small incidents and enormous arguments to accumulate together. Children, servants, monks, officials, and lovers all contribute to the world in which the family acts. The question of how to live remains inseparable from the question of what is owed to the people nearby, including those it would be convenient to regard as someone else's concern.
