Aeneas is trying to find a future for people whose world has been destroyed. Behind him is Troy; ahead is a country he has not yet learned to call home. The gods promise a destiny larger than any single life, but that promise does not tell him how to bear the losses already suffered or how to recognise the claims of people he meets along the way.

Virgil takes the voyages and warfare of Homer into the founding story of Rome. The change of perspective matters. Aeneas has obligations to the dead, to his companions, and to descendants he will never know. Heroism becomes entangled with carrying something forward when personal happiness might recommend stopping elsewhere.

The poem gives this duty considerable dignity without allowing its costs to become invisible. Other people have homes, loyalties, and hopes that do not fit the future promised to the Trojans. Virgil can turn towards them with a sympathy that complicates the grandeur of Rome's beginnings. A victorious history contains lives that cannot experience its outcome as a blessing.

This makes the Aeneid a remarkably rich poem about belonging. A refugee needs somewhere to settle, but arrival can mean danger to those already there. Memory preserves identity and can also make the present difficult to inhabit. The gods themselves argue over a future whose human participants must act with far less knowledge.

Virgil's tenderness often resides in a brief attention to someone the larger story could easily pass by. Youth, parental hope, the attachment to a particular place: these remain valuable even when history has other business. The poem imagines the majesty of a civilisation while keeping close to the people who must pay for its existence.
