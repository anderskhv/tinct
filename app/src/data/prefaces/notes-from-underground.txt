Notes from Underground is about a man who wants other people’s affection but cannot bear how much he wants it. He resents their company, studies their opinions, and conducts arguments with them long after they have left. Alone, he can arrange these encounters to his satisfaction. In company, there is always the possibility that nobody will recognise the importance of what is happening to him.

Dostoevsky’s narrator is an unnamed former civil servant in St Petersburg, educated, isolated, and fiercely conscious of his own intelligence. He knows a great deal about his less attractive motives. He can catch himself being petty almost as soon as the feeling arises, then find something contemptible in the satisfaction of having caught himself. His thoughts keep moving while his life becomes harder to change.

This gives the book an unusual intimacy. Most accounts of a life acquire some order in the telling: mistakes become lessons, suffering leads to understanding, and the person remembering knows more than the person who acted. The Underground Man keeps disturbing that arrangement. He revises his claims, anticipates objections, and quarrels with the sympathy he seems to be asking for. His confession is still an encounter in which he hopes to come out ahead.

He is often very funny. Much of the humour comes from the enormous significance he gives to things that scarcely register with anyone else. A minor humiliation can sustain elaborate preparations for revenge, while the imagined triumph requires an audience whose indifference would ruin everything. He regards ordinary social confidence with contempt and envy, suspecting that people who act without endlessly questioning themselves must be less intelligent than he is. It is a consoling explanation for being unhappy.

Published in 1864, the novel also takes up the promise that a better understanding of human interests could produce a better society. Its narrator objects that people are capable of knowing what would benefit them and deliberately choosing otherwise. They may sacrifice comfort, opportunity, even affection for the pleasure of refusing an arrangement made on their behalf. A life organised entirely for someone’s happiness can still leave that person wanting to kick a hole in it.

The objection matters, but so does the man making it. Dostoevsky allows him to expose something inadequate in an orderly account of human behaviour while also showing how readily he uses the argument to dignify his own spite. A defence of freedom can become a justification for hurting someone. A perceptive criticism of society can spare its author the trouble of examining what he owes the person in front of him.

Beneath all this reasoning is the difficulty of accepting kindness without turning it into a question of power. Being pitied is humiliating; being loved would require trusting someone whose judgment cannot be controlled. The narrator longs for an encounter in which he can finally stop defending himself, yet carries his defences into every possibility of one.

Dostoevsky makes these habits visible with a precision that can outlast the details of the story. The private rehearsal of an argument, the pleasure of nursing an injury, the confession offered before anyone can make an accusation: each becomes harder to mistake for innocence. The book understands how much effort can go into protecting a self that its owner would dearly like to escape.
