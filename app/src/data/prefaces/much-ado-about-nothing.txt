Beatrice and Benedick have made being unimpressed into a form of entertainment. Each can anticipate the other's vanity, find the vulnerable point in a remark, and keep an audience amused. Their exchanges in Much Ado About Nothing have the pleasure of two people whose attention to one another has become too elaborate to look entirely indifferent.

The comedy takes place in a sociable world of visits, celebrations, rumours, and overheard conversation. People are continually learning what someone else supposedly thinks. Shakespeare finds both pleasure and danger in this indirect knowledge: a story can encourage affection, but it can also give suspicion the appearance of proof.

The contrast matters because reputation is distributed unequally. A man's confidence in what he has heard can possess more authority than a woman's account of her own conduct. The cheerful community can become harsh remarkably quickly when its assumptions about honour are activated.

Beatrice's wit acquires weight within this atmosphere. The ability to laugh at conventional courtship is one kind of independence; knowing when a joke is no longer an adequate response requires another. Benedick's cleverness likewise has to encounter demands that cannot be satisfied by a memorable line.

Even the foolish officials contribute something the sophisticated company lacks. Shakespeare allows mistaken words and sound perception to coexist, just as elegant speech can accompany poor judgment. The play's warmth comes from a romance in which intelligence remains attractive but must become capable of loyalty. Being excellent company is a promising beginning; being someone another person can rely on is a more substantial achievement.
