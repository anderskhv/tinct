Machiavelli is interested in the ruler whose admirable qualities help ruin the state. Generosity can require oppressive taxation; mercy can permit disorder; keeping a promise can become dangerous when the other party has no intention of doing the same. The Prince asks political judgment to attend to consequences that a flattering description of character can conceal.

Its examples come from a world of unstable governments, invading armies, and rapidly changing alliances. Holding power is a practical problem involving soldiers, money, institutions, and the expectations of the governed. The book's notorious advice belongs to that setting, where an error may cost considerably more than the reputation of the person making it.

Appearances matter because most people judge a ruler without direct access to his motives or deliberations. A reputation for virtue can be useful even when the conduct needed to maintain authority conflicts with it. Machiavelli explores this distance with a frankness that remains disquieting. Political success and moral admiration cannot be assumed to coincide.

Yet the argument is more demanding than a celebration of cruelty. Hatred is dangerous, dependence on borrowed force is precarious, and an apparent triumph can leave its author exposed. He repeatedly distinguishes an effective action from an impulsive or theatrical one. Ruthlessness offers no exemption from bad judgment.

The Prince also recognises how much lies beyond calculation. A temperament that succeeds in one set of circumstances may fail when conditions change, precisely because success has taught its owner to trust it. Machiavelli's attention to this problem makes the book more than a collection of clever manoeuvres. It considers the unsettling possibility that political responsibility may require capacities that ordinary ideas of being a good person do not adequately describe.
