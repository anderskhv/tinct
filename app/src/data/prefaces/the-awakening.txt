The Awakening is about the difficulty of wanting a different life when the life already given to someone is considered a good one. Edna Pontellier is married, comfortably situated, and a mother of two. There are people who love her, and people who depend on her. Her unhappiness has consequences for all of them, which makes it tempting to wish she would simply get over it.

Kate Chopin published the novel in 1899, but the arguments it provokes are remarkably unsettled. Readers still disagree about whether Edna is courageous or selfish, whether she asks too much of life or has been allowed too little. Those disagreements often become personal. How someone feels about her can depend on whether they remember a parent who wanted to be elsewhere, or recognise the experience of becoming so necessary to other people that there is scarcely any room left to be a person oneself.

Chopin gives both experiences weight. Love brings obligations, and those obligations can occupy a whole life. A woman may love her children without finding motherhood sufficient, just as she may appreciate her husband without feeling that being his wife is enough. The difficulty is finding words for what is missing when everyone can point to what she has.

This is part of what makes the novel more interesting than a simple story of an unhappy marriage. A better husband would not necessarily answer all of Edna’s desires. Neither would another man. There are pleasures and ambitions that belong to her alone, though she has had little practice in recognising them or deciding what they are worth. Discovering a desire is easier than knowing how to build a life around it, particularly when other lives are already bound up with one’s own.

For a book that produces such fierce arguments, The Awakening is full of physical pleasure. Its Louisiana is warm, languorous, sociable, alive with music and the presence of the sea. Chopin makes the attraction of being alive palpable. The wish for freedom acquires substance through the possibility of enjoying a body, an afternoon, a talent, rather than remaining an admirable idea.

Edna herself can be frustrating. Her privilege leaves her blind to things, and her dissatisfaction does not confer any special wisdom. Chopin nevertheless gives her an attention that literature has often reserved for men: the freedom to be an imperfect person whose longing deserves a whole book. She need not become an exemplary mother, an exceptional artist, or a woman with an unbearable husband before her inner life is worth taking seriously.
