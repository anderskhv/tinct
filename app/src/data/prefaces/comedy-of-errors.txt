In The Comedy of Errors, perfectly reasonable people keep receiving evidence that the world has become impossible. A stranger knows their name, a promised object has apparently vanished, or someone remembers an encounter that never happened. Shakespeare builds a farce from the confidence with which people interpret incomplete information.

Two sets of identical twins provide the machinery, but the confusion reaches into serious attachments. A wife hears rejection where a bewildered stranger intends none. A servant receives blame for failing to understand an instruction given to someone else. People keep trying to restore order by insisting more forcefully on an account that is locally reasonable and entirely wrong.

The speed is a large part of the pleasure. Explanations produce further misunderstandings, anger changes the meaning of a reply, and each new encounter inherits trouble from the last. Wordplay gives the servants room to resist a world in which the powerful are certain of their own sense and remarkably ready to use their hands.

Around the comic activity is a story of separation and the search for family. The wish to find another person matters before the errors begin, giving the play's questions about identity an emotional foundation. Being recognised is more than having a name correctly attached to a face; it means recovering a place in someone else's life.

A person can know who he is and still depend on others to confirm it. Shakespeare makes that dependence briefly unreliable, then follows the increasingly inventive efforts of people trying to make the world familiar again.
