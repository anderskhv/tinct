Aristotle takes seriously the strange pleasure of watching invented people suffer. An audience can know that a story is made up, find its events distressing, and still want the experience. In the Poetics, this becomes something to investigate with the same care that might be given to the workings of a living thing.

His attention falls on arrangement. Events acquire force through their relation to one another: a discovery changes the meaning of an earlier action, or an attempt to secure one result brings about its opposite. Surprise alone is insufficient. The strongest turn of events feels unforeseen and, once it happens, intimately connected to what came before.

This approach makes the book valuable far beyond the Greek stage. It provides a vocabulary for noticing why one story remains affecting while another, with equally dramatic ingredients, feels arbitrary. Character, language, and spectacle all matter, but Aristotle keeps returning to what people do and how their actions form a whole.

The surviving work is compressed and sometimes tantalisingly incomplete. Its famous account of tragedy's effect on pity and fear has generated centuries of disagreement. It is more rewarding as an exacting attempt to understand art than as a set of rules guaranteed to produce it. Aristotle argues from works he admires, and those loyalties give his reasoning a particular shape.

There is pleasure in watching a powerful response become available for thought without being dismissed as mere feeling. A story can reveal something general about human action through events that never occurred. Aristotle's inquiry grants that achievement its own kind of seriousness, and helps explain why an invented fate can become part of a real person's experience.
