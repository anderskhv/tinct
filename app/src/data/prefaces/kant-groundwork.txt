An honest shopkeeper may deal fairly because cheating would damage business. Another person may do the same thing because a customer deserves fair treatment, whether honesty pays or not. Kant begins from differences of this kind: outwardly similar actions can express very different principles, and the distinction matters to what makes conduct morally worthy.

The Groundwork searches for a basis of morality that does not depend on a fortunate temperament, a promised reward, or the outcome someone hopes to produce. Sympathy and happiness have value, but they vary. Kant wants to understand an obligation that can still make a claim when inclination points elsewhere.

This leads him to ask whether the principle behind an action could be willed as a law for everyone. A person cannot simply reserve a convenient exception for himself while relying on others to maintain the practice that makes the exception useful. Reason asks the agent to consider what he is authorising, beyond the immediate attraction of his own case.

The other enduring formulation concerns humanity as an end in itself. People have purposes of their own, and their capacity to choose cannot be treated as just another resource for someone else's plans. The thought gives dignity a basis distinct from usefulness, status, and the affection a person happens to inspire.

Kant's prose takes work because he is trying to justify these claims rather than leave them as noble sentiments. His account of freedom is particularly ambitious: acting freely involves giving oneself a rational law, not merely following whichever desire is strongest. The book attempts to explain why an ordinary person's obligation can have an authority that neither power nor preference is entitled to cancel.
