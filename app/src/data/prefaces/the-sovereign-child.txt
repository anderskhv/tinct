Aaron Stupple, a physician and father of five, makes the case for raising children with no rules at all. His source is Taking Children Seriously, the noncoercive parenting movement that Sarah Fitz-Claridge and the Oxford physicist David Deutsch started in Britain in the 1990s. He found its archived journals in 2018, just before his first child was born, and the book reports how he and his wife have applied the philosophy since.

It opens where control seems most obviously necessary: food, sleep and screens. Stupple then names four harms that enforced rules do to a child, proposes problem-solving towards a solution that both parent and child want, and answers the standard objections, from the toddler who runs into the street to the sibling who hits. Later chapters walk through an ordinary day, deal with siblings, and describe how a family can give up rules in small, reversible steps.

The last two chapters set out the theory underneath. Karl Popper's critical rationalism holds that knowledge grows through guesses corrected by criticism, and Stupple treats parenting as the stewardship of a child's growing knowledge. He links the coercion of children to the long stagnation of human societies before the Enlightenment. Read for how a theory of knowledge turns into decisions about dinner, bedtime and YouTube, and for the points where you disagree.
