Rousseau wants to understand how people can live under laws without merely living under other people's will. A government may provide order and still leave its subjects dependent on someone else's purposes. The Social Contract seeks a form of political association in which obedience can be compatible with freedom.

The ambition is demanding because freedom has to survive collective life. Private interests do not disappear when people become citizens, and agreement among powerful groups may still neglect the common good. Rousseau's general will names an orientation towards that common good, not simply whatever a majority happens to want at a particular moment.

This distinction creates as many difficulties as it resolves. Someone must judge what the shared interest requires, and the claim to speak for everyone can become dangerous. The work's most troubling formulations belong to this problem: an ideal of self-government can appear to authorise coercion in the name of the freedom it promises.

Rousseau is attentive to the conditions beneath institutions. Vast inequalities can make formal political equality hollow. Civic habits, scale, dependence, and the relationship between citizens and representatives affect whether a people can meaningfully govern itself. A constitutional arrangement cannot do all the work while its members remain passive.

Citizenship is an activity through which people participate in making a common world, with obligations that cannot be reduced to buying protection from a state. Rousseau makes political freedom more ambitious than being left alone, and leaves a difficult argument about whether that ambition can be realised without giving collective authority too much power.
