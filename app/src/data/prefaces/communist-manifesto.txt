The Communist Manifesto describes a world in which almost nothing is allowed to remain settled. Production changes, markets expand, inherited occupations disappear, and familiar relationships are reorganised around money. Marx and Engels give capitalism an extraordinary transformative power before making their case against the society it creates.

Their admiration for its energy is part of the argument. The bourgeoisie has accomplished things earlier ages could scarcely imagine, while making continual disruption a condition of economic life. A person may experience this movement as opportunity or dispossession; an entire society can become dependent on forces that no individual seems able to direct.

The pamphlet interprets those changes through class conflict. Work, property, and political authority form an arrangement whose beneficiaries can mistake their own interests for the natural interests of humanity. What looks permanent may be historical, and what has been historically made may be capable of being changed.

Its form is important. This is a manifesto written to persuade and mobilise, with the compression, confidence, and polemical exclusions that purpose encourages. It is neither a full account of Marx's later economic work nor a detailed design for a future society. Its predictions and proposed measures invite examination alongside the historical experiences that followed.

Marx and Engels join an account of global economic upheaval to a claim about who might act within it. Their prose makes social arrangements appear newly contingent, and gives an urgent political direction to the thought that the circumstances governing ordinary lives are human creations rather than facts of nature.
