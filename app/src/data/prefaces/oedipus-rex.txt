Oedipus is a ruler accustomed to solving a problem others cannot solve. His city needs that ability again, and he commits himself to discovering the cause of its suffering. Sophocles makes the pursuit of truth urgent, public, and inseparable from the authority of the person conducting it.

The play has the concentration of an investigation. Testimony must be obtained, memories compared, and evasions challenged. Each answer changes what the next question can mean. Speech is a form of action here: a person who knows something possesses the power to alter another person's world, and may have strong reasons for remaining silent.

Oedipus's determination is part of what makes him formidable. It can also make disagreement look like disloyalty. The qualities that support his leadership do not remain safely divided from those that threaten his judgment. Confidence is useful until it begins deciding in advance what evidence is allowed to show.

Sophocles places this human activity within a world of prophecy and divine claims without making the people into empty instruments. They inquire, resist, interpret, and attempt to protect one another. The drama depends on their choices even as those choices encounter limits they do not understand.

A fact can be present long before its meaning becomes bearable. Sophocles gives that distance an extraordinary dramatic precision, making the desire to know and the need to remain oneself part of the same struggle.
