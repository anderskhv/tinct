Paradise Lost gives rebellion some of the most persuasive language ever written. Satan can turn defeat into proof of courage, obedience into humiliation, and his refusal to yield into a claim upon admiration. Milton lets the speech have its force. The fascination lies partly in hearing how much a magnificent argument can conceal from the person making it.

The poem's ambition extends far beyond its rebel. Milton attempts to imagine creation, freedom, temptation, and the place of human beings before God. These are enormous subjects, but their emotional life often rests on familiar desires: to be loved without dependence, to possess knowledge without limitation, or to believe that another person's happiness takes nothing away from one's own.

Adam and Eve have work, conversation, affection, and a world full of things worth noticing. Their relationship gives the theological argument a domestic intimacy. Difference can become companionship or hierarchy; an act undertaken out of love can still require judgment. Milton is interested in the conditions under which willing obedience could have meaning, and therefore in the terrible possibility of choosing otherwise.

His language asks for attention and repays it with scale. A sentence can gather classical history, biblical memory, astronomy, and the detail of an earthly landscape into one movement. Hell has political debates as well as fire; heaven raises questions about authority that the poem's own argument must address. The imagination keeps making its declared subject larger.

Error has intelligence and eloquence here, and an inward attractiveness that simple moral examples seldom possess. Milton's Christian understanding of freedom requires him to imagine how a creature could find disobedience persuasive. The resulting struggle concerns what a free creature might do with everything it has been given.
