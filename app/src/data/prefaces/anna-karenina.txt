Anna Karenina understands how much can happen inside an ordinary conversation. Someone waits for a particular answer, hears a slight where none was intended, or discovers that a person who seemed entirely familiar has become strange. Tolstoy follows these small changes with enough attention to make a social visit feel as consequential as an event that will alter a life.

Love is the novel's great subject, but it appears in forms that are difficult to reconcile. Desire promises escape from an unsatisfactory existence. Marriage requires living through hours in which no such promise is being made. Children create attachments that cannot be rearranged simply because the adults have become unhappy. A person's need for another person can be sincere and still become an impossible demand.

Anna's society has elaborate rules about these things, though it enforces them with considerable selectivity. Charm, position, and the ability to preserve appearances can determine which conduct is tolerated. Tolstoy makes social judgment visible as an activity conducted by particular people, with their own interests and convenient failures of memory.

Alongside this world, Levin tries to discover what kind of life he can honestly believe in. Work on the land, family, intellectual argument, and religious doubt all offer possibilities. He can understand something intensely for a moment and find that the understanding does not survive the next irritation. The novel gives this unevenness the room it occupies in life.

Its abundance matters because nobody remains only the person seen in a single scene. A character who has been ridiculous can become vulnerable; someone who appears cold may be struggling with feelings for which he has no usable language. Tolstoy can expose self-deception without making the person who practises it cease to matter. Even minor figures seem to have lives continuing beyond the edge of the page.

The book's length creates an intimacy that a shorter account could scarcely obtain. People become familiar through meals, work, illnesses, conversations, and changes they do not immediately notice in themselves. Its interest keeps moving between the life imagined from a distance and the life experienced from within. Tolstoy makes the difference so palpable that judgment has to slow down to accommodate it.
