The lovers in A Midsummer Night’s Dream are entirely convinced that their feelings tell the truth. Each beloved seems uniquely deserving, each rejection intolerable, each obstacle an offence against something sacred. Shakespeare takes these convictions into a wood where desire can change with humiliating ease, while the person experiencing it remains just as certain as before.

The joke has a generous reach. The fairy rulers are scarcely better at managing their attachments than the mortals, and their quarrels disturb a world much larger than their marriage. Love can make someone exquisitely attentive to one person and remarkably careless about everyone else. Magic makes these habits visible without making them tidy.

Alongside the lovers, a group of working men is preparing a play. They worry about convincing an audience, frightening the ladies, and representing things for which their resources are inadequate. Their solutions are absurd, but their eagerness is moving. Bottom's confidence, in particular, has a resilience that more sophisticated characters might envy. He enters the business of theatre with the uncomplicated conviction that he could contribute considerably more if allowed.

The play moves between these worlds with the freedom of a dream. Its woodland language is full of flowers, insects, weather, and the small activities of creatures that usually escape notice. Beauty shares the stage with bad acting, wounded vanity, and people behaving very badly because they are in love.

For all its lightness, the comedy leaves something substantial to consider. Imagination helps people discover beauty and invent nonsense, sometimes in the same moment. A performance can be ridiculous and still offer pleasure; a feeling can have foolish causes and still be deeply felt. Shakespeare gives these imperfect creations room to exist, and makes a midsummer night large enough to hold them.
