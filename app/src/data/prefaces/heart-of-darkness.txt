Heart of Darkness is preoccupied with the language people use when they want exploitation to sound like a vocation. Trade becomes progress, conquest becomes service, and a man's reputation can grow from words whose relation to his conduct has scarcely been examined. Conrad sends Marlow into a colonial enterprise surrounded by explanations that become less convincing the closer he gets to its operations.

The atmosphere is dense with delays, rumours, broken equipment, and the strange authority of people who appear to accomplish very little. Violence coexists with administrative absurdity. The machinery of the enterprise can be incompetent without becoming harmless, because those exposed to its power cannot simply walk away from its failures.

Marlow tells the story afterwards, to listeners whose world is implicated in it. His hesitations and indirect approaches belong to the difficulty of making an experience intelligible without turning it into another reassuring account. The distance between witnessing something and knowing how to speak about it becomes part of the narrative.

The book's own vision has limits that matter. African people are too often denied the individual voices and inward lives granted to Europeans, even as the colonial project is exposed. Its criticism of empire and its participation in colonial ways of seeing have to be considered together. That tension helps explain why the work continues to provoke serious disagreement.

A civilisation's account of its purposes may be least reliable where it is most eloquent. Conrad makes reputation, idealism, and the wish to be reassured difficult things to separate from the material realities they are supposed to explain.
