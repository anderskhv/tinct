Cymbeline gives people an extraordinary amount of confidence in things they have not properly understood. A report can outweigh a relationship, an appearance can stand in for a fact, and a private injury can seem to explain an entire world. Shakespeare sends these convictions through a story whose abundance continually exceeds anyone's knowledge of it.

At its emotional centre is Imogen, whose loyalty has energy and intelligence rather than mere patience. Other people make decisions about her worth, but the play gives her a life beyond their interpretations. She can be sharp, resourceful, frightened, and tender without becoming a simple emblem of innocence.

The action moves between a British court, Rome, and a Welsh landscape, crossing political conflict with the freely inventive possibilities of romance. Disguise, separation, and incomplete knowledge make the world unstable. The pleasure includes the sense that more connections exist than any character can yet perceive.

Its poetry can become unexpectedly quiet within that extravagance. Hospitality, affection, and the response to vulnerability create moments whose feeling is more direct than the elaborate plot around them. A stranger can receive care before anyone knows the name or status that might explain why she should matter.

Certainty can damage a relationship long before the facts are known, while kindness can act without waiting for a full account. Shakespeare allows these different responses to uncertainty to inhabit the same generous, unruly work, where the limits of a person's knowledge are continually larger than he imagines.
