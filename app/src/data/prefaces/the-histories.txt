Herodotus wants to understand a great war, and finds that almost everything might help explain it. A people's burial customs, a ruler's dream, the course of a river, and a story told about someone's grandfather can all claim his attention. The Histories has the excitement of an inquiry whose author has not yet decided that most of the world is irrelevant to his subject.

Its central conflict is between the Greeks and the Persian Empire. Around that conflict, Herodotus gathers accounts of the societies drawn into it, including the peoples whom a narrower patriotic history might treat as a background. He is curious about how customs work and how readily a community mistakes its own practices for the natural order of things.

His stories are often marvellous, and they require judgment. He reports conflicting accounts, sometimes states his doubts, and sometimes preserves claims that modern investigation cannot accept. The interest lies partly in seeing evidence and explanation being negotiated in a world with very different resources. A memorable story is not automatically a reliable one, but the stories people tell can themselves be historical facts worth understanding.

Power repeatedly encounters limits that its possessors have failed to imagine. Wealth encourages confidence, success invites another undertaking, and advice becomes harder to hear as authority grows. Herodotus can appreciate courage and ingenuity while remaining alert to how quickly fortune changes the scale of an achievement.

The book's digressions give it a distinctive hospitality. They make room for the unexpected person who has something to say and the unfamiliar practice that deserves more than mockery. Its enormous curiosity preserves a world of human differences alongside the struggle to explain what brought those worlds into collision.
