Rousseau imagines the distance between needing something and needing to be seen possessing it. Food and shelter answer bodily requirements. Prestige depends on other people's attention, and can remain unsatisfied however much is acquired. His Discourse on Inequality asks how human beings became so deeply dependent on judgments they cannot control.

The work constructs a speculative history rather than a documented account of prehistoric life. By imagining stages through which social relations might develop, Rousseau tries to distinguish what belongs to human nature from what particular arrangements have made familiar. The apparent naturalness of inequality is itself part of the problem.

Comparison changes the character of desire. People begin wanting esteem, advantage, and a position relative to their neighbours. Abilities that make cooperation possible also create new forms of rivalry. Progress in skill or wealth need not bring a corresponding increase in independence or contentment.

Property and political authority enter this history as institutions with beneficiaries, not neutral solutions appearing outside conflict. A settlement presented as common protection can preserve advantages already acquired. Rousseau asks who gets to define the arrangement as reasonable and who must live within its consequences.

The imagined history makes the present available for questioning, without supplying a route back to an untouched wilderness. Ambition, shame, and dependence may have social histories, which means they cannot be explained entirely as private failures. Rousseau examines the unsettling possibility that a society can cultivate capacities it celebrates while also creating much of the unhappiness those capacities are then expected to overcome.
