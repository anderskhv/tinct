Faust has devoted his life to learning and finds that learning has not made life feel sufficient. He possesses the signs of achievement and remains hungry for an experience that would justify them. Goethe begins with a dissatisfaction that can survive almost any success, because each completed pursuit becomes another reason to suspect that the real thing lies elsewhere.

Mephistopheles is excellent company for such a man. He can puncture solemnity, expose vanity, and supply a practical route towards what desire imagines. His wit has the attraction of someone who refuses to be impressed by respectable explanations. It also has a habit of making the value of everything seem smaller than the cleverness with which it can be dismissed.

Their encounter gives the drama its restless movement. Scholarship, magic, drinking, beauty, and religious longing occupy a world in which no single form seems capable of containing the appetite at work. Goethe moves between elevated verse and earthy comedy, allowing the desire for transcendence to keep meeting the body and its ordinary needs.

The presence of Gretchen gives that desire a different scale. A life can become part of someone else's search for fulfilment while remaining, to its owner, an entire world of attachments and obligations. The drama is attentive to the difference between experiencing something intensely and understanding what the experience costs another person.

Faust's ambition is compelling because it includes a refusal to live mechanically, to accept achievement as a substitute for being alive. Goethe does not make that refusal harmless. The first part of his great drama asks how an appetite for everything can remain answerable to anything particular, especially another human being whose life cannot simply become the next stage of a quest.
