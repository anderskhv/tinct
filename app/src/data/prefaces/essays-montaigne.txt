Montaigne does not wait to become a consistent person before making himself a subject of inquiry. His memory is unreliable, his habits are peculiar, his opinions change, and his body keeps interrupting his thoughts. The Essays begin from the possibility that this unfinished, ordinary material might reveal more than an ideal account of what a human being ought to be.

He writes about friendship, education, fear, cruelty, books, sleep, and a remarkable number of things that refuse to remain within the announced subject. An example from ancient Rome can lead to an observation about his own digestion. The movement is part of the method. General claims have to remain answerable to the inconvenient particularities of a life.

His reading supplies companions rather than a tribunal of unquestionable authorities. Classical authors can clarify an experience, provoke disagreement, or give him something to test against himself. Learning becomes valuable when it enters judgment. Accumulating impressive references is a much easier activity, and one he regards with suspicion.

That suspicion reaches his own culture. Customs become difficult to defend merely by calling them natural when other peoples live differently. Montaigne's scepticism has limits, but it repeatedly loosens the confidence with which familiarity appoints itself the measure of humanity. The admission of uncertainty can be an opening for attention rather than a retreat from it.

The companionship of the voice is inseparable from its candour. He can be vain about describing his vanity, amused by his limitations, and unexpectedly serious about a small pleasure. He neither transforms himself into a model nor treats imperfection as a reason to stop trying to understand. The person on the page remains someone whose next thought might revise the last.

Across the collection, an education in living emerges from this willingness to stay close to experience. Age, illness, affection, and pleasure require responses that no borrowed maxim can settle once and for all. Montaigne makes thinking feel like an activity that can accompany a life throughout its changes, without demanding that the life first be brought into exemplary order.
