The Imitation of Christ begins with a demanding distinction between knowing about the spiritual life and living it. A person may speak eloquently about humility, understand difficult theology, and remain intensely preoccupied with being admired. Thomas à Kempis returns attention to the conduct and desires that learning can leave untouched.

The book grows from Christian devotion and keeps Christ at the centre of its counsel. Prayer, grace, the cross, and the Eucharist belong to its substance. Its aim is an inward life less dependent on the unstable rewards of praise, comfort, and success, and more capable of answering God's love in ordinary practice.

Its observations about vanity are unusually searching. The wish to be useful can become a wish to be needed; correction can feel intolerable because self-respect has become attached to appearing faultless. Even spiritual achievement can be recruited into the desire for distinction. The author is attentive to motives that can survive beneath apparently admirable behaviour.

There is severity here, especially in the suspicion of worldly attachment, but also a recognition of weakness. Religious feeling is not always available on demand. Weariness and dryness do not necessarily mean that devotion has become false, and a life of faith cannot depend entirely on moments of consolation.

The short chapters invite a pace different from the appetite to finish a book. A familiar sentence may acquire force only when circumstances reveal why it was needed. For a believer, the work offers searching companionship in discipleship; its examination of pride and dependence can also be legible to someone approaching from outside the faith. It asks what remains of a person's convictions when there is no audience to reward them.
