John Stuart Mill begins from the thought that happiness matters wherever it occurs. The suffering of a stranger does not become less real because it is inconvenient to notice, and a person's own interests cannot automatically count for more simply because they are his. Utilitarianism develops the moral demands of taking that equality seriously.

The principle immediately raises questions about what happiness contains. Mill refuses to treat every pleasure as interchangeable. The exercise of thought, imagination, and feeling can matter enough that someone would retain those capacities even with the dissatisfaction they bring. A good life cannot be measured adequately by imagining a more comfortable animal.

He is also concerned with how an impartial principle could become a motive in actual people. Affection, education, conscience, and the experience of living together help form a concern for others. The argument has to account for character as well as decisions; nobody can calculate every consequence from the beginning each morning.

Justice presents a particularly strong challenge. Rights and security seem to possess an authority that should not dissolve whenever a convenient benefit is proposed. Mill tries to explain their exceptional importance within an account based on human well-being. Whether that explanation is sufficient remains one of the work's central points of dispute.

The essay rewards attention because its apparent simplicity keeps opening into difficult distinctions. Good intentions can cause harm, attractive outcomes can conceal an unfair distribution of costs, and familiar moral feelings still need an account of why they should govern conduct. Mill attempts to make morality answerable to the quality of lives, including lives that the decision-maker may never encounter.
