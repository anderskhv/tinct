Marcus Aurelius repeatedly reminds himself to be patient with difficult people. The repetition is part of what makes Meditations affecting. An emperor could command armies, appoint officials, and punish an insult, yet still needed to work on getting out of bed, accepting criticism, and resisting the pleasure of feeling aggrieved. Authority had not relieved him of the ordinary labour of managing a mind.

These notes were written for his own use, which gives them a different character from advice prepared for an audience. He returns to the same problems because knowing an argument is not the same as being able to live by it. A conviction that seems clear in a quiet moment has to survive fatigue, praise, disappointment, and the behaviour of colleagues.

The Stoic discipline behind the writing asks him to distinguish his judgments and actions from the many things he cannot secure. Reputation, health, and other people's choices remain uncertain. His own conduct is where responsibility must begin. This can sound severe, particularly when he strips cherished things of their glamour, but it accompanies a persistent insistence on cooperation and service. Other people are the reason for the work, even when they make it unpleasant.

His thoughts about death often serve the same practical purpose. A grievance looks different beside the brief lives of the people conducting it. So does the wish to be remembered by generations who will themselves vanish. The scale is meant to loosen an attachment to applause and return attention to what can be done now.

There are flashes of unexpected tenderness in this austere exercise: affection for teachers, gratitude for example, pleasure in the irregular beauty of natural things. The book's companionship comes from meeting a person who keeps trying to bring his conduct closer to what he believes. He has considerable power and has not finished learning how to behave.
