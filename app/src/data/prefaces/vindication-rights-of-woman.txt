Mary Wollstonecraft observes that women are trained to be dependent and then criticised for the habits dependence produces. An education organised around pleasing others encourages vanity, indirect influence, and the need for approval. These results are then presented as evidence that women are naturally unsuited to independence. A Vindication of the Rights of Woman attacks the arrangement at its source.

Her demand for education concerns more than access to information. Reason has to be exercised through responsibility, judgment, and participation in a world that takes those capacities seriously. A person kept in perpetual childhood cannot be expected to display the virtues of an adult while being denied the conditions in which they develop.

The argument extends into marriage and affection. Being admired for charm offers a precarious form of power, especially when charm must compete with novelty and age. Wollstonecraft imagines relationships with a stronger basis in friendship and mutual respect, where one person's development does not depend on remaining agreeable to another's authority.

Her standards are exacting. She criticises women's conduct as well as men's expectations, because equal moral standing includes equal responsibility. Some judgments carry the severity and limitations of her period, but the central refusal remains clear: apparent weakness cannot be understood apart from the institutions that cultivate it.

Society teaches people what to want before congratulating or blaming them for wanting it. Wollstonecraft asks what women might become if their lives were organised around the development of their faculties, and what men might have to relinquish to recognise those lives as fully human.
