Augustine addresses his life to God, including the parts he would once have preferred God to leave alone. His Confessions is a prayer made from memory, an attempt to understand how ambition, love, friendship, and error belong within a life that has been sought before it knew how to seek.

The intimacy is striking because Augustine examines the motives beneath apparently minor events. An act can offer little practical benefit and still be attractive because it is forbidden, because friends are watching, or because it briefly makes the will feel sovereign. He is interested in why a person can understand something as wrong and continue to want it.

His intellectual searching is equally personal. Different teachings promise an account of evil, reality, and the life worth living. An argument's appeal may involve its ability to excuse a habit or preserve an image of oneself. Augustine does not assume that finding the truth and becoming willing to live by it are the same achievement.

The people around him give the inquiry its emotional substance. His mother's faith, friendships, attachments, and losses belong to the formation of a self that cannot explain itself in isolation. The movement towards God does not make the remembered world unimportant; it alters what its importance can mean.

The later books extend the search into memory, time, and creation. The change of subject is less abrupt than it first appears. Telling a life already requires thinking about a past that is absent yet present within the mind, and about the relation between passing experience and what does not pass. Augustine makes these immense questions part of a voice that remains urgent, grateful, searching, and capable of finding its own depths strange.
