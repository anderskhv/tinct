Symposium is about what people believe they are looking for when they fall in love. Its setting is a drinking party in Athens, where the guests agree to make speeches in praise of love. Each has something to say, and each has reasons for wanting his account to sound convincing. They are speaking among friends, rivals, and people whose admiration matters to them.

That gives the philosophy a particular intimacy. An explanation of love can disclose rather more about the speaker than he intends. It can justify his tastes, dignify his disappointments, or make his own behaviour seem part of a universal truth. Plato lets the speeches be eloquent while leaving the people who deliver them visible. The pleasure lies partly in listening to an argument and partly in noticing who needs it to be true.

The conversation touches an uncertainty that survives almost any amount of experience. Loving someone can involve wanting to possess them, to be understood by them, to become better in their presence, or to escape the feeling of being incomplete. These desires can coexist without fitting comfortably together. A person may want to be loved exactly as they are and also hope that love will transform their life.

Among the guests are poets as well as a philosopher, and their different ways of speaking matter. A story can make longing intelligible before an argument has established whether its explanation is true. It can give someone an image of love so satisfying that they spend years trying to find it in another person. Philosophy asks what such an image promises, and whether that promise can be kept. Plato understands the attraction of both: the wish for a story in which our desires make sense, and the wish to know whether we have been persuaded by something beautiful into believing something false.

But philosophy has desires of its own. The pursuit of wisdom begins with the feeling that something essential is missing, that a fuller understanding might change the quality of a life. Socrates brings this hunger into the conversation about love. Wanting another person and wanting to understand the world begin to look less remote from one another, though they may lead in different directions. Love can make the beloved seem sufficient; it can also awaken interests and ambitions that carry someone beyond the relationship itself.

The world of the party is distinctly its own. Male desire is spoken of openly, within conventions of age, status, and courtship that do not map neatly onto modern relationships. Its participants can be perceptive about love while taking inequalities for granted. Their distance from the present helps make familiar assumptions visible, including the assumption that everyone means roughly the same thing by love.

For all its influence, Symposium retains the liveliness of an evening among clever people. There is teasing, vanity, comic invention, and the possibility that a beautiful speech will be interrupted by something less manageable. Nobody’s account of love is entirely secure while the people they desire are still in the room. Plato gives the ideas a life among those people, where understanding a feeling and knowing what to do with it remain very different accomplishments.
