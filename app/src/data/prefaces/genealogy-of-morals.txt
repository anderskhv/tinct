Nietzsche proposes that moral values have histories, and that those histories may be less flattering than the values' present authority suggests. A judgment experienced as self-evident could have begun in a struggle, a need for protection, or a wish for revenge. On the Genealogy of Morals investigates the possibility that the language of goodness preserves conflicts its users no longer recognise.

Its most provocative account concerns resentment that becomes creative. Someone unable to act directly against an enemy can remake the terms of judgment, turning the enemy's strength into evidence of evil and his own powerlessness into virtue. Nietzsche's interest is in how such an interpretation can become a whole moral world, rather than remain an individual's passing grievance.

The inquiry reaches inward as well. The formation of conscience involves a creature learning to turn force against itself, to remember obligations, and to make its own suffering meaningful. Guilt and discipline have a history in this account; they do not simply arrive as transparent voices of truth.

These are combative interpretations, and their historical claims require scrutiny. Nietzsche's suspicion of pity and his attraction to rank are substantial parts of the work, not incidental provocations that can be removed to produce an agreeable message. He asks difficult questions while making judgments that deserve difficult questions in return.

An accusation can be justified and still give its author a pleasure worth examining. A practice that once answered a need can continue commanding loyalty after its origins have vanished from view. Nietzsche makes the history of values part of the question of what they are worth now.
