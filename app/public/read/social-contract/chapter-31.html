Summary

Rousseau begins from a structural claim: the particular will of government is constantly in tension with the general will of the sovereign. The government naturally tends to act for its own interest rather than the common good, and the more it does so, the more the constitution changes. Since there is no external force naturally counteracting this drift, it is an inherent and inevitable defect that every body politic carries from birth — as the human body carries the seeds of its own death.

Degeneration takes two paths. The first is contraction: government passes from the many to the few — from democratic forms through aristocratic to royal. This is the natural direction because concentrated power is more efficient at maintaining itself. The reverse sequence — from monarchy toward wider participation — is possible only when the government is already so weak it cannot hold what it has. Springs, Rousseau says, must be tightened as they give way, not loosened.

The second path is dissolution, which takes two forms. A government usurps sovereignty when the prince begins to issue laws rather than execute them — at which point a new state forms within the old one, composed solely of the rulers, and all other citizens recover their natural liberty. Or the State itself dissolves: when each member acts for himself, when the name 'public good' becomes a cover for private interest, the body politic has died even if its formal shell still functions. Both forms of dissolution are not accidents but the destination toward which every government moves unless the sovereign people actively resists.
