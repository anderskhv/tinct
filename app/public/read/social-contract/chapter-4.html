Book 1, Chapter 4 — Slavery

To renounce your liberty is to renounce being a man. Such a renunciation is incompatible with human nature — and a self-contradictory convention gives rise to no obligation.

Summary

Rousseau begins from the brute conclusion already established: since no man has natural authority over another, and force creates no right, all legitimate authority rests on convention. But what conventions can establish legitimate rule? Grotius's move — if a man can sell himself into slavery, why can't a people sell itself to a king — is examined and found wanting on its own terms. A man who becomes a slave sells himself for his subsistence at minimum; but a king draws his subsistence from his subjects, not the reverse. What, then, is the price the people receives? There is nothing left to preserve.

The despot's offer of peace is the next item. Rousseau grants tranquility but asks what it is worth when the despot's wars, greed, and administrators impose heavier burdens than the subjects' own quarrels would have done. The Cyclops's cave is his example: the Greeks imprisoned there lived very tranquilly, waiting their turn to be devoured. Tranquility in a dungeon is not a benefit. To accept suffering now in exchange for future security that never arrives — and to call this a rational bargain — is a fantasy of the theorists of absolute power.

Two further arguments close the chapter. First, even if an individual could alienate his freedom, he cannot alienate his children's — they are born free, and no gift to a master made before they reach reason can bind them. Second, to renounce liberty is to renounce the moral nature of one's actions: a will stripped of all freedom produces no duties, only behavior. A convention that places absolute authority on one side and unlimited obedience on the other is not a contract at all; it creates no obligation, because there is no exchange. Rousseau has now cleared the ground entirely. Every false foundation for political authority has been removed.
