The Social Contract

1762 CE · by Jean-Jacques Rousseau

The book in brief

The Social Contract argues that political authority is legitimate only when it springs from the consent of the people governed, that this consent takes the form of a unanimous founding agreement, and that the resulting body politic is sovereign through what Rousseau calls the general will. The book opens with the most famous line in political philosophy — man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains — and then sets out to answer not how that came to be but what could make it legitimate. The four books move from the foundation of political society to a final chapter on civil religion. The argument is short, intricate, and deliberately paradoxical.

Rousseau published the book in April 1762 in Amsterdam, having failed to get it past the Paris censors. Within two months it was banned in France and Geneva, his birthplace. Copies were burned publicly; a warrant was issued for his arrest. He spent the rest of the decade fleeing across Europe — to Bern, to Neuchâtel under Frederick the Great's protection, briefly to England under Hume's. He was fifty when he wrote it, already famous for his Discourses on inequality and the arts, working in the high noon of the French Enlightenment alongside Voltaire and Diderot.

It is the founding text of modern democratic theory and the most contested book in the canon of political philosophy. The Jacobins read it as a manual for the French Revolution; Robespierre carried a copy. Liberals from Constant onward have argued that the general will is the philosophical ancestor of modern totalitarianism. Both readings are defensible — which is part of what makes the book durable. Rousseau's prose is unlike anything else in eighteenth-century political theory: hot, paradoxical, willing to contradict itself. The Social Contract poses the founding question of modern politics — what makes legitimate authority? — and answers it in a way that has not stopped being argued about.

Why it still matters

The Source of Modern Sovereignty

The Social Contract argues that legitimate political authority comes from the people, not from kings, gods, or tradition — and that this is not a metaphor but a strict philosophical claim. The principle is now so deeply built into modern politics that it can seem self-evident; in 1762, when most European thrones still claimed authority by divine right, it required arguments like Rousseau's to make it so. The American and French Revolutions both invoked some version of popular sovereignty, but Rousseau supplied the most uncompromising form of the argument: not consent at the founding moment alone, but continuous active participation in the collective making of law as the only basis of political legitimacy. Every modern constitution that locates sovereignty in the people stands in this book's lineage. The strongest theoretical critiques of representative government — that it substitutes the will of representatives for the will of the people — also descend directly from Rousseau.

The General Will

Rousseau's concept of the general will — the collective interest of the people, distinct from the sum of individual interests, distinct from any particular majority's preference — has been one of the most influential and disputed ideas in political theory. It shaped the French Revolution and has been used to justify everything from participatory democracy to one-party rule. The Jacobins invoked it; Robespierre claimed to embody it; Hegel built his theory of the state on a version of it; twentieth-century totalitarianisms of both right and left claimed descent from it; modern theorists of deliberative democracy from Rawls to Habermas have tried to recover it on better foundations. The concept's instability is part of its power. The general will is what every modern claim to speak for the people, against the merely apparent preferences of actual people, must invoke and try to ground — and what every critic of populism must try to dismantle.

Freedom and Obligation

Rousseau's most paradoxical claim is that being subject to law one helped make is not constraint but freedom — that civil liberty consists in obedience to a law one has prescribed to oneself. The argument inverts the usual liberal picture, in which freedom is freedom from coercion and law is the price one pays to live in society. For Rousseau, natural freedom — doing whatever one wants — is not freedom at all but slavery to appetite, the same drives that make a tyrant of any man who indulges them; civil freedom, which requires submitting to the general will, is the only kind worth having. The argument is the foundation of modern republican political theory and the target of two centuries of liberal critique from Constant onward. Whether obedience to a self-given law counts as freedom, or whether the move smuggles authoritarianism into the heart of democracy, is still being argued in every contemporary debate about majoritarianism and minority rights.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The general will.

The concept at the heart of the book — and the most contested idea in modern political philosophy. The general will is not majority opinion, not the sum of private desires, not what a representative decides for you. It is what the people would will if they were genuinely thinking as citizens. Track how Rousseau defines it, where it can go wrong, and why it has been used to justify both democracy and totalitarianism.

Forced to be free.

Book I's most explosive line: whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be constrained to do so by the whole body — which means nothing other than that he will be forced to be free. It is either the most coherent statement in the book or its most frightening. Rousseau is not being careless here. He is making a precise argument about what freedom actually is — and it does not mean doing whatever you want.

The Lawgiver.

Book II introduces one of the strangest figures in political philosophy: the Lawgiver, the near-mythical founder who gives a people its laws, then disappears. He cannot use force; he has no authority of his own; he must persuade the people to accept a constitution they cannot yet understand. Rousseau compares him to Moses, Lycurgus, and Calvin. The paradox is that the general will, which is supposed to be self-governing, apparently needs a god-like founder to get started.

Civil religion.

Book IV ends with a chapter that shocked Rousseau's contemporaries more than anything else in the book: the argument that every republic needs an official civil religion — a set of social dogmas citizens must profess or be exiled. Not Christianity, not any existing faith, but a civic creed built around the existence of God, the sanctity of the social contract, and the intolerance of intolerance itself. It is the most controversial chapter in an already controversial book.

Key figures

Jean-Jacques Rousseau
THE AUTHOR

A Genevan philosopher and composer, fifty at publication, by 1762 the most famous and most isolated thinker in Europe. He had been the prodigy of the Encyclopédie and then quarreled with Voltaire, Diderot, and most of the Parisian intellectual world. He wrote The Social Contract during the same period as Émile, his treatise on education, and considered the two books inseparable. The voice in the Social Contract is the man at full philosophical force — paradoxical, hot, willing to follow an argument into territory his contemporaries refused to enter.

The General Will
THE LEGITIMATE SOVEREIGN

Rousseau's central and most contested concept. The general will is not what people happen to want, nor the sum of private interests, nor a majority vote. It is what the people would will if they were thinking as citizens concerned with the common good. It is always right — but the assembly can be wrong about what it is. That gap, between the general will itself and any actual collective decision, is where the book's most dangerous implications live.

The Sovereign
THE COLLECTIVE PERSON

When individuals enter the social contract, they create something new: a single moral and collective body — the republic, the state, the sovereign — which has a will, interests, and identity of its own. The sovereign is the people considered as the source of law. It cannot be represented, cannot be divided, cannot bind itself by contracts with its members. Rousseau's account of sovereignty is the strongest version of popular sovereignty in the canon and the source of most modern democratic theory.

The Lawgiver
THE FOUNDER

A near-mythical figure introduced in Book II. The Lawgiver gives a people its fundamental constitution but holds no office, commands no army, and possesses no legal authority. He cannot reason with a people who cannot yet think as citizens, so he attributes his laws to the gods. Rousseau cites Moses, Lycurgus of Sparta, and Numa Pompilius of Rome as examples. The Lawgiver is necessary to the theory and an embarrassment to it: a theory of radical self-government that needs a superhuman founder to start.

Thomas Hobbes
THE PRECURSOR

The English philosopher whose Leviathan, published in 1651, set the terms Rousseau is working against. Hobbes argued that the state of nature is a war of all against all, and that legitimate authority is whatever absolute sovereign the people contract into existence to escape it. Rousseau accepts the contractual framework and rejects almost everything else: he denies that the state of nature is war, denies that the sovereign should be absolute, and denies that obedience to power is the same thing as legitimate political life.

John Locke
THE OTHER PRECURSOR

The English philosopher whose Second Treatise had given the contractual tradition its liberal form, with natural rights, limited government, and the right of revolution. Rousseau respects Locke and steals from him liberally — but he is not satisfied with Locke's solution. Where Locke makes consent the basis of authority, Rousseau radicalizes it: only continuous, active, unanimous participation in the general will produces legitimate government. Liberalism, in Rousseau's view, settles for too little.
