Summary

The chapter's premise is that government is not a free-floating design choice but a social institution embedded in material conditions. Every public administration consumes without producing; the surplus available to sustain it depends on soil, climate, the productivity of labor, and the consumption habits of the people. Where surplus is thin, the government must be cheap. Where it is ample, more elaborate administration is possible without crushing the people.

From this economic logic Rousseau derives his matching principle: democracy — requiring constant assembly, no professional magistrates, and minimal administration — suits small, poor, austere states. Aristocracy suits states of middling wealth and size. Monarchy, the most expensive and voracious form, suits only large, wealthy nations that can afford the apparatus of court, military, and bureaucracy without being bled dry. This is not a normative ranking — monarchy is not better because it suits wealthy nations — but a claim about feasibility.

The chapter extends the argument to climate and geography. Hot countries are naturally less productive per unit of labor; their populations tend toward passivity; their governments tend toward despotism, which extracts without being accountable. Cold countries produce energetic, hard-working peoples who resist domination. Temperate zones can go either way. Rousseau closes with a long footnote attacking the common equation of cultural brilliance with political health. A country in which letters and arts flourish is admired; but admiration may conceal slavery. Population is the true mark of prosperity — a growing people is well governed; a shrinking one is not.
