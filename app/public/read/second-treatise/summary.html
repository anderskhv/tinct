Second Treatise of Government

1689 CE · by John Locke

The book in brief

The Second Treatise of Government argues that legitimate political authority derives from the consent of the governed, that human beings have natural rights to life, liberty, and property which precede the state, and that when government violates those rights the people may resist or replace it. The argument proceeds from a starting point — the state of nature — through accounts of property, of consent, of the structure of legitimate government, and of conquest, usurpation, and tyranny, ending in a chapter on the dissolution of government and the right of revolution. The book is short, methodical, and unusually clear. Locke is doing political philosophy in the style of a careful lawyer rather than a system-builder, and the argument is built to be cited.

Locke published the Two Treatises of Government anonymously in 1689, just after the Glorious Revolution that brought William and Mary to the English throne. He was fifty-six and had spent much of the previous decade in Holland, in exile from a regime that suspected him of involvement in plots against Charles II and James II. The First Treatise is a detailed refutation of Robert Filmer's Patriarcha, which had argued that royal authority descended from Adam by divine appointment. The Second Treatise is the constructive replacement: an account of legitimate government that owes nothing to scripture or to royal genealogy and everything to natural law and consent.

It is the canonical statement of the liberal political tradition. Thomas Jefferson borrowed Locke's vocabulary almost verbatim for the Declaration of Independence, substituting the pursuit of happiness for property in the famous triplet of rights. The American Revolution and the constitutional traditions of nearly every modern democracy descend from this book. Locke's prose is plainer than Hobbes's and less rhapsodic than Rousseau's; the argument is stronger for it. The Second Treatise is the rare work of philosophy whose conclusions became the working assumptions of an entire civilization.

Why it still matters

Government by Consent

Locke argues that no government has legitimate authority over people without their consent. The principle is now so deeply embedded in political life that it can be hard to register how radical it was in 1689, when most European thrones still claimed authority by divine right and hereditary succession sanctified by the church. Locke's argument was that no such claim could withstand examination: if government rests on anything, it rests on the agreement of the people governed, and any claim to rule that cannot be traced back to that agreement is illegitimate by definition. The American Revolution stated the principle, the French Revolution radicalized it, and every constitutional democracy since has built itself on some version of it. The argument has not been refuted; it has been absorbed. Its difficulties — what counts as consent, who is included in the people, what binds the unwilling minority — are still the working problems of democratic theory.

Natural Rights

Locke defends life, liberty, and property as natural rights — claims held by individuals before any government exists, claims no legitimately constituted government can justly take away. The phrase Jefferson made famous in the Declaration of Independence ("life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness") is Locke with one substitution, and the substitution is itself a tell about what the American framers thought property required. The vocabulary of natural rights, of human rights, of inalienable rights — the entire moral language modern liberal politics speaks — descends from Chapter 2 of the Second Treatise. The framework has been criticized from many directions: by utilitarians who reject the metaphysics, by communitarians who reject the individualism, by historians who note that Locke himself accepted enslavement and colonial dispossession his own theory should have ruled out. The framework has nevertheless outlasted every alternative anyone has proposed for it.

The Right of Resistance

The most radical argument in the treatise comes at its end. When a government violates the rights it was instituted to protect, the people have the right — and Locke insists, the duty — to overthrow it and constitute a new one in its place. Locke wrote this in 1689 to justify the deposition of James II that had just occurred. Jefferson cited the principle in 1776 to justify a revolution Locke had not foreseen and probably would not have endorsed in all its details. Every later argument that political legitimacy has limits, and that those limits can be enforced by the people against the state, descends from Chapter 19. The argument has been used by liberators and by tyrants, by the American founders and by every modern revolutionary movement that has bothered to write a manifesto. Every later argument that the people may judge their rulers — and may, when judgment fails, replace them — descends from the same chapter.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The state of nature — and why it is not Hobbes.

Locke's most fundamental move: before government, men are not at war with each other. They live under the law of nature — reason — which tells every person that no one may harm another in life, health, liberty, or possessions. The state of nature is a state of freedom and equality, not chaos. Understanding this is the key to everything that follows.

Property as labour mixed with the world.

Chapter 5 is one of the most influential arguments in the history of philosophy. Locke's claim: the earth is given in common, but every person owns their own body and their own labour. When you mix your labour with something, you make it yours. The argument grounds property rights not in law or convention but in the nature of persons — and it does so with surprising elegance.

Consent — tacit, express, and its limits.

Locke argues that no one can be subject to political authority without their own consent. But most people never explicitly consent to their government. His answer — that living in a territory and using its roads constitutes tacit consent — is the most contested move in the book. Track how far the argument stretches and where it breaks.

The right of revolution — Chapter 19's logic.

If government is a trust and trust can be betrayed, what follows? Locke's answer is direct: when a legislature acts against the ends for which it was constituted, power devolves back to the people, who may erect a new government. Jefferson did not invent this argument — he borrowed it whole. Read Chapter 19 as the logical conclusion of everything before it.

Key figures

John Locke
THE AUTHOR

An English philosopher, physician, and political theorist, fifty-six at publication. He had spent the late 1670s and early 1680s in the household of the Earl of Shaftesbury, then in exile in Holland after Shaftesbury's failed plotting against the crown. By 1689 he was the most rigorous political thinker England had produced, and the Two Treatises were the constructive statement of the position he had spent twenty years working out under conditions where naming it openly could have cost him his life.

The State of Nature
THE PRE-POLITICAL CONDITION

Locke's starting point and most important premise. Before government exists, all people are naturally free and equal, governed only by the law of nature — reason — which prohibits harming another in life, health, liberty, or possessions. It is not war. It is the condition that makes government voluntary rather than inevitable, and it is the standard against which any actual government must be measured.

Sir Robert Filmer
THE OPPONENT

The Royalist theorist whose posthumously published Patriarcha argued that royal authority descended from Adam by divine appointment, and that all subjects owed kings the obedience children owe fathers. Locke's First Treatise dismantles Filmer line by line. The Second Treatise is the constructive replacement — and the urgency of its argument comes partly from Locke's conviction that Filmer's theory had to be replaced, not merely refuted, before legitimate government could be defended at all.

Property
LABOR MIXED WITH THE WORLD

Locke's foundational argument in Chapter 5: since every person owns their own body and their own labor, whatever they mix their labor with becomes their own. The earth is given in common, but private property arises prior to government, prior to law, from the basic fact of human action in the world. The argument has been called the most influential single chapter in modern political philosophy. It has also been criticized for justifying colonial dispossession and economic inequality. Both judgments are accurate.

Consent
THE BASIS OF AUTHORITY

The hinge of the whole book. No person can be subjected to political authority without their own consent. Locke distinguishes express consent — explicit agreement, by oath or covenant — from tacit consent, which he describes as remaining in a territory and using its protections. The distinction is both the solution to the problem of political obligation and the most contested part of Locke's argument. Critics from David Hume onward have accused him of stretching tacit consent past breaking point.

The Right of Revolution
THE FINAL APPEAL

When government betrays the trust for which it was created — acting against life, liberty, and property rather than in their defense — power devolves back to the people. They may then constitute a new government. Locke calls this an appeal to heaven. The argument was the philosophical justification of the Glorious Revolution Locke had just lived through, and a hundred years later it became the philosophical justification of the American Revolution. Jefferson did not invent the argument; he borrowed it whole.
