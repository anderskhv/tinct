Proverbs — chapter by chapter

All 31 chapters summarized — from the opening discourses to the woman of valour.

Proverbs divides into five distinct collections across 31 chapters. The opening discourses (chapters 1–9) are sustained essays addressed by a father to his son; they should be read in sequence. The two long Solomonic collections (10–22:16 and 25–29) are best sampled column by column, not read straight through. The Words of the Wise (22:17–24:34), Agur's oracle (30), and Lemuel's instruction with the closing eshet chayil poem (31) are short enough to read in a single sitting each.

Proverbs 11

Proverbs 12

Proverbs 14

Proverbs 14

Proverbs 15

Proverbs 15

Proverbs 16

Proverbs 17

Proverbs 17

Proverbs 18

Proverbs 18

Proverbs 19

Proverbs 19

Chapter 19 examines wealth's distortion of friendship, divine purpose standing over human plans, and the discipline that leads to hope.

Proverbs 20

Proverbs 20

Proverbs 21

Proverbs 21

Proverbs 22

Words of the Wise · Chapters 22–24

The sayings related to the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope, plus a short appendix.

Proverbs 23

Proverbs 23

Chapter 23 is the wine chapter: the most extended set-piece on drunkenness in the book, alongside warnings against exhausting yourself to get rich.

Proverbs 24

Proverbs 24

Chapter 24 closes the Words of the Wise with the prohibition on enemy-rejoicing and the parable of the lazy man's overgrown field.

Solomon collection II · Chapters 25–29

Copied by the men of Hezekiah — the second Solomonic collection.

Proverbs 25

Proverbs 25

The Hezekiah collection begins with royal proverbs on the unsearchable heart of kings, then extended similes, and the coal-on-the-head enemy-bread saying.

Proverbs 26

Proverbs 26

Chapter 26 is the book's fool-and-sluggard catalogue — concentrated, satirical, and vivid — closing with the contentious person and the flatterer.

Proverbs 27

Proverbs 27

Chapter 27 gives the iron-sharpens-iron friendship proverb and the do-not-boast-of-tomorrow observation, closing with pastoral imagery of flocks and fields.

Proverbs 28

Proverbs 28

Chapter 28 opens with the guilt-as-flight proverb and gives the book's clearest statement on confession: whoever conceals transgressions will not prosper.

Proverbs 29

Proverbs 29

Chapter 29 closes the Hezekiah collection with the vision-and-people proverb, royal justice, and the contrast between the fear of man and trust in the Lord.

Agur and Lemuel · Chapters 30–31

Agur's oracle and numerical proverbs. Lemuel's instruction and the woman of valour.

Proverbs 30

Proverbs 30

Agur's oracle opens with radical humility — I am the most ignorant of men — and then moves through the famous numerical sayings on things too wonderful to understand.

Proverbs 31

Proverbs 31

The book closes with Lemuel's mother's royal instruction and the eshet chayil — the alphabetic poem of the woman of valour, whose fear of the Lord is the book's final word.
