Proverbs 17

A merry heart does good like a medicine; a broken spirit dries the bones. A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity.

Summary

Chapter 17 opens with a domestic image that reframes the wisdom ethic in economic terms: better is a dry morsel with quiet than a house full of feasting with strife. The first observation grounds the chapter's consistent preference for inward peace over outward abundance. The chapter includes a cluster on testing: the crucible is for silver, and the furnace is for gold, and the Lord tests hearts. The imagery of the refinery — of testing by heat — applies to the moral character what the metallurgist applies to metal. The observations on family and friendship follow: children's children are the crown of old men, and the glory of children is their fathers. Then the friendship proverb at verse 17: a friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity. The claim is about loyalty under conditions — it is not the fair-weather friend that the book values but the one whose love is constant even when conditions make loyalty costly.

The chapter's most famous single observation is the psychosomatic pair at verse 22: a joyful heart is good medicine, but a crushed spirit dries up the bones. The connection between the inward state and the physical body — between the disposition of the heart and the condition of the bones — is an observation that the wisdom tradition makes without requiring a theoretical account of the mechanism. It simply names the pattern. The chapter's observations on the fool cluster around several images: the fool's lips enter into contention; a foolish son is a grief to his father; whoever mocks the poor insults his Maker. The closing proverb on silence is one of the collection's driest: even a fool who keeps silent is considered wise; when he closes his lips, he is deemed intelligent. The book's consistent preference for few words over many finds its limit case here — silence as the fool's best strategy.
