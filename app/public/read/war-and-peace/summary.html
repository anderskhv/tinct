War and Peace

1869 CE · by Leo Tolstoy

The book in brief

War and Peace follows several aristocratic Russian families through the Napoleonic wars from 1805 to 1820. It opens at a soirée in St. Petersburg as the news of Napoleon's latest moves reaches the salons; it ends fifteen years later in a quiet country household with the survivors raising children. At its center are four figures. Pierre Bezukhov, the awkward illegitimate son of an old count, inherits an enormous fortune in the first volume and spends the rest of the novel asking what to do with his life — through a disastrous marriage, through Freemasonry, through captivity in occupied Moscow. His friend Prince Andrei Bolkonsky leaves a pregnant wife to seek glory at Austerlitz and returns to find that glory is not what he expected. Natasha Rostova, a count's daughter, dances at her first ball, falls disastrously in love with the wrong man, and slowly grows into someone the novel takes seriously. Andrei's sister Marya keeps faith under an impossible father at the family estate of Bald Hills.

Tolstoy worked on the novel from 1863 to 1869 at Yasnaya Polyana, with help from his wife Sofia who copied the manuscript by hand seven times. He was in his thirties, just married, and had begun the spiritual searching that would eventually push him out of fiction altogether. He published it serially in The Russian Messenger from 1865. He did not consider what he had made a novel — too much history, too many essays. The book is about 1,200 pages, in four volumes plus two long epilogues, and moves freely between battlefield, ballroom, country estate, hunting field, prisoner's column, and Tolstoy's own theory of history.

It is the most ambitious work of fiction in any language. The book attempts to explain how history actually happens, what individuals can and cannot do inside it, and what a meaningful life looks like when so much of it is out of one's control.

Why it still matters

History As A Crowd, Not A Plan

Tolstoy's central historical argument runs against the grain of every nineteenth-century history written about the period. He argues that Napoleon does not move armies; armies move themselves and Napoleon takes credit. The famous chapters on Borodino describe a battle in which neither commander could see what was happening, orders were carried by riders who arrived too late or were killed on the way, and the outcome was decided by hundreds of thousands of unrelated small acts — a sergeant who held a position, a soldier who panicked, a regiment that marched the wrong direction. The two long epilogues argue the case explicitly: history is the sum of motives so numerous and small that no historian can recover them, and the great-man theory is a flattering illusion the powerful tell about themselves. The argument is still controversial and still compelling, and any contemporary debate about whether one leader or another "made" some large outcome happen is, knowingly or not, in this argument.

Every Life At Full Length

Tolstoy gives each of his four central characters — Pierre, Andrei, Natasha, Marya — the time to develop, fail, change, marry the wrong person, lose what they thought they wanted, and become someone unrecognizable from where they began. There are no shortcuts. Pierre's arc spans fifteen years and includes a duel, a Masonic conversion, captivity, and a slow ordinary marriage; Natasha is thirteen at her first appearance and pushing thirty by the second epilogue, where she is a slightly heavy mother of four who has stopped singing. Tolstoy's method is to refuse summary: a character who matters is allowed to be all the things they are over time, including the embarrassing and the dull. Reading the novel is closer to watching real people live than any other novel of the nineteenth century. The form has rarely been attempted at this scale since.

What A Meaningful Life Could Be

Pierre and Andrei spend most of the novel asking how to live well. They try wealth, love, war, philosophy, Freemasonry, religion, asceticism, and ordinary family life. Andrei is given the dazzling answer at Austerlitz — staring up at the infinite sky after his wound, deciding the glory he had wanted is nothing — and then is given a life in which that revelation has to be lived day by day, and dies trying. Pierre is given the more durable answer through Platon Karataev, a peasant fellow prisoner whose cheerful acceptance of suffering and immediate kindness toward strangers is the closest the novel comes to a model. Tolstoy refuses to offer a clean formula. He does not say the search ends; Pierre is still searching at the second epilogue, married, distracted by his children, arguing politics with his brother-in-law. What the novel offers is the search itself, taken seriously across thousands of pages — the willingness to put a serious adult question at the center of fiction and let it stay open. Most novels do not even try.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Finding peace in a life that won't stop.

The novel's title is not a binary. Tolstoy is asking what peace means inside the noise of a life — family, ambition, loss, war. Pierre's arc is the novel's extended meditation on this question.

The great man illusion.

Napoleon appears as a figure of pure self-delusion — a man who believes he directs history but is swept along like everyone else. Tolstoy argues this in the epilogue explicitly, but the novel shows it on every page.

Natasha as the novel's heart.

She is not an idea or a symbol — she is simply alive in a way few fictional characters are. Her first ball, her infatuation with Anatole, her grief, her later life: follow her and you have followed the novel's emotional argument.

History from inside.

The battle of Borodino seen by Pierre is chaos and confusion, not the clean manoeuvre shown on maps. Tolstoy is making a philosophical argument: history is not intelligible in the moment, only in retrospect — and barely even then.

Key figures

Pierre Bezukhov
The Seeker

The illegitimate son of a great count, returned from Paris at the start of the novel as an awkward, overweight, near-sighted young man with no place in society. The unexpected death of his father makes him the wealthiest bachelor in Russia. He marries the beautiful and shallow Hélène, fights an absurd duel, joins the Freemasons, watches the burning of Moscow as a French prisoner, and is changed by a captured peasant named Platon Karataev. He is hapless, sincere, and the closest thing the book has to a protagonist.

Natasha Rostova
The Heart of the Novel

Thirteen years old when we first meet her, dancing in a corridor in her parents' Moscow house. She is Tolstoy's most beloved character, and he allows her to be everything in turn — exuberant, cruel without meaning to be, devastated by grief, foolish in love with the wrong man, transformed by nursing a dying soldier, and finally, at thirty, an entirely different sort of person. She is not the cleverest character in the book; she is the most fully alive. The novel's emotional argument runs through her.

Prince Andrei Bolkonsky
The Idealist

A brilliant, proud aristocrat trapped in an unhappy marriage. He goes to war to escape it, seeks glory at Austerlitz, and is left wounded on the battlefield staring up at a vast indifferent sky — the moment his old life ends. He returns home to find his wife has died in childbirth. He becomes engaged to Natasha, loses her, fights at Borodino, and is wounded fatally. His arc is the novel's meditation on the gap between how we imagine our lives and how they arrive.

Napoleon
The Delusion

The Emperor of the French, who in this novel is reduced from legend to a small, vain man who believes he is moving the armies he commands. Tolstoy renders him as petty in his bath, satisfied with his own bons mots, increasingly bewildered as Russia refuses to behave as his theory of history requires. The famous portrait of him surveying Borodino through a spyglass and issuing orders that arrive too late to matter is the novel's central image of the great-man theory of history reduced to its actual size.

Field Marshal Kutuzov
The Real Wisdom

The aging, one-eyed Russian commander, slow, fat, and apparently lazy. He sleeps through war councils, refuses battles he is ordered to fight, retreats when others want him to attack, surrenders Moscow to save the army. Tolstoy treats him as the genuine hero of the war: a man who understands that the best thing is often nothing, that armies move by their own weight, and that a good commander feels where things are going. The novel's image of leadership.

Princess Marya Bolkonskaya
Faith Under Pressure

Andrei's plain, devout sister, kept on the family estate at Bald Hills by her bullying father, an old general whose rages get worse as he ages. She prays, teaches her young nephew, takes in religious wanderers, and endures her father's cruelty without quite breaking. She inherits the estate as the French invasion approaches and barely escapes the peasants' revolt against her authority. Her quiet, slow path to a happiness she had stopped expecting is one of the most carefully drawn lives in the novel.
