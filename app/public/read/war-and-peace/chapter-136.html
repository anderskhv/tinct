Book Seven (1810 - 11) — Chapter 4

Summary

The great hunt assembles: fifty-four hounds, eight borzoi keepers, forty or more borzois, the old count in high spirits, Nicholas grave with the authority of a master of hounds, Natasha and Petya insisting on joining despite Nicholas's official disapproval. More than a hundred people and animals spread across the field in disciplined array, each knowing their role in the complex choreography of a serious hunt.

The ride out has the quality of a ceremony — formal, traditional, governed by tacit codes that everyone understands. Ilagin, a neighboring landowner, joins with his own pack, and there is the underlying social drama of competitive huntsmen meeting on shared ground, each proud of his dogs, each careful of his dignity. The hounds are released, the lines separate, and what follows is one of the novel's great set-pieces.

Tolstoy's hunting descriptions operate on several registers simultaneously: sensory immersion (the sounds, smells, and textures of a morning field), social comedy (the aristocratic rituals of competing establishments), and moral seriousness (the claim that this form of life, however impractical, is one of the things Russia loses in the transition to modernity). The chapter is among the most purely pleasurable in the novel.
