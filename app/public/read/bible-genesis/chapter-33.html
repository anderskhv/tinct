Genesis 33

The reunion Jacob dreaded across twenty years arrives: Esau runs to meet him, and they fall on each other's necks and weep.

Summary

Jacob's elaborate defensive arrangement — wives and children ranged in tiers from least to most loved — signals the depth of his remaining fear even after the Jabbok encounter. He prostrates himself seven times as he walks toward Esau's armed party, performing the full ancient Near Eastern gesture of submission. Then the text abandons the suspense it has been building since chapter 27: Esau runs, embraces, kisses, weeps. No explanation for the change of heart is offered. The four hundred men are never mentioned again. The fury of chapter 27 has dissolved in twenty years, and the narrative lets the dissolution happen without forcing a theological gloss onto it.

The exchange of pleasantries has the slight awkwardness of long-separated men who have less in common than they did: Esau asks about the women and children, admires the drove-gifts, offers them back (Jacob insists he keep them), and suggests they travel together or that he leave an escort. Jacob's declining of both offers is cordially evasive — the children and animals cannot march fast — and Esau seems content with it. Esau returns to Seir; Jacob moves to Succoth, then purchases land in Shechem. The land purchase is significant — it is only the second piece of the promised land any patriarch has bought outright, following Abraham's purchase of Machpelah. Jacob builds an altar there, the first act of explicit worship attributed to him in his own person.
