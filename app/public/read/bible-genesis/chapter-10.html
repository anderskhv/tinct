Summary

Genesis 10 is the most comprehensive ancient table of nations known from the ancient Near East. It lists seventy peoples — a number later treated in Jewish tradition as the total number of nations in the world — as descendants of Shem, Ham, and Japheth. The Japhethite peoples include the Greeks (Javan), the Medes, the people of the Black Sea region, and the maritime nations of the western Mediterranean. The Hamite peoples include Egypt (Mizraim), Cush (Ethiopia), Put (Libya), and Canaan — and the Hamite list contains the notable figure of Nimrod, described as a mighty hunter before the LORD and the builder of Babylon, Erech, Akkad, Calneh in the land of Shinar, and then Nineveh and other great cities of Assyria. The Shemite peoples include Aram and its subdivisions, the Elamites, Asshur, and the line that leads to Eber — the eponymous ancestor of the Hebrews.

The chapter functions simultaneously as an ancient ethnography and as a theological statement: all nations have a common origin. That origin is Noah, and Noah's origin is Adam. The list does not privilege any one people over the others — Canaan appears alongside Israel's ancestors without hierarchy at this point. The Table of Nations is the widest frame of the book before the camera narrows to Abraham in chapter 12. For a reader following the narrative, the chapter does the work of establishing that the post-flood world is populated, diverse, and fully inhabited before the story of Babel explains the diversity of languages and before the story of Abram explains the narrowing of the promise to one family.
