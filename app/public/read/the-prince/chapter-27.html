Chapter 26 — An Exhortation to Liberate Italy from the Barbarians

Italy lies waiting, more enslaved than the Hebrews, more scattered than the Athenians — and the moment has never been more propitious.

Summary

The exhortation begins with the logical bridge from Chapter 25: just as great founders needed the historical conditions that called them forth — Moses needed the enslaved Israelites, Cyrus needed the oppressed Persians, Theseus needed the scattered Athenians — so a great Italian prince needs a moment of Italian humiliation. That moment, Machiavelli argues, has arrived. Italy has been beaten, robbed, wounded, overrun, and subjected to every kind of ruin. She is leaderless, more enslaved than the Hebrews in Egypt, more scattered than the Athenians under the Persians, without a head, without order, beaten, despoiled, torn asunder, overrun, subject to every kind of disaster. The rhetorical purpose is to make the misery of the Italian situation legible as an opportunity rather than a defeat.

Machiavelli traces a recent near-success — a figure he does not name directly, almost certainly Cesare Borgia — in whom it seemed for a moment that God had ordained a redeemer for Italy, but who was defeated at the summit of his career by fortune's rejection. This implicit reference situates the exhortation within the logic of Chapter 7: Borgia did everything a man could do, and was undone by fortune at the last moment. The Medici, Machiavelli argues, have what Borgia had — ability, fortune, divine favour, the prestige of the Church — and they have the advantage of coming after a moment of near-success that has demonstrated the method works.

The practical argument is military. Italy has great individual valour — its soldiers excel in duels and single combat — but fails in organised armies because the commanders are incompetent. A prince who can solve the military problem will find the material of an army ready to hand. The chapter closes with a passage from Petrarch's Italia Mia — an ancient valour will take up arms against foreign fury, and Italian hearts will show that the old spirit is not dead. For a book that has maintained analytical distance from sentiment throughout, the quotation is striking in its directness. Lorenzo never read it. But it remains as evidence that the cold counsel of the preceding chapters was in service of something Machiavelli felt, and felt strongly: not merely the career of a particular prince, but the redemption of Italy.
