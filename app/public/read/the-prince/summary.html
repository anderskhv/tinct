The Prince

1532 CE · by Niccolò Machiavelli

The book in brief

The Prince is a short manual of political advice in twenty-six chapters, written in 1513 by an out-of-favor Florentine diplomat. It tells a ruler how to seize power, how to keep it, and what to do when fortune turns. The book divides principalities by type — hereditary, mixed, ecclesiastical, those won by virtue, those won by crime — and gives concrete advice on each. It uses Cesare Borgia's pacification of the Romagna as a working model and examines the failures of Louis XII, the genius of Pope Julius II, and the ruin of Florence when it relied on mercenaries. The famous middle chapters ask whether it is better for a prince to be loved or feared, how a prince should keep faith, and how to avoid contempt. The last chapter is a plea to liberate Italy from the foreign powers that had been trampling it for twenty years.

Machiavelli wrote the book at his small farm at Sant'Andrea in Percussina, seven miles outside Florence. The Medici had returned to power in 1512 and dismissed him from his post as Second Chancellor of the Republic. Early in 1513 he was tortured on suspicion of conspiracy, released, and exiled. The Prince was an attempt to demonstrate his usefulness to the new regime and possibly get a job. He dedicated it to Lorenzo de' Medici and never received a position. The book circulated in manuscript for two decades and was not printed until 1532, five years after his death.

It was placed on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1559 and remained there until 1890. The adjective "Machiavellian" entered English in the 1560s and immediately became an insult. Frederick the Great wrote a refutation of it as crown prince, then ruled like its most attentive student. Napoleon kept a copy with marginal notes. The book has been read as a cynical handbook, as an honest description of politics, and as a secret defense of republican liberty. It is short, under 30,000 words, and reads like a man who has watched a great deal of power up close and refuses to flatter what he saw.

Why it still matters

Politics Without Pretense

The Prince was the first major Western book to describe political action as it actually occurs rather than as moralists said it should occur. Machiavelli says directly in Chapter XV that he is leaving aside what has been imagined about princes and going only to the truth of the matter, because a man who insists on professing goodness in every situation must come to ruin among so many who are not good. The candor was shocking in 1513 and has never quite stopped being shocking. The book was placed on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1559 and remained there for three centuries. Every honest political thinker since — Hobbes, Spinoza, Hume, Tocqueville, Weber, Berlin, Kissinger — has had to address it. Even contemporary game-theoretic analyses of authoritarian succession quote him.

Virtue and Necessity

Machiavelli does not advocate cruelty for its own sake. He argues, more uncomfortably, that princes who refuse to do necessary harm fail, and that their failure costs more lives and more order than the harm would have. He distinguishes carefully in Chapter VIII between cruelty well used — applied decisively, all at once, and then stopped — and cruelty badly used, which drags on, breeds resentment, and never settles. The argument is the founding statement of political realism: the proposition that ethics in office is not the same as ethics in private life, that the duties of someone responsible for other people's safety are not identical to the duties of someone responsible only for his own soul. The argument has not been refuted. It has only been re-fought.

A Permanent Vocabulary

The book gave Western political language a permanent vocabulary. "Machiavellian" entered English in the 1560s as a synonym for cunning unscrupulousness and has been an insult ever since, applied to figures from Cromwell to Talleyrand to Bismarck to Henry Kissinger. "The ends justify the means" is a paraphrase of his argument in Chapter XVIII, though Machiavelli never wrote that line. "Fortune is a woman" is from his Chapter XXV. "It is better to be feared than loved, if you cannot be both" is verbatim from Chapter XVII. People who have never read The Prince still speak in its terms. Reading it returns the words to context and shows what Machiavelli actually said versus what five centuries of commentary have made him say.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The first political realist.

Every political thinker before Machiavelli asked: what should a ruler be? He asked instead: what does a ruler need to do to survive and succeed? That shift — from moral ideal to observed reality — is the founding move of modern political science. Track how consistently he holds to it.

Virtù and Fortuna — agency versus fate.

Machiavelli divides the forces shaping a prince's outcome into two: virtù (personal skill, energy, decisiveness — not virtue in the moral sense) and Fortuna (luck, circumstance, the tide of events). His argument is that a great prince can master roughly half of what happens to him. The other half is the flood.

Being feared versus being loved.

The most famous question in the book: is it better for a prince to be loved or feared? Machiavelli's answer is more careful than its reputation. He is not saying cruelty is good. He is saying that the prince who relies on love is vulnerable in ways the prince who commands fear is not — and that the two can be managed.

Machiavelli against Machiavelli — the republican reading.

Rousseau noted that Machiavelli dedicated The Prince to the Medici family that had destroyed the republic he loved, and then published it — which achieved nothing for him politically. Some read this as a secret subversion: a book that looks like advice to princes but actually exposes, for the people, exactly what their rulers do. The text supports both readings.

Key figures

Niccolò Machiavelli
The Author in Exile

Forty-four when he wrote the book, dismissed after fourteen years as Second Chancellor of the Florentine Republic, briefly tortured on suspicion of conspiracy, and living on a small farm seven miles from Florence. He composed The Prince in the evenings, after spending the days at the local inn. A famous letter to Francesco Vettori describes him changing into formal clothes at sundown to enter "the courts of the ancients" and write. The voice of the book is the voice of a man who has lost his career and refuses to lose his observations.

Cesare Borgia
The Working Model

Son of Pope Alexander VI. Used his father's patronage to carve a personal state out of the Romagna between 1499 and 1503 through a combination of diplomacy, betrayal, calculated cruelty, and one famous massacre at Senigallia. Machiavelli met him three times as a Florentine envoy and watched him at work. Cesare's state collapsed within a year of his father's death, but Machiavelli treats him in Chapter VII as the closest contemporary model of how a new prince actually consolidates power, naming his single error: not having ensured a friendly successor in the papacy.

The New Prince
The Central Subject

Not a hereditary ruler with centuries of legitimacy behind him but someone who has just seized power and now has to keep it. He is Machiavelli's real subject, the hardest case, the figure who must build everything without inherited authority to lean on. The book is structured around his problems: how to handle the conquered population, when to act decisively, when to delay, what to do with mercenaries, how to manage advisors, how to be feared without being hated. Hereditary princes get one short chapter. The new prince gets the rest.

The Armed Prophet
The Founder Type

Machiavelli's category for the rulers who do not just take power but found new orders: Moses, Romulus, Theseus, Cyrus. He notes coldly in Chapter VI that all of them succeeded because they were armed, while Savonarola, the Florentine friar who tried to refound the republic on prophetic authority alone, was hanged and burned in the Piazza della Signoria in 1498. The point is not that prophecy is empty. The point is that ideas without force do not survive contact with the people who are losing under them. Unarmed prophets come to ruin.

Hannibal
The Discipline Of Cruelty

Machiavelli's example in Chapter XVII of how cruelty well used keeps an army together. Hannibal led a polyglot force of Africans, Spaniards, and Gauls through Italy for sixteen years without a single mutiny — Machiavelli attributes this to the "inhuman cruelty" that ran alongside his other virtues, because it produced the respect on which a multi-ethnic army depended. Set against Scipio, whose excessive mercy provoked a rebellion of his own troops in Spain. The pairing argues that in a leader of armies, discipline applied with severity is itself a kind of order.

Fortuna
The Tide Of Events

The force in events no ruler controls — the death of a pope, the arrival of a foreign army, the timing of plague. Machiavelli devotes Chapter XXV to her and arrives at a famous image: she is like a flooding river. You cannot stop her, but you can build embankments in the years she is calm so that when she rises she does less damage. She rules half of what happens to a prince. The remaining half is virtù, and preparing for the flood is the closest the book comes to a philosophy of action.
