Chapter 6 — New Principalities Acquired by One's Own Arms and Ability

Moses, Romulus, and Cyrus succeeded because they were armed; Savonarola failed because he was not.

Summary

Machiavelli opens with an epistemological caution: he will follow the paths beaten by others and imitate their methods, because that is the most honest thing a writer can do. But he will adduct the highest examples precisely because the most extreme cases reveal the principles most clearly. Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, Theseus — founders of peoples, states, and religions — are examined not as moral exemplars but as political case studies in what it takes to impose new orders of things on human populations that have to be persuaded to change their habits.

The crucial distinction the chapter introduces is between armed and unarmed prophets. The great founders succeeded not only because of their intelligence or their vision but because they had the capacity to compel. When the multitude began to doubt — as it always does — the armed prophet could force belief through punishment; the unarmed prophet had no such recourse. Machiavelli gives the contemporary example without softening it: Savonarola, the reforming friar who briefly seized Florence, was ruined as soon as the multitude lost faith in him, because he had no means of keeping the faithful firm and making the faithless believe. The army matters more than the argument.

The chapter ends with a minor example intended to illustrate the same principle at smaller scale: Hiero of Syracuse, who rose from private citizen to prince by his own ability and the opportunity the Syracusans gave him. Finding the existing army useless, he disbanded it and formed a new one. Machiavelli's comment is characteristically dry: he who already has his soldiers, and cannot use foreign ones, in every other part he was a most excellent man. The example closes the spectrum: from Moses at one end to Hiero at the other, the same principle holds.
