Deuteronomy — chapter by chapter

All 34 chapters, from Moses's opening speech to his death on Nebo.

Deuteronomy runs through four movements: the historical review and exhortation (chapters 1–11), the legal code with its laws of worship, society, and kingship (chapters 12–26), the great covenant ceremony of blessings and curses (chapters 27–30), and the final acts of Moses — his commissioning of Joshua, his song, his blessing of the tribes, and his death (chapters 31–34). The Pentateuch ends on a mountain, with a man looking at a country he cannot enter.

Deuteronomy 2

Deuteronomy 3

The rout of Og of Bashan completes Israel's eastern conquests, and Moses receives his final refusal from God—he may see the land, but not enter it.

Appears: Moses · Joshua · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 4

Deuteronomy 4

Moses grounds the ban on idolatry in the nature of the Sinai encounter itself: Israel heard a voice but saw no form, so no image can represent God.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel

Second discourse · Chs 5–26

The Decalogue, the Shema, and the full legal code.

Deuteronomy 5

Deuteronomy 5

Moses retells the Decalogue, grounding Sabbath rest not in creation but in the memory of Egypt, where Israel itself laboured without rest.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 6

Deuteronomy 6

The Shema — Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one — commands undivided love of God and saturates every domain of daily life with the confession.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 7

Deuteronomy 7

Israel is commanded to destroy the Canaanite nations without intermarriage or treaty—not because of Israel's merit, but because the Lord loves Israel and swore to the ancestors.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 8

Deuteronomy 8

The wilderness years were education, not punishment: manna taught Israel that human life depends on every word from God, not on bread or wealth or human power.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 9

Deuteronomy 9

Israel's entry into Canaan is no reward for virtue—the people are stubborn, the land is a gift and a mercy, and Moses reminds them of every rebellion from Sinai to Kadesh.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 10

Deuteronomy 10

After the golden calf, new tablets are made, the Levites are consecrated, and Israel is summoned to an inward covenant: circumcise your heart, love the stranger, fear a God who owns the universe.

Appears: Moses · The Levites · The Stranger / The Widow / The Orphan · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 11

Deuteronomy 11

Moses closes the first speech by setting the choice before the whole land: faithfulness brings rain from heaven; idolatry brings drought, destruction, and swift perishing from the good land.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 12

Deuteronomy 12

Deuteronomy's most consequential legal provision: all sacrifice at one chosen place, all local altars destroyed—the foundation of Jerusalem's supremacy and Josiah's reform.

Appears: Moses · The Levites · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 13

Deuteronomy 13

Three cases of idolatrous seduction—false prophet, intimate friend, whole city—and the verdict in each case is death, because love of God is the first loyalty and admits no rival.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 14

Deuteronomy 14

The dietary laws frame Israel's holiness in bodily terms; the tithe law frames it in economic terms—feeding the Levites, the strangers, the orphans, and the widows every third year.

Appears: Moses · The Levites · The Stranger / The Widow / The Orphan · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 15

Deuteronomy 15

The seventh-year debt remission commands creditors to lend generously even as the cancellation year approaches, and to release Hebrew slaves with provisions, not empty-handed.

Appears: Moses · The Stranger / The Widow / The Orphan · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 16

Deuteronomy 16

The three pilgrimage festivals—Passover, Weeks, and Booths—are centralized at the chosen sanctuary, with provisions for every member of the household including servants, Levites, and strangers.

Appears: Moses · The Levites · The Stranger / The Widow / The Orphan · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 17

Deuteronomy 17

A difficult cases procedure leads to the law of the king: he may rule, but he is bound by the law he must copy and read all his life—no excess of horses, wives, or wealth.

Appears: Moses · The King (anticipated) · The Levites · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 18

Deuteronomy 18

Between prohibitions of Canaanite divination and the rules for the Levites, Moses promises that God will raise up a prophet like himself — and gives criteria for distinguishing genuine prophecy from false.

Appears: Moses · The Levites
Deuteronomy 19

Deuteronomy 19

Cities of refuge protect the unintentional killer from blood vengeance; the requirement of two witnesses and the rule of reciprocal punishment for false accusers build the safeguards of a credible criminal procedure.

Appears: Moses
Deuteronomy 20

Deuteronomy 20

Detailed laws govern Israelite warfare: the priest's pre-battle speech, the exemptions for the newly settled and the fearful, and the sharp legal distinction between cities outside and inside the promised land.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 21

Deuteronomy 21

From the unexplained corpse in an open field through the rights of captive brides, despised wives' firstborn sons, and incorrigible children, chapter 21 systematically handles cases where social order has broken down or is under strain.

Appears: Moses · The Levites
Deuteronomy 22

Deuteronomy 22

Neighborly duties about lost animals give way to purity rules about mixed crops and mixed fibers, then to the detailed and sometimes brutal marriage laws governing accusations of premarital unchastity and the various cases of sexual violation.

Appears: Moses
Deuteronomy 23

Deuteronomy 23

Assembly membership rules that weigh national histories of hospitality, sanitary laws for the military camp, protection for runaway slaves, prohibitions on commercial religion, and a strict law on vows make chapter 23 a catalogue of the boundaries that hold the covenant community together.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel · The Stranger / The Widow / The Orphan
Deuteronomy 24

Deuteronomy 24

Divorce regulation, exemptions from military service for newlyweds, and a dense cluster of protections for debtors, day laborers, and the landless poor make chapter 24 the social legislation heart of the Deuteronomic Code.

Appears: Moses · The Stranger / The Widow / The Orphan
Deuteronomy 25

Deuteronomy 25

Limits on judicial flogging, the duty of the brother-in-law to perpetuate a dead brother's name through levirate marriage, honest weights and measures, and the standing command to remember and eventually destroy Amalek conclude the legal core.

Appears: Moses
Deuteronomy 26

Deuteronomy 26

The firstfruits liturgy gives every Israelite farmer a few lines to recite — wandering Aramean to promised land — that compress the entire salvation story into the act of bringing a basket of grain; the tithe declaration closes the loop.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel · The Levites

Blessings and curses · Chs 27–30

The covenant at Ebal and Gerizim, and the closing peroration.

Deuteronomy 27

Deuteronomy 27

Detailed instructions for a covenant ceremony at Mounts Ebal and Gerizim, including the writing of the law on plastered stones and a liturgy of twelve curses covering secret violations of the covenant — each answered by the people's Amen.

Appears: Moses · The Levites · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 28

Deuteronomy 28

The blessings of obedience take fourteen verses; the curses of disobedience take fifty-four, escalating from drought and defeat through madness, siege, and cannibalism to the ultimate horror of return to Egypt — read by later editors as a preview of the exile.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 29

Deuteronomy 29

A second covenant ceremony at Moab, explicitly distinguished from Horeb, opens with the disturbing observation that the people still lack understanding despite everything they witnessed — and closes with the verse separating the secret things that belong to God from the revealed things that belong to Israel.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 30

Deuteronomy 30

Moses's closing peroration promises restoration after exile, a divine circumcision of the heart that enables obedience, and the insistence that the law is not beyond reach — before delivering the book's most celebrated line: choose life so that you and your descendants may live.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel

Final acts of Moses · Chs 31–34

Joshua commissioned. The Song. The blessing. The death on Nebo.

Deuteronomy 31

Deuteronomy 31

Moses announces his approaching death, commissions Joshua before the assembly, deposits the written law with the priests for seven-year public reading, and receives divine instructions to compose the Song of Moses as a lasting witness against Israel's future unfaithfulness.

Appears: Moses · Joshua · The Levites · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 32

Deuteronomy 32

The Song of Moses — one of the oldest texts in the Hebrew Bible — traces the arc from God's care in the wilderness to Israel's faithless prosperity to the disasters that will follow, and finally to divine vengeance on the enemies and atonement for the land.

Appears: Moses · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 33

Deuteronomy 33

Moses's individual blessings of the twelve tribes — the last formal act before his death — range from brief to elaborate and are enclosed in a poetic frame about the God who came from Sinai and will be Israel's eternal shield.

Appears: Moses · The Levites · The People of Israel
Deuteronomy 34

Deuteronomy 34

Moses climbs Nebo, surveys the promised land he will not enter, dies at the Lord's command, and is buried by God in an unmarked valley — the Pentateuch's final sentence declares that no prophet like him has arisen in Israel since.

Appears: Moses · Joshua · The People of Israel
