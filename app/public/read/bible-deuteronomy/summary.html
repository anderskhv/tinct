Deuteronomy a guided tour

c. 7th–6th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Torah

Moses stands on the east bank of the Jordan with a generation that has never seen Egypt and will not see him cross. He has one speech left. Everything depends on what they do with it.

The book in brief

Deuteronomy is Moses's last sermon. The people are camped on the plains of Moab, the Jordan in front of them, the promised land just visible on the other side, and Moses — who is not going to cross — has them sit down for one final, urgent address. He retells the law, recasts the covenant, and warns them, in the most rhetorically charged Hebrew prose in the Bible, what is at stake. Choose life so that you and your descendants may live. The book has shaped Jewish liturgy, Christian theology, the political imagination of the early American Puritans, and almost every later attempt to write a constitution as a moral document.

Deuteronomy reaches its present form during the reign of King Josiah of Judah in the late seventh century BCE — the central legal core of the book, often called the Deuteronomic Code (chapters 12–26), is widely identified with the book of the law found in the temple in 2 Kings 22 that triggers Josiah's sweeping religious reform of 622 BCE. The book speaks in a single voice — rhetorically powerful, theologically focused, urgent — that no other book of the Pentateuch quite matches. At its centre is a claim about what religion is: not ritual performance, but love. You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might.

Deuteronomy 1 of 34
Deuteronomy 1

Moses Recalls Horeb and the Spies

Chapter 1 is the opening of Moses's first great speech, which runs through chapter 4. He begins not with law but with history: the departure from Horeb (Sinai), the appointment of tribal judges to share the burden of leadership, the sending of twelve spies from Kadesh-barnea into the land. The spies return with good news about the land but the people refuse to go up, fearing the tall cities and the people of Anak. God's anger falls on the generation that refused: they will not see the good land. The judgement applies even to Moses himself—because of you the Lord was angry with me also, he says, and I too will not cross the Jordan. Only Caleb, who followed the Lord fully, will enter. The speech establishes the pattern: the wilderness is not merely past geography but the moral explanation of why the present generation is here and the previous one is not.

Deuteronomy 2

Wandering and the First Victories

Chapter 2 covers the long wilderness circuit and the beginning of the conquest east of the Jordan. God commands the people to pass through the territories of Esau (Edom), Moab, and Ammon without engaging them—these lands belong to their kinsmen and are not part of Israel's inheritance. The text notes that Israel must pay for whatever food and water they consume. The chapter then pivots to Sihon, king of the Amorites at Heshbon. Israel requests peaceful passage; God has hardened Sihon's spirit so that he refuses. The battle is joined, the Lord delivers Sihon, and every city is put under the ban—men, women, and children destroyed, livestock and goods taken as plunder. The destruction of Sihon marks the first actual conquest and sets the template for what is coming across the Jordan: divine command, offer of peace refused or accepted, and engagement. The chapter is military geography in Moses's mouth.

Deuteronomy 3

Og of Bashan and Moses's View of the Land

Chapter 3 completes the conquest east of the Jordan with the defeat of Og, king of Bashan, whose iron bedstead nine cubits long testifies to his legendary size. Like Sihon, Og is delivered into Israel's hand; every city is devoted to destruction. Moses then allocates the territory: Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh receive the land east of the Jordan on condition that their fighting men cross over with the rest of Israel to help conquer Canaan. Joshua is singled out for a direct charge: your eyes have seen everything the Lord has done to these two kings; so will the Lord do to all the kingdoms into which you are about to cross. The chapter's emotional climax is Moses's plea to the Lord to let him cross the Jordan and see the good land and the fine mountains and Lebanon. God refuses: enough from you; do not speak to me of this matter again. Moses is told to climb Pisgah and look, and to commission Joshua. The combination of military triumph and personal exclusion gives the chapter its peculiar pathos.

Deuteronomy 4

The Warning Against Idolatry

Chapter 4 is the theological climax of the first speech. Moses argues from the Sinai experience itself: you heard a voice out of the fire but saw no form—therefore do not make any image. The prohibition against idolatry is grounded not in arbitrary law but in the phenomenology of the original encounter. The chapter surveys the history of divine acts—Egypt, the wilderness, the defeat of Baal-peor—as evidence that the Lord alone has done these things and there is no other. It pronounces the warning starkly: if you act corruptly and make a carved image of any form, you will be scattered among the peoples, few in number, and will worship wood and stone there. But it also holds open the door of return: when you are in tribulation and all these things come upon you in the latter days, you will return to the Lord and obey his voice, for the Lord your God is a merciful God. The chapter closes by establishing cities of refuge east of the Jordan for unintentional homicide—a flash of practical law inside the sermon.

Deuteronomy 5

The Decalogue Recalled at Horeb

Chapter 5 is the second giving of the Decalogue—the first was in Exodus 20. Moses frames it carefully: the Lord made this covenant not with your fathers but with you, here, today, all of you who are alive. The covenant is not historical; it is present. The Ten Commandments are then recited in order, with one striking change: in Exodus the Sabbath is grounded in the creation narrative (God rested on the seventh day); here it is grounded in the exodus (you were a slave in Egypt, and the Lord your God brought you out). The humanitarian dimension of Sabbath rest—your male and female servants shall rest as well as you—is given heavier weight. The chapter closes with the people's terrified response at Horeb: they stood far off and begged Moses to go near and hear everything and tell them, because they feared to die if God spoke to them directly. God honours the request and commends the sentiment—they have spoken rightly—and sends Moses back with the law.

Deuteronomy 6

The Shema and the Great Commandment

Chapter 6 is perhaps the single most influential chapter in Deuteronomy. It opens with the statement of purpose: these statutes are commanded so that you may fear the Lord all the days of your life and that it may go well with you. Then the Shema: Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one. And immediately after: You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might. These words shall be on your heart. You shall teach them to your children, and talk of them at home and on the road and lying down and rising. You shall bind them on your hand and wear them between your eyes and write them on your doorposts. This is the origin of tefillin and mezuzot, but also the conceptual basis for the saturation of ordinary life by the confession of God's oneness. The chapter then warns against the prosperity that will come in the land: when you eat and are satisfied and build good houses and your flocks and herds multiply, take care lest you forget the Lord who brought you out of Egypt. The danger of forgetting is the book's second great theme, always shadowing the first.

Deuteronomy 7

Set Apart from the Nations

Chapter 7 confronts the reader with the most difficult material in Deuteronomy: the command to destroy the seven nations of Canaan without mercy, to break down their altars and burn their idols. The rationale is theological. These peoples will turn your sons away from following me to serve other gods, and the anger of the Lord will be kindled against you. Intermarriage is forbidden for the same reason. But the chapter also insists on the reason for election: it was not because you were more in number than any other people that the Lord set his love on you, for you were the fewest of all peoples. It is because the Lord loves you and is keeping the oath he swore to your fathers. Election is asymmetrical grace, not merit. The chapter then promises military success through divine action—the Lord will clear away these nations before you, little by little, so the wild animals do not overtake you—and lists the blessings of faithfulness: prosperity, fertility, health, the destruction of enemies. No disease that fell on Egypt will fall on you. The chapter is militaristic but its theological logic is the exclusivity of love.

Deuteronomy 8

Man Does Not Live by Bread Alone

Chapter 8 is one of the most theologically subtle chapters in the book. Moses interprets the wilderness years not as divine abandonment but as divine pedagogy. The Lord humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna that you did not know, nor did your fathers know—that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of the Lord. The manna episode, which in Exodus is about provision, is here read as instruction in the nature of dependence on God. The chapter then shifts to the danger of prosperity: when you eat and are satisfied, when you build good houses and your herds multiply and your silver and gold multiply—then take care lest you say in your heart, my power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth. The danger of self-sufficiency is the obverse of the wilderness lesson. You shall remember the Lord your God, for it is he who gives you the power to get wealth. The chapter is the source of the verse Jesus quotes in the temptation narrative: man does not live by bread alone.

Deuteronomy 9

Stiff-Necked People, Merciful God

Chapter 9 dismantles any complacency about merit. Moses tells the people explicitly: the Lord is not driving out the nations because of your righteousness, for you are a stubborn people. The gift is because the nations are wicked and because of the oath God swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Moses then rehearses the golden calf episode in detail—the forty days and nights on the mountain, his descent to find them worshipping the calf, his smashing of the tablets, his intercession on their behalf for another forty days and nights. The intercession is the emotional centre: Moses prostrates himself before God and prays for the people's survival, citing God's reputation among the nations as the reason not to destroy Israel. The Lord listened. The prayer is presented as the type of the kind of intercessory prayer the whole of Moses's ministry has been—not based on Israel's merit but on God's own commitment. The chapter accumulates examples of rebellion: Horeb, Massah, Kibroth-hattaavah, Kadesh. The list is a confession, not a compliment.

Deuteronomy 10

Circumcise the Foreskin of Your Heart

Chapter 10 narrates the renewal after the golden calf. God tells Moses to cut two tablets like the first, and the second set of tablets is made and deposited in the ark of acacia wood—the first mention of the ark in Deuteronomy. The Levites are set apart at that time to carry the ark, to stand before the Lord, to minister and to bless in his name. Israel then sets out from Beeroth Bene-jaakan through the wilderness. Aaron dies at Moserah and Eleazar his son ministers in his place. The chapter then pivots to exhortation: and now, Israel, what does the Lord your God require of you but to fear him, walk in all his ways, love him, serve him with all your heart and all your soul, and keep his commandments. God owns the heavens and the earth, yet chose the patriarchs and loved their offspring. The chapter's theological high point is the demand to circumcise the foreskin of your heart and no longer be stiff-necked—inward transformation, not merely outward ritual. God executes justice for the fatherless and the widow and loves the stranger. You shall love the stranger, for you were strangers in Egypt.

Deuteronomy 11

Blessing and Curse Set Before the Land

Chapter 11 is the conclusion of Moses's first extended speech before the legal code begins in chapter 12. It gathers the threads of the previous ten chapters into a final exhortation. You shall love the Lord your God and keep his commandments always. The chapter appeals to the first generation's direct experience—your eyes have seen the great work the Lord did in Egypt and in the wilderness—as the ground for obedience. It then describes the land in terms that contrast with Egypt: Egypt is a land watered by foot (by human irrigation channels), but the land of Canaan drinks water from the rain of heaven, a land the Lord your God cares for. His eyes are on it from the beginning of the year to the end. The consequence of faithfulness is the agricultural blessing of rain; the consequence of disobedience is the stopping of rain and the perishing of the land. The chapter closes with instructions for the covenant ceremony at Gerizim and Ebal—the blessing mountain and the curse mountain—and with the land boundaries. The choice is geographical and the choice is immediate.

Deuteronomy 12

One Altar, One Place

Chapter 12 opens the legal core of Deuteronomy—the Deuteronomic Code running from chapter 12 to chapter 26—with its most structurally significant provision: the centralization of worship. You shall break down their altars and burn their sacred pillars. You shall not worship the Lord your God in that way. But you shall seek the place that the Lord your God will choose out of all your tribes to put his name and make his habitation there. The place is unnamed in the text, but its identification with Jerusalem by later editors is clear. The chapter regulates what this means in practice: sacrifices, tithes, firstlings, and votive offerings must all be brought to the central sanctuary. Ordinary slaughter of animals for meat is now permitted anywhere—a significant change from earlier law, where any slaughter was a sacrificial act. The chapter also warns against the Canaanite practices at their high places: you shall not inquire about their gods. Everything that is an abomination to the Lord they have done for their gods. The structure of the chapter is the structure of Josiah's reform put into the mouth of Moses.

Deuteronomy 13

The Prophet Who Leads Astray

Chapter 13 is one of the most demanding chapters in Deuteronomy and one of the most important for the biblical theology of false prophecy. Three cases are given. First: a prophet arises who performs a sign or wonder that comes true, but then invites the people to go after other gods. He is to be executed, because the Lord is testing whether you love him with all your heart. The truth of the prediction does not validate the prophet. Second: a close relative or intimate friend privately invites you to go after other gods. He is to be put to death; your hand shall be first against him. The loyalty test is personal as well as public. Third: a whole city is seduced into idolatry. The city is to be put under the ban—every inhabitant killed, the city burned, never to be rebuilt. The logic of each case is the same: the attraction to idolatry does not become more legitimate by being verified or beloved. The chapter makes clear that the primary threat to covenant fidelity is not the Canaanite on the outside but the seducer within.

Deuteronomy 14

Clean and Unclean, Tithes and Feasts

Chapter 14 opens with the holiness rationale for the dietary laws: you are the sons of the Lord your God and a people holy to him. It then lists clean and unclean animals—the same categories as Leviticus 11 but in a shorter form. Land animals must have split hooves and chew cud; fish must have fins and scales; birds are listed by name. The blood of the dead shall not be eaten. The prohibition on boiling a kid in its mother's milk appears again, as in Exodus. The chapter then turns to tithes. Every year you shall tithe all the yield of your seed from the field. This tithe is to be eaten in the presence of the Lord at the chosen sanctuary with your household. If the journey is too far to carry the goods, sell them, take the money, and buy whatever you desire at the sanctuary—including oxen, sheep, wine, or strong drink. Every third year the tithe is stored locally and given to the Levite, the stranger, the orphan, and the widow who have no portion. The chapter is practical as well as theological: holiness is embodied in food discipline and expressed in economic provision for the vulnerable.

Deuteronomy 15

The Seven-Year Remission

Chapter 15 contains the most economically radical provision in the Deuteronomic Code: the seven-year remission of debts. At the end of every seven years you shall grant a release. Every creditor among you shall release what he has lent to his neighbour. He shall not exact it of his neighbour, his brother, because the Lord's release has been proclaimed. The law then anticipates the obvious abuse: the text explicitly addresses the person who refuses to lend as the seventh year approaches, saying there will be a poor person making a claim but the debt will not be repaid. The Lord's warning is direct: your eye shall not be evil toward your poor brother. You shall open wide your hand to him. The poor will not cease from the land, so you shall open wide your hand to your brother. The chapter then covers the release of Hebrew slaves in the seventh year of service—with goods: send him out with provisions, not empty-handed. The chapter closes with the law of the firstling animals, to be eaten at the central sanctuary. The economic and liturgical are interwoven as aspects of the same covenant life.

Deuteronomy 16

The Three Pilgrimage Feasts

Chapter 16 regulates the three pilgrimage festivals that structure the Israelite year. Passover: in the month of Abib, slaughter the Passover lamb at the chosen place—not at home as in Exodus, but centralized—eat it with unleavened bread for seven days, for you came out of Egypt in haste. The Festival of Weeks: seven weeks after the first grain harvest, bring a freewill offering proportional to how the Lord has blessed you, and eat at the central sanctuary—you and your household and your servants and the Levite and the sojourner and the orphan and the widow. The Festival of Booths: seven days after the final harvest, rejoice at the place the Lord chooses—all your household and all those within your gates. Three times a year all males shall appear before the Lord at the chosen sanctuary. Each shall give as he is able. None shall appear before the Lord empty-handed. The chapter closes with the appointment of judges and officers—justice, and only justice, you shall pursue.

Deuteronomy 17

Courts, Appeals, and the Law of the King

Chapter 17 addresses the administration of justice and, in its second half, anticipates the institution of kingship. The chapter opens with the prohibition of blemished sacrifices and moves to the procedure for difficult cases: when a case is too hard for a local judge, it is brought to the central sanctuary for decision by the Levitical priests and the judge in office. Their verdict is final; the person who acts presumptuously and does not obey it shall die. Then comes the law of the king—the most extraordinary constitutional text in the Hebrew Bible. When you enter the land and say I will set a king over me like all the nations, you may do so—but only one of your own brothers, not a foreigner. He shall not multiply horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt to acquire more horses. He shall not multiply wives for himself, lest his heart turn away. He shall not greatly multiply silver and gold. He shall write for himself a copy of this law, and read in it all the days of his life, so that he may learn to fear the Lord his God. The king is a constitutional monarch before the term existed.

Deuteronomy 18

The Prophet Like Moses

Chapter 18 opens with the economic arrangements for the Levitical priests, who have no land and depend entirely on the portions of sacrifice assigned to them by law. The chapter then turns to a categorical prohibition of every form of Canaanite divination: child sacrifice, soothsaying, augury, sorcery, charming, necromancy. The reason given is not that these things do not work but that they are abhorrent to the Lord — the nations that practice them are being dispossessed precisely for these practices. In their place, Moses promises that the Lord will raise up from among the Israelites a prophet like Moses himself. The people had asked at Horeb not to hear God's voice directly — too terrifying — and the prophet is the mediated answer to that request. The test of a true prophet is whether the word comes true. A word that does not come true was not spoken by the Lord; there is no need to fear it.

Deuteronomy 19

Cities of Refuge and Witnesses

Chapter 19 establishes the institutional infrastructure of a just criminal procedure. Three cities of refuge are to be set apart in the land, accessible by maintained roads, to give shelter to anyone who kills a neighbour unintentionally — the text gives the specific case of an axe-head flying off its handle in the forest. The city of refuge is not for the deliberate murderer, whom the elders of his city are to send back and deliver to the blood-avenger. If the land expands, three additional cities are to be added, so that innocent blood is not shed in the land. The second subject is the law of witnesses: a single witness cannot establish guilt in any case, especially a capital one. Two or three witnesses are required. If a malicious witness rises against someone, both the accuser and the accused are to stand before the priests and judges, and if the witness is found to have testified falsely, the punishment the false witness intended for the accused is to fall on the witness instead. The principle is stated: your eye shall not pity — life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot.

Deuteronomy 20

Laws of War

Chapter 20 is one of the Hebrew Bible's most systematic treatments of warfare law. It begins with the priest's pre-battle address: do not be afraid of the horses and chariots and the army larger than yours, because the Lord your God is with you. Then come the officers' announcements: anyone who has built a house and not yet dedicated it, planted a vineyard and not yet eaten of it, or betrothed a woman and not yet married her is to be sent home, so that he does not die in battle and another man enjoy what he worked for. And anyone who is afraid is also to be sent home, so that his fear does not spread through the ranks. The law then distinguishes between cities far from the land — those offered peace terms first, enslaved if they accept, destroyed if they refuse — and cities inside the land, which are to be placed under the ban: nothing living is to be left alive. The chapter closes with an agricultural provision: fruit trees are not to be cut down in sieges. They are not an army; they provide food. Other trees may be used for siege works.

Deuteronomy 21

Unsolved Murder to Family Law

Chapter 21 is a collection of case laws that share an underlying concern with the disruption of proper order — by death, by family conflict, by the ambiguous status of a captured woman. The chapter opens with the most arresting procedure: a body found in a field with no known killer. The elders of the nearest town must bring an unworked heifer to an unworn valley, break its neck over running water, and wash their hands, declaring that their hands did not shed this blood. The rite locates moral responsibility within a geographic community and formally absolves the community through enacted disavowal. What follows is a law governing a captive woman whom an Israelite soldier wishes to marry: she must be brought into the house, shave her head, pare her nails, remove her captive's garments, and mourn her parents for a full month — only then may the man marry her. If he then does not want her, he must let her go; he may not sell her or treat her as a slave, because he has humiliated her. The chapter then covers the rights of the firstborn of an unloved wife, and closes with the case of a rebellious son — who may, after a formal complaint by both parents to the elders, be stoned.

Deuteronomy 22

Property, Purity, and Marriage Law

Chapter 22 opens with a cluster of neighborhood duties: return a neighbor's stray animal; if you cannot return it immediately, bring it home until the neighbor asks for it. This applies to any lost property. Do not ignore a neighbor's fallen donkey or ox on the road — help him. Then a series of purity distinctions: do not wear a garment of mixed wool and linen; do not plow with an ox and a donkey yoked together; do not plant two kinds of seed in a vineyard. The roof of a new house must have a parapet, so that bloodguilt does not fall on you if someone falls from it — the building code as covenant law. The chapter then moves to marriage law: the case of a man who marries a woman, makes accusations about her virginity, and is either vindicated or refuted by her parents' presenting the cloth evidence; the case of adultery with a married woman; the case of a betrothed woman assaulted in the city versus assaulted in the open country; the case of a man who lies with an unbetrothed woman. The chapter is held together by a consistent concern with proper categories — things properly distinguished must not be mixed; boundaries once established must be maintained.

Deuteronomy 23

Membership, Enemies, and Vows

Chapter 23 opens with a series of rules about who may be admitted to the assembly of the Lord — the qahal, the formal covenant community. The eunuch is excluded; the son of a forbidden union to the tenth generation; the Ammonite and the Moabite forever — because they did not meet Israel with bread and water in the wilderness and because Moab hired Balaam. The Edomite and the Egyptian, however, are not to be abhorred: the Edomite is a brother; the Egyptian provided a home in which Israel lived. Their third-generation descendants may enter the assembly. There follows a set of rules for a military camp's ritual purity — disposing of excrement outside the camp, keeping the camp holy because God walks in it. Then the prohibition on returning a runaway slave to his master: he is to be allowed to settle wherever he chooses and not oppressed. The chapter continues with prohibitions on cult prostitution (male or female), the bringing of a prostitute's fee or a dog's price as a vow-offering to the sanctuary, and charging interest to a fellow Israelite. It closes with the vow law: if you make a vow, pay it promptly; if you refrain from vowing, no sin attaches; but what proceeds from your lips you must keep.

Deuteronomy 24

Divorce, Pledges, and the Poor

Chapter 24 opens with the divorce provision that will be cited by the Pharisees in the Gospels and that has generated centuries of legal debate. A man who writes his wife a certificate of divorce and sends her away, and she then marries another man who later divorces her or dies, may not take his first wife back. She has been defiled; to take her back would be an abomination and would bring sin upon the land. The chapter moves through a cluster of humanitarian provisions: a newly married man is exempt from military duty for a year; no one may take a handmill or upper millstone in pledge, since that would deprive the household of its means of making bread; kidnapping and selling a fellow Israelite is punishable by death. Then two provisions about credit: the creditor may not enter the debtor's house to take the pledge himself — he must wait outside while the debtor brings it out. If the debtor is poor and the pledge is his cloak, it must be returned by nightfall because it is his only covering for sleep. Wages must be paid to day laborers on the day they are earned, because they are poor and count on it. The chapter closes with gleaning rights: sheaves left in the field, olives on the branch, grapes on the vine after the first pass are to be left for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow. You shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt.

Deuteronomy 25

Limits on Punishment and Levirate Marriage

Chapter 25 opens with the law governing corporal punishment: when a dispute is adjudicated and the guilty party is sentenced to flogging, the sentence is to be carried out in the judge's presence, and forty is the maximum number of strokes permitted. One more would be degrading to your brother — the formulation is striking, locating the limit in the convict's dignity as a member of the community. Continuing the theme of unexpected dignity, the law forbids muzzling an ox while it is threshing grain. The central provision of the chapter is the levirate marriage law: if brothers live together and one of them dies without a son, his brother must take the widow as his wife and perform the duty of a husband's brother to her; the first son born shall carry the dead brother's name to keep his name from being blotted out in Israel. If the surviving brother refuses, the widow performs the chalitzah ceremony before the elders — removes his sandal, spits in his face, declares that this is what is done to the man who will not build up his brother's house. The chapter then regulates a woman who grabs an attacker's genitals while helping her husband — her hand is to be cut off with no pity. Two provisions about honest weights close the chapter. And the final command is to remember what Amalek did — attack from behind when the people were faint and weary — and blot out their memory.

Deuteronomy 26

Firstfruits and the Covenant Declaration

Chapter 26 closes the legal core of Deuteronomy with two liturgical acts that frame everything that has gone before. The first is the presentation of firstfruits: when the farmer brings the first of the ground's produce to the sanctuary, he sets the basket before the altar and recites a short confession of salvation history — a wandering Aramean was my father; he went down to Egypt few in number; the Egyptians oppressed us; we cried out; the Lord heard us and brought us out with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm and signs and wonders; and he brought us into this place and gave us this land flowing with milk and honey. Therefore I bring the firstfruits. The recitation is remarkable for its economy — the whole of the patriarchal and exodus story in a few lines, always in the first person plural, always connecting the present farmer to the ancient events. The second act is the triennial tithe declaration: every third year the full tithe has been given to the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow; nothing has been eaten in mourning or given to the dead; the farmer has obeyed. And then: look down from your holy habitation, from heaven, and bless your people Israel, and the land you have given us, as you swore to our ancestors.

Deuteronomy 27

The Curse Formula at Ebal

Chapter 27 is a set of stage directions for a covenant ceremony. When the people cross the Jordan, they are to set up large stones, plaster them, and write on them all the words of the law. On Mount Ebal they are to build an altar of uncut stones — no iron tool has touched them — and offer burnt offerings and peace offerings there. Six tribes are to stand on Mount Gerizim for the blessing, and six on Mount Ebal for the curse. Then the Levites are to proclaim twelve curses, each beginning with Cursed be the one who — and after each curse the entire people are to say Amen. The curses cover: making idols, dishonoring parents, moving a neighbor's boundary marker, misleading the blind on the road, perverting justice for the stranger or the fatherless or the widow, lying with a father's wife, lying with any animal, lying with a sister or a mother-in-law, striking a neighbor in secret, taking a bribe to kill an innocent person, and failing to keep all the words of this law. The list does not cover every possible violation of the law. Its logic is specific: it addresses violations that might be committed in secret, unseen by human witnesses, where no ordinary legal proceeding could take effect.

Deuteronomy 28

The Great Blessings and Curses

Chapter 28 is the rhetorical climax of the legal section and one of the most powerful chapters in the Hebrew Bible. Verses 1-14 describe the blessings that will follow obedience: blessed in the city and in the field, in the basket and in the kneading bowl, when you come in and when you go out. Your enemies will flee before you on seven roads. The Lord will open his good treasury, the heavens, to give rain in its season. You will be the head and not the tail. The curses begin at verse 15 and do not stop for fifty-four verses. They are the mirror image of the blessings, and then much more. Cursed in the city and the field. Disease and drought. Defeat in battle. Madness, blindness, confusion of mind. An unfaithful wife. An outsider who rises higher and higher while you sink. Siege so terrible that parents will eat their children. Foreign gods of wood and stone. Return to Egypt on ships, to be sold as slaves, but no one will buy. The curses are arranged in escalating waves. They end at verse 68 without any relief. The chapter is the longest chapter in Deuteronomy and one of the most formally elaborate sequences in the Pentateuch.

Deuteronomy 29

The Renewed Covenant in Moab

Chapter 29 opens a new covenant ceremony, explicitly distinguished from the covenant at Horeb: today you are entering into a covenant with the Lord your God that he is making with you in Moab. Moses begins with a review of what the people have seen over the forty years of wilderness wandering: the signs and wonders in Egypt and in the wilderness, the great ordeals their own eyes have seen. But to this day, the Lord has not given you a mind to understand, or eyes to see, or ears to hear. The observation is sharp: the events were real, the witnessing was real, but comprehension has not followed. The chapter warns against any individual or tribe who turns away to serve other gods — against the person who hears the words of this covenant and blesses himself in his heart, thinking, I will be safe though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart. The Lord will not spare such a person. The chapter closes with the famous verse: The secret things belong to the Lord our God, but the revealed things belong to us and to our children forever, so that we may follow all the words of this law.

Deuteronomy 30

Choose Life

Chapter 30 is the rhetorical climax of Deuteronomy. It begins, unusually, after the curses have been described — after the exile has already been assumed as a future reality. When all these things have come upon you — the blessings and the curses — and you take them to heart among all the nations where the Lord your God has driven you, and return to the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul, then the Lord your God will restore your fortunes and have compassion on you. He will bring you back. He will circumcise your heart and the hearts of your descendants, so that you will love the Lord with all your heart and with all your soul, so that you may live. This is the theological surprise: the circumcision of the heart — the deep change that the earlier chapters demanded as a human achievement — will be done by God himself. The chapter then makes the accessibility argument: the commandment is not too hard, not too far away, not in heaven, not beyond the sea. The word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart. And then: I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. Choose life.

Deuteronomy 31

Joshua Commissioned, the Law Deposited

Chapter 31 is the first of the transition chapters, moving from Moses's speeches to the events of his last day. He announces to all Israel: I am now one hundred and twenty years old. I am no longer able to come and go — the phrase may mean he is simply at the end of his time, not physically incapacitated, since verse 2 of his famous undimmed eyes and unabated vigor will come later. The Lord will cross before you; Joshua will cross before you. Be strong and courageous. Moses then commissions Joshua publicly in front of all Israel, repeating the phrase be strong and courageous three times — a phrase that will become Joshua's defining charge. He writes the law and gives it to the Levitical priests who carry the ark and to all the elders, commanding that it be read publicly every seven years at the Festival of Booths. The Lord then appears to Moses and Joshua at the tent of meeting and announces that after Moses's death, the people will break the covenant. God therefore commands Moses to write down a song and teach it to the people — a song that will stand as a witness against them when they have forgotten. Moses writes the song the same day and teaches it to Israel.

Deuteronomy 32

The Song of Moses

Chapter 32 is the Song of Moses — the longest and most formally elaborate poem in the Pentateuch, occupying the entire chapter and written in a distinctive two-stress-per-half-line ancient verse form. Moses calls heaven and earth as witnesses — legal language, calling the cosmos to court — and declares that he will proclaim the name of the Lord. The song's first movement praises God as the Rock, whose work is perfect and whose ways are just, against a faithless and crooked generation. The second movement is historical: God found Israel in a howling wilderness, protected and cared for them like an eagle stirring its nest, gave them the fat of the land. But they grew fat and kicked; they abandoned the God who made them. They stirred his jealousy with false gods and provoked his anger with idols. God's anger blazes: I will heap disasters on them; I will make my arrows drunk with blood. But then a turn: God will not allow the enemies to claim the victory; lest they say, our own hand has triumphed. God will vindicate his people when their power is gone. He will say: See now that I, I am he; there is no god beside me. I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal. Vengeance is mine. In the end the nations will join Israel in shouting for joy, for the Lord will avenge the blood of his servants.

Deuteronomy 33

Moses's Blessing of the Tribes

Chapter 33 is Moses's farewell blessing of the twelve tribes — parallel in form and function to Jacob's blessing in Genesis 49, though the content and the emphasis differ substantially. The poem opens with a theophany: the Lord came from Sinai; he shone forth from Seir; he appeared from Paran; he came with ten thousands of holy ones. The frame establishes that what follows is not merely patriarchal sentiment but a divine word spoken through Moses. The tribes are then blessed individually, with varying lengths and degrees of clarity — the text contains some of the most difficult Hebrew in the Pentateuch, with several phrases that have been translated in radically different ways by different scholars and versions. Reuben: may he live and not die. Judah: bring him to his people; his hands contend for him against his enemies. Levi: the priestly tribe, charged with the Thummim and Urim, the oracular objects. Benjamin: the beloved of the Lord, who dwells between his shoulders. Joseph: blessed with the finest gifts of heaven and earth. Zebulun and Issachar: rejoice in going out and sitting in tents. The poem closes with a general blessing for all Israel: there is no one like the God of Jeshurun, who rides through the heavens to your help, who is your shield and your sword.

Deuteronomy 34

Moses Dies on Mount Nebo

Chapter 34 is the final chapter of the Pentateuch, and it is eight verses. Moses climbs from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, which is opposite Jericho. The Lord shows him the whole land — Gilead as far as Dan, all of Naphtali, the land of Ephraim and Manasseh, all the land of Judah as far as the western sea, the Negeb, and the plain of Jericho, the city of palm trees, as far as Zoar. The panorama is a completion of the promise: this is the land I swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob. I will give it to your descendants. You have seen it with your eyes, but you shall not cross over there. Then Moses dies, at the Lord's command, in the land of Moab. The Lord buries him in a valley in the land of Moab, opposite Beth-peor, but no one knows his burial place to this day. He was a hundred and twenty years old at his death. His eyes were not dim and his vigor had not abated. Israel weeps for him thirty days. Then the text turns to Joshua, who is full of the spirit of wisdom because Moses had laid hands on him. And the Pentateuch closes: there has never since arisen in Israel a prophet like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Shema and the Oneness of God

Deuteronomy 6:4 is the verse Jewish children have been taught first for two and a half thousand years. Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one. Six Hebrew words, and they have been the central confession of Jewish faith ever since.

The Law Repeated and Refined

The Greek name of the book — Deuteronomy, second law — captures something true. The legal material in chapters 12–26 is not new law but the retelling of the law from Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers, with significant humanitarian developments.

The Land and the Memory of Slavery

Deuteronomy is obsessed with the land. Almost every passage connects what is being commanded to the land you are about to enter. And woven through every appeal is a counter-memory: you yourselves were slaves in Egypt.

Love as Covenant Bond

The most theologically distinctive feature of Deuteronomy is its language of love. You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart. The verb is ahav, the same word used for love between human beings, including erotic love.

Choose Life — The Closing Peroration

Deuteronomy 30 gathers everything into a final appeal. Moses has laid out the blessings and the curses. He has rehearsed the history. Now he names what is in front of the people: life and death, blessing and curse.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Moses
Prophet and Lawgiver

At the end of his life. One hundred and twenty years old, his eyes still undimmed and his vigour unabated, the text says — but the time is over. He has led the people out of Egypt, through the wilderness for forty years, almost to the Jordan. He himself will not cross. The book is, in its setting, his final speech: three long discourses delivered on the plains of Moab in the last weeks of his life. He commissions Joshua, writes the law in a book, blesses the tribes, climbs Mount Nebo, sees the land from a distance, and dies. No one knows his burial place to this day.

Joshua
Moses's Successor

Son of Nun, of the tribe of Ephraim, Moses's assistant since the wilderness years. He was one of the twelve spies sent from Kadesh into Canaan, and one of only two — with Caleb — who came back urging the people to go up. In Deuteronomy he is publicly commissioned as the next leader: be strong and of good courage, for you shall go with this people into the land. The book that bears his name picks up the story across the Jordan.

The People of Israel
Audience and Covenant Partner

The people standing on the plains of Moab listening to Moses. Most are the children or grandchildren of the generation that came out of Egypt — the original generation has died in the wilderness. They are about to cross the Jordan and enter a land most of them have never seen. Deuteronomy addresses them in the second person singular — not you all but you, the individual reader — with such consistency that the rhetoric reaches across three thousand years.

The Stranger / The Widow / The Orphan
Protected Classes

The recurring trio in Deuteronomy's social legislation — the ger, the almanah, the yatom. The stranger is the resident alien, without full property rights or kin protection. The widow is the woman without economic support. The orphan is the child without a father. Together they are the legal categories most exposed to exploitation in an ancient agricultural society, and Deuteronomy returns to them at every juncture. The reason it gives is always the same: you yourselves were strangers in the land of Egypt.

The King (anticipated)
Future Constitutional Office

Israel does not yet have a king when Moses speaks, and will not for centuries, but Deuteronomy 17 anticipates the office and writes its constitution. The king must be a fellow Israelite. He shall not multiply horses, nor cause the people to return to Egypt. He shall not multiply wives, lest his heart turn away. He shall not greatly multiply silver and gold. And when he sits on the throne, he shall write for himself a copy of this law and read in it all the days of his life. The king is bound by the law. Solomon, when he comes, will violate every prohibition in the list.

The Levites
Tribe of Priests

The tribe set apart for the service of the sanctuary, who receive no land of their own when the land is divided. Deuteronomy is particularly attentive to their economic vulnerability: they have no inheritance with their brothers, the text says again and again — the Lord is their inheritance — and the practical implication is that they depend on the offerings, tithes, and gifts of the rest of Israel. They are also the keepers of the law: Moses gives the written Torah to the priests and elders to be read aloud to the people every seven years at the Festival of Booths.
