The Imitation of Christ

1418 CE · by Thomas à Kempis

The book in brief

The Imitation of Christ is a manual for the inner Christian life, written in plain Latin for monks and lay readers in the early fifteenth century. It does not argue. It does not narrate. It instructs. Book I gives counsel against the vanity of learning, the love of being seen, the readiness to judge other people. Book II turns inward to the cultivation of the soul — purity of heart, contempt of self, the bearing of difficulties. Book III is the longest and strangest: a sustained dialogue in which Christ speaks directly to the soul of the reader, addressing the soul as "my son" through more than fifty chapters. Book IV is a treatise on the Eucharist. The whole runs to about 200 pages of short chapters and shorter sentences, structured to be read a paragraph at a time and returned to.

It was written in the Low Countries between roughly 1418 and 1427 and is traditionally attributed to Thomas à Kempis, an Augustinian canon at the monastery of Mount St Agnes near Zwolle, in what is now the Netherlands. He spent most of his ninety-one years there copying manuscripts, including the Bible at least four times by hand, and writing books of devotion in the tradition of the Brethren of the Common Life. The book circulated anonymously for decades and the question of authorship was disputed for centuries; the consensus now identifies him as the author of all four books.

After the Bible it has been the most widely printed Christian book in history. There are over 2,000 editions in Latin and translations into more than fifty languages. Thomas More carried it with him to the Tower. Ignatius of Loyola made it required daily reading at the Society of Jesus. John Wesley translated it as a young man and credited it with his early conversion. Thomas Merton named his autobiography after a phrase in it. The book is short enough to finish in an afternoon, but it is meant to be carried for years. It rewards slow reading the way most other books reward fast reading, and the difference is the whole argument.

Why it still matters

A Devotional Manual That Lasted

The Imitation has been continuously read for six centuries. It has gone through more than 2,000 Latin editions and has been translated into more than fifty languages. Most devotional literature of the early fifteenth century is now read only by specialists. This book stayed because what it says about the inner life kept finding readers in circumstances Kempis never anticipated. Thomas More carried it with him into the Tower of London while awaiting execution. Ignatius of Loyola made it the daily reading of every Jesuit. John Wesley translated it at twenty-two and credited his early conversion to it. Dag Hammarskjöld kept a copy in his briefcase as Secretary-General of the UN. The same handful of short chapters keeps finding new readers in conditions Kempis could not have imagined.

The Inner Christian Life

The Imitation is not interested in theology, controversy, or the institutional politics of the late medieval church — even though Kempis was writing inside the Western Schism, with three claimants to the papacy in living memory. The book passes over all of it. Its single subject is what it is like, day by day, to try to live a Christian life inwardly: the small recurring failures of patience, the persistent return of vanity, the difficulty of bearing a minor humiliation, the easy rationalizations of self-love. The specialty is psychological rather than doctrinal. That is part of why the book outlived the controversies of its own century and still reads as if written for the reader's particular weaknesses.

Brief, Read Slowly

The chapters of the Imitation are short — usually a single page, sometimes less. The sentences are aphoristic, often biblical in cadence. The book is structured to be carried, opened at random, and returned to. It is meant to be read a paragraph at a time and re-read until the words begin to operate on the reader rather than the reader on them. The form is the argument. A book about the patient cultivation of the inner life had to take a form that resisted being finished. Most of what Kempis criticized in the learned men of his time was their habit of moving quickly through too many books. The Imitation refuses that mode. Modern readers who race it lose almost everything.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Practical philosophy for daily life.

Strip away the theology and this is a manual for a certain kind of attention — to oneself, to one's habits, to the gap between what one says and what one does. I want to test whether Kempis's advice still works.

The contempt for learning.

Kempis opens by saying that knowing about humility is worth less than being humble. He had clearly read everything and chose to say scholarship is vanity. I want to understand that argument on its own terms.

Humility as a discipline, not a virtue.

Kempis doesn't ask you to feel humble — he asks you to act humbly until it becomes your character. I want to understand what that kind of training does to a person, and whether it is wisdom or defeat.

Liberation or self-erasure.

Kempis asks for total surrender — of ambition, of comfort, of the desire to be seen. I want to read carefully and decide whether this is genuine freedom or an elaborate way of making yourself small.

Key figures

Thomas à Kempis
The Hidden Author

An Augustinian canon at the monastery of Mount St Agnes near Zwolle, where he lived from roughly 1399 to his death in 1471 at ninety-one. He copied manuscripts, including the entire Bible by hand at least four times, preached, served as novice master, and wrote in the tradition of the Brethren of the Common Life. He published nothing in his own name. The Imitation circulated anonymously for decades, and the question of who wrote it was contested for centuries before the consensus settled on him.

The Soul
The Reader Addressed Directly

Kempis writes in the second person almost throughout. Christ in Book III addresses the soul as "my son." The reader is placed inside the text rather than at a critical distance from it. You are the student. You are the one whose pride keeps reasserting itself. You are the one who keeps returning to the same failures. This grammatical decision is most of what makes the book feel so unnervingly direct compared to almost any other devotional text.

Christ
The Voice in Book Three

In Book III — fifty-nine chapters, the longest of the four — Christ speaks directly to the soul in a sustained dialogue. The book moves from instruction in the third person to conversation in the second. The voice is gentle, unrelenting, and refuses to leave the soul any place to hide. Nothing else in late medieval literature sounds quite like it. Ignatius of Loyola read this section of the Imitation every day for the last decade of his life.

The World
What Must Be Renounced

Not the physical world Kempis lived in but the social one — reputation, status, the opinions of other people, the desire to be admired, the readiness to compare oneself with peers. Kempis returns to this opponent again and again. He treats it as the most persistent enemy of the inner life because it is the one that disguises itself best as legitimate ambition. The world is never fully defeated in the book. Only watched, named, and refused one more time.

The Learned Man
Kempis's Foil

The recurring negative figure of the Imitation. The erudite man who knows the categories of virtue but does not practice them. The cleric who can dispute about the Trinity but cannot bear a small discourtesy. The scholar who has read everything and changed nothing. Kempis spent his life among such men in the monastic schools, and the figure may be partly self-portrait — what he feared becoming if he let scholarship do the work that interior life was supposed to do.

The Humble Peasant
Kempis's Hero

Knows nothing, fears God, does his duty, and goes home. Kempis sets him repeatedly against the scholar and finds him closer to God. The argument is not anti-intellectual. It is about what knowledge is finally for, and whether unused knowledge is a kind of vanity. The peasant has nothing to defend, nothing to be seen as, nothing to perform. Kempis treats this poverty of inner equipment as itself a form of clarity. The figure descends from the gospel beatitudes and points forward to figures like Tolstoy's Platon Karataev.
