US Founding Documents

1776 CE · by Jefferson, Madison, Hamilton & others

The book in brief

The United States Founding Documents collect the four texts that established the political and legal foundation of the United States — the Declaration of Independence (1776), the Articles of Confederation (1781), the Constitution (1787), and the Bill of Rights and subsequent amendments (1791 onward). The Declaration argues that legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed and that revolution is justified when government becomes destructive of the rights it was instituted to protect. The Constitution constructs a federal republic with enumerated powers, a separation of branches, and a system of checks designed to prevent the accumulation of authority in any one place. The Bill of Rights and the later amendments mark the points where the original design was found insufficient and was deliberately changed.

The Declaration was drafted in June 1776 by a committee in which Jefferson did most of the writing, signed by men who knew they were committing treason against the British crown. The Articles of Confederation, ratified in 1781, demonstrated within a few years that a national government without taxing power could not function. The Constitution was drafted at Philadelphia in 1787 by fifty-five delegates working in secret, ratified after a yearlong argument, and supplemented by ten amendments in 1791 to answer Anti-Federalist objections. The Civil War amendments remade the document in the 1860s. Subsequent amendments through 1992 extended the logic further.

The documents are short, plainly written, and unusually durable. The Constitution is among the briefest founding charters of any nation; its brevity is the point. Together the four texts record an experiment: the deliberate construction of a federal republic on Enlightenment principles, by men aware that no government had been built this way before. The question the documents pose — whether a society can build legitimate government from reflection and choice rather than accident and force — is the question that has organized American political life for two and a half centuries.

Why it still matters

A Government Built on a Page

The United States Constitution is one of the shortest founding documents of any nation. Its brevity is the point: a few enumerated powers, a structure of checks, and a procedure for amendment that allows the document to evolve without being rewritten. Every word was argued over. Madison's notes from the Philadelphia Convention show clauses tightened, removed, and put back across four months of debate. The framers chose to specify what the federal government could do and to leave the rest to the states or the people. The choice has shaped two centuries of American constitutional argument: when later generations have wanted the federal government to do something not enumerated, they have had to amend, reinterpret, or stretch the original text. Reading the document shows how much can be done in a small number of pages and how much was deliberately left out.

The Declaration as Argument

The Declaration of Independence is a piece of moral and political reasoning before it is a list of grievances. Jefferson moves from claims about human nature — that all men are created equal, endowed with certain unalienable rights — through claims about the purpose of government, to claims about when government may rightfully be replaced, and only then to the specific accusations against George III. The argument structure is borrowed from John Locke, but the rhetorical force is Jefferson's. Generations of subsequent movements — the abolitionists, the women's rights conventions of Seneca Falls, the civil rights movement — have invoked the Declaration's premises against the country's actual practice. The document has been, in Lincoln's phrase, an apple of gold in a frame of silver: the moral argument the Constitution was supposed to enact, and the standard by which the Constitution itself is judged.

The Bill of Rights as Backstop

The first ten amendments were added because the Constitution as ratified did not adequately protect individual rights against the federal government. The Anti-Federalists had pressed the point through the ratification debates, refusing to ratify in several states without a written guarantee; Madison drafted the amendments to answer them, and the states adopted ten in 1791. The Bill of Rights is the founding generation's admission that the structure they had built was not enough on its own — that government even of consenting people needs limits beyond the procedural ones the Constitution provided. The arguments behind those amendments are still being relitigated in nearly every Supreme Court term. The First Amendment's free speech and religion clauses, the Second Amendment's right to bear arms, the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches, the Fifth and Sixth Amendments' procedural guarantees: these are not settled questions in American law. They are the working questions, and they trace back to a single document drafted to fix the original.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The Declaration’s argument structure.

Most people know the famous lines but miss the architecture: philosophical premises, then a list of specific injuries as evidence, then the conclusion. Jefferson is making a legal case, not a speech. Track how the argument builds — and whether the grievances actually support the philosophical claims that precede them.

The Constitution’s institutional design.

Separation of powers, federalism, checks and balances — these are not abstractions but specific engineering choices. The framers had read about every republic that had ever collapsed, and they built against those failure modes. As you read, ask: what problem is each clause solving? What would happen if this particular safeguard were absent?

What the Bill of Rights protects — and what it doesn’t.

The first ten amendments protect speech, religion, arms, due process, jury trial, and more. But notice what is absent: no right to education, no economic rights, no right to housing or healthcare. The framers chose to protect procedural rights against government overreach rather than substantive entitlements. That choice still shapes every major American policy debate.

The Civil War amendments and the second founding.

The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments did not just add rights — they rewrote the constitutional order. They abolished slavery, made citizenship national rather than state-level, guaranteed equal protection, and extended the vote to Black men. This was a second founding, carried out through the amendment process the framers had built. The later amendments (women’s suffrage, presidential term limits, voting age) continue the same logic.

Key figures

Thomas Jefferson
THE WRITER

Principal author of the Declaration of Independence. A thirty-three-year-old Virginia delegate to the Second Continental Congress in 1776, Jefferson distilled Enlightenment political philosophy — Locke, Montesquieu, the Scottish moralists — into a document that could justify revolution to a skeptical world. His tension with his own ideals, as an enslaver who wrote that all men are created equal, runs through everything that follows. He would later become the third President and the architect of the Louisiana Purchase.

James Madison
THE ARCHITECT

The Constitution's principal designer and the primary author of the Bill of Rights. A Virginia planter and constitutional scholar who had spent the years before Philadelphia studying every republic in history, Madison was convinced that the great danger was faction — organized groups capturing government for their own ends. His answer was structural: pit ambition against ambition, divide power, let the design do what human virtue could not. He would become the fourth President and is the figure most scholars credit with the Constitution's actual architecture.

Alexander Hamilton
THE DEFENDER

Co-author of the Federalist Papers and the most forceful advocate for the Constitution's ratification. Hamilton argued that a strong central government was not a threat to liberty but its precondition — that a weak union would fracture, invite foreign intervention, and produce the very tyranny the framers feared. As the first Secretary of the Treasury, he built the financial institutions that allowed the federal government to function. His vision of executive energy still shapes American government two centuries later.

George Washington
THE PRECEDENT

Presided over the Constitutional Convention in 1787 and became the first President under the document the Convention produced. Washington's presence gave the proceedings legitimacy — every delegate knew he would be the first executive under whatever they wrote, and that he would not abuse the office. His decisions to refuse a third term and to surrender power voluntarily set precedents that no constitutional clause could have enforced. The American presidency is partly a constitutional office and partly the institutional shadow of one man's restraint.

John Adams
THE ADVOCATE

The most relentless advocate for independence in the Second Continental Congress, who pushed Jefferson to draft the Declaration and pushed the Congress to adopt it. A Massachusetts lawyer who had defended the British soldiers of the Boston Massacre on principle, Adams brought to the founding a combination of legal rigor and republican conviction that few of his contemporaries matched. He served as the second President. The Massachusetts state constitution, which he largely wrote, shaped the federal document.

King George III
THE GRIEVANCE

The British monarch the Declaration of Independence was written against. The bulk of the Declaration is a list of charges against him — that he had refused his assent to laws, dissolved colonial assemblies, kept standing armies in peacetime, imposed taxes without consent. Whether the historical George deserved each specific charge is debated. His function in the document is clear: he embodies the kind of authority the new republic was being constructed against, and defines what legitimate government, on the framers' account, is not.
