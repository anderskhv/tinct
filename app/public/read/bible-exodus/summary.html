Exodus a guided tour

c. 5th c. BCE final form · Hebrew Bible · Torah

A people in slavery. A God who hears them. A reluctant deliverer. Ten plagues that break the most powerful empire on earth. A sea that opens. A mountain that smokes. And a covenant law that arrives in fire and stone.

The book in brief

Exodus tells the story that has anchored the political imagination of the West for three thousand years. A people enslaved in Egypt multiply faster than Pharaoh can suppress them; their cry rises to a God who remembers a covenant with their ancestor Abraham; a reluctant deliverer named Moses is conscripted from the wilderness; and through ten plagues, a crossing of the sea, and an encounter at a smoking mountain, the slaves become a people under law. The book does not pretend these events were easy or the people who experienced them were good. By chapter 32 they have already broken the covenant they have just received, dancing around a gold calf while Moses is still on the mountain with the stone tablets.

Exodus reaches its present form in the fifth or sixth century BCE, woven from older sources — the Yahwist, Elohist, and Priestly traditions, joined and reworked by editors working in the period when Israel had lost its land and its temple and was urgently asking what it meant that the God who had once led them out of slavery now let them be carried into exile. Whether the historical exodus happened on the scale the book describes is a matter of ongoing archaeological debate. What is not debated is that the text, as literary and theological monument, shaped everything after it: the Passover liturgy, the Decalogue, the legal codes of the ancient Near East and eventually the West, the structural model for Christian readings of the cross, and the master story of liberation movements from the Puritan settlers to Martin Luther King. Read it for the story; read it for the law; read it because almost every later moral imagination of revolution and freedom is, somewhere underneath, an argument with this book.

Chapter 1 of 40
Chapter 1

A New Pharaoh, an old fear

Exodus opens where Genesis ended but a generation has passed. The seventy descendants of Jacob have multiplied until the land is full of them. A new pharaoh, unnamed and unimpressed by the memory of Joseph, declares the Hebrews a national security threat and sets taskmasters over them to break them with labour. The harder they are worked, the more they multiply. Pharaoh escalates: he orders the two Hebrew midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, to kill every boy born to a Hebrew woman on the delivery stool. The midwives fear God more than Pharaoh and let the boys live, explaining to Pharaoh that Hebrew women give birth before the midwife arrives. God rewards them with households of their own. Pharaoh's final answer is a standing order to every Egyptian: throw every Hebrew boy into the Nile. The chapter sets the conditions for everything that follows — the oppression is total, the regime is murderous, and the people are multiplying anyway. The empire has decided that the existence of this people is a problem it must solve.

Chapter 2

The basket, the well, the cry

A Levite woman gives birth to a boy and hides him three months. When she can hide him no longer she makes a waterproof basket of reeds, places him in it, and sets it among the reeds at the edge of the Nile. His sister watches from a distance. Pharaoh's daughter comes to bathe, sees the basket, has it brought to her, sees the crying child, and has compassion. The sister materialises and offers to find a Hebrew wet-nurse. Pharaoh's daughter agrees. The nurse, of course, is the child's own mother. When he is old enough, he goes to Pharaoh's daughter and becomes her son. She names him Moses — she drew him out of the water. Grown, Moses goes out to see his people labouring and watches an Egyptian beating a Hebrew. He kills the Egyptian and buries him in the sand. The next day he tries to separate two Hebrews fighting; one of them turns on him: who made you a prince and a judge over us — do you mean to kill me as you killed the Egyptian? Moses flees to Midian, sits by a well, defends Jethro's daughters from rough shepherds, is taken in, and marries Zipporah, who bears him a son he names Gershom — a stranger there. The chapter ends with the Israelites still groaning, their cry rising to God, and God hearing.

Chapter 3

The burning bush and the Name

Moses is keeping his father-in-law Jethro's flock in the wilderness of Midian when he leads them to Horeb, the mountain of God. He sees a thorn bush burning but not consumed. He turns aside to look. God calls from within the bush: Moses, Moses — here I am. Take off your sandals; the ground you are standing on is holy. Then God identifies himself: I am the God of your father, of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Moses hides his face because he is afraid to look at God. God tells him he has seen the affliction of his people in Egypt, heard their cry, knows their suffering, and has come down to deliver them and bring them to a good land flowing with milk and honey — the land of the Canaanites. Moses is to go to Pharaoh and bring the people out. Moses objects immediately: who am I to do this? God's answer is not a credential: I will be with you, and as a sign, when you have brought the people out you will worship on this mountain. Moses asks a harder question: if the people ask the name of the God who has sent me, what shall I say? God answers: ehyeh asher ehyeh — I am who I am, or I will be what I will be. Tell them I Am has sent you. Then the familiar name: the Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. God sketches the plan: Moses will gather the elders, they will go to Pharaoh, Pharaoh will refuse, God will strike Egypt, and eventually Pharaoh will let them go.

Chapter 4

Signs, reluctance, and the road back

Moses is still at Horeb, still pushing back. He raises four more objections. They will not believe me — God gives him two signs: his staff becomes a serpent; his hand becomes leprous and is healed; a third sign in reserve, water from the Nile becoming blood. Moses objects again: I am slow of speech, slow of tongue. God answers: who made the human mouth? I will be with your mouth. Moses asks God to send someone else. God is angry, but concedes: Aaron your brother can speak well. He is already on his way to meet you. You will speak to him; he will speak to the people; you will carry the staff. Moses asks Jethro's leave to return to Egypt. Jethro agrees. God tells Moses on the road that all the men who sought his life are now dead. Moses takes his wife and sons, mounts them on a donkey, and sets out with the staff of God. God gives him precise instructions: perform all the signs before Pharaoh, warn him that Israel is God's firstborn son, and tell him: let my firstborn go, or I will kill your firstborn. Then the strange scene: at a lodging place on the road, God met him and tried to kill him. Zipporah takes a flint, circumcises their son, touches Moses's feet with the foreskin, says twice: you are a bridegroom of blood to me. God lets Moses alone. No explanation is given. Aaron meets Moses at the mountain of God, they embrace, Moses tells Aaron everything, and together they gather the elders of Israel in Egypt. The elders believe. The people bow their heads and worship.

Chapter 5

First audience with Pharaoh

Moses and Aaron go before Pharaoh with their first request: let my people go, that they may hold a festival to me in the wilderness. Pharaoh's response is contemptuous and theologically precise: who is the Lord that I should obey his voice and let Israel go? I do not know the Lord, and I will not let Israel go. He accuses Moses and Aaron of distracting the people from their work and sends them away. That same day Pharaoh issues new orders to the taskmasters and the Israelite foremen: stop supplying straw for the brickmaking. The Israelites must gather their own straw while still meeting the same daily quota of bricks. The foremen are beaten when the quota is not met. They go to Pharaoh and plead. Pharaoh dismisses them — you are idle, you are idle, that is why you are saying, let us go and sacrifice to our God. The Israelite foremen leave Pharaoh's presence and encounter Moses and Aaron waiting for them. They turn on them: you have made us offensive in the eyes of Pharaoh and his servants, and have put a sword in their hand to kill us. Moses goes back to God in distress: Lord, why have you done evil to this people? Why did you ever send me? Since I came to Pharaoh to speak in your name he has done evil to this people, and you have not delivered your people at all.

Chapter 6

God renews the commission

God answers Moses's protest from the end of chapter 5 directly: now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh. By a mighty hand he will let them go. The response continues into what scholars identify as the Priestly source's own version of the call narrative. God announces: I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shaddai — God Almighty — but by my name YHWH I did not make myself known to them. I have established my covenant with them to give them the land of Canaan. I have heard the groaning of the Israelites, I have remembered the covenant. Therefore say to the Israelites: I am the Lord, I will bring you out from under the burden of the Egyptians, I will deliver you from slavery, I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and great acts of judgment, I will take you as my people, I will be your God. Seven first-person promises. Moses reports this to the Israelites; they do not listen, crushed as they are by spirit and hard service. God then gives Moses and Aaron genealogies — the Levitical line traced back to Jacob's son Levi, arriving at Moses and Aaron. Then the commission is renewed a second time: go, tell Pharaoh to let the Israelites go. Moses objects again: I am of uncircumcised lips — how will Pharaoh listen to me?

Chapter 7

Aaron's staff and the first plague

God tells Moses: I have made you like God to Pharaoh, and Aaron your brother shall be your prophet. The roles are clear. God also tells Moses plainly that Pharaoh will not listen: I will harden his heart, and I will multiply my signs and wonders in Egypt. When the Egyptians finally let the people go it will be through an upheaval so complete that they will know I am the Lord. Moses is eighty and Aaron eighty-three when they stand before Pharaoh. Aaron throws his staff before Pharaoh; it becomes a serpent. Pharaoh summons his wise men and sorcerers, who do the same by their secret arts — their staffs become serpents too. But Aaron's staff swallows theirs. Pharaoh's heart is hardened and he does not listen. God sends Moses to the Nile bank in the morning to meet Pharaoh. Aaron strikes the Nile with the staff and the waters turn to blood. The fish die. The river stinks. The Egyptians cannot drink. The magicians replicate this also by their secret arts. Pharaoh walks away, unmoved, and goes into his palace. The Egyptians dig along the river bank for drinking water. The chapter establishes the contest's structure: each plague is a divine act, the magicians can replicate the first two, and Pharaoh's heart remains hard through divine purpose and his own refusal simultaneously.

Chapter 8

Frogs, gnats, flies, and the first negotiation

The second plague: frogs swarm from the Nile and cover the land — beds, ovens, kneading bowls, the people. The magicians replicate frogs. Pharaoh summons Moses and asks him to pray that God remove the frogs; he will let the people go. Moses says: when do you want this? Tomorrow, says Pharaoh. Moses prays. The frogs die everywhere except in the river. Pharaoh sees there is relief, hardens his heart, and does not listen. The third plague: Aaron strikes the dust and gnats cover humans and animals throughout Egypt. The magicians try to replicate gnats and cannot. This is the finger of God, they tell Pharaoh. Pharaoh does not listen. The fourth plague is flies — dense swarms of flies covering all the land of Egypt, except the land of Goshen where the Israelites live. God makes a distinction between his people and Pharaoh's people. Pharaoh calls Moses and Aaron: go, sacrifice to your God within the land. Moses refuses — the Hebrews' sacrifices would be offensive to Egyptians and they might stone them. Pharaoh concedes: go into the wilderness, but not too far. Moses prays. The flies leave. Pharaoh hardens his heart and does not let the people go.

Chapter 9

Livestock, boils, and devastating hail

Plague five: disease strikes all Egyptian livestock — horses, donkeys, camels, herds, and flocks — but none of the Israelites' animals are touched. Pharaoh sends to investigate: not one of Israel's animals has died. His heart is hardened. Plague six: Moses and Aaron throw soot from a furnace into the air before Pharaoh. It becomes boils on humans and animals throughout Egypt. The magicians cannot stand before Moses because they too are covered in boils — they disappear from the narrative and do not reappear. Plague seven: God tells Moses to warn Pharaoh that tomorrow he will send a hail unlike any Egypt has seen since its founding. Those among Pharaoh's officials who fear the word of the Lord bring their slaves and livestock inside. Those who do not leave them out. Hail comes, heavy hail mixed with fire — the text uses lightning — that kills everything left in the field: people, animals, crops, trees. The land of Goshen has no hail. Pharaoh summons Moses: I have sinned; the Lord is righteous; my people are wicked; pray to God, and I will let you go. Moses goes out to the city and spreads his hands; the thunder and hail cease. Pharaoh sees the cessation. He and his officials harden their hearts. He does not let the people go.

Chapter 10

Locusts, darkness, and Pharaoh's last offer

God tells Moses that the hardening of Pharaoh's heart has a purpose: so that you may tell your children and grandchildren what I have done in Egypt, and know that I am the Lord. The plague of locusts comes after a final warning. Pharaoh's officials now appeal to Pharaoh: let the men go. How long will this man be a snare to us? Egypt is ruined. Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron: who exactly will go? Moses: we will go with our young and old, our sons and daughters, our flocks and herds. Pharaoh responds: the Lord be with you, if I ever let you go with your little ones — clearly you have evil intent. Go, the men only. He drives them out. Locusts come on an east wind, cover the face of the whole land, eat everything the hail left. Pharaoh summons Moses: I have sinned against the Lord your God and against you; forgive my sin just this once and pray to God to take this deadly thing from me. Moses prays; a west wind blows the locusts into the Red Sea. Pharaoh hardens his heart. Plague nine: Moses stretches his hand toward the sky and a darkness so thick it can be felt covers Egypt three days. No one can see anyone. But all the Israelites have light in their homes. Pharaoh's last offer: go, including your children, but leave your flocks. Moses refuses: our animals must go. Not one hoof can be left behind. We will not know what to worship until we arrive. Pharaoh: get away from me. The day you see my face you will die. Moses: you have spoken correctly; I will not see your face again.

Chapter 11

The announcement of the final plague

God tells Moses that one more plague is coming and that after it Pharaoh will drive them out of Egypt entirely. Moses is to tell the people to ask their Egyptian neighbours for silver and gold jewellery. God gives the people favour in the sight of the Egyptians, and Moses is regarded as very great by Pharaoh's officials. Moses then delivers to Pharaoh — in the same audience that ended with their parting in chapter 10, the narrator clarifying the sequence — the announcement: at midnight I will go through Egypt, and every firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die, from the firstborn of Pharaoh sitting on his throne to the firstborn of the slave girl behind the handmill, to the firstborn of the cattle. There shall be a great cry throughout Egypt such as there has not been and never will be again. But among the Israelites not even a dog shall growl — so that you may know that the Lord makes a distinction between Egypt and Israel. All these your officials shall come to me, bow down, and say: leave, you and all the people who follow you. Then I will go. Moses goes out from Pharaoh in hot anger. The chapter is brief; it is an announcement, not a narration. The death has not happened yet. What follows in chapter 12 is the institution of the Passover, the instructions given to the people before the plague falls.

Chapter 12

The Passover and the departure

God institutes the Passover in precise liturgical detail. This month — the first month of the year for Israel from this moment forward — on the tenth day each household is to take a lamb without blemish. On the fourteenth day at twilight, they slaughter it, put the blood on the two doorposts and the lintel, roast the whole animal with fire, eat it with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, staff in hand, sandals on feet, in haste. It is the Lord's Passover. The blood will be a sign; God will see it and pass over, and the plague will not come on those houses. This is a statute forever; keep it as a feast. Unleavened bread is to be eaten for seven days; any house with leaven in it is cut off. The people bow and worship. They do as they were commanded. At midnight God strikes every firstborn in Egypt — from Pharaoh's firstborn to the prisoner's in the pit to the cattle. A great cry rises throughout Egypt. Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron in the night: rise up, go, take your flocks and herds as you said, and go and bless me also. The Egyptians urge the people out, giving them silver and gold jewellery and clothing when asked. The Israelites travel from Rameses toward Succoth — about six hundred thousand men on foot, plus women and children. A mixed multitude goes with them. They bake unleavened cakes from the dough they brought out. They have been in Egypt four hundred and thirty years to the day. The chapter closes with Passover regulations: it is for Israelites only; no foreigner uncircumcised may eat of it; but any slave or foreigner circumcised and joined to Israel may participate.

Chapter 13

Firstborn, unleavened bread, the pillar of cloud

God speaks to Moses: consecrate to me every firstborn, the first of the womb among the Israelites, human and animal alike — they are mine. The unleavened bread feast is mandated for seven days in the month of Abib, when the people enter the promised land, so that the people remember the day God brought them out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. When your son asks what this means, you will tell him. Moses tells the people: remember this day — today you came out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery, with a strong hand the Lord brought you out. God does not lead them by the short route through Philistine territory — the people might lose heart if they faced war immediately and return to Egypt. Instead he leads them through the wilderness toward the Red Sea. They take Joseph's bones with them, because Joseph had made the Israelites swear an oath — when God visits you, carry my bones from here with you. They camp at Etham, on the edge of the wilderness. God goes before them in a pillar of cloud by day to lead them on the way, and a pillar of fire by night to give them light, so they can travel day and night. Neither the pillar of cloud by day nor the pillar of fire by night departs from before the people.

Chapter 14

The sea crossing

God tells Moses to turn back and camp by the sea — he will harden Pharaoh's heart so Pharaoh pursues them, and God will gain glory through Pharaoh's army, and the Egyptians will know that he is the Lord. Pharaoh regrets letting his labour force go. He takes six hundred choice chariots and all the chariots of Egypt, overtakes the Israelites camped by the sea. The Israelites, seeing the Egyptians, are terrified and cry out to God. They turn on Moses: were there no graves in Egypt that you brought us out here to die? Moses says: do not be afraid. Stand firm. See the salvation of God which he will work for you today. The Lord will fight for you. You only need to keep still. God tells Moses to stop crying to him and move. Moses stretches his hand over the sea; the Lord drives the sea back by a strong east wind all night, and the waters are divided. The Israelites go into the sea on dry ground with a wall of water on their right and left. The Egyptians pursue. God looks down through the pillar of fire and cloud at the Egyptian army and throws it into panic. He jams their chariot wheels. Let us flee from the Israelites — the Lord is fighting for them against Egypt. God tells Moses to stretch his hand out. The waters return. The entire Egyptian army — horses, chariots, charioteers — is drowned. The Israelites see the Egyptians dead on the seashore. They fear the Lord and believe in him and in his servant Moses.

Chapter 15

The Song of the Sea and bitter water

Then Moses and the Israelites sing to the Lord. I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea. Scholars date the Song of the Sea among the oldest surviving Hebrew poetry. It sings the sea crossing as divine warrior narrative, addresses the moment Egypt hears and trembles, anticipates the conquest of Canaan, and ends: the Lord will reign for ever and ever. Miriam the prophet, Aaron's sister, takes a tambourine and leads all the women in dancing and in her own song: sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; horse and rider he has thrown into the sea. Then the wilderness begins. Moses leads the people from the Red Sea and they go three days into the wilderness of Shur without finding water. They come to Marah; the water is bitter and they cannot drink it. The people murmur against Moses: what shall we drink? Moses cries to God. God shows him a piece of wood; he throws it into the water and the water becomes sweet. There God makes a statute and an ordinance and tests them: if you listen carefully to the voice of the Lord your God and do what is right in his sight and keep all his statutes, I will put none of the diseases I put on Egypt on you. They come to Elim, where there are twelve springs and seventy palm trees, and they camp there by the water.

Chapter 16

Manna and quail in the wilderness

A month and a half out of Egypt, the whole congregation of Israel murmurs against Moses and Aaron in the wilderness of Sin: if only we had died in Egypt when we sat by meat pots and ate our fill of bread. You have brought us out into this wilderness to kill the whole assembly with hunger. God tells Moses: I will rain bread from heaven for you. The people are to gather a day's portion each day — so God can test whether they will keep his instruction. On the sixth day they are to gather twice as much, because the seventh is a sabbath to the Lord; on the seventh there will be none. In the evening quail come and cover the camp. In the morning a fine flaky substance lies on the ground around the camp. The people ask: what is it? — manna in Hebrew is perhaps from man hu, what is it? Moses explains: this is the bread God has given you to eat. Each person is to gather an omer, one per person. Those who gather more and those who gather less each have exactly an omer. Those who keep it until morning find it wormy and rotten. On the sixth day they gather twice as much. Moses tells them: tomorrow is a day of solemn rest, a holy sabbath to the Lord. On the seventh day some go out to gather and find nothing. God is displeased: how long will you refuse to keep my commandments? An omer of manna is to be preserved in a jar before God as a monument for future generations. The Israelites eat manna forty years, until they reach the land of Canaan.

Chapter 17

Water from the rock and the war with Amalek

The congregation of Israel moves to Rephidim and finds no water. The people quarrel with Moses: give us water to drink. Moses answers: why do you quarrel with me? Why do you test the Lord? The people's thirst grows and they murmur against Moses — why have you brought us out of Egypt to kill us and our children and our livestock with thirst? Moses cries to God: what shall I do with this people? They are almost ready to stone me. God tells him to take the elders and his staff and go to the rock at Horeb and strike it; water will come out. Moses does so, naming the place Massah and Meribah — testing and quarrelling — because the Israelites quarrelled and tested the Lord. Then Amalek comes and fights with Israel at Rephidim. Moses tells Joshua to choose men and go out and fight Amalek; tomorrow I will stand on the top of the hill with the staff of God in my hand. When Moses holds up his hand, Israel prevails; when he lets his hand down, Amalek prevails. His arms grow heavy. Aaron and Hur hold them up, one on each side, until the sun sets. Joshua defeats Amalek. God tells Moses to write this as a memorial: I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven. The Lord is my banner, Moses names the altar — the Lord is my banner.

Chapter 18

Jethro's visit and the birth of the judiciary

Jethro, priest of Midian, Moses's father-in-law, hears everything God has done for Moses and Israel — how he brought them out of Egypt. He takes Zipporah and Moses's two sons — Gershom and Eliezer — and comes to Moses in the wilderness where he is camped at the mountain of God. Moses goes out to meet him, bows, kisses him. They go into the tent. Moses tells him everything: what God did to Pharaoh for Israel's sake, all the hardships they had encountered, and how God delivered them. Jethro rejoices: blessed be the Lord who delivered you from the Egyptians. Now I know that the Lord is greater than all gods. He offers a burnt offering and sacrifices to God, and Aaron and all the elders of Israel come to eat bread with Moses's father-in-law before God. The next day Moses sits to judge the people. The people stand before him from morning until evening. Jethro watches and says: what is this you are doing for the people? Why do you sit alone, with all the people standing around you from morning until evening? Moses explains: they come to me to inquire of God. Jethro says: what you are doing is not good. You will wear yourself out, and the people with you. The task is too heavy for you. Listen to me. Choose capable, God-fearing, honest, incorruptible men — set them over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. Let them judge the small cases. Bring the hard ones to you. Moses listens. He does all Jethro says. Then Jethro goes home.

Chapter 19

Israel arrives at Sinai

Three months after leaving Egypt, the Israelites enter the wilderness of Sinai and camp at the foot of the mountain. Moses goes up to God; God calls to him from the mountain. Tell the house of Jacob, the Israelites: you yourselves have seen what I did to Egypt, and how I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself. Now, if you obey me fully and keep my covenant, then out of all nations you will be my treasured possession, a kingdom of priests, a holy nation. Moses brings the words down and the people answer: we will do everything the Lord has said. Moses reports to God. God tells Moses he will come in a dense cloud so the people hear him speaking with Moses and believe in Moses forever. God gives three days of preparation: consecrate the people, wash their clothes, be ready. Do not go up to the mountain or touch its foot; anyone who does will be killed. On the third morning there is thunder, lightning, a thick cloud, the blast of a trumpet so loud the people in the camp tremble. Moses leads the people out to meet God at the foot of the mountain. The whole mountain is covered in smoke because the Lord descended on it in fire. The smoke billows up; the whole mountain trembles violently. The trumpet blast grows louder and louder. Moses speaks and God answers in thunder. God calls Moses to the top of the mountain. Moses goes up. God tells him: go back, warn the people not to break through to see the Lord. Let the priests who approach the Lord consecrate themselves. Moses goes down and tells the people. God calls him back up. He goes back.

Chapter 20

The Ten Commandments

God speaks all these words: I am the Lord your God who brought you out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. Then the ten. You shall have no other gods before me. You shall not make for yourself a carved image, or any likeness of what is in heaven above or on the earth below or in the waters beneath the earth; you shall not bow down to them or serve them — for I, the Lord your God, am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate me, but showing steadfast love to thousands of those who love me and keep my commandments. You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain. Remember the Sabbath day to keep it holy — six days you shall labour, but the seventh is a sabbath to the Lord; for in six days the Lord made heaven and earth and on the seventh day he rested. Honour your father and your mother so your days may be long in the land the Lord gives you. You shall not murder. You shall not commit adultery. You shall not steal. You shall not bear false witness against your neighbour. You shall not covet your neighbour's house, or wife, or servant, or ox, or donkey, or anything that belongs to your neighbour. The people see the thunder, lightning, the smoking mountain, hear the trumpet; they tremble and stand far off. Do not fear — God has come to test you, that the fear of him may be before you so that you do not sin. The people stand far off. Moses draws near to the thick darkness where God is. God speaks further: do not make gods of silver or gold; build me an altar of earth and offer on it; wherever I cause my name to be remembered I will come to you and bless you.

Chapter 21

Slave rights and personal injury law

Exodus 21 opens the Book of the Covenant — the casuistic law code that translates the Decalogue's principles into specific cases. The chapter begins with the Hebrew slave who serves six years and goes free in the seventh; if he loves his master and chooses to stay, his ear is pierced against the door and he remains a slave for life. The female slave who is taken as a concubine has protections: if her master takes another wife, he may not reduce her food, clothing, or conjugal rights, or she goes free without payment. The chapter then moves through capital offences — striking or cursing a parent, kidnapping — and into the talion formula: eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, burn for burn. This is not cruelty; in context it is a cap, a limit on escalation, the replacement of blood feuds with proportionate justice administered by courts. It concludes with the ox that gores — a case study in liability that scholars have traced into Roman, common, and modern legal traditions.

Chapter 22

Property, sexual ethics, and care for the vulnerable

Exodus 22 continues the Book of the Covenant, moving through property disputes — theft of livestock, damage to crops by fire, bailment — into sexual offences and then into what the tradition calls the social ethics laws. A man who seduces a virgin who is not betrothed must pay the bride price and marry her; if her father refuses the marriage, he pays the bride price anyway. Sorcery, bestiality, and sacrificing to other gods are capital offences. Then the chapter shifts register entirely. You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt. You shall not abuse any widow or orphan. If you lend money to my people, to the poor among you, you shall not exact interest. If you take your neighbour's cloak in pledge, you shall return it before sunset, for it is their only covering — it is their cloak for their body. The moral logic of the exodus — we were slaves; therefore — is doing explicit work in the laws about the powerless. The chapter ends with instructions about firstfruits and firstborn offerings.

Chapter 23

Justice, sabbath, and the angel of the way

Exodus 23 closes the Book of the Covenant. It opens with judicial ethics: no false witness, no siding with the majority to pervert justice, no taking a bribe — for a bribe blinds the officials, and subverts the cause of those who are in the right. Even the enemy's ox that has gone astray must be returned. The sabbath applies not only to Israelites but to slaves, resident aliens, and the land itself — the seventh year is a fallow year, and what the land produces during it belongs to the poor and the wild animals. Three pilgrimage festivals are commanded: Unleavened Bread at the exodus anniversary, Harvest at the first fruits of the wheat harvest, and Ingathering at the end of the year. Then a remarkable promise: God will send an angel before them to guard them on the way and bring them to the place he has prepared. If they obey the angel and do not provoke him, God will be an enemy to their enemies. Little by little he will drive out the nations before them — not all at once, so that the land does not become desolate. The covenant code ends in geographical promise.

Chapter 24

Blood, a meal, and the mountain of God

Exodus 24 is the hinge chapter of the book — the moment the legal material is gathered and the covenant is formally enacted. Moses tells the people all the words and ordinances of the Lord, and they answer: all the words that the Lord has spoken we will do. He writes them down, rises early, and builds an altar at the foot of the mountain with twelve pillars for the twelve tribes. Young men offer burnt offerings and sacrifice oxen. Moses takes half the blood and dashes it against the altar. He reads the book of the covenant to the people, who again declare they will do it. Then he throws the other half of the blood on the people: see the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you. It is a ratification ceremony of extraordinary physicality — the text makes no effort to soften the blood. Then the seventy elders of Israel, with Moses and Aaron and Hur and Joshua, go up the mountain. They see the God of Israel. Under his feet was something like a pavement of sapphire stone, like the very heaven for clearness. And they ate and drank. God then calls Moses alone higher up the mountain, and he disappears into the cloud for forty days.

Chapter 25

Instructions for the ark, table, and lampstand

Exodus 25 begins the long central section of the book: six chapters of detailed instructions for the tabernacle and its furnishings. The reason is stated plainly at the outset — have them make me a sanctuary, so that I may dwell among them. The God who freed them from Egypt wants to travel with them, and the tabernacle is how that becomes possible. The chapter instructs the people to bring an offering of gold, silver, bronze, fine linen, goat hair, tanned skins, acacia wood, oil, spices, and gemstones — whatever each person's heart moves them to give. Then come the blueprints: the ark of the covenant (a gold-overlaid acacia chest with carrying poles, topped by a mercy seat flanked by two golden cherubim), the table for the bread of the Presence, and the seven-branched lampstand — the menorah — hammered from a single talent of pure gold. Each item is described in measurements that scholars and craftsmen have been reconstructing for millennia. And I will meet with you there, God says, speaking of the space between the cherubim. The architect of the tabernacle is God; Moses is the builder who receives the plans.

Chapter 26

The tabernacle: curtains, boards, and the veil

Exodus 26 specifies the structure of the tabernacle itself: the curtains of fine linen, the curtains of goat hair, the frames of acacia wood overlaid with gold, the crossbars to hold them, the silver bases, the curtain of blue and purple and crimson yarns and fine linen that divides the holy place from the most holy place, and the screen at the entrance. The measurements create a rectangular structure roughly fourteen metres long and five metres wide and high, divided by the inner veil into two spaces — the outer holy place and the inner most holy place, the Holy of Holies, where the ark sits. The arrangement has been compared by scholars of religious architecture to the concentric zones of approach in Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Canaanite temples — the innermost space is the divine dwelling, and each successive boundary is a threshold of holiness. What is distinctive about the tabernacle is its portability. This is not a fixed mountain temple. It is a collapsible sanctuary built to travel with a wandering people through a wilderness, and its gold-and-linen opulence is an argument that the God who dwells in it is worth building for with everything you have.

Chapter 27

The altar, the court, and the oil

Exodus 27 completes the tabernacle complex by describing the altar of burnt offering and the courtyard that surrounds the sanctuary. The altar is five cubits square and three cubits high, made of acacia wood overlaid with bronze — not gold, since it is an outer, public space for sacrifice rather than the divine inner chamber. It has horns at its four corners (to which offerings are tied), grating of bronze network beneath the ledge, and bronze rings and carrying poles. The courtyard that encloses the whole complex is one hundred cubits long and fifty cubits wide, defined by linen hangings five cubits high on bronze posts with silver bands and hooks, set in bronze bases. The entrance from the east is a thirty-cubit screen of blue, purple, and crimson yarn and fine linen — a deliberate colour match with the inner veil and the tabernacle curtains, so that the aesthetic of holiness flows from perimeter to centre. The chapter ends with an instruction that may seem incongruous: pure olive oil, beaten, for the lampstand, to keep it burning continually. The light must not go out. From evening to morning, before the Lord, Aaron and his sons shall tend it.

Chapter 28

The priestly garments for Aaron and his sons

Exodus 28 commissions the sacral vestments of the high priest — garments the text explicitly calls glorious and beautiful — and the simpler garments of the ordinary priests. Aaron's vestments are described in careful detail: the ephod of gold, blue, purple, and crimson yarn with two onyx stones on the shoulder-pieces, each engraved with six of the twelve tribal names, so that Aaron bears the names of Israel when he goes before the Lord. The breastpiece — folded fabric set with twelve stones in four rows, each stone engraved with a tribal name — hangs over his heart. The Urim and Thummim, the oracular objects by which priestly decisions are made, are placed inside it. Aaron thus carries the names of Israel on his shoulders and his heart when he enters the sanctuary: a visual theology of priestly intercession, the priest as the people's representative before God. Also described: the blue robe, the fringed tunic, the turban with its gold plate engraved Holy to the Lord, the sash, and the linen undergarments. The ordinary priests wear tunics, sashes, and turbans. The vestments are specified because the priest's clothing is part of the priestly office — the garment and the role are not separable.

Chapter 29

The consecration of Aaron and his sons

Exodus 29 gives detailed instructions for the seven-day ordination ceremony of Aaron and his sons. The rite involves washing them, dressing them in their vestments, anointing Aaron with oil, presenting three animals — a bull for a sin offering and two rams — and performing the following: the bull is slaughtered as an atonement at the altar's base; the first ram is a burnt offering, wholly consumed; the second ram is the ram of ordination, whose blood is applied to the right earlobe, thumb, and toe of Aaron and each of his sons — a gesture connecting hearing, action, and standing with the sacrificial blood. Their garments are also splashed with oil and blood. They eat from the breast of the wave offering and the shoulder of the ram for seven days at the entrance to the tent of meeting. The altar is also consecrated through a week of daily sacrifices. Then the instructions for the daily perpetual burnt offering are given: two lambs every day, one at morning and one at twilight, with grain and oil and wine. There I will meet with the Israelites, God says, and it shall be sanctified by my glory. This is the chapter where the worship system becomes operational.

Chapter 30

Incense altar, census tax, laver, and anointing oil

Exodus 30 adds four elements not covered in the earlier chapters. The altar of incense — a small gold-overlaid acacia-wood altar with horns, set just outside the veil before the ark — is to receive fragrant incense burned by Aaron every morning and evening when he tends the lamps. Its incense offering is perpetual; its coals are used once a year for the atonement rite in the most holy place. The chapter then institutes the half-shekel census tax: every male twenty years and above gives a fixed contribution to the sanctuary, rich and poor alike — a striking levelling in a text that otherwise distinguishes between economic capacities. The bronze laver, placed between the altar and the tent of meeting for priestly hand-and-foot washing, is required before any approach to the altar — the transition from profane space to holy service requires physical preparation. The chapter closes with formulas for the anointing oil and the sacred incense — specific mixtures of spices and aromatic substances, both declared holy and both forbidden for ordinary use. Whoever makes them for personal use shall be cut off from the people. The sanctuary's sensory world — its smell, its light, its cleanliness — is part of what makes it different.

Chapter 31

Bezalel, Oholiab, and the gift of craft

Exodus 31 is a brief chapter that does two things. First, it names and commissions the craftsmen: Bezalel son of Uri son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah, whom God has filled with divine spirit — ruach elohim — with skill, intelligence, and knowledge in every kind of craft, to devise artistic designs, to work in gold, silver, and bronze, and to cut stones. With him is Oholiab son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan. The naming is significant: the great act of construction is not anonymous labour but the inspired work of specific persons, named and gifted. The filling with divine spirit for craftsmanship is the same language used for prophets and judges — the Hebrew Bible treats artistic skill as a form of divine giftedness, not a merely human competence. The chapter then turns to the Sabbath. All the instructions for the tabernacle's construction have been given; now the reminder: even this work — sacred, commanded — must stop on the seventh day. Whoever does work on the Sabbath shall be put to death. The Sabbath is a sign between God and Israel throughout your generations. The point is that the rest itself is part of the covenant, not an interruption of it.

Chapter 32

The golden calf and Moses's intercession

Exodus 32 is the catastrophe at the centre of the book. Moses has been on the mountain for forty days. The people go to Aaron and demand gods to go before them; Aaron complies, making a golden calf. The people declare: these are your gods, O Israel, who brought you out of Egypt. God tells Moses what is happening below and threatens to consume the people and start over. Moses argues. He invokes the promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He asks what the Egyptians will say. The Lord relents. Moses descends with the tablets, sees the dancing, and in fury smashes them at the foot of the mountain. He grinds the calf, scatters it on water, makes the people drink. He calls for those on the Lord's side; the Levites come. He sends them through the camp with swords: three thousand men fall. Moses then returns to God and makes one of the boldest intercessory speeches in the Hebrew Bible — if you will not forgive their sin, blot me out of the book you have written. God tells Moses he will blot out those who have sinned and sends a plague on the people. The chapter presents the full range of human religious failure and the full range of Moses's greatness: he descends from receiving the law to finding the law already broken.

Chapter 33

The tent of meeting and Moses's prayer to see God

Exodus 33 is the theological and emotional heart of the golden calf aftermath. God tells Moses to lead the people up to the land he promised, but says he himself will not go with them — if I go with you for one moment I will consume you, for you are a stiff-necked people. The people mourn when they hear this. Moses pitches the tent of meeting outside the camp; when he goes there, a pillar of cloud stands at its entrance, and God speaks with Moses face to face, as a man speaks with his friend. Joshua, Moses's young assistant, does not leave the tent. Moses then makes his great demand: show me your glory. He is told that God's goodness will pass before him and God will proclaim his name before him. But you cannot see my face, for no one can see me and live. God will put Moses in a cleft of the rock and cover him with his hand while he passes by, then remove his hand so that Moses sees his back — but my face shall not be seen. The chapter sits in deliberate tension with chapter 24, where the elders saw God and ate before him. The Bible does not resolve the tension; it preserves it, as though the question of whether and how God can be seen is one that the text itself will not close.

Chapter 34

New tablets and the shining face of Moses

Exodus 34 is the covenant renewal after the golden calf catastrophe. Moses cuts two new tablets of stone and goes up the mountain again. God descends in the cloud, stands with Moses, and proclaims his name: The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness, keeping steadfast love for the thousandth generation, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, yet by no means clearing the guilty, but visiting the iniquity of the parents upon the children to the third and fourth generation. This divine self-description — the thirteen attributes of mercy in later Jewish tradition — is one of the most quoted passages in the Hebrew Bible; it is repeated or echoed in Numbers, Nehemiah, Joel, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, and Psalms. It is the name that was withheld at the burning bush, now given in full after the darkest moment of the relationship. A renewed covenant is then given, with warnings against intermarriage with Canaanite peoples and against idol worship. Moses is on the mountain for forty days, neither eating nor drinking. When he descends, his face is shining — the skin radiant from proximity to God — and Aaron and the people are afraid. Moses puts a veil over his face when he speaks with the people, removing it when he goes before the Lord. The veil will be a text 2 Corinthians argues with at length.

Chapter 35

The freewill offering and the call for craftsmen

Exodus 35 begins the long final section of Exodus — chapters 35 through 40 — which narrates the actual construction of the tabernacle, largely by repeating the instructions of chapters 25-31 in the past tense. The chapter opens with Moses relaying the Sabbath law: six days you shall work, but the seventh day shall be holy to you, a Sabbath of complete rest to the Lord — even now, with the tabernacle work about to begin. He then announces the freewill offering: everyone whose heart is stirred is to bring gold, silver, bronze, blue and purple and crimson yarns, fine linen, goat hair, tanned skins, acacia wood, oil, spices, and onyx and other stones. And everyone who is skilled is to come and make all that the Lord has commanded. What follows is one of the Bible's more remarkable collective scenes: men and women, all whose hearts were moved and whose spirits were willing, bring earrings and nose rings and signet rings, all the gold objects they had, and the women spin with their own hands and bring the spun yarn, and the leaders bring the oils and spices and precious stones. They bring far more than enough — more than is needed. The offering is an act of public generosity that the rest of the tabernacle narrative is built upon.

Chapter 36

Bezalel builds: more than enough is brought

Exodus 36 begins the construction narrative. Bezalel, Oholiab, and every skilled person begin work using the materials brought. But the offerings keep coming. Every morning the people bring more. The craftsmen interrupt their work to report to Moses: the people are bringing much more than enough for doing the work that the Lord has commanded. Moses issues a proclamation through the camp — let no man or woman make anything more for the sanctuary offering. The people are restrained from bringing. The materials are sufficient — even more than sufficient. This brief scene is often read as a comment on the character of Israel in its better moments: when moved, they give beyond asking, beyond need. The chapter then describes the construction of the tabernacle curtains and goat-hair coverings in language that closely follows the instructions of chapter 26. The repetition is deliberate — the theological point is that the building is executed exactly as commanded. The tabernacle that emerges from chapters 35-40 is the instructions of chapters 25-31 incarnated. What God designed, Israel builds.

Chapter 37

Bezalel makes the ark, table, lampstand, and incense altar

Exodus 37 describes the construction of the tabernacle's four main furnishings by Bezalel. The ark of the covenant — its acacia-wood chest overlaid in gold inside and out, with its gold carrying rings and poles, its gold mercy seat and two hammered-gold cherubim — is made first. The chapter is careful to name Bezalel as its maker: he made it, the gold-work, he overlaid it, he made the rings, he made the poles. Then the table of the bread of the Presence, with its gold moulding and rings and poles and gold dishes and flagons and bowls and incense cups. Then the menorah, hammered from a single talent of pure gold with its shaft and six branches, its almond-blossom ornaments, its seven lamps and snuffers and trays. Finally the incense altar — small, gold-overlaid, horned, with rings and poles. The narrative of the construction mirrors the instructions of chapters 25-26 and 30 in exact order. What the text communicates is compliance: nothing was changed, nothing improved, nothing abbreviated. The tabernacle Israel builds is the tabernacle God designed. The faithfulness is the point.

Chapter 38

The altar of burnt offering, the court, and the total materials

Exodus 38 describes the construction of the altar of burnt offering and the courtyard enclosure, then appends a complete inventory of the materials consumed. The altar — square, bronze-overlaid acacia, with bronze grating, bronze horns, and the network for the fire — is made, with its carrying poles and all its utensils. The bronze laver and its stand are made from the mirrors of the women who served at the entrance to the tent of meeting — one of the most arresting details in the construction narrative, suggesting a community of women already present and serving at the sanctuary before it is formally consecrated. The courtyard is hung: linen hangings on bronze-based posts with silver bands and hooks, the entrance screen of blue, purple, and crimson on four posts. Then comes the accounting. The total gold used: 29 talents and 730 shekels. The silver: 100 talents and 1,775 shekels, taken from the census tax of a half-shekel per person from 603,550 men. The bronze: 70 talents and 2,400 shekels from the offerings. The accounting is presented without commentary, as though the fact of the record matters — the sanctuary was built on specific material, from specific people, through specific acts of giving.

Chapter 39

The priestly vestments are made and Moses inspects the work

Exodus 39 describes the making of the priestly vestments — the ephod, the breastpiece, the robe, the tunics, the turbans, and the sashes — closely mirroring the instructions of chapter 28. The ephod is woven from gold, blue, purple, and crimson yarn and fine linen, with two onyx shoulder stones engraved with the twelve tribal names. The breastpiece, folded double, is set with twelve precious stones in four rows, each stone engraved with one name: sardius, topaz, carbuncle; emerald, sapphire, diamond; jacinth, agate, amethyst; beryl, onyx, jasper. The robe of the ephod — all of blue — has a hem hung with pomegranates of blue, purple, and crimson, and gold bells alternating among them, so that the ringing of the bells would be heard as Aaron went into the holy place and when he came out. The turban bears the gold plate: Holy to the Lord. The chapter ends with the presentation to Moses and his inspection. He looks at every finished piece — tent, ark, table, lampstand, altars, laver, courtyard, all the utensils — and blesses the people. The phrase the text uses is directly parallel to Genesis 1: Moses saw all the work, and they had done it; as the Lord had commanded, so they had done it. And Moses blessed them.

Chapter 40

The tabernacle is raised and the glory fills it

Exodus 40 is the consummation of the book. God instructs Moses to erect the tabernacle on the first day of the first month — the beginning of the new year, the anniversary of nothing yet, a new beginning in time to match the new structure in space. Moses does so, step by step, in order: the tabernacle, the ark, the table, the lampstand, the incense altar, the hanging at the entrance, the altar of burnt offering, the laver, the courtyard, the screen at the gate. The narrator repeats the refrain: as the Lord commanded Moses. Then Moses anoints and consecrates each element with the anointing oil, and Aaron and his sons are washed and clothed and anointed in priestly office. When Moses finishes — the chapter uses the same verb as Genesis 2:1-2, Moses finished the work — the cloud covers the tent of meeting and the glory of the Lord fills the tabernacle. Moses is unable to enter the tent of meeting because the cloud rests on it. The journey resumes: when the cloud lifts, the Israelites travel; when it settles, they camp. And the fire is in the cloud over the tabernacle through all their journeys by night. The book ends with God dwelling in a travelling house among the people he freed from Egypt.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The God Who Hears Slaves

Before Moses, before the burning bush, before any of the famous scenes, Exodus establishes what kind of book it is. Chapter 2 ends with four verbs: God heard the groaning of the people, God remembered his covenant, God looked upon the Israelites, God knew. The deliverance does not begin with a hero. It begins with a cry.

The Burning Bush and the Name

Moses turns aside to look at a bush burning but not consumed. God calls his name twice. And when Moses asks what name to give the people who ask who has sent him, God answers with a phrase that has been argued about for three thousand years: I am who I am.

The Plagues and the Passover

Ten plagues, each one breaking something Egypt held sacred. The Nile turned to blood. Darkness that can be felt. The death of every firstborn. And on the night of the tenth plague, the Passover instituted — a liturgy written into the people while the deliverance is still happening.

Sinai and the Decalogue

Three months from Egypt, at the foot of a smoking mountain, the people receive ten utterances that have been argued about, carved on courthouse walls, and argued about again for three thousand years. The first word is not a command. It is the basis of all the commands: I am the Lord your God who brought you out of Egypt.

The Golden Calf

Moses is on the mountain receiving the law. He has been gone forty days. The people at the foot of the mountain tell Aaron to make them a god. The book's greatest scene of idolatry is placed directly after its greatest scene of covenant — a structure the rest of the Hebrew Bible never quite recovers from.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Moses
Prophet and deliverer

Born under Pharaoh's death decree, hidden in a basket by the Nile, raised in the Egyptian court, fled to Midian after killing an Egyptian. Called at eighty at the burning bush, returns to Egypt with his brother Aaron, confronts Pharaoh through ten plagues, leads the people through the sea and into the wilderness, climbs Sinai for the law, and dies on Mount Nebo within sight of the promised land without entering it. The Bible calls him the meekest man on earth and says he spoke with God face to face as a man speaks with his friend.

Aaron
Priest and brother

Moses's older brother by three years, his spokesman before Pharaoh, eventual high priest of Israel. The necessary public voice for an inarticulate prophet. He goes with Moses through the plagues, helps lead the people out, and is consecrated as the first priest of the tabernacle. He also presides over the golden calf disaster in Moses's absence, and his easy capitulation to the mob is recorded without softening.

Pharaoh
Antagonist

Unnamed in the book — deliberately. Egyptian pharaohs in the historical record have names; the book's refusal to name this one is part of its theological argument. Pharaoh is the principle, the system, the embodiment of the empire that grinds the slaves. The text alternates between saying Pharaoh hardened his heart and saying God hardened Pharaoh's heart, and the alternation has been argued about for two thousand years.

Miriam
Prophet and sister

Older sister of Moses and Aaron, the girl who watches the basket among the reeds in chapter 2 and offers to find a Hebrew wet-nurse — her own mother. She reappears in chapter 15, after the crossing of the sea, with a tambourine, leading the women of Israel in song. The text calls her a prophet, one of very few women in the Hebrew Bible given the title.

Jethro
Midianite priest

Moses's father-in-law. Takes Moses in when he flees Egypt, gives him work and a wife. In chapter 18 he visits Moses in the wilderness, hears what God has done, blesses the Lord — and then advises Moses to delegate his judicial workload. The judicial system of Israel is established, in the book's own telling, on the advice of a Midianite priest.

Zipporah
Moses's wife

Daughter of Jethro, met at a well in Midian, mother of Moses's two sons. She is at the centre of one of the strangest passages in Exodus — chapter 4:24–26, on the road back to Egypt, where God tries to kill Moses; Zipporah circumcises her son and touches Moses with the foreskin, saying truly you are a bridegroom of blood to me. Three verses, three thousand years of unresolved interpretation.
