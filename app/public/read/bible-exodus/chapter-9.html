Summary

Plagues five through seven escalate by touching the Egyptian economy and then its people directly. The death of livestock strikes at the agricultural and military base of Egyptian society; the separation between Egyptian and Israelite cattle is detailed and Pharaoh investigates and confirms it, which makes his continued refusal a matter of deliberate policy rather than ignorance. The boils mark a qualitative shift: for the first time the plague touches human bodies, not just animals and natural elements, and for the first time the magicians are incapacitated — they vanish from the text.

The hail plague introduces another new element: differentiation within Egypt. The officials who fear the word of the Lord protect their people and animals; those who do not lose them. The plague cycle is beginning to sort Egyptian society according to its response to the God of Israel. Pharaoh's response to the hail is his most explicit confession of wrongdoing in the book — the Lord is righteous, I and my people are wicked — which makes the subsequent hardening all the more pointed. The text is not portraying a man who simply doesn't know better; it is portraying a man who knows and refuses.
