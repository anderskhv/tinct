Summary

The train passes through Utah, and at a station near the Great Salt Lake a passenger boards who is visibly not a businessman: William Hitch, a Mormon missionary, who settles himself in the forward car and begins a lecture on the history of Joseph Smith, Brigham Young, the migration to the Great Basin, and the current state of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. He speaks without apparent expectation of a large audience; the lecture is its own justification.

Most passengers ignore him. Passepartout listens. He finds the story genuinely interesting — the migration, the hardship, the self-organization of a new community in the desert, the architecture of the religious practice. He has never heard of any of this before. He asks questions. Hitch answers them. The conversation goes on for some time.

Fogg, in the next car, is asleep. This is the clearest single illustration of the Fogg-Passepartout contrast: same train, same landscape, same available information. Passepartout finds everything. Fogg finds nothing worth noting.
