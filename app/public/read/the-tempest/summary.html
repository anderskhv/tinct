The Tempest

1611 CE · by William Shakespeare

The book in brief

Twelve years before the play opens, Prospero was Duke of Milan. While he buried himself in his books, his brother Antonio conspired with the King of Naples and seized the dukedom, setting Prospero and his three-year-old daughter Miranda adrift in a rotten boat. They washed up on a small island inhabited by the spirit Ariel and the half-human Caliban, son of a dead witch. Prospero became a magician. The play opens with him conjuring the storm of its title, wrecking a ship that happens to be carrying Antonio, the King of Naples, and the king's son Ferdinand onto his shore. Over the course of a single afternoon, Prospero takes a controlled revenge, marries his daughter to Ferdinand, frees Ariel, and finally renounces his magic.

The Tempest is Shakespeare's last solo-authored play, written around 1611 and performed at court for King James. It is short — under three hours — and unusually obedient to the classical unities of time, place, and action. The verse is dense and patient, the comedy genuinely funny, and the central character is a magician who gets to direct the entire action of the play from inside it.

The play has been read in more incompatible ways than perhaps any other Shakespeare. As a meditation on art and the artist's renunciation of his powers. As a coded farewell to the theater — Prospero's epilogue is often quoted as Shakespeare's own. As a colonial document, with Prospero the European invader and Caliban the dispossessed islander. As a study in forgiveness, or a study in the failure of forgiveness. The play accommodates all these readings because it is uneasy with each of them. Prospero's magic is benign and coercive at once, his mercy partial, his reconciliation incomplete. The Tempest is at its most powerful in the gaps between the readings it invites.

Why it still matters

A Late Style

The Tempest is the work of a writer at the end of a long career — Shakespeare was forty-seven and would write only collaborations after this, returning to Stratford to retire. The pacing is patient. The forgiveness is hard-won and partial. The verse is more concentrated than in the comedies and less harrowing than in the tragedies, with the strange, time-stilled music that critics from Coleridge to Edward Said have called "late style." Beethoven's last string quartets, Titian's final paintings, Yeats's last poems, Picasso's late drawings, Bob Dylan's recent records — the same quality of an artist no longer worried about pleasing, no longer interested in their old virtuosity, working closer to the grain of what they have always been doing — turns up across forms and centuries. The Tempest is one of the founding examples of late style in any art, and contemporary critics still reach for the term whenever a master makes something this stripped-back near the end.

Caliban's Question

Caliban is given language by Prospero and uses it, the first time he speaks, to curse him. He claims the island is his by inheritance from his mother. He is enslaved by someone who calls it education. Shakespeare gives him some of the play's most beautiful verse and also makes him drunken and murderous — a portrait that does not let either side off the hook. Twentieth and twenty-first century readings have placed him at the center: the founding figure of postcolonial literature, the indictment Shakespeare may not have known he was writing. Aimé Césaire rewrote the play as Une Tempête; George Lamming's The Pleasures of Exile reads the play as the Caribbean's. The protest "this island's mine" is a question the play does not answer, and refusing to answer it is part of what has kept the play urgent.

Prospero Renounces His Magic

The play ends with Prospero breaking his staff, drowning his book, and forgiving his enemies. "Now my charms are all o'erthrown," he says in the epilogue, addressing the audience directly across the proscenium, "and what strength I have's mine own." The gesture has been read as Shakespeare's own farewell to the theater — he co-wrote a few more plays but this was his last as sole author — and the autobiographical reading has shaped centuries of how the play is staged, with the staff-breaking moment treated as the climax even though strictly it is offstage. Whether or not Shakespeare intended the parallel, the moment of an artist setting down his instrument is one of the great gestures of renunciation in Western drama. Prospero asks the audience for the breath of their applause to release him; the play makes the asking part of the meaning. Any contemporary novelist, filmmaker, or musician stepping back from the work that defined them is, knowingly or not, performing some version of Prospero's ending.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Prospero's power as colonial power.

Caliban says "This island's mine, by Sycorax my mother, which thou tak'st from me." The play gives him that speech and then doesn't resolve it. I want to read how honestly Shakespeare presents the claim — and whether Prospero's magic is shown as liberation or domination.

Prospero as Shakespeare.

It was probably his last solo play. Prospero's epilogue — asking the audience to release him — reads like a playwright's valediction. I want to follow that thread and ask what it means for the play's themes: the renunciation of power, the letting-go of control.

Caliban's claim.

He cursed Prospero with the language Prospero taught him. He was enslaved by someone who called it education. I want to track every scene Caliban appears in and assess whether Shakespeare intends us to sympathise with him or merely to observe him.

Forgiveness vs. justice.

Prospero's enemies wronged him genuinely and severely. He chooses not to punish them. I want to read that choice carefully — is it moral wisdom, strategic calculation, or something closer to exhaustion? — and see how the play frames the difference between forgiveness and the surrender of justice.

Key figures

Prospero
The Exiled Magus

The deposed Duke of Milan, twelve years on the island, master of every event in the play. He raised the storm, separated the survivors, set Ferdinand's trial, exposed his brother's continuing plots, and frightened Caliban into submission — and he narrates almost all of this himself, to Miranda and to the audience. He is wise, obsessive, controlling, and exhausted. His final act is to break his staff, drown his book, and forgive his enemies. Whether that makes him saint, tyrant retired, or simply tired is the play's open question.

Miranda
Raised in Isolation

Prospero's daughter, fifteen, who has known no human face since she was three except her father's and Caliban's. When she first sees Ferdinand and the rest of the shipwrecked court, she utters the line the twentieth century has not let go of — "O brave new world, that has such people in't." Her wonder is genuine; her frame of reference is desperately small. The play loves her and is clear-eyed about her ignorance. Aldous Huxley took the title of his dystopia from her line precisely because the irony was already there in Shakespeare.

Caliban
The Native Inhabitant

Son of the witch Sycorax, the island's only previous human resident, now Prospero's slave. Prospero taught him language. Caliban uses it to curse him: "You taught me language; and my profit on't is, I know how to curse." The play gives him some of its most beautiful poetry — the speech about the isle being full of noises — and also makes him a coarse, drunken figure who plots Prospero's murder with two shipwrecked clowns. He is the most morally uncomfortable character Shakespeare wrote, and modern readings place him at the play's center.

Ariel
The Indentured Spirit

A spirit of air and fire whom Prospero rescued from a tree where Sycorax had imprisoned him, and immediately bound to his own service. Ariel performs every magical task in the play — the storm, the music, the banquet that vanishes, the harpy's curse — and reminds Prospero, with increasing edge, that he was promised freedom. The relationship is the play's most ambiguous: a master who calls the slave "my dainty Ariel" and a slave who serves brilliantly because the alternative is being put back in the tree.

Ferdinand
The Prince

The son of the King of Naples, separated from his shipmates by Prospero's storm and washed ashore alone. He believes his father drowned. He meets Miranda and falls in love within minutes. Prospero immediately accuses him of being a spy, sets him to hauling logs, and tests his constancy — the trial succeeds and the engagement is made. He is decent, well-mannered, and politically convenient: marrying him to Miranda makes Prospero the future grandfather of a king of Naples and seals the reconciliation the play needs.

Antonio
The Usurper

Prospero's younger brother, the man who took the dukedom of Milan twelve years earlier and exiled his brother and infant niece in a rotten boat. He is unrepentant. On the island, when he discovers the King of Naples is asleep and unguarded, he proposes that the king's brother Sebastian murder him on the spot to take the throne — exactly the same crime he committed against Prospero. The play forgives him formally at the end. He says nothing. His silence is the open wound on the comedy's reconciliation.
