Psalm 32

The psalm of confession and absolution: 'Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered.'

Summary

Psalm 32 belongs to the genre of individual thanksgiving — a psalm looking back on a crisis that has passed — but it opens with a beatitude that has made it one of the most quoted psalms in Christian theology. 'Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered; blessed is the man unto whom the Lord imputeth not iniquity.' The theological term 'imputation' that recurs in Paul's letter to the Romans and in later Protestant debates about justification has its lyric origin here. The speaker describes the somatic experience of unconfessed sin — bones wasting, vitality drained — and its resolution in the act of acknowledgment: 'I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid.'

The latter half of the psalm shifts from testimony to instruction. Having narrated his own experience of confession and forgiveness, the speaker instructs others to pray in a time of trouble and promises that God will be a hiding place in distress. The psalm closes with a contrast between the wicked, surrounded by sorrow, and the righteous, invited to rejoice. In Christian tradition Psalm 32 is the second of the seven penitential psalms (the others are 6, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143). Augustine, who called it one of his four favorite psalms, reportedly had it written on the wall beside his deathbed so he could read it as he died.
