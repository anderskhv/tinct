History of the Peloponnesian War

400 BCE · by Thucydides

The book in brief

The History of the Peloponnesian War is Thucydides's account of the twenty-seven-year war between Athens and Sparta — and the alliances that fought alongside each — that ended Athens's Golden Age. It opens with the diplomatic crisis at Corcyra, then the speeches in Sparta that committed both sides to fight, then the first invasions of Attica. It tracks the plague that killed Pericles and a third of the Athenian population. It records the revolt of Mytilene and the debate over whether to massacre the city. It builds toward the Melian Dialogue, where Athenian envoys tell a small neutral island that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must. It ends mid-sentence in 411 BCE, seven years before the war's actual end, in the middle of an account of the war in Ionia.

Thucydides served as an Athenian general in the war's early years and was exiled after losing the city of Amphipolis to a Spartan attack in 424 BCE. He spent the rest of his life writing the history. Composed in Greek prose toward the end of the 5th century BCE, the work is divided into eight books and structured around set-piece speeches Thucydides reconstructs at the major decision points — Pericles in the Funeral Oration, the Mytilenean debate, the Melian Dialogue, the assembly that voted for Sicily.

Thucydides is the founding work of Western historical writing and the founding text of political realism. He refuses to attribute events to gods or fate. He is interested in the political and psychological causes of human action, and he writes as if he expects his readers to face the same situations again. The book has been read as a manual for statesmen for twenty-four hundred years.

Why it still matters

The Founding of History as a Discipline

Thucydides invented historical writing as it is now practiced. Where Herodotus, writing one generation earlier, included gods, oracles, gold-digging ants, and stories told to him in Egypt, Thucydides limited himself to what could be argued from human action and verified by witnesses. He explicitly rejects the marvelous. He cross-checks his sources. He identifies the difference between the immediate cause of a war and its real cause — between what the participants said the dispute was about and the underlying balance of power that made conflict inevitable. Every later historian who tries to explain why something happened, rather than simply narrate it, is working in a discipline Thucydides built. Modern political science still treats his account of the war's causes as a starting point.

The Melian Dialogue

In Book V, the Athenians demand the small neutral island of Melos surrender. The Melians refuse. The dialogue between them — short, formal, the Athenians explaining that questions of justice apply only between equals and that the strong do what they can while the weak suffer what they must — is the founding text of political realism. The Melians argue for the gods, for fortune, for honor. The Athenians dismiss each in turn. The Melians refuse anyway; Athens takes the island, executes the men of military age, sells the women and children into slavery. Thucydides reports the outcome without moralizing. Every honest argument about power since Machiavelli, Hobbes, and Kissinger has had to come back to this passage.

A Plague That Changed a City

Thucydides's account of the Athenian plague of 430 BCE, which he survived, is the first sustained description of an epidemic in Western literature. He describes the symptoms in clinical detail so that future doctors might recognize the disease. Then he describes what the plague did to the city: bodies stacked in the temples, the dying lying in the streets, funeral pyres seized by strangers. Religious observance collapsed. People stopped keeping promises because they did not expect to be alive to honor them. Crime increased because the law had visibly stopped functioning. Twenty-four hundred years later, after the COVID years, the description is still recognizable — what catastrophe does to the agreements that hold a society together.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Speeches as politics in action.

Thucydides gives each side its own voice — Athenians argue for empire, Spartans argue for stability, the Melians argue for justice. He does not say who is right. I want to read tracking how each speech reveals the inner logic of a position, not just the stated reasons.

The Melian Dialogue and political realism.

In 416 BCE, Athens sent envoys to the neutral island of Melos and told them: the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must. Negotiate or die. I want to read the whole work as the argument that leads to this moment — and to decide whether Thucydides endorses it or mourns it.

The plague and the collapse of norms.

In Book II, plague strikes Athens. Thucydides does not only describe the disease — he describes what it does to social behaviour, to religious belief, to the willingness to keep promises. I want to read his account as a clinical study of how crisis dissolves the moral framework of a society.

The Sicilian Expedition as self-destruction.

Books VI–VII describe Athens sending its greatest fleet to conquer Sicily, losing it entirely, and never recovering. It is one of the most gripping strategic narratives in any language. I want to read tracking the decision-making failures — how a democracy talked itself into disaster.

Key figures

Pericles
Athenian Statesman

The dominant figure of the war's opening years. He persuaded Athens to retreat behind its walls and let the Spartan army burn the countryside while the Athenian fleet held the sea. He gave the Funeral Oration over the first year's dead, defining what Athens believed it was fighting for. He died in the plague in 429 BCE. Thucydides regarded him as the one leader who understood the war's logic and how Athens could win without overreaching.

Cleon
Athenian Demagogue

A leather-tanner who became Athens's most powerful voice after Pericles died. He argued for executing every adult male of rebellious Mytilene and selling the women and children — the assembly voted yes, then reversed itself the next day. He won an unexpected victory at Pylos and used it ruthlessly. Thucydides describes him with barely concealed contempt, treating him as the type case of what democracy produces when it loses its better men.

Alcibiades
Athenian General

The most brilliant and destructive figure in the war — Pericles's ward, Socrates's student, beautiful and reckless. He argued Athens into the Sicilian Expedition, was recalled to face charges of sacrilege, defected to Sparta and advised them how to defeat his own city, then defected to Persia, then returned to Athens and won battles for it again. Charisma without loyalty, applied at scale, over twenty years.

Nicias
Cautious Commander

An Athenian general known for piety and wealth. He opposed the Sicilian Expedition on the floor of the assembly, was outvoted, and was then appointed to lead it. When the army was trapped outside Syracuse and could still escape, he delayed the retreat by twenty-seven days because of a lunar eclipse. The army was destroyed; he was captured and executed. Thucydides treats him with a complicated mixture of pity and judgment.

Brasidas
Spartan Commander

The most gifted Spartan general of the war — quick, persuasive, and effective in ways Sparta rarely was. He marched a small force into northern Greece and detached cities from the Athenian empire by argument as much as by siege, including Amphipolis, the loss of which exiled Thucydides. He died at Amphipolis in 422 BCE in the moment of his greatest victory. Thucydides, who had reason to dislike him, writes him with respect.

Hermocrates
Syracusan Defender

The Syracusan statesman who warned his fellow citizens that Athens was coming, organized the defense of Sicily, and helped destroy the largest fleet Athens ever sent out. He understood Athenian strategy better than most Athenians did. Thucydides gives him long speeches and treats him as the equal of any commander on either side — the man whose competence determined the outcome of the war.
