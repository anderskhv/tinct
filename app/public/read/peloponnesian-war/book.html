Book 1, Chapter 1 — The State of Greece from the earliest Times to the Commencement of the Peloponnesian War

Modern English

Thucydides of Athens wrote the history of the war between the Peloponnesians and the Athenians, beginning the moment it broke out. He believed it would be a great war, more worth recording than any before it. He had grounds for that belief. Both sides were prepared in every department to the highest pitch, and he could see the rest of the Hellenic world taking sides in the quarrel — those who hesitated were already considering it. This was in fact the greatest movement ever known in history, not only among the Hellenes but across much of the barbarian world too — almost, one could say, across all of mankind. The events of remote antiquity, and even those just before the war, could not be clearly established because so much time had passed. But the evidence I have been able to gather, by inquiry pushed back as far as is practicable, all points to the same conclusion: nothing on a great scale had happened before, either in war or in any other sphere.

Crawley (1874)

Thucydides, an Athenian, wrote the history of the war between the Peloponnesians and the Athenians, beginning at the moment that it broke out, and believing that it would be a great war and more worthy of relation than any that had preceded it. This belief was not without its grounds. The preparations of both the combatants were in every department in the last state of perfection; and he could see the rest of the Hellenic race taking sides in the quarrel; those who delayed doing so at once having it in contemplation. Indeed this was the greatest movement yet known in history, not only of the Hellenes, but of a large part of the barbarian world—I had almost said of mankind. For though the events of remote antiquity, and even those that more immediately preceded the war, could not from lapse of time be clearly ascertained, yet the evidences which an inquiry carried as far back as was practicable leads me to trust, all point to the conclusion that there was nothing on a great scale, either in war or in other matters.
