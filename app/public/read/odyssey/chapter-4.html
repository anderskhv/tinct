Summary

Telemachus and Nestor's son Pisistratus drive on to Sparta after a night at Pherae. They arrive at Menelaus's palace mid-celebration: two weddings on the same day. Menelaus is sending his daughter Hermione to marry Neoptolemus, Achilles's son, and welcoming a bride for his own son Megapenthes. The hall is full of music and dancing, and the two travelers from Pylos slip in almost unnoticed — until Menelaus, the great king of Sparta, sees them and orders them brought to the table without first asking who they are. The law of xenia, the guest-bond, is older than knowing the guest's name.

At dinner Menelaus speaks of Troy, of all the captains lost coming home, and most of all of his oldest friend Odysseus, whose fate he never learned. Telemachus weeps quietly into his cloak. Helen comes down from her chambers — and immediately, looking at the young guest she has never met, asks her husband whether this might be Odysseus's son. The likeness is too exact to be anyone else. Pisistratus, taking his cue, says yes: he came to find news of his father. The whole hall begins to weep, even those who never met Odysseus. The grief is ten years old and still fresh.

To break the spell, Helen drops a drug into the wine — nepenthe, "without grief," given to her in Egypt by a queen named Polydamna. Whoever drinks it forgets sorrow for a day. She then tells a story to put Telemachus at ease, which is also, very pointedly, a story about herself. Once during the war, Odysseus disguised himself as a beggar and infiltrated Troy. Helen alone recognized him — and helped him. She helped the Greek spy. She tells this as evidence that her heart had already turned toward home long before the city fell.

Menelaus, listening, gently tells a different story. The night the Greeks lay hidden inside the wooden horse, Helen walked around it three times, calling out the men's names in the voices of their wives. The men inside almost broke. Only Odysseus, clamping his hand over each man's mouth in turn, kept them silent. The two stories are about the same person, and they are not the same Helen. The poem makes no attempt to reconcile them. Whoever Helen is now, she is not penitent in any simple way — and Menelaus, who has taken her back, is not the same man he was.

The next day Menelaus tells Telemachus the longest tale of his own homecoming. Stranded on the Egyptian island of Pharos with no wind, he was instructed by Eidothea, daughter of the sea-god Proteus, to ambush her father in his midday sleep. Proteus changes into a lion, a serpent, running water, fire, a leafy tree — but Menelaus and his men hold on. Pinned at last in his own shape, the old shape-shifter has to answer. Yes: Agamemnon is dead, murdered by his wife on the day he came home. Ajax is dead, drowned by Poseidon for his arrogance. And Odysseus is alive — held, weeping, on the island of the nymph Calypso, with no ship and no way to leave. It is the first hard news Telemachus has had in twenty years.

Proteus also tells Menelaus his own destiny: because he is married to a daughter of Zeus, he will not die but be carried to the Elysian Plain, where the air is mild and there is no rain. It is the closest thing to heaven the Greek imagination allows. Menelaus reports it almost as an aside. The contrast with Odysseus, weeping on Calypso's beach, is the point of the chapter: one homecoming is over and serene and slightly hollow; the other has not yet begun.

The scene cuts back to Ithaca. The suitors discover that Telemachus has sailed and they are furious — Antinous especially, who had assumed the boy was harmless. They commission a ship and prepare to ambush him in the strait between Ithaca and the island of Same on his way back. Penelope hears all this from a herald and collapses; she cannot eat, cannot speak, and cries herself to sleep. Athena, in pity, sends Penelope a dream in the form of her absent sister Iphthime, who tells her not to fear: the boy is under the gods' protection. The dream eases her, but only for a night.
