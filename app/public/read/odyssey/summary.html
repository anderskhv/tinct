The Odyssey

Homer · c. 700 BCE · 24 chapters · about 9 hours to read

The ten-year journey home from the Trojan War — and the harder question of whether you can come home at all when you have been gone so long.

The story

The Odyssey is the ten-year journey of Odysseus home from the Trojan War. He survives sea monsters, witches, the Cyclops, the underworld, and Calypso's island. He arrives home twenty years after he left to find his palace turned against him: a hundred suitors eating his food and courting his wife by day, his son a stranger to him, his old dog the only soul who recognizes him at the gate. The poem is the homecoming after the war the Iliad described — and the question of whether you can come home at all when you have been gone so long.

Composed orally in Greek around the 8th century BCE and attributed to Homer, the Odyssey is shorter than the Iliad and structured differently: episodic, told partly in flashback, and centered on a single man rather than a ten-year siege. The first four chapters follow Telemachus searching for news of his father. The next eight are Odysseus's wanderings, told around Calypso's hearth and at a feast in the court of the Phaeacians. The last twelve chapters are the return to Ithaca and the bloody reclamation of his house.

It is the founding adventure story in Western literature, and almost every adventure story since — Sinbad, the Aeneid, Dante's Inferno, Robinson Crusoe, Joyce's Ulysses, Star Wars — is in conversation with it. To read it only as adventure misses the harder argument underneath. The Odyssey is a serious treatment of cunning as a virtue, of what marriage means after twenty years apart, and of the duties a household owes to strangers who arrive unannounced. Odysseus does not come home as a hero who has earned his rest. He comes home disguised as a beggar; he is tested by his own wife; and the violence of his arrival is the price the poem charges for his absence.

Chapter 1

A council of the gods, and a young man at his wits' end

The poem opens not on a hero but on a council of the gods. Athena pleads with Zeus to free Odysseus, who has been stranded for seven years on Calypso's island. Then she travels to Ithaca and finds his son Telemachus — twenty years old, never having known his father — miserable among the hundred suitors who have moved into the palace. She gives him, in disguise, what no one has given him in twenty years: a plan.

Chapter 2

Telemachus calls the assembly

Telemachus stands before the men of Ithaca with his father's staff in his hand and denounces the suitors. They refuse to leave. Helped quietly by Athena (now disguised as Mentor), he gathers a ship and crew and sails at sunset for Pylos and Sparta to find news of his father. The suitors, drinking in the great hall, do not yet know he has gone.

Chapter 3

At Pylos with old Nestor

At Pylos the wise old Nestor receives him with full hospitality and tells stories — long ones — about the homecomings of the other Greek captains. The most pointed of them is Agamemnon's: murdered in his own bath by his wife and her lover. The story is a quiet warning to Telemachus that hangs over the rest of the poem.

Chapter 4

At Sparta — the first news of Odysseus

At Sparta, Menelaus and Helen receive him with extraordinary hospitality. Helen drops a drug into the wine to ease everyone's grief, and the stories begin. Menelaus, who once wrestled the shape-shifting Proteus to learn the fate of his fellow captains, tells Telemachus the news he came for: Odysseus is alive, held captive on Calypso's island.

Chapter 5

Calypso releases him; Poseidon wrecks the raft

The narrative finally finds its hero. Hermes is sent to compel Calypso to release him; she gives Odysseus tools to build a raft and lets him go. He sails for seventeen days. On the eighteenth, Poseidon, returning from a feast, sees him and raises a storm that destroys the raft. Odysseus washes up half-dead on the shore of Scheria, the magical island of the Phaeacians.

Chapter 6

A princess on the beach

The young princess Nausicaa goes down to the river to wash clothes. Her maids' scream wakes the half-naked stranger sleeping under a pile of leaves on the beach. He emerges, salt-crusted and terrifying. Only Nausicaa stands her ground; he addresses her with extraordinary tact. She gives him food and clothing and sends him on toward her father's palace.

Chapter 7

In the palace of Alcinous

Hidden by a mist Athena throws around him, Odysseus walks alone into the Phaeacians' palace and throws himself at the queen's knees. The court is astonished. He is fed, seated, asked who he is — but he tells them only what he has just survived, not yet his name.

Chapter 8

A bard sings of Troy, and Odysseus weeps

At the feast in his honor, the blind bard Demodocus sings of the Trojan War. Odysseus pulls his cloak over his head and weeps. After the athletic games — where, stung by an insult, he throws the discus twice as far as anyone else — King Alcinous, certain now this is no ordinary man, asks him gently to say his name. The next four chapters are Odysseus's reply, told in his own voice.

Chapter 9

The Cyclops — "My name is Nobody"

He tells of the Cicons, the Lotus Eaters, and most famously the Cyclops Polyphemus — the one-eyed son of Poseidon, in whose cave Odysseus and his men were trapped. He introduced himself only as Outis, "Nobody." He blinded the giant in his sleep, escaped clinging to the underside of a ram, and could not resist shouting his real name as he sailed away. Polyphemus prayed to his father — and Poseidon's curse begins now.

Chapter 10

Aeolus, the Laestrygonians, Circe

Then Aeolus, the wind-god, who gave him a bag of contrary winds; his men opened it within sight of Ithaca and were blown all the way back. The Laestrygonians, cannibal giants, sank eleven of his twelve ships. The witch Circe turned his men into pigs; with Hermes's help he forced her to restore them, and then shared her bed for a year. When his men reminded him of home, Circe gave him the next instruction: he must visit the dead.

Chapter 11

The visit to the dead

He sailed to the edge of the world, dug a trench, poured offerings of milk and honey and the blood of black sheep, and called the dead. The prophet Tiresias spoke first. His mother came forward, and he learned for the first time that she had died of grief waiting for him. Then Agamemnon, then Achilles — who said he would rather be a slave above ground than king of all the dead.

Chapter 12

Sirens, Scylla, and the cattle of the sun

Back from Hades. The Sirens, whose song he heard alone, tied to the mast. Scylla, who took six of his men screaming his name. The forbidden cattle of the sun, which his men ate while he slept — for which Zeus shattered their ship with a thunderbolt. Odysseus alone survived, washing up on Calypso's island where the present finds him, seven years on.

Chapter 13

Home in Ithaca, in disguise

His story ends. The Phaeacians load him with gifts and their magic ships sail him home in a single night. They lay him gently on the shore of Ithaca, asleep. He wakes and does not recognize his own country. A young shepherd appears on the path — Athena, dropping the disguise — and the two old allies sit on the beach and plan. She turns him into a beggar.

Chapter 14

In the hut of Eumaeus

He goes, in disguise, to the hut of Eumaeus, his old swineherd. Eumaeus does not recognize him. What he does is keep the law of hospitality: he slaughters two suckling pigs from his own stock and serves them to the stranger, apologizing for not having more. He talks lovingly of his absent master, who he believes is dead. Odysseus tests him and finds him true.

Chapter 15

Telemachus comes home

Athena flies to Sparta and rouses Telemachus. He sails home, diverts the suitors' ambush at sea, and walks inland to the hut of Eumaeus — where Athena has arranged for him to meet a beggar. The two stories are about to come together.

Chapter 16

"I am your father"

Telemachus walks into the swineherd's hut. The dogs, who normally bark at strangers, fawn on him. Eumaeus is sent to the palace; Athena restores Odysseus to his real form for one moment. I am your father, he says. They embrace and weep, and the sun would have set on their tears if Telemachus had not asked at last what they were going to do about the hundred armed men in the great hall.

Chapter 17

A beggar in his own house, and a dying dog

Disguised again, Odysseus enters the palace as a beggar. At the gate lies an old dog on a heap of dung, half-blind and full of fleas. He is Argos, the puppy Odysseus raised as a boy. He lifts his head, recognizes him, wags his tail once, and dies. Inside, Antinous mocks the beggar and throws a footstool at him. Penelope asks to speak with him later that evening.

Chapter 18

A warning the suitor refuses to hear

A series of trials in the great hall. Odysseus knocks down the actual beggar Irus with one punch. Penelope appears before the suitors and extracts gifts from each of them; Odysseus, watching from his corner, smiles at her cleverness. He warns the better-mannered Amphinomus to leave before the master returns. Amphinomus is troubled. He does not leave.

Chapter 19

The interview, and the scar

After the suitors have gone to bed, Penelope sends for the beggar. They speak for hours; she does not yet recognize him. The old nurse Eurycleia is told to wash his feet — and feels the scar on his thigh from a boyhood boar-hunt. She gasps; he silences her with a hand at her throat. Then Penelope, on impulse, announces a contest for tomorrow: whoever can string Odysseus's old bow and shoot through twelve axe-heads will be her husband.

Chapter 20

The last quiet hour

Odysseus cannot sleep. He lies in the hallway planning. Penelope, upstairs, prays to Artemis to die before another day of this. The suitors gather for one final meal; the seer Theoclymenus reads omens of disaster — the walls running with blood. They laugh and drive him from the hall.

Chapter 21

The trial of the bow

Penelope brings out the great bow. Telemachus tries first and very nearly strings it — then catches his father's eye and stops. The suitors try, one by one. None can string it. The beggar asks for a turn. He turns the bow over like a singer turning over a lyre, strings it without effort, and shoots an arrow through the twelve axe-heads from his seat. Outside, Zeus thunders.

Chapter 22

The slaughter

Odysseus throws off his rags, leaps onto the threshold, and announces himself. The doors are locked. He, Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius kill the suitors one by one. Antinous goes first — a wine cup in his hand, an arrow through his throat. Eurymachus tries to negotiate; Odysseus refuses. The disloyal maids are made to clean the blood and are then hanged in the courtyard.

Chapter 23

The secret of the bed

Eurycleia runs upstairs to wake Penelope. She does not believe it. She comes down and sits across the hall from him by the firelight, in silence. He tells the maids to make up the bed in the great chamber. Penelope tells them to drag the bed out — and Odysseus snaps. That bed cannot be moved; he built the room around an olive tree growing on the site, the trunk of the tree is one of the bedposts. No one in the world knows this. Penelope's knees give. Athena makes the night long for them.

Chapter 24

Peace in Ithaca

At dawn Odysseus walks out to the country farm of his old father Laertes, who is in the orchard, planting a tree. Odysseus tests him too — names the trees Laertes once gave him as a child — and the old man faints from joy in his arms. The families of the slain suitors gather to take revenge. Athena calls peace. The poem ends.

Key themes

The five threads that hold the poem together. Full analysis →

Mētis — cunning over force

Odysseus is great because of intelligence, not strength. He survives the Cyclops by claiming his name is "Nobody." He passes the Sirens tied to the mast. The poem argues, against the older heroic code it inherited, that thinking outlasts force.

Xenia — the law of hospitality

The duty owed to a stranger at your door, protected by Zeus himself. The quiet engine of the poem — the Phaeacians and Eumaeus keep it; Polyphemus and the suitors break it. The slaughter at the end is, in the poem's moral logic, the punishment for that breaking.

Anagnorisis — the recognition scenes

Argos the dog. The scar Eurycleia feels on his thigh. Telemachus, finally seeing his father after twenty years. Penelope and the secret of the bed. After such an absence, identity has to be earned by tokens — a scar, a tree, a bow no one else can string.

Women in the poem

The Odyssey is, more than any other Greek epic, a poem of female agency. Calypso, Circe, Athena, Nausicaa, Helen, and above all Penelope drive the plot more often than they decorate it. Penelope is the structural answer to Agamemnon's wife — the proof that not every homecoming ends in betrayal.

Nostos — the meaning of homecoming

The journey ends in Chapter 13, with eleven chapters still to go. Arriving home is one thing; being home is another, and the second is harder. You have to become a stranger to your own house before you are allowed back into it.

Key figures

The six who matter most. Twenty more in the full character guide.

Odysseus
The cunning one

King of Ithaca, called polytropos — the man of many turns. Twenty years away from home: ten at Troy, ten trying to get back. He survives by lying, planning, disguising himself, and using words.

Penelope
His wife

Twenty years of waiting. For the last three, she has held a hundred suitors at bay in her own house — telling them she will choose a husband when she finishes weaving a shroud, and unweaving every night what she wove that day. The female equivalent of Odysseus.

Telemachus
The son

Twenty years old when the poem begins; he has never known his father. The first four chapters are his — he leaves Ithaca to find news of Odysseus, and the journey is his coming-of-age.

Athena
Goddess of wisdom

Odysseus's champion among the gods. She loves him because he thinks. She appears in disguise throughout the poem — as a young man, a shepherd, a beggar — steering events toward his return.

Poseidon
God of the sea

The antagonist. Odysseus blinded his son the Cyclops, and Poseidon spends the next ten years trying to drown him for it. Most of what stands between Odysseus and home traces back to Poseidon's grudge.

The Suitors
The problem at home

A hundred and eight young aristocrats from Ithaca and the neighboring islands, camped in Odysseus's palace for three years. The poem makes you know them as individuals before Odysseus kills them — and that is part of its weight.
