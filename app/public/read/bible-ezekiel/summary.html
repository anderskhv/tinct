Ezekiel a guided tour

6th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Latter Prophets

A priest in Babylonian exile sees the heavens open and the glory of God arrive on a throne of living fire. The strangest book in the Hebrew prophetic canon begins here, and never becomes less strange.

The book in brief

Ezekiel is the third of the major prophetic books and the strangest. A priest named Ezekiel ben Buzi has been deported to Babylon in 597 BCE, eleven years before the final destruction of Jerusalem. In the fifth year of his exile, the heavens open and he sees four living creatures bearing a throne of fire, a wheel within a wheel, and the appearance of the glory of God. He is told to speak to a rebellious house. He begins.

The book that follows contains the most elaborate visionary literature in the Hebrew Bible: the chariot, the silent prophet, the divided hair, the boiling pot, the unmourned wife, the dry bones, and forty-eight chapters of vision culminating in a new temple with a river of life flowing from beneath its threshold. Ezekiel is the prophet whose vocation has cost him most visibly — struck dumb for years, his body pressed into service as a sign — and the book preserves that cost without diminishing it.

Ezekiel 1 of 48
Ezekiel 1

The Chariot Vision

Chapter 1 is the founding vision of the book and one of the most visually elaborate passages in the Hebrew Bible. Ezekiel, settled with other deportees by the Chebar canal, sees a great storm cloud from the north, flashing fire, and within it four living creatures with four faces each — man, lion, ox, and eagle — and four wings, and legs straight as columns. Beside each creature is a wheel with a wheel within it, the rims full of eyes, moving in whatever direction the spirit moves. Above the creatures stretches a firmament like crystal, and above the firmament a throne of sapphire, and on the throne a figure of light and fire surrounded by a rainbow. The prophet identifies this, carefully, as the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the Lord, and falls on his face. The vision simultaneously places God's presence in Babylon — outside the temple, beyond the land — and establishes the theological stakes of the entire book.

Ezekiel 2

The Commissioning

Chapter 2 is the moment of commissioning that follows the vision. The spirit sets Ezekiel on his feet, and the voice speaks: son of man — the phrase that will be used of Ezekiel more than ninety times across the book — I send you to the children of Israel, a rebellious nation that has transgressed against me. The commission carries no promise of success; the people are obstinate and hard of heart, and whether they hear or refuse to hear, they will know that a prophet has been among them. The prophet is told not to be afraid of the people or their words, even when briars and thorns surround him and he dwells among scorpions. Then a hand is stretched out to him holding a scroll, and the scroll, unrolled, is written on both sides — lamentations, mourning, and woe. The double-sided scroll, dense with suffering, is what he is about to be sent with.

Ezekiel 3

Eating the Scroll

Chapter 3 completes the call narrative. Ezekiel is told to eat the scroll offered to him, to fill his stomach with it. He eats, and it is in his mouth as honey for sweetness — even though it is written with lamentations. He is then told a second time of the commission's difficulty: the house of Israel is not willing to listen, because they are not willing to listen to God, and all of them are hard of forehead and stubborn of heart. The spirit lifts him and he hears behind him the great rushing sound of the wings and wheels of the chariot departing, then sets him down in Tel-abib among the exiles, where he sits overwhelmed for seven days. At the chapter's end, the watchman's commission is introduced for the first time: Ezekiel is set as a watchman for the house of Israel, responsible for conveying warnings, and if he fails to warn and someone dies in their iniquity, the death is on the prophet's own hands. His mouth is then closed; he will be silent except when God opens it to speak.

Ezekiel 4

The Siege in Miniature

Chapter 4 opens the sequence of symbolic acts that are among the most sustained and physically demanding in any prophetic book. Ezekiel is told to take a clay brick, engrave Jerusalem on it, and set siege works against it — a miniature of the coming attack, performed in full view of the exile community. Then he is to lie on his left side for three hundred and ninety days, bearing the iniquity of the house of Israel; and then on his right side for forty days, bearing the iniquity of Judah. He is bound with cords so that he cannot turn from side to side during this time. The period of lying on his side corresponds, in the book's symbolism, to the years of exile and punishment the two communities will bear. He is given rations — a minimal amount of grain, water, and fuel — by weight and measure, the diet of a besieged city. The fuel for cooking is specified as human dung, to which the prophet objects; at his request it is changed to animal dung, but the point of defilement and scarcity stands.

Ezekiel 5

The Divided Hair

Chapter 5 continues the symbolic acts of the previous chapter and delivers the first extended oracle of judgement. Ezekiel is told to shave his head and beard with a sharp sword — itself a striking image, the sword as a barber — and then weigh and divide the hair into three equal portions. One third is to be burned in the city at the end of the days of lying on his side; one third is to be struck with the sword around the city; one third is to be scattered to the wind. A small number of hairs are to be bound in the skirts of his garment — a remnant — and then even some of those are to be cast into the fire. The oracle that follows explicates the sign: Jerusalem, set in the middle of the nations, has defied God's statutes more than the nations around her. Therefore a third of her people will die of pestilence and famine within the city, a third will fall by the sword around it, and a third will be scattered to every wind. The passage contains one of the harshest statements in the book: Surely, because you have defiled my sanctuary with all your detestable things and with all your abominations, I will also withdraw, and my eye will not spare, nor will I have pity.

Ezekiel 6

Against the Mountains

Chapter 6 is the first oracle directed not to a person or community but to a landscape — the mountains of Israel — which in the prophetic convention stand for the land as a whole and, specifically, for the sites of the high places where illicit worship had been practiced. The oracle announces that the high places will be destroyed, the altars broken, the incense altars cut down, and the people slain before their idols. The slain will fall among their altars, their bones scattered around the shrines they had served. The oracle moves between total destruction and the signature remnant theology: a few will escape the sword among the nations, and they will remember God when they are in captivity. The chapter introduces the recognition formula that recurs throughout Ezekiel: they shall know that I am the Lord. This phrase, which appears in some form over sixty times in the book, is the theological telos of judgment and restoration alike — catastrophe and mercy both serve the purpose of making Israel and the nations know who God is.

Ezekiel 7

The End Has Come

Chapter 7 is a sustained poetic oracle — one of the most rhythmically concentrated in the book — announcing the coming day of judgment on the land of Israel. The phrase the end has come is repeated four times in the opening verses, and its insistence gives the chapter a drumbeat quality. The oracle moves through the instruments of catastrophe: the sword is without, pestilence and famine within; every hand is feeble and every knee runs with water; sackcloth covers the outside and horror the inside. Silver and gold will be worthless on that day — men will throw them into the streets, for silver and gold cannot deliver them in the day of the Lord's wrath. The temple treasury will be profaned. The vision of violence is present in the city, and the oracle closes with a statement about the silence of the divine presence: the king mourns, the prince is clothed in desolation, the hands of the people of the land are troubled — and the Lord will do to them according to their way, and judge them as they have judged others.

Ezekiel 8

Abominations in the Temple

Chapter 8 opens the extended vision of chapters 8-11, in which Ezekiel is lifted by the spirit and transported from Babylon to Jerusalem. He is shown the temple in 592 BCE — still standing, not yet destroyed — and four abominations committed within it. First, at the north gate of the inner court, a statue described as the image of jealousy that provokes jealousy. Second, in a room discovered by digging through a wall, seventy elders of Israel burning incense before images of creeping things and beasts and idols painted on the wall, each man in his own room of pictures, saying the Lord does not see us, the Lord has forsaken the land. Third, at the north gate of the outer court, women sitting and weeping for Tammuz — the dying and rising god of Mesopotamian religion, whose cult was a direct import from the Babylonian world the exiles now inhabited. Fourth, between the porch and the altar, twenty-five men with their backs to the temple of the Lord, their faces east, worshipping the sun. Each abomination is presented as worse than the last. The chapter closes with God saying he will act in wrath and his eye will not spare, nor will he have pity.

Ezekiel 9

The Mark and the Slaughter

Chapter 9 is the vision of Jerusalem's judgment carried out. Six men come from the north gate of the inner court, each with a weapon in hand. A seventh man is among them, clothed in linen, with a writing case at his side. The glory of the God of Israel rises from the cherubim, where it had been, and moves to the threshold of the house — the first movement in the sequence of the glory's departure from the temple. The man in linen is commanded to go through the city and put a mark on the forehead of every person who sighs and groans over all the abominations done within it. The other six are then told to follow and kill without pity — old men, young men, maidens, little children, women — beginning at the sanctuary. Only those with the mark are spared. Ezekiel falls on his face and cries: Ah, Lord God! Will you destroy all the remnant of Israel? God's answer is that the iniquity of the house of Israel and Judah is exceedingly great, the land is full of blood, and the city full of injustice. The man in linen returns and reports: I have done as you commanded.

Ezekiel 10

The Glory Departs

Chapter 10 is the structural pivot of the temple vision of chapters 8-11. The cherubim of chapter 1 appear again — recognised explicitly by the prophet as the creatures he had seen by the river Chebar — and the man in linen from chapter 9 is told to take coals from between the wheels and scatter them over the city. The glory of the Lord, which had risen to the threshold of the house in chapter 9, now rises from above the cherubim, and the court is filled with the cloud and the brightness of the Lord's glory. The chariot — the four creatures, the four wheels with eyes on their rims — is described again in sustained detail, each element identified. Then the glory rises up from the cherubim and pauses over the threshold; the cherubim lift their wings and the wheels rise with them; the glory pauses at the east gate. The chapter ends with the glory still at the gate, not yet departed from the city, but the direction is clear: the divine presence is leaving the temple.

Ezekiel 11

The Glory Leaves Jerusalem

Chapter 11 closes the temple vision of chapters 8-11. At the east gate Ezekiel sees twenty-five men — leaders of the people, named here as Jaazaniah and Pelatiah — who are counselling false security: the city is the pot and we are the meat. Their metaphor means they believe the city walls protect them and the deportees are the disposable portion. The oracle reverses the image: they are not the meat in the pot; they are those who will be brought out and slain on the borders of Israel by the sword they fear. As the oracle is delivered, Pelatiah falls dead — a sign that the judgment is already underway. Ezekiel intercedes again: Lord God, will you make a full end of the remnant of Israel? The answer is the new heart promise: the exiles scattered among the nations are themselves the true sanctuary; God will gather them, give them the land, take the stony heart out of their flesh and give them a heart of flesh, put his spirit within them. Then the glory of the Lord rises from the city and stands on the mountain to the east of the city. The glory has left Jerusalem.

Ezekiel 12

The Exile's Pack

Chapter 12 contains two symbolic acts performed in view of the exile community. In the first, Ezekiel is told to prepare the baggage of an exile, dig through the wall of his house in their sight in the evening, carry his pack out through the hole in the dark, and cover his face so that he cannot see the ground — all of it a symbol of the coming fate of the prince of Jerusalem and those who flee with him. The exiles watch and ask what he is doing; the explanation given names the prince specifically: he will carry his things on his shoulder in the dark, dig through the wall, and go out through it, and his face will be covered, because he will not see the land with his eyes (a detail that proved accurate — Zedekiah was blinded before being deported). In the second symbolic act, Ezekiel is to eat his bread with quaking and drink his water with trembling and anxiety, representing the conditions of siege. The chapter closes with an oracle against those who say the vision is far off and concerns many years hence: it will happen, says God, and the days are near.

Ezekiel 13

Whitewash and Pillows

Chapter 13 is a sustained oracle against the prophets and prophetesses who prophesy out of their own hearts rather than from God's word. The male prophets are compared to builders who build a wall with untempered mortar — whitewash — that will not hold when the storm and rain and hailstones come. The image is architectural: there is a real structural failure, and cosmetic surface treatment cannot prevent it from collapsing. These prophets have prophesied peace when there was no peace, have not gone up into the gaps or built the wall for the house of Israel, and therefore they will not be in the council of God's people. The prophetesses are then addressed separately: they have sewed pillows on every elbow and made veils for people of every height, charging fees to kill souls that should not die and keep alive souls that should not live. The exact nature of the pillows and veils is disputed — the most likely reading is that they were instruments of divination or magic — but the theological point is clear: the prophetesses are using ritual objects to give false assurance, profaning God before a people who listen to falsehood.

Ezekiel 14

Idols of the Heart

Chapter 14 presents the elders of Israel coming to Ezekiel to inquire of the Lord — the standard prophetic consultative visit. But the response is unexpected: God says that these men have taken idols into their hearts and set stumbling blocks of iniquity before their faces, and he will answer them himself, according to the multitude of their idols. The chapter develops a principle: if any person, whether of Israel or a stranger, who has idols in their heart comes to a prophet, and God answers them, both the prophet who is deceived and the inquirer will bear their iniquity. The principle extends to the individual: God will set his face against that person. The chapter then introduces the figure of a righteous man (Noah, Daniel, or Job) who might intercede for a sinful land — a triad of ancient figures famous for righteousness. The conclusion is stark: even if these three were in the land, they would deliver only themselves by their righteousness; they would save neither sons nor daughters. The coming judgment is not avoidable by accumulated righteousness.

Ezekiel 15

The Useless Vine

Chapter 15 is the shortest sustained unit in the book — a brief parable with its interpretation. The comparison is simple: what is the vine-wood good for compared to any branch of the trees of the forest? The vine is valued, in the prophetic and wisdom traditions, as the image of Israel's fruitfulness and covenantal identity. But a vine-branch stripped of fruit is, Ezekiel's oracle points out, worth less than forest timber. It cannot be made into a peg on which to hang a vessel; it burns quickly and leaves nothing useful. If it is charred at both ends and scorched in the middle, it is even more useless. The application is immediate: so will God treat the inhabitants of Jerusalem. Just as the vine-wood is cast into the fire, so the city will be consumed by fire. The chapter is the shortest theological argument in the book, and its force is precisely in the brevity: the case against Jerusalem is as simple as the case against a fruitless vine — there is nothing it can do that would justify its preservation.

Ezekiel 16

The Foundling Jerusalem

Chapter 16 is the longest chapter in the book and one of the most disturbing in the Hebrew Bible. It is an extended allegory in which Jerusalem is a foundling infant, discovered newborn in a field, unwashed, umbilical cord uncut, abandoned — an Amorite mother, a Hittite father, cast out on the day of her birth. God passes by, tells her to live, raises her to womanhood, enters into a covenant of marriage with her, clothes her with embroidered garments and fine linen, adorns her with gold and silver, feeds her on fine flour and oil and honey. She becomes exceedingly beautiful, renowned among the nations. Then she uses the beauty God gave her to engage in repeated infidelities, building shrines for herself, worshipping idols, sacrificing the sons and daughters she bore to God. The oracle accumulates the specific forms of her prostitution: with the Egyptians, with the Assyrians, with the Babylonians. The judgment follows: she will be stripped, stoned, and burned by those she had chosen as lovers. But the chapter closes with a promise of restoration: God will remember the covenant made in the days of her youth, establish an everlasting covenant, and forgive all that she has done — though she will bear her shame and never open her mouth again because of it.

Ezekiel 17

The Eagle and the Cedar

Chapter 17 is presented explicitly as a riddle and a parable — the only time the book announces itself in this way. A great eagle with great wings and many feathers comes to Lebanon and takes the top of a cedar, carries it to a land of trade, plants a seed that grows into a spreading vine, low-growing but fruitful, with its branches turning toward the eagle. Then a second great eagle appears, and the vine bends its roots toward this second eagle. An oracle follows: the vine's roots will be pulled up, its fruit will wither, and when the east wind strikes it, it will wither on the bed where it grew. The explanation follows: the first eagle is Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon; the cedar top is Jehoiachin; the vine is Zedekiah, who took an oath of loyalty to Babylon but then sent envoys to Egypt — the second eagle — for help. Because he broke the covenant and the oath sworn in God's name, he will not escape. The chapter closes with a brief counter-promise: God himself will plant a tender branch from the high cedar on the mountain of Israel, and it will become a noble cedar sheltering all manner of birds — a messianic image that stands over against the failed vine's collapse.

Ezekiel 18

Individual Accountability

Chapter 18 is the most sustained piece of moral theology in the book and the clearest statement of individual accountability in the Hebrew Bible. It begins by quoting a proverb that was evidently current among the exile community: the fathers eat sour grapes and the children's teeth are set on edge. The proverb encodes a theory of inherited punishment — that the suffering of the exile generation is the consequence of sins they did not personally commit. God's response through the prophet is direct: as I live, this proverb shall no more be used in Israel. The soul that sins, it shall die — not its father, not its children, but itself. The chapter works through three cases: a righteous man (who does all that is right and shall live), his son who is violent and wicked (who shall die), and that son's son who sees all his father's sins and does not do them (who shall live). The pattern is that personal righteousness and personal wickedness are the operative categories, not inherited status. The chapter closes with a call to repentance: cast away from you all the transgressions you have committed, make yourself a new heart and a new spirit, for why will you die, O house of Israel?

Ezekiel 19

The Lamentation for the Princes

Chapter 19 is explicitly marked as a lamentation — the form used for the dead — and it is the most formally poetic chapter in the first half of the book. It is structured in two parts, each an extended metaphor. In the first, a lioness raises her cubs; the first cub becomes a lion who learns to catch prey among the peoples but is taken in the nations' pit, brought to Egypt with hooks. She raises another cub; he too becomes a lion who devours people, and his roar is heard in the mountains; he is taken in the nations' net and brought to Babylon. In the second part, the mother is compared to a vine planted by the water, fruitful and tall, with a strong branch that becomes a ruler's sceptre — but the vine is plucked up, cast down, the east wind withers its fruit, its strong branch is withered and fire consumes it. The chapter is, as a lament, a grief song for what has been lost rather than an oracle of future destruction, and its place in the book marks the transition from the sign-acts of chapters 4-7 and the temple vision of 8-11 to the extended oracles of 20-24.

Ezekiel 20

Israel's History of Rebellion

Chapter 20 is the most historically comprehensive oracle in the book. The elders of Israel come to inquire of the Lord, and God refuses the inquiry — three times he states he will not be inquired of by them — but delivers instead a sustained rehearsal of Israel's history as a history of repeated rebellion and repeated divine patience. The narrative covers Egypt (Israel in Egypt already had idols; God acted for his name's sake rather than because of their faithfulness), the first wilderness generation (given statutes and sabbaths; rebelled; God withheld judgment for his name's sake), the second wilderness generation (same pattern), and the entry into the land (which was followed by the same pattern of idolatry and sabbath violation). The oracle then describes a third wilderness — God will bring the new exiles back out into the wilderness of the nations and purge the rebels before bringing the remnant into the land of Israel. The chapter closes with an oracle against the high places of Israel — but this time in the coming land, not in Babylon.

Ezekiel 21

The Sword of the Lord

Chapter 21 is among the most sustained war-oracle sequences in the prophetic literature. It opens with the instruction to sigh — to perform public grief in front of the community, and when asked why, to say: because of the news that is coming; every heart will melt and every hand will be feeble. Then comes the sword oracle proper: the sword is sharpened, it is polished, it is given into the hand of the slayer. Ezekiel is told to cry and wail, for it is against the Lord's people and all the princes of Israel, and they will be thrust through with the sword. A second sword oracle follows: the king of Babylon stands at the parting of the way, at the head of two ways, using divination to decide which way to go — to Jerusalem or to Ammon. The divination falls on Jerusalem: the battering rams will be set, the siege mounds cast up. The chapter closes with a threat against Ammon and a sword oracle against the wicked prince of Israel — whose crown will be removed, whose diadem will be taken off, who will be overthrown until the one comes who has the right to judge.

Ezekiel 22

The Bloody City

Chapter 22 delivers three distinct oracles against the city of Jerusalem, all focused on the comprehensive moral failure of its institutions. The first oracle catalogs the city's specific sins: shedding blood, making idols, dishonoring father and mother, oppressing the stranger, the widow, the orphan, eating sacrifices on the mountains, committing sexual offenses, taking bribes, charging interest. The city is convicted of blood guilt that brings its days near. The second oracle uses the image of smelting: Israel has become dross — the impure waste left when metals are refined — and God will gather them into Jerusalem as silver and other metals are gathered into a furnace, blow on them with the fire of his wrath, and they will be melted. The third oracle indicts all the social groups of the city in sequence: princes, priests, officials, prophets, and people of the land. Each group is convicted of a specific form of failure appropriate to its role. The chapter closes with God seeking a man to stand in the breach — the figure of prophetic intercession that would prevent judgment — and finding none. Because none was found, he will pour out his indignation upon them.

Ezekiel 23

Oholah and Oholibah

Chapter 23 is the twin of chapter 16: another extended allegory of sexual infidelity representing Israel's political and cultic failures. Here the vehicle is two sisters, Oholah (representing Samaria, the capital of the northern kingdom) and Oholibah (representing Jerusalem, the capital of the south). Both committed infidelity in Egypt in their youth. Oholah doted on the Assyrians — soldiers with bronze helmets and shields — and they exposed her nakedness and took her sons and daughters and killed her with the sword. Oholibah saw her sister's fate and went further in her own infidelities — first with the Assyrians, then with the Babylonians, whose rulers she saw in pictures on the wall and sent messengers to bring them to her bed. Because she has gone further than her sister, the judgment will be harsher: those she sought as lovers will become her destroyers. The chapter closes with a sustained oracle against Oholibah, and with a command: you shall bear your lewdness and your idolatry, and you shall know that I am the Lord God.

Ezekiel 24

The Boiling Pot and the Unmourned Wife

Chapter 24 is the conclusion of the first major section of the book and the climactic symbolic act. It begins on a specific date — the ninth year, the tenth month, the tenth day — which Ezekiel is told to write down: this very day the king of Babylon has set himself against Jerusalem. An allegory of the boiling pot follows, in which the pot is Jerusalem and the city's blood guilt cannot be washed out; the bones are burned, the pot is set on the coals to be made clean, but its rust will not go out of it. Then the sign-act of the wife: Ezekiel is told that the desire of his eyes will be taken from him with a stroke, and that he is not to weep, not to let his tears run down, not to mourn for the dead, not to eat the bread of mourners, to bind his turban and put his shoes on his feet. His wife dies that evening, and he does in the morning as he was commanded. The community asks: will you not tell us what these things mean to us? He tells them: the temple — the desire of your eyes — will be desecrated and destroyed; your sons and daughters in the land will fall by the sword; you will not mourn, but will pine away in your iniquities. And when the surviving refugee comes to you, your mouth will be opened, and you will no longer be silent.

Ezekiel 25

Oracles against Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia

Chapter 25 opens the section of foreign-nation oracles that runs through chapter 32. Four nations are addressed in rapid succession, each in the same structure: the charge (what they did when Jerusalem fell), and the sentence (what will follow). Ammon rejoiced when the sanctuary was profaned and Judah went into exile; the people of the East will possess them. Moab said that the house of Judah was like all the other nations; the flank of Moab will be opened and its cities taken. Edom took revenge when the house of Judah fell; the Lord will stretch out his hand against Edom, and they will know what vengeance feels like. The Philistines acted in revenge with everlasting enmity; they will be destroyed. The logic of each oracle is retaliation-in-kind: the nations exulted over what they should have mourned, and the punishment is to have the same thing happen to them. The chapter is the shortest and most structurally regular in this section, serving as an introduction to the much longer oracles against Tyre and Egypt that follow.

Ezekiel 26

Tyre's fall foretold

Chapter 26 is the first and most sustained of the three chapters addressed against Tyre, the great Phoenician trading city on a rocky island off the Lebanese coast. The charge is stated bluntly: Tyre said of Jerusalem, 'She who was the gate of the peoples is broken; she has been turned over to me. I shall become rich now that she is laid waste.' The sentence comes in three stages. First, Nebuchadnezzar will come against Tyre with a great army, throwing up a siege mound and setting up battering rams. Then, after Nebuchadnezzar, many nations will come, wave after wave, and the very stones and timber of Tyre will be thrown into the sea. Finally, the city will be a bare rock — a place for the spreading of nets — and will never be rebuilt. The oracle contains some of the most vivid military imagery in the book: the dust, the chariots, the horses, the noise of cavalry filling the streets. It closes with a lament over the silenced city, the city that was once great on the seas and is now a bare rock. The image of the bare rock — the Hebrew word for Tyre, tsur, means rock — runs through the chapter as a grim pun on the city's name.

Ezekiel 27

The lament over Tyre's great ship

Chapter 27 is entirely a lament — a qinah, a dirge — over Tyre, written in the form of a ship's manifest. The prophet is told to take up a lamentation for Tyre. The city is addressed directly: you were perfect in beauty. The extended metaphor then runs through the whole chapter: Tyre is a great ship, and the lament catalogues its construction and its crew. The planks are from fir trees of Senir; the mast from a cedar of Lebanon; the oars from oaks of Bashan; the deck from cypress of the coasts of Kittim. Then the crew: the men of Sidon and Arvad are the oarsmen; the wise men of Tyre are the pilots; the men of Gebal are the caulkers. Then the cargo — the commercial network of the ancient world is mapped by the items traded: Tarshish paid in silver, iron, tin, and lead; Javan, Tubal, and Meshech traded in persons and vessels of bronze; Damascus paid in wine of Helbon; Arabia and Kedar in lambs and rams and goats. The list is enormous and systematic. Then the ship goes out on the sea and the east wind breaks it — all the goods, the mariners, the pilots sink into the sea. The merchants howl and weep and mourn over Tyre. The lament is one of the most extraordinary economic documents in ancient literature, a catalogue of the ancient Mediterranean and Near Eastern trade system seen from the vantage point of its greatest market's collapse.

Ezekiel 28

The prince of Tyre and the eden lament

Chapter 28 contains two oracles against Tyre's ruler and a brief oracle against Sidon. The first oracle addresses the prince of Tyre directly. The charge: your heart has become proud and you have said 'I am a god; I sit in the seat of the gods, in the heart of the sea.' The sentence: you are a man, not a god, and you will die the death of the uncircumcised. The second oracle is in the form of a lament for the king of Tyre — but the language immediately shifts into mythological register. The king is addressed as one who was in Eden, the garden of God; who walked among the stones of fire; who was the anointed cherub that covereth; who was perfect in beauty from the day he was created until unrighteousness was found in him. He was cast out by the guardian cherub from the midst of the stones of fire. His sin: pride in his beauty and corruption of his wisdom. The passage has been read across the centuries as a description of the fall of Satan — a reading absent from the text itself but enormously influential in Christian tradition. On its own terms it is a court lament using mythological language to describe the overreach and fall of a proud king. The chapter closes with a brief oracle against Sidon, and then a promise of restoration to the house of Israel.

Ezekiel 29

Oracle against Pharaoh the great dragon

Chapter 29 opens the four chapters of oracles against Egypt, the longest of the foreign-nation sections and second only to the Tyre section in literary elaboration. The oracle is dated to 587 BCE, the tenth year of Jehoiachin's exile — the year before Jerusalem fell. The charge against Egypt is specific and theological: Egypt has been a staff of reed to the house of Israel, and when Israel leaned on that reed it splintered and pierced their hands and made their shoulder shake. The metaphor of Egypt as a broken reed was already current in the tradition (it appears in the Assyrian envoy's speech in Isaiah 36); Ezekiel takes it and develops it. The oracle then shifts to a direct address to Pharaoh using a startling image: you are the great dragon lying in the midst of your Nile, saying 'My Nile is my own; I made it.' The sentence: the Lord will put hooks in Pharaoh's jaws and drag him out of the Nile, and him and the fish that stick to his scales, and throw them into the wilderness. Egypt will be a desolation for forty years. The oracle also contains a secondary promise: after forty years Egypt will be restored — but it will never again be the great power it was; it will be the least of the kingdoms, so that Israel will never again trust in Egypt as a support.

Ezekiel 30

The day of Egypt

Chapter 30 contains two oracles. The first is a general lament over Egypt in the language of the Day of the Lord — a day of clouds, a time of doom for the nations, a day that will be dark for Egypt. The sword will come, the hired troops of Cush and Put and Lud will fall by the sword, the multitude of Egypt will perish. The cities named are systematically desolated: Memphis, Pathros, Zoan, Thebes, Pelusium, Heliopolis. The list moves through the geography of Egypt from north to south and from delta to upper Nile, as if the oracle is mapping the full extent of the destruction. The second oracle, precisely dated to the first month of the eleventh year (586 BCE), returns to the image introduced in chapter 29 — the arms of Pharaoh. Nebuchadnezzar has broken the arm of Pharaoh at the battle of Carchemish (605 BCE) and will break the other arm too; the arms that once held the sword will hang limp. The oracle is a sustained military image: Pharaoh's power is figured as arms that hold a sword, and the breaking of those arms is the figure of his military defeat. Against the broken arms of Pharaoh the oracle sets the strong arms of Nebuchadnezzar, which the Lord will strengthen to take Egypt.

Ezekiel 31

Pharaoh as the cedar of Lebanon

Chapter 31 is an allegory addressed to Pharaoh in the form of a comparison to the great cedar of Lebanon. The oracle is dated to the third month of the eleventh year (June 587 BCE). The rhetorical structure is unusual: the oracle begins by addressing Pharaoh and then, as the comparison develops, appears to address Assyria as the cedar — and then returns to Pharaoh as the nation that will share Assyria's fate. The cedar is described at length: its height reached the clouds, it was beautiful in greatness, its boughs gave shade, the birds of the air nested in its branches, all the great nations lived under its shadow. No cedar in the garden of God was its equal; no fir tree compared to its branches; no plane tree was like its boughs. The nations trembled at its fall when the Lord delivered it to the hand of the mighty one of the nations who dealt with it as its wickedness deserved. It was cast down to Sheol, and the trees of Eden — the choice and best of Lebanon — were comforted below the earth, knowing that they too lay in the realm of the dead. The allegory then addresses Pharaoh directly: you will be brought down with the trees of Eden to the earth below, among the uncircumcised, with those who were slain by the sword. This is Pharaoh, and all his multitude.

Ezekiel 32

Lamentation for Egypt in the underworld

Chapter 32 closes the Egyptian oracle section with two laments. The first is dated to the twelfth month of the twelfth year (February or March 585 BCE) and is addressed to Pharaoh. The prophet is told to take up a lamentation for him. Pharaoh thought he was a young lion, but he is more like a great dragon in the sea. The Lord will spread a net over him, haul him up in his drag-net, throw him on the open field, and the birds and the beasts will feed on him. Then the Lord will cover the heavens and make the stars dark, the sun covered with a cloud, the moon will not give its light — the cosmic darkness that accompanies great falls in the prophetic tradition. The second lament is dated two weeks later and is a sustained vision of the underworld. Egypt will be brought down to Sheol and laid in the pit. Then the chapter opens out into an extraordinary list of nations that are already in Sheol: Assyria is there with all its multitude; Elam is there; Meshech and Tubal are there; Edom is there with all its princes; the princes of the north are all there; Egypt will come and lie with them, among the uncircumcised, those slain by the sword. The repetition of the phrase — the uncircumcised, slain by the sword — becomes a kind of liturgical drumbeat, and the chapter functions as a gathering of the empires in the realm of the dead, with Egypt the latest arrival.

Ezekiel 33

The watchman recommissioned; news of Jerusalem's fall

Chapter 33 is the great hinge of the book of Ezekiel. The prophet's commission as watchman, given in chapters 2-3, is reaffirmed at the start of this chapter in almost identical language: if the watchman sees the sword coming and does not warn the people, and the people die in their iniquity, the watchman's blood is required. But if the watchman blows the trumpet and the people do not heed, the people die in their iniquity and the watchman has saved his soul. The individual accountability doctrine of chapters 18 and 3 is reaffirmed: each person will be judged for their own conduct, not their ancestors'. Then, in one of the most dramatic moments in the book, the chapter describes what the prophet has been waiting for: in the twelfth year of the exile, in the tenth month, on the fifth day, a refugee from Jerusalem comes to Ezekiel and says, 'The city has fallen.' The night before the refugee arrived, the hand of the Lord had been upon Ezekiel, and in the morning his mouth was opened and he was no longer mute. The fall of Jerusalem, which the first half of the book has been prophecying and preparing for, has now arrived in the text — as news, brought by a survivor, in the mouth of a newly-speaking prophet. The second half of the book begins here.

Ezekiel 34

False shepherds and the one good shepherd

Chapter 34 is the parable of the shepherds, one of the most sustained pastoral allegories in the Hebrew prophets. The shepherd metaphor for rulers was common across the ancient Near East, and Ezekiel's development of it here is among the most elaborate. The charges against the false shepherds (the rulers of Israel) are catalogued with precision: they ate the fat, they clothed themselves with the wool, they slaughtered the fatlings, but they did not feed the flock. The weak they have not strengthened, the sick they have not healed, the injured they have not bound up, the strayed they have not brought back, the lost they have not sought. With force and harshness they have ruled the flock. Because there was no shepherd, the sheep were scattered over all the face of the earth and became food for wild beasts. The sentence: the Lord will take the flock out of the shepherds' hands and hold the shepherds accountable. Then the restoration: the Lord himself will seek his sheep, gather them from the nations, and bring them back to their own land. He will feed them on the mountains of Israel. He will seek the lost and bring back the strayed and bind up the injured. And he will set over them one shepherd, his servant David, who will feed them and be their shepherd. The chapter is one of the key Old Testament sources for the New Testament's shepherd imagery.

Ezekiel 35

Oracle against Mount Seir

Chapter 35 is a brief oracle against Mount Seir, the territory of Edom, the neighbouring nation to the south and east of Judah that was Israel's perennial enemy. The oracle functions as a brief interruption in the restoration sequence — placed between chapter 34 (the good shepherd) and chapter 36 (the new heart) — because Edom's perpetual enmity is the negative context that makes the restoration more pointed. The charge is precise: Edom had perpetual enmity against Israel, and when Jerusalem fell they said, These two nations, Israel and Judah, will be mine and we will possess them. The sin is not merely political hostility but opportunistic greed at the moment of another people's catastrophe. The sentence: the Lord will stretch out his hand against Mount Seir and make it a desolation and a waste. Because Edom rejoiced over the inheritance of the house of Israel when it was desolate, so the Lord will deal with Edom. You will be desolate, Mount Seir, and all of Edom — and they will know that I am the Lord. The oracle mirrors the structure of the oracles against the nations in chapters 25-32, and its placement here, just before the restoration of Israel, gives the restoration its negative counterpart: the nation that exulted over Israel's desolation will itself be desolate.

Ezekiel 36

The new heart and the new spirit

Chapter 36 is the theological centre of the restoration section and contains one of the most theologically significant promises in the Hebrew prophetic tradition. The chapter opens with an oracle to the mountains of Israel — a deliberate counterpart to the oracles against the mountains of Edom — promising their restoration and fruitfulness. Then comes the great theological declaration. The Lord had scattered Israel among the nations not because Israel deserved restoration but because his holy name, which the house of Israel had profaned among the nations, required vindication. I am not doing this for your sake, says the Lord, but for the sake of my holy name. Then the promise: I will sprinkle clean water on you and you will be clean from all your uncleanness. I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you. I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh. I will put my spirit within you and cause you to walk in my statutes and be careful to obey my rules. The oracle then returns to the land: the desolate land will be cultivated instead of lying desolate. The nations will know that I, the Lord, have rebuilt the ruined places. The passage is one of the principal Old Testament foundations for the New Testament's language of new birth, regeneration, and the indwelling of the Spirit.

Ezekiel 37

The valley of dry bones and the two sticks

Chapter 37 contains two visions. The first and most famous is the valley of dry bones. The hand of the Lord brings the prophet out and sets him in the middle of a valley full of bones — a great many, very dry. The Lord asks: Son of man, can these bones live? Ezekiel answers: O Lord God, you know. He is told to prophesy to the bones. As he prophesies there is a rattling, and the bones come together. Sinews appear, then flesh, then skin — but there is no breath. He is told to prophesy to the four winds. Breath comes, the slain live, and they stand as an exceeding great army. The vision is then interpreted explicitly: the bones are the whole house of Israel, who say their bones are dried up and their hope is lost. The Lord will open their graves and bring them home. The second vision is the two sticks. The prophet is told to take a stick and write on it 'For Judah and all Israel associated with him,' and another stick 'For Joseph, the stick of Ephraim.' He is to join them together in his hand into one stick. When the people ask what this means, the answer is: the Lord will join the stick of Ephraim with the stick of Judah and make them one nation in the land, on the mountains of Israel, with one king over them. One nation, one king, one shepherd — his servant David — one covenant. The reunification of the divided kingdoms is the second great restoration promise of this chapter.

Ezekiel 38

The war of Gog: the great coalition comes

Chapters 38 and 39 are the oracle against Gog of Magog, the strangest and most apocalyptically charged passage in Ezekiel. Gog is addressed directly: the Lord is against him and will put hooks in his jaws and bring him out from his place in the far north. He will come with a great coalition — Persia, Cush, Put, Gomer, Beth-togarmah from the far reaches of the north — mounted warriors, a great host, with bucklers, shields, and swords. The oracle is set in a future time — in the latter days, after Israel has been restored and is living securely in the land. At that future time Gog will be stirred up and come against the mountains of Israel like a storm cloud covering the land. The purpose of the oracle is stated with unusual explicitness: Gog will be brought to the land so that the nations may know the Lord, when through Gog the Lord shows his holiness before their eyes. The shaking of the earth that accompanies Gog's coming is described in cosmic terms: the fish and the birds and the beasts and all the people on the face of the earth will quake before the Lord. Chapter 38 describes the approach of Gog and the terror of the day; chapter 39 narrates the destruction of Gog and its aftermath. The two chapters together are the last great prophetic intervention before the vision of the new temple in chapters 40-48.

Ezekiel 39

Gog's defeat and Israel's restoration

Chapter 39 narrates the destruction of Gog's army and its aftermath. The oracle continues directly from chapter 38. Gog will fall on the mountains of Israel, he and all his hordes, and the Lord will give him as food to every kind of bird and to the wild animals. It will take seven months for the house of Israel to bury the dead so as to cleanse the land; there will be special burial parties, and any traveller who sees a bone will mark it with a sign for the burial parties. It will take seven years to burn the weapons — the shields, bucklers, bows, arrows, war clubs, and spears. The scale of the destruction is figured in the time required to clean it up. Then the oracle turns to the nations: the Lord will display his glory among the nations, and all the nations will see his judgement that he has executed and the hand that he has laid on them. The chapter then closes with what is structurally the climax of the restoration section before the temple vision: I will restore the fortunes of Jacob and have mercy on the whole house of Israel. I will not hide my face from them anymore, for I have poured out my spirit on the house of Israel. The spirit poured out on Israel closes the arc that began with the new spirit promised in chapter 36 and the breath breathed into the dry bones in chapter 37.

Ezekiel 40

Vision of the new temple: the outer courts

Chapter 40 opens the last major section of the book — nine chapters describing a visionary temple in elaborate architectural detail. The date is precise: the twenty-fifth year of the exile, on the tenth day of the first month, in the fourteenth year after the destruction of Jerusalem. The prophet is brought in visions of God to a very high mountain where he sees, on the south side, something like the structure of a city. A man is standing in the gateway whose appearance is like bronze, with a linen cord and a measuring reed in his hand. The man takes the prophet through the new temple, measuring as he goes, and Ezekiel is told to pay attention and report everything he sees. The chapter measures the outer east gate — its threshold, its cells, its vestibule, its windows and palm trees — the outer north gate, the outer south gate, the outer court and its pavement, the inner south gate, the inner east gate, the inner north gate. Each gate is described with the same set of measurements: the gate has a threshold, then three alcoves on each side with posts between them, then a vestibule facing inward with posts and a palm tree on each post. The measurements are given in cubits, using a cubit-and-a-handbreadth measuring reed. The chapter is architectural in its precision and cumulative in its effect; the reader is walked through a gate-by-gate, court-by-court assembly of a building that no one has ever built.

Ezekiel 41

The inner sanctuary measured

Chapter 41 continues the architectural tour into the inner parts of the temple building itself. After the gates and courts of chapter 40, the measuring man brings the prophet to the nave — the main hall of the temple — and measures its jambs, the threshold, its breadth. The man then enters the inner sanctuary, the most holy place, and measures its width and length; Ezekiel apparently stays outside while the man enters. Then the wall of the temple is measured — its thickness, and the side chambers built against the outer wall, three stories high, thirty chambers in each story. The width of the side chambers increases from story to story. Then the width of the space around the temple building and the outer court; then the length of the building to the west with its wall on both sides; then the measurements of the broad court at the rear. The interior of the temple is then described: the nave had a wooden threshold, and the walls on all sides — from the floor to the windows — were covered with carved figures of cherubim and palm trees alternating, each cherub with two faces (a human face toward the palm tree on one side and a lion's face toward the palm tree on the other). This interior decoration — cherubim and palms all around — echoes the Solomonic temple but is more systematically described here, giving the whole interior the character of the garden of God expressed in architecture.

Ezekiel 42

The priestly chambers and the total boundary

Chapter 42 completes the measuring of the main temple complex with two elements: the priestly chambers on the north and south sides of the building, and the measurement of the total outer boundary of the sacred area. The priestly chambers are described in terms of their function: they are the holy chambers where the priests who approach the Lord will eat the most holy offerings. There they will deposit the most holy offerings — the grain offering, the sin offering, and the guilt offering — because the place is holy. The priests who enter the inner court must change clothes when they go out to the outer court, so that they do not bring holiness out to the people. The concern here is the theology of gradations of holiness — the inner court is more holy than the outer court, and the priests must be the mediators who manage the transition. The chapter closes with the man measuring the outer wall of the entire complex: five hundred cubits on each side — a perfect square, five hundred by five hundred — to separate the holy from the common. The total boundary measurement is the last act of the measuring tour that began in chapter 40. The complete temple complex is now measured, and it is a perfect square of five hundred cubits: a symmetrical and bounded sacred space, with holiness concentrated at the centre and decreasing toward the edges.

Ezekiel 43

The glory returns to the new temple

Chapter 43 is the theological culmination of the entire temple vision: the glory of the God of Israel returns. The man brings Ezekiel to the east gate, and the glory of the God of Israel is coming from the east, and the sound of his coming is like the sound of many waters, and the earth shines with his glory. The vision is exactly like the vision Ezekiel had seen when he came to destroy the city — the chariot vision and the glory — and exactly like the vision he had seen by the river Chebar. The glory comes from the east gate — the gate by which it departed in chapter 11 — and enters the temple, and fills the house. The glory has returned. The spirit lifts the prophet and brings him to the inner court, and the glory of the Lord fills the temple. The Lord then speaks from the temple: this is the place of my throne, and the place of the soles of my feet, where I will dwell in the midst of the people of Israel forever. The chapter then gives instructions for the altar of burnt offering: its dimensions, the feast of dedication that will consecrate it, the seven days of atonement rituals. The return of the glory and the reconsecration of the altar together mark the moment at which the new temple becomes inhabitable — the divine presence has come home, and the sacrificial system that will be its permanent context is described.

Ezekiel 44

The east gate sealed; the Levites and Zadokite priests

Chapter 44 deals with two major subjects: the sealing of the east gate and the regulations for the Levites and priests. The man brings Ezekiel back to the outer east gate, and it is shut. The Lord tells him: this gate shall remain shut and no one shall enter by it, for the Lord, the God of Israel, has entered by it; it shall remain shut. The prince, however, may sit in the gateway to eat bread before the Lord — he may enter by the way of the vestibule and go out by the same way. The gate is sealed for ordinary use because it is the gate of the divine presence; it becomes the reserved and sacred east face of the complex. Then comes the extensive legislation on access. Foreigners who are uncircumcised in heart and flesh may not enter the sanctuary. The Levites who went far from the Lord when Israel went astray — who served the people before their idols — will bear their punishment; they will be keepers of the gates of the temple and ministers of the house, slaughtering burnt offerings and sacrifices for the people, but they will not approach the holy things of the inner court. Only the Levitical priests, the sons of Zadok, who kept the charge of the sanctuary when the people of Israel went astray — they shall come near to the Lord and minister to him. The Zadokite distinction is a major polemic within the postexilic priestly politics of the period: the Zadokites, Solomon's high-priestly line, claim the inner sanctuary; the ordinary Levites are demoted to outer-court service.

Ezekiel 45

The sacred district and just weights

Chapter 45 moves from the temple regulations to the regulations for the sacred district and for the prince. When the land is allotted, a sacred portion shall be set apart: a square of twenty-five thousand cubits by twenty thousand, with the sanctuary in the middle. Within it a section will be for the priests who serve the sanctuary; another section for the Levites who serve the temple; the city will be in the middle of what remains. On both sides of the sacred district and the city — to the west and to the east — the prince shall have a portion of the land. The prince thus flanks the sacred district; his territory surrounds the holy area on both sides. This geographical arrangement ensures that the prince cannot encroach on the sanctuary's land, a provision that addresses one of the recurring prophetic complaints about the pre-exilic kings' behaviour toward the temple's property. Then comes the legislation about justice: the prince shall use just balances, a just ephah, and a just bath. The ephah and the bath shall be the same measure — a tenth of a homer. The shekel shall be twenty gerahs. These are the regulations for the offerings that the prince shall provide: for the Passover, for the Feast of Booths, for the regular grain offerings and burnt offerings — all specified with precise quantities. The chapter is a piece of political theology: the prince's territorial and commercial life is regulated in relation to the sacred centre.

Ezekiel 46

Regulations for the prince and the regular offerings

Chapter 46 provides detailed regulations for the use of the gates and for the prince's regular offerings. The inner east gate — which was the main ceremonial access to the inner court — is shut on the six working days but opened on the Sabbath and on the new moon. On those days the prince shall enter by the vestibule of the gate from outside and stand at the post of the gate while the priests offer his burnt offering and peace offerings; he shall worship at the threshold of the gate and then go out. The people shall also worship at the entrance of that gate on the Sabbaths and new moons. The prince's entrance and exit regulations are specified further: when the people come for the appointed feasts, he who enters by the north gate shall go out by the south gate, and vice versa, so that no one returns the way they came. Then the daily offerings for the prince — a lamb without blemish every morning with a grain offering of one-sixth of an ephah. The chapter then addresses the prince's land grants: if the prince makes a gift from his inheritance to one of his sons, it belongs to his sons; but if he makes a gift to one of his servants, it belongs to the servant only until the jubilee, when it reverts to the prince. The prince may not take land from the people's inheritance. The chapter closes with a description of the kitchen arrangements for boiling the sacrifices and baking the offerings.

Ezekiel 47

The river from the threshold and the division of the land

Chapter 47 is the most beautiful chapter in the final section and one of the most celebrated images in the Hebrew Bible. The man brings Ezekiel back to the entrance of the temple, and water is flowing out from under the threshold of the temple toward the east, from below the right side of the temple, south of the altar. The man takes the prophet out through the north gate and around to the outer east gate, and here the water is coming out from the south side of the east gate. The man measures one thousand cubits into the water: it is ankle-deep. One thousand more: knee-deep. One thousand more: to the loins. One thousand more: a river to swim in, a river that cannot be crossed. The man asks: have you seen this, son of man? He leads the prophet back along the bank. On both sides of the river, trees are growing — very many trees on both sides. The man tells him: wherever the river goes, every living creature that swarms will live. The river flows into the sea — the eastern sea, the Dead Sea — and the water of the sea becomes fresh. Fishermen will stand beside it from En-gedi to En-eglaim. The trees that grow on the bank will never wilt; they will bear fresh fruit every month, and their leaves will be for healing. The chapter then specifies the boundaries of the land for the restored Israel — from the great sea in the west to the Jordan in the east, from Lebo-hamath in the north to Tamar in the south — and directs that the land shall be shared with the foreigners who dwell among Israel, who shall be treated as native-born.

Ezekiel 48

The tribal allotments and the city named 'The LORD Is There'

Chapter 48, the final chapter of Ezekiel, lists the allotments of land for the twelve tribes and gives the dimensions of the city and its gates, closing the whole book with the naming of the city. The allotments run from north to south: Dan, Asher, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, Reuben, Judah — then the sacred district with the sanctuary, the priests' portion, the Levites' portion, and the city — then Benjamin, Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun, Gad. The sacred district is at the exact centre of the north-south arrangement, with seven tribes above and five below. The city — a square of forty-five hundred cubits on each side — has three gates on each of its four sides, named for the twelve tribes: on the north, Reuben, Judah, Levi; on the east, Joseph, Benjamin, Dan; on the south, Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun; on the west, Gad, Asher, Naphtali. The city gate arrangement closely prefigures the new Jerusalem of Revelation 21, which has twelve gates named for the twelve tribes. The book closes with the naming of the city: the name of the city from that day shall be, The LORD Is There — in Hebrew, Yhwh Shammah. The name is the answer to everything the book has described: the departure of the glory in chapters 8-11, the exile and the suffering, the restoration promises, the river and the trees, the measured temple. All of it converges in the closing three words: the Lord is there.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Chariot Vision

The opening chapter of Ezekiel is one of the most visually elaborate and theologically dense passages in the Hebrew Bible, and one of the most influential single visions in the entire history of Jewish and Christian mysticism.

The Prophet's Body as Sign

More than any other prophet in the Hebrew Bible, Ezekiel is required to perform his message with his own body, and the requirement is sustained, costly, and at times extreme.

The Dry Bones

The most quoted vision in Ezekiel, and one of the most influential single passages in the Hebrew prophetic tradition: the prophet is brought to a valley full of dry bones and told to prophesy to them.

The Glory Departing and Returning

One of the most carefully developed theological themes in Ezekiel is the movement of the divine glory — the kavod — out of and back into the Jerusalem temple.

The Visionary Temple

The last nine chapters of Ezekiel — chapters 40 through 48 — contain the longest and most architecturally detailed visionary passage in the Hebrew Bible.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Ezekiel
Priest-prophet in exile

Born into a priestly family in Jerusalem; deported to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BCE. Settled by the river Chebar (probably the Kabaru canal near Nippur) in the exile community. Called to prophecy in 593 BCE by the chariot vision. Struck dumb for years, recovering his speech only when news of Jerusalem's fall arrives. Performs more sustained symbolic acts with his body than any other prophet in the Hebrew Bible. His wife, called the desire of his eyes, dies suddenly and he is forbidden to mourn — the last and most personally costly of the bodily signs.

The Four Living Creatures
Bearers of the divine throne

The four creatures of the opening vision of chapter 1 (identified explicitly as cherubim in chapter 10). Each creature has four faces — man, lion, ox, eagle — and four wings; they move straight forward without turning. Beside each creature is a wheel with a wheel within it, their rims full of eyes. The founding image of the Merkavah tradition and, in Christian iconography, the symbols of the four evangelists. They are the most visually elaborated angelic figures in the Hebrew Bible.

The Elders of Israel
Exile community leadership

The leaders of the Jewish exile community in Babylon, who appear repeatedly sitting before Ezekiel and asking for a word from the Lord. They come to him in chapter 8 (mid-conversation the prophet is taken in vision to Jerusalem), in chapter 14 (told they carry idols in their hearts), in chapter 20 (the whole history of Israel rehearsed as continuous rebellion), and in chapter 33 (listening as one listens to a beautiful song, without doing what they hear). They are the community to whom the prophecy is addressed and on whom it works — or does not.

The Dry Bones
Vision of restoration

The image at the centre of the most quoted vision in the book — chapter 37. The prophet is taken to a valley full of dry bones, asked whether they can live, told to prophesy to them. As he prophesies, the bones come together, take on flesh, receive the breath of life, and stand as an exceeding great army. The bones are interpreted explicitly as the whole house of Israel, who say their bones are dried up and their hope is lost. They have been read for two and a half thousand years as the scriptural foundation of the doctrine of bodily resurrection and as a vision of the future restoration of Israel.

Gog of Magog
Eschatological enemy

The figure named in chapters 38–39, an enemy from the far north — Gog, prince of the land of Magog, ruler of Meshech and Tubal — who in the latter days will lead a great coalition of nations against the restored Israel and be destroyed by direct divine action. The historical and geographical referents have been the subject of two thousand years of speculation. In Ezekiel he serves a structural function: after the restoration prophecies of chapters 33–37, before the visionary temple of 40–48, the war with Gog is the last decisive intervention God will make on Israel's behalf, and the prelude to the new temple. The figure is invoked again in Revelation 20.

The Man with the Measuring Reed
Visionary guide

The figure who appears at the start of chapter 40 — a man whose appearance was like brass, with a line of flax in his hand and a measuring reed — and who guides the prophet through the entire visionary temple of chapters 40–48. He measures the outer wall, the gates, the chambers, the courts, the temple building, the altar, and the depth of the river at successive distances. He is not named; he is the type of the divine guide, the angelic interpreter, who in later apocalyptic literature (in Daniel, in Revelation, in 4 Ezra) becomes a recognisable figure. His measuring is the vision's theology: what God has built will be measured, and what God has measured stands.
