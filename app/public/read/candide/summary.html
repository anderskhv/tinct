Candide

1759 CE · by Voltaire

The book in brief

Candide is the story of a sweet-natured young man raised in a Westphalian castle and taught by his tutor Pangloss that this is the best of all possible worlds. He is in love with Cunégonde, the baron's daughter. They are caught kissing behind a screen; Candide is kicked out of the castle and into the world Pangloss has been describing. Within a few chapters he has been press-ganged into the Bulgarian army, flogged for desertion, witnessed a battlefield massacre, survived the 1755 Lisbon earthquake, watched Pangloss hanged at an auto-da-fé, and stabbed Cunégonde's brother in self-defense. He flees to Paraguay, finds the lost city of El Dorado, loses most of his fortune again, and finally tracks Cunégonde down on the shore of the Sea of Marmara, where she has become, the narrator notes, both ugly and bad-tempered. The novel ends with the survivors farming a small plot of land outside Constantinople.

Voltaire wrote it in 1758, in his sixties, at his estate at Les Délices near Geneva. He published it anonymously in January 1759 in five countries simultaneously to outrun the censors. It was instantly banned and instantly read; thirty editions appeared the first year. It is short — about 30,000 words across thirty brisk chapters — and written in an unhurried, ironic French that makes the violence land harder. Voltaire denied authorship for the rest of his life and never bothered to convince anyone.

It is the founding work of philosophical satire and one of the funniest serious books ever written. The comedy is the argument: the cheerful prose narrating slavery, rape, and mass killing forces the reader to register what Pangloss's optimism actually has to be optimistic about.

Why it still matters

Optimism Killed By Comedy

The novel's target is a serious eighteenth-century philosophical position: Leibniz's argument that since God is perfectly good and chose this world out of all possible worlds, this world must be the best one available, evil included. Voltaire could have written a treatise against it; he had written treatises before. Instead he sent a boy named Candide through one disaster after another, with Pangloss interpreting each as further evidence of optimism, and let the absurdity of the gap between theory and event do the demolition. Pangloss diagnoses syphilis as a necessary ingredient of the best world; he is hanged at the auto-da-fé and concludes the rope was for the best. The argument lands because it makes you laugh at exactly the moments you should not be laughing. Few philosophical refutations in any century have done their work more efficiently — and the book is still the test case for what comedy can do that essays cannot.

A Tour of the Eighteenth Century It Lived In

Candide is a guided tour of every cruelty the European Enlightenment was actually presiding over while its philosophers wrote about progress. Voltaire moves Candide through the Seven Years' War ("a million regimented assassins"), the 1755 Lisbon earthquake and its accompanying auto-da-fé where heretics were burned to placate God, the Atlantic slave trade as encountered in Suriname — Candide meets a sugar-mill slave with no hand and no leg, and is told this is the price of the sugar Europeans eat — Jesuit colonial governance in Paraguay, and the depredations of European warfare on Ottoman territory. Each setting is a real place doing real things in 1758. The novel is short on exposition because its readers had read the same newspapers Voltaire had; he could allude to a horror in two sentences and be confident his audience would supply the rest. Its method — the comic travelogue that exposes systemic violence — has been borrowed by every political satirist since, from Twain to the present.

Cultivate Your Garden

After everyone has been beaten down to size, the survivors settle on a little farm outside Constantinople. They are bored. The old woman complains it is worse than the misery they have known. They visit a Turkish farmer who lives quietly with his children and ignores court politics, and his calm impresses them. Pangloss tries one last metaphysical lecture; Candide cuts him off with the line that has become the novel's slogan: "Il faut cultiver notre jardin." Voltaire is not telling readers to retreat from the world, and not exactly endorsing quietism. He is rejecting the grand theological systems he has spent thirty chapters dismantling and proposing in their place useful work, near at hand, on a problem one can actually affect. As an answer to suffering it is modest. It is also one of the few sentences in eighteenth-century literature that anyone alive today can use without translation.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

An attack on optimism.

Pangloss's doctrine — that everything is arranged for the best in the best of all possible worlds — is Voltaire's target from the first page. Each disaster is a refutation. I want to follow the argument: how does the novel build its case against Leibniz, and does it ever offer anything in return?

The Lisbon earthquake and theodicy.

In 1755, an earthquake killed sixty thousand people in Lisbon on All Saints' Day. Voltaire was appalled that philosophers tried to reconcile it with divine goodness. Candide was partly written in response. The earthquake scene is where the satire turns darkest — and most serious.

The picaresque as moral argument.

Candide is a picaresque novel: a naive hero dragged through a series of episodes, each exposing a different corner of human cruelty or folly. The form is the argument. By accumulating disasters — war, slavery, Inquisition, colonial violence — Voltaire makes his case not through logic but through sheer accumulation.

"Cultivate your garden" as a way of life.

The famous closing line is not a gardening tip. It is a rejection of abstract theorising in favour of concrete, useful work. Candide's final answer to suffering is not stoicism, not optimism, not pessimism — it is a form of pragmatism that Voltaire seems to mean seriously. I want to read the whole novel asking what it is building toward.

Key figures

Candide
The Innocent

The illegitimate nephew of a Westphalian baron, raised on Pangloss's optimism and in love with the baron's daughter. He is gentle, credulous, and durable in a way that becomes almost comic — he survives flogging, shipwreck, the Inquisition, and the loss of nearly everyone he loves while trying to keep believing his tutor was right. His gradual disenchantment is the novel's spine. By the last chapter he has stopped quoting Pangloss and started planting onions.

Pangloss
The Philosopher

Voltaire's parody of Leibniz, a tutor who teaches "metaphysico-theologo-cosmolo-nigology" and the doctrine that everything has a sufficient reason for being precisely as it is. He survives syphilis from a chambermaid, hanging at the Lisbon auto-da-fé, dissection by an anatomist, and slavery in the Ottoman galleys, and concludes from each disaster that this is still the best of all possible worlds. His optimism is not stupidity — it is the specific intellectual failure of a system that cannot be falsified by any evidence.

Cunégonde
The Beloved

The baron's daughter, fresh and rosy and the first thing Candide loses. The novel reports what happens to her with brutal economy: her parents murdered, her body raped by soldiers, her person sold from a merchant to a Grand Inquisitor and finally to a Turkish prince. Candide spends the novel trying to find her. When he does, she is ugly and ill-tempered, and he marries her anyway. Voltaire's joke is that the object of romantic idealism is also a person, and people do not stay what the lover imagined.

Cacambo
The Pragmatist

Candide's valet, picked up in Cadiz: a former Jesuit chorister of mixed Spanish and Inca descent. He speaks several languages, knows how to bribe and bargain, and consistently solves the problem in front of him while Candide and Pangloss philosophize. He is the one who navigates them through Paraguay and into El Dorado. He is loyal, inventive, and ironic, and the closest thing in the novel to someone Voltaire wholly admires.

Martin
The Pessimist

A Manichaean scholar Candide selects on the voyage from Suriname specifically because his bleak view of the world will balance Pangloss's. Martin believes the earth has been abandoned to a malevolent power and that suffering is not a glitch in providence but its operating principle. Almost every prediction he makes turns out correct, which is its own kind of problem. He is right and useless, while Cacambo is partly wrong and effective. The novel takes that contrast seriously.

The Old Woman
The Survivor

Cunégonde's servant on the run from Lisbon. Her chapters of autobiography are the bleakest in the book: born the daughter of a pope and a princess, she has been raped by pirates, sold into slavery in Morocco, used as currency on a starving ship where her left buttock was cut off and eaten. She tells all of this without self-pity. She asks every passenger she meets whether they have not at some point wanted to kill themselves. Most have. She finds the question oddly clarifying, and she keeps living.
