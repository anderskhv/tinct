Summary

Cacambo persuades the Turkish skipper sailing Sultan Achmet back to Constantinople to take Candide and Martin. Candide tells Martin on the way: we have just dined with six dethroned kings, and one of them was a beggar; perhaps Pangloss was right — all is for the best. Martin only hopes so. Candide runs to Cacambo: where is Cunégonde? On the Propontis, washing dishes for a deposed prince named Ragotski; ugly now — horribly ugly. Candide is dismayed but commits himself: a man of honor keeps his word.

They reach the Bosporus. Candide pays a high ransom for Cacambo and they all board a galley to search the banks of the Propontis for Cunégonde. Among the crew are two slaves who row very badly and to whose bare shoulders the Levantine captain applies blows from a bull's pizzle. By a natural impulse Candide looks at them with more attention, and draws closer in pity. Their features, though greatly disfigured, bear a slight resemblance to those of Pangloss and the unhappy Jesuit Baron. He says to Cacambo: if I had not seen Pangloss hanged, and if I had not had the misfortune of killing the Baron, I would think those were the two rowing.

At the names of the Baron and Pangloss the two galley slaves let out a loud cry, cling to their bench, drop their oars. The captain runs up and redoubles his blows. Candide cries: stop! I will give you whatever money you please. "What! is it Candide?" says one. "What! is it Candide?" says the other. The Levantine captain demands fifty thousand sequins to release the two — since these dogs of Christian slaves are barons and metaphysicians. Candide agrees on the spot but asks first for Cunégonde. The captain has already turned his ship about. Candide sells a hundred-thousand-sequin diamond for fifty thousand and pays the ransom. Pangloss throws himself at Candide's feet and bathes them with tears. The Baron thanks him with a nod and promises to repay at the first opportunity. They set out together to deliver Cunégonde.
