The Art of War — who's who

The Treatise — the figures behind the text.

The Art of War has no characters in the narrative sense. It is a treatise, not a story. The cast here is the handful of historical figures who shaped how later readers received it: the attributed author, his royal patron, the first commentator who used it as a working general, and the British officer whose translation is the English text in the Tinct reader.

The author and his tradition

AUTHOR
Sun Tzu
Master Sun — attributed author

Sun Wu, whose existence as a single historical person is disputed. Sima Qian places him at the court of King Helü of Wu in the late sixth century BCE, but the language and military detail of the text point to a later period. Many scholars read the book as the work of a tradition rather than one author. In Calthrop's translation each chapter opens with "Sun the Master said," and the voice that follows — terse, practical, unsentimental — is what readers now mean by Sun Tzu.

PATRON
King Helü of Wu
The patron of the tradition

Ruler of Wu from 514 to 496 BCE and the king before whom Sun Wu is said to have shown his methods. In the story Sima Qian tells, Sun Wu drills the palace women, executes the two royal favourites leading them when they laugh at his orders, and turns the rest into an obedient company. Whatever its truth, the scene makes the book's point about command: the ruler appoints the general and then stays out of his way. Chapter 3 lists the ways a prince can hobble his own army.

Commentators and translators

COMMENTATOR
Cao Cao
First commentator, warlord of the late Han

The most influential reader in the text's history. Cao Cao (155–220 CE) was a campaigning general who annotated the Art of War for use in the field, and his notes stand at the head of the Chinese commentary tradition. He is also the historical model for the villain of the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, though the real man was more complicated than the novel allows.

TRANSLATOR
E. F. Calthrop
British Army officer, 1908 translator

Captain Everard Ferguson Calthrop brought the Art of War into English from the text as transmitted in Japan. His first version, Sonshi, appeared in 1905; the revised translation, The Book of War, was published by John Murray in 1908. His English is plain and soldierly — "War is a thing of pretence," "An army may be likened to water" — and is the original text in the Tinct reader. Calthrop died in 1915.
