The Art of War — themes & analysis

Five threads through the treatise

The Art of War can be read in an afternoon, and generals, ministers and commentators have annotated it for more than two thousand years. Five threads run through all thirteen chapters.

In this analysis
  1. Subduing the enemy without fighting
  2. Knowing the enemy and knowing yourself
  3. Form, spirit, and the stone on the mountain
  4. Pretence, initiative, and emptiness
  5. The cost of war and the closing argument

1 · Subduing the enemy without fighting

the supreme art

Chapter 3 sets out the ranking directly. The best general defeats the enemy's plans by stratagem. The next best stops the enemy from uniting his forces. Below that comes meeting his forces in open battle, and at the bottom comes the siege, which Calthrop calls "the worst expedient." Each step down costs more men and money and leaves fewer choices open. A general who finds himself besieging walls has already missed every better option.

This stands against the heroic picture of war common in the ancient world. Sun Tzu shows no interest in glory or single combat, and he does not treat battle as a test of virtue. The book opens by calling war "a great matter" on which the existence of the state depends, and it treats the general's first task as winning the contest before the armies meet. Chapter 4 adds that the able commander "wins thereby no reputation for wisdom or brave deeds," because his preparation made the result look easy.

The principle travels well beyond soldiering. Wherever there is conflict — in law, negotiation, trade or politics — the side that settles the matter through position and preparation spends less than the side that settles it by force. Readers who have applied the book this way are drawing on something the text itself makes general: the strongest result is the one the other side cannot contest.

The corollary is often overlooked. If the best outcome is victory without battle, then every battle fought marks a shortfall in planning. Sun Tzu does not treat that as a moral failing; he treats it as an operational one. The general who had to fight did not reckon carefully enough or did not arrange his forces so that the result was settled in advance. The teaching is less about avoiding conflict than about preparing so thoroughly that fighting becomes unnecessary.

Where to follow it: Chapter 1 (reckoning and pretence), Chapter 3 (the ranking of strategies), Chapter 6 (emptiness and strength), Chapter 13 (the closing argument).

2 · Knowing the enemy and knowing yourself

"Know thyself; know the enemy; fear not for victory"

Chapter 3 ends with a three-part formula. In Calthrop's translation: "he who knows both sides has nothing to fear in a hundred fights; he who is ignorant of the enemy, and fixes his eyes only on his own side, conquers, and the next time is defeated; he who not only is ignorant of the enemy, but also of his own resources, is invariably defeated." The structure is precise, and it asks more of a commander than a quick reading suggests.

Sun Tzu does not treat self-knowledge as automatic. A general can overrate his own army's discipline and judgement, or underrate strengths he has not noticed. The text gives this inward study the same weight as intelligence about the enemy, and it proceeds by the same means: observation, calculation, patient gathering of facts, and refusal to be flattered. The seven comparisons of Chapter 1 are a checklist for exactly this double inquiry.

Chapter 10 widens the principle to include the ground. It sets out the partial cases — confidence in one's own men without knowing whether the enemy should be attacked, or the reverse — and says that each leaves victory uncertain. It then quotes the saying "Know thyself; know the enemy; fear not for victory," and adds that if season, opportunity and ground are also known, "complete victory is certain." Knowing oneself turns out to mean knowing the whole situation.

Readers have long compared the precept with the Greek command to know oneself. What Sun Tzu adds is that self-knowledge is a practical asset rather than a private virtue. It is the base on which every other advantage stands, and a general who has not done this work cannot be trusted with the lives of his soldiers.

Where to follow it: Chapter 3 (knowing both sides), Chapter 4 (making the army invincible), Chapter 10 (ground and self-knowledge), Chapter 13 (knowledge gained through men).

3 · Form, spirit, and the stone on the mountain

xing and shi — formation and force

Chapters 4 and 5 hold the most distinctive idea in the treatise: the difference between formation (xing, the visible arrangement of an army — numbers, position, ground, supply) and force (shi, the momentum an army gains when that arrangement suits the situation). Calthrop renders the title of Chapter 5 as "The Spirit of the Troops," and the chapter's images make the idea concrete.

The central image closes Chapter 5: a log or rock lies "motionless on flat ground," yet moves "with ever-increasing force when set on an incline," and the spirit of well-led troops is "as the impetus of a round stone rolled from the top of a high mountain." The stone at rest is formation; its descent is force. The general works on the arrangement so that, when the moment comes, the force released is overwhelming. The outcome is fixed in the preparation, and no brilliance on the day can make up for a poor arrangement.

Chapter 5 adds the pairing Calthrop calls the normal and the abnormal force. The enemy "is engaged with the normal and defeated by the abnormal force." Like five notes, five colours and five tastes, the two forces are few in kind but endless in combination. The good fighter "seeks victory from spirit, and does not depend entirely upon the skill of his men" — he chooses his people carefully and lets the momentum of the whole carry them.

The idea has had a long life in strategic thought, from Chinese commentators to twentieth-century theorists of indirect approach and guerrilla war. Its practical consequence is that preparation counts for more than command in the heat of action. The fighting is when the work pays out, not when it is done.

Where to follow it: Chapter 4 (the order of battle), Chapter 5 (the spirit of the troops), Chapter 6 (emptiness and strength), Chapter 11 (the nine grounds).

4 · Pretence, initiative, and emptiness

"War is a thing of pretence"

Chapter 1 states that "War is a thing of pretence." This is not a licence for ordinary dishonesty. It means controlling the enemy's picture of the situation: appearing incapable when ready to act, far when near, near when far. An army that controls what the enemy believes controls the engagement before it starts.

Chapter 6, "Emptiness and Strength," puts the idea to work, beginning with initiative. Calthrop's opening lines: "To be the first in the field, and there to await the enemy, is to husband strength. To be late, and hurrying to advance to meet the foe, is exhausting." The good fighter makes the enemy come to him and is not drawn out himself. The rest of the chapter examines how to choose the time, place and form of the encounter and how to deny that choice to the other side.

The practical rules follow. Appear where the enemy is not and strike points he did not expect. Keep the place of attack secret, so that the enemy must prepare everywhere and is "everywhere weak." Unite while he divides, so that the whole falls on a part. These rules have outlasted the age that produced them because they apply to anyone with limited means deciding where to commit them.

The chapter ends with its best-known image: "An army may be likened to water." Water leaves the high places and runs to the hollows; an army "turns from strength and attacks emptiness." Water takes its course from the ground, and the general takes his from the state of the enemy. Because the shape of water is not fixed, neither is the conduct of war, and the leader who adapts to each adversary "may be called the God of war."

Where to follow it: Chapter 1 (pretence), Chapter 5 (normal and abnormal force), Chapter 6 (emptiness and strength), Chapter 8 (the nine changes).

5 · The cost of war and the closing argument

Chapter 13 — the chapter most readers skip

Chapter 2 begins with the bill. A thousand light chariots, a thousand leather-covered chariots and a hundred thousand armoured men cost a thousand pieces of gold a day. A long campaign wears out soldiers and weapons, empties the treasury and invites neighbouring princes to attack. Calthrop's version is blunt: "there never has been a country which has benefited from a prolonged war." Speed matters because every day in the field is paid for at home.

Chapter 13 returns to the same arithmetic from the other side. Raising a hundred thousand men disturbs "the occupations of 700,000 homes," and years of stand-off may be settled by a single day. To save small sums on spies and stay ignorant of the enemy, the text says, "is to be without humanity." It then names five kinds of spy: village spies, inner spies, converted spies, death spies and living spies. Used together, their working cannot be discovered; Calthrop calls this "the Mysterious Thread" and "the Lord's Treasure."

The chapter widens from espionage to the book as a whole. Rulers and generals who win are those who are "informed beforehand," and that knowledge "is not to be got by calling on gods and demons; nor does it come of past experience nor calculation. It is through men that knowledge of the enemy is gained." The book that opened with the reckoning made before a campaign closes with the source on which all reckoning depends.

Read in sequence, the thirteen chapters form one argument. However many hands shaped the text, it holds together. It opens by ranking victory without fighting above all else and ends with the most common means of reaching it: advance knowledge of the enemy, and the people who supply it.

Where to follow it: Chapter 2 (the cost of war), Chapter 3 (the attack by stratagem), Chapter 12 (assault by fire — passion and restraint), Chapter 13 (the employment of spies).
