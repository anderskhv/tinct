Emptiness and Strength

To be first in the field and wait for the enemy is to husband strength. The chapter on initiative: drawing the enemy where you want him and never being drawn yourself.

Summary

The chapter begins with initiative as a matter of physical fact. The side that reaches the field first and waits saves its strength; the side that arrives late and hurries forward wears itself out. The good fighter makes the enemy approach and is not drawn out by him. He offers what looks like an advantage to lead the enemy into a losing position and places obstacles to keep him from threatening his own. If the enemy is resting, he harasses him; if well fed, he cuts his supplies; if waiting calmly, he forces him to move. Marching where the enemy is not, an army can cover a thousand leagues without fatigue, and attacking undefended places, it takes them every time.

By feints that leave the enemy unsure of his movements, the attacker stays united while the enemy must divide. One body then attacks ten separate parts, and numbers are on the attacker's side at the point that matters. The place of attack must be kept secret, because an enemy who does not know where the blow will fall must prepare everywhere: strengthening the front weakens the rear, strengthening the right weakens the left. Sun Tzu notes that the soldiers of Wu are fewer than those of Yueh, yet numbers alone do not bring victory. Provoke the enemy to learn his condition, feint to test his position, and never repeat a stratagem that has already worked.

The chapter closes with the image that made it famous. "An army may be likened to water." Water leaves the high places dry and seeks the hollows; an army turns from strength and attacks emptiness. Water's course is set by the ground, and victory comes from acting according to the state of the enemy. Water has no fixed shape, and neither has the spirit of war. The leader who changes his tactics to suit his adversary "may be called the God of war." Among the five elements none always prevails, the seasons pass, the days lengthen and shorten, the moon waxes and wanes, and in war, too, nothing is fixed.
