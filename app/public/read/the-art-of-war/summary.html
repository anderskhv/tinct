The Art of War a guided tour

c. 500 BC · Chinese military strategy

Thirteen short chapters from ancient China on the nature of conflict. The central claim: the best general wins before any fighting starts. Tinct reads it in E. F. Calthrop's 1908 English translation, The Book of War.

The book in brief

The Art of War is the oldest and best-known military treatise to survive from ancient China. It runs to thirteen short chapters, each taking up one part of the business of war — calculation, supply, stratagem, formation, ground, fire, spies — and treating it in a few pages of compressed precepts. Tradition credits it to Sun Wu, Master Sun, a general said to have served the state of Wu in the late sixth century BCE. Whether he lived, or whether the text gathers up a school of military thought over several generations, is a question scholars have not settled.

The surprising thing about the book is that it argues against battle as the aim of strategy. Sun Tzu is no pacifist and takes war entirely seriously, but he ranks fighting low among a general's options. In Calthrop's words, "the supreme art is to subdue the enemy without fighting." A commander who must fight has already given up something a better commander would have kept. That idea explains why soldiers, statesmen and business readers have returned to the book for centuries. The edition in the Tinct reader is the English translation by Captain E. F. Calthrop, published by John Murray in 1908 as The Book of War, alongside a Tinct modern English rendering of it.

Chapter 1 — Preliminary Reckoning

Preliminary Reckoning

Chapter 1 lays the foundation for the whole book. It opens with the statement that war is "a great matter," the means by which a state survives or is destroyed, and so "must be diligently studied." Five matters govern every campaign: the Way (the bond between ruler and people), Heaven (season and weather), Earth (distance and position), the Leader (his intelligence, truthfulness, benevolence, courage and strictness), and Law (the organisation of the army). Seven comparisons follow, setting the two sides against each other on the prince's virtue, the general's ability, natural advantages, discipline, strength, training and fairness in reward and punishment. From these, Sun Tzu says, he can foretell the victor. The chapter ends with the principle that runs through the rest of the book: "War is a thing of pretence." Seem unable when able, far when near, near when far, and keep these methods secret beforehand.

Chapter 2 — Operations of War

Operations of War

Chapter 2 is the book's account of what war costs. A thousand light chariots, a thousand leather-covered chariots and a hundred thousand armoured men, supplied over long distances, consume a thousand pieces of gold a day. If the campaign drags on, the soldiers lose heart, weapons wear out, money runs short and neighbouring princes see their chance. Quick action sometimes works even for the unskilled, but a skilful general never profits from a long campaign. The practical advice follows: raise troops once, do not keep sending supplies, take food from the enemy, reward those who capture enemy chariots, and treat prisoners well so that the army grows stronger as the enemy weakens. The aim of war is victory, not long operations, and the good general is the lord of the people's lives.

Chapter 3 — The Attack by Stratagem

The Attack by Stratagem

Chapter 3 is the centre of the treatise. It opens with the claim that taking a country without strife is better than conquering it by fire and sword, and capturing an army whole is better than destroying it. "To fight and conquer one hundred times is not the perfection of attainment, for the supreme art is to subdue the enemy without fighting." A ranking follows: outwit the enemy by stratagem; failing that, stop him uniting his forces; failing that, engage his army; and only as a last resort besiege his citadel. The chapter warns against the siege, gives ratios for when to surround, attack or divide, and lists the three ways a prince can hamper his own army. It ends with five signs by which victory can be foretold and the saying that whoever knows both sides "has nothing to fear in a hundred fights."

Chapter 4 — The Order of Battle

The Order of Battle

Chapter 4 is about making an army impossible to beat and then letting the enemy supply the opening. The ancient masters, Sun Tzu says, made their own armies invincible first. "The causes of defeat come from within; victory is born in the enemy's camp." Defence suits a shortage of strength and attack suits a surplus. The skilful defender hides "in the deepest shades" and the skilful attacker strikes from "the topmost heaven." A victory that the whole kingdom applauds is not necessarily a true success; the able warrior wins without bloody struggle and earns no fame for it. "The army that conquers makes certain of victory, and then seeks battle." The chapter ends with a five-step method — rule, measure, tables, scales and the foretelling of victory — and the image of the winning army as a beam weighed against a feather.

Chapter 5 — The Spirit of the Troops

The Spirit of the Troops

Chapter 5 moves from formation to force. Large armies can be handled like small ones through subdivision and through signals of drum, bell and flag. In battle the enemy is engaged with the normal force and defeated by the abnormal one, and the two can be combined without end, as five notes, five colours and five tastes yield endless harmonies, shades and flavours. Calthrop gives the chapter's images directly: "As the rush of rock-shouldering torrents, so is the spirit of the troops," and the stroke should be timed like a falcon's. The good fighter's spirit is like a stretched crossbow released at the touch of the trigger. Disorder, fear and weakness can be feigned by an army that is truly disciplined, bold and strong. The chapter ends with the log or rock that moves with growing force on a slope, and the troops of a skilful general compared to a round stone rolled from the top of a high mountain.

Chapter 6 — Emptiness and Strength

Emptiness and Strength

Chapter 6 puts the pretence of Chapter 1 to work and gives the book's fullest account of initiative. Arriving first and waiting conserves strength; arriving late and hurrying exhausts it. The good fighter makes the enemy come to him and is never lured out himself. He offers an apparent advantage to draw the enemy into a bad position and places obstacles to keep him away from his own. He harasses an enemy at rest, cuts off one who is well supplied, and appears where he is not expected. Keeping the point of attack secret forces the enemy to prepare everywhere, so that he is everywhere weak, while the attacker unites and falls on the divided parts. The chapter closes with its best-known image: an army is like water, which leaves the heights and fills the hollows, takes its course from the ground and has no fixed shape. The leader who adapts in the same way "may be called the God of war."

Chapter 7 — Battle Tactics

Battle Tactics

Chapter 7 moves from principle to the practical problem of moving armies. After the general receives his orders and assembles his forces, he faces the hardest task of all, battle tactics, whose difficulty lies in time, distance and the reversal of misfortune. A show of gain can send the enemy on a roundabout route while one's own army sets out later and still arrives first. The chapter is blunt about forced marches: push a whole army a hundred leagues and only a tenth arrives; fifty leagues, and half; thirty, and two-thirds. Without supplies and stores an army is lost, and without knowledge of neighbours, terrain and local guides it cannot be led. Then the famous formula: attack swift as the wind, march calm as the forest, occupy like fire, hold firm as a mountain. The chapter ends on morale and restraint — strike when the enemy's spirit flags, keep order against his confusion, and leave a surrounded enemy a way out.

Chapter 8 — The Nine Changes

The Nine Changes

Chapter 8 is one of the shortest chapters and one of the most important. Its premise is that a general who understands the Nine Changes — the art of varying his conduct to fit the case — knows how to use troops, while one who does not cannot put even good knowledge of the ground to use. The chapter lists exceptions to the obvious course: roads to avoid, forces not to attack, castles not to besiege, ground not to fight on, and orders from the lord not to obey. The wise man weighs advantage and disadvantage together. Rather than counting on the enemy staying away, he relies on his own preparations. The chapter ends with five dangerous faults in a general: blind impetuosity, over-cautiousness, quick temper, a too rigid sense of propriety, and over-regard for his troops. Each can ruin an army.

Chapter 9 — Movement of Troops

Movement of Troops

Chapter 9 is the most down-to-earth chapter in the treatise, full of specific advice on ground, observation and signs. It deals in turn with mountains, water, marshes and open country. Cross mountains and camp in valleys; after crossing water pass on immediately to a distance; when the enemy is crossing, strike once half his force is over; cross salt marshes quickly; in open country keep rising ground to the right rear. Soldiers do better on high, sunny ground, where disease is avoided. The chapter then lists the signs by which an enemy gives himself away: "The rising of birds shows an ambush"; startled beasts mean a stealthy approach; high, straight spurts of dust mean chariots, and long, low masses mean infantry. It closes on command: troops must know their general before he punishes them, and humane treatment combined with firm authority wins their obedience.

Chapter 10 — Ground

Ground

Chapter 10 sorts ground into six kinds — open ground, broken ground, suspended ground, defiles, precipices and far countries — and gives a rule for each. On open ground, occupy any nearby heights first and secure the line of supply. Broken ground is easy to enter and hard to leave. On suspended ground the side that moves first is at a disadvantage, so refuse the enemy's bait and draw him out instead. The chapter then lists six calamities that come not from the ground but from the general's own failings: repulse, relaxation, distress, disorganisation, confusion and rout. It closes with the principle that joins the two lists: "Ground is the handmaid of victory," but judging the enemy, planning the win and reading steepness, command and distance are what make a good general.

Chapter 11 — The Nine Grounds

The Nine Grounds

Chapter 11 is the longest in the treatise and the most wide-ranging. It names nine kinds of ground: distracting ground (fighting in one's own territory), disturbing ground (just inside the enemy's border), ground of contention (valuable to whoever holds it), intersecting ground (open to both sides), path-ridden ground (where three provinces meet), deeply-involved ground (deep in enemy country), difficult ground (mountains, ravines, marshes), enclosed ground (a narrow way in and a winding way out), and death ground (where delay means disaster). A rule follows for each. The chapter then turns to the psychology of desperate positions: soldiers with no refuge do not waver and, deep in hostile country, hold together. Its best-known image compares a well-handled army to the snakes of Mt. Chang, whose head and tail strike together. The chapter ends with the advice to behave first "with the discretion of a maiden," then dart in like a rabbit when the enemy gives an opening.

Chapter 12 — Assault by Fire

Assault by Fire

Chapter 12 treats attack by fire as a powerful weapon and uses it to reach one of the book's most serious conclusions. There are five kinds of fire attack: burning barracks, the commissariat, equipment, stores and the company. The timing must be right — dry weather and days of wind — and the means must be at hand. The chapter describes how to respond as the fire develops: attack at once from outside when the enemy's camp catches fire within, but wait if his soldiers stay quiet. Water can cut an enemy off, and fire can burn his camp, but unless victory follows the war drags on and money is wasted. The chapter ends not on tactics but on restraint. Do not move unless there is advantage to gain. Do not go to war because the lord is in a passion or fight because the general is angry. Anger passes, but "a country, once overturned, cannot be restored; the dead cannot be brought to life."

Chapter 13 — The Employment of Spies

The Employment of Spies

Chapter 13 closes the treatise and sums it up. It begins with the arithmetic of Chapter 2: calling a hundred thousand men to arms and moving them a hundred leagues costs a thousand taels a day, causes unrest at home and abroad and disturbs the work of 700,000 homes. Opposing armies can stand off for years only for the issue to be settled in a day. To stay ignorant of the enemy in order to save on spies' pay is "to be without humanity." Advance knowledge cannot be had from gods and demons, from past experience or from calculation; "It is through men that knowledge of the enemy is gained." There are five kinds of spy: village, inner, converted, death and living. Used together they form what Calthrop calls "the Mysterious Thread," the lord's treasure. The converted spy is the key to all the others and must be treated generously. The chapter ends: "The spy is a necessity to the army. Upon him the movement of the army depends."

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

Subduing the enemy without fighting

The best-known sentence in the book: "the supreme art is to subdue the enemy without fighting." It is not a paradox or a call to pacifism. It is a ranking of a general's options, with the siege at the bottom.

Knowing the enemy and knowing yourself

The book's second great principle. Sun Tzu does not assume that self-knowledge comes naturally. Understanding one's own army takes as much work as finding out about the enemy's.

Form, spirit, and the stone on the mountain

Chapters 4 and 5 develop the book's most distinctive idea: the link between an army's visible formation and the force that formation releases. The general's task is arrangement. The battle collects what the arrangement has already earned.

Pretence, initiative, and emptiness

The governing idea of Chapter 1, developed in Chapter 6. Pretence here means shaping what the enemy believes, so that he places his forces where they do the least good.

The cost of war and the closing argument

The last chapter, on spies, is the one most readers pass over, yet it sums up the book. Intelligence costs little compared with an army, and stinting on it is the worst saving a ruler can make.

Key figures

The 4 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Sun Tzu
Attributed author

Sun Wu, known as Master Sun, the figure tradition credits with the Art of War. Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, written around 100 BCE, describes him as a man of Qi who served King Helü of Wu in the late sixth century BCE. Modern scholars cannot confirm this account from contemporary records, and the language of the surviving text suggests a later date. A cautious view is that a Sun Wu may have begun the tradition and that later writers shaped the book that survives. Either way, the name and the text are now inseparable.

King Helü of Wu
Patron

Ruler of the southern state of Wu from 514 to 496 BCE, the king Sun Wu is said to have served. Sima Qian tells how Helü asked Sun Wu to demonstrate his methods on the palace women, and how Sun Wu had the two royal favourites who led them executed when the drill dissolved into laughter. Historical or not, the story fixes the relationship the book describes between ruler and commander: once a general is appointed, he must be left to do his work.

Cao Cao
First commentator

General, statesman and poet (155–220 CE), founder of the power that became the state of Wei at the end of the Han dynasty, and the earliest commentator on the Art of War whose notes survive. Later commentary builds on his, and collected editions of the text with multiple commentators place his notes first. He wrote as a working commander, and his glosses read as practical guidance rather than scholarship.

E. F. Calthrop
English translator

Captain Everard Ferguson Calthrop, a British Army officer, made one of the earliest English translations of the Art of War. He worked from the text as it had been transmitted in Japan, publishing a first version titled Sonshi in 1905 and a revised translation, The Book of War, with John Murray in London in 1908. He died in 1915. His translation is in the public domain and is the original English text in the Tinct reader.
