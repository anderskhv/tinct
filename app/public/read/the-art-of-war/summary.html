The Art of War

500 BCE · by Sun Tzu

The book in brief

The Art of War is a short Chinese treatise on military strategy, traditionally attributed to the general Sun Tzu, who is said to have served the king of Wu in the late 6th or early 5th century BCE. It is structured in thirteen brief chapters, each addressing one element of warfare: laying plans, waging war, attack by stratagem, tactical dispositions, energy, weak and strong points, maneuvering, variation in tactics, the army on the march, terrain, the nine situations, attack by fire, and the use of spies. The chapters are short. The sentences are aphoristic. The argument is that real victory comes from understanding rather than from force, from preparation rather than from boldness, from knowing the enemy better than the enemy knows himself.

The text was preserved continuously in Chinese military and political tradition for two and a half thousand years and entered European awareness through a French translation in 1772. It was studied by Napoleon, by Mao Zedong, by Ho Chi Minh, by every modern Chinese military academy and most American ones. Its bamboo-strip original was excavated from a Han-dynasty tomb in 1972, confirming the basic shape of the received text.

The book is the most concentrated philosophical treatment of conflict in any tradition. Its central insight is that battle, when it occurs, is the failure of strategy — that the supreme commander wins by maneuver, deception, and the management of supply and morale before any troops engage. The Art of War is also a meditation on self-knowledge, on patience, on the cost of misjudgment. It has been read for centuries by people who never expected to fight a war, because what it says about preparation, intelligence, and the timing of action turns out to apply wherever serious decisions are made.

Why it still matters

Winning Without Fighting

The book argues that the highest form of victory is the one in which no battle is fought. Sun Tzu prefers strategies that make engagement unnecessary — understanding the opponent so completely that they surrender, occupying terrain that makes attack pointless, dividing the enemy from his allies before he reaches the field. To break the enemy's resistance without fighting, he writes, is the supreme excellence. The next-best is to attack the enemy's strategy. After that, his alliances. Only after that, his army. Last, and worst, his fortified cities. The principle has shaped Chinese military thinking for twenty-five hundred years and Western military thinking since Napoleon read the first French translation. It now shapes business school strategy curricula, professional negotiation training, and the way intelligence services think about deterrence.

Knowing the Self and the Enemy

Sun Tzu's most quoted line — if you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles; if you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory you will also suffer a defeat; if you know neither, you will succumb in every battle — captures the book's method. Strategy, before anything else, is a discipline of knowledge. Most defeats, Sun Tzu argues, come from not knowing what one is doing or what one is facing. The line is simple. The requirement it makes is severe. Self-knowledge here means knowing not just one's own forces but one's own temperament — whether one tends toward boldness or caution, whether anger or pride is the more likely failure mode. Most commanders, executives, and competitors have continued to fail at this in roughly the proportions Sun Tzu predicted.

A Manual That Survived Empires

The book has been continuously read in China since the 5th century BCE — through the warring states, the Han, the Tang, the Song, the Mongols, the Ming, the Qing, the Republic, and the People's Republic. It survived because what it says about conflict is recognizable in every era of organized human activity. Twenty-five hundred years of generals have tested it against actual war and have not improved on it. A version of it is on the reading list of every modern military academy in both China and the United States. The text is shorter than a single chapter of most modern strategy books and has outlasted nearly all of them. Sun Tzu compresses each principle to its operative form, and the brevity is part of what generals across twenty-five centuries have judged worth keeping.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Winning without fighting.

Sun Tzu's central claim is that the supreme victory is the one achieved without battle. I want to follow that principle through all thirteen chapters and understand what it demands of a commander — in terms of preparation, intelligence, and patience.

War as a psychological contest.

Sun Tzu is less interested in weapons than in morale, deception, and information. I want to track how consistently he treats the outcome of battles as already decided by psychological conditions — and whether that holds up against history.

Beyond the battlefield.

The book has been applied to business strategy, sports, and personal decision-making for decades. I want to read it with those applications in mind — not to flatten the military context, but to find where the principles genuinely generalise and where the analogy breaks.

Self-knowledge as strategy.

"Know your enemy and know yourself and you need not fear the result of a hundred battles." I want to take that claim seriously — what does it mean to truly know yourself in a conflict, and what does Sun Tzu say it requires?

Key figures

Sun Tzu
The Author

Chinese general and military theorist, traditionally identified with Sun Wu, who served the southern state of Wu against the larger northern state of Chu in the late 6th century BCE. Whether the text is the work of one historical man or a tradition compiled under his name is still debated. The voice in the book is unified: calm, exact, pitched at a king who must commit a state to war and at the general who must execute it without losing the army or the country.

The General
The Ideal Commander

A composite figure described throughout the text — calm, adaptable, informed, holding the full complexity of a campaign in mind without showing strain. He is an instrument of precision rather than a heroic personality. He must be willing to disobey his sovereign when the situation demands it, willing to refuse battle when offered, willing to retreat without loss of face. Sun Tzu names five faults that destroy generals: recklessness, cowardice, a quick temper, vanity, and over-solicitude for his men.

The Sovereign
The Ruler

The political authority who commits the state to war and then must keep his hands off its execution. Sun Tzu is explicit. A ruler who interferes in the army out of pride, ignorance of military affairs, or impatience with delay will lose. The book contains a precise list of the ways sovereigns destroy their own armies — issuing orders that cannot be obeyed, treating the army as a court, applying civil principles to military discipline. Authority and expertise must stay in their lanes.

The Enemy
The Subject of Study

Not to be hated, not to be underestimated, not to be made into a moral category. The enemy is a system to be read: their morale, their supply lines, their commanders' temperament, the quarrels in their court, the season and the terrain in which they must fight. Sun Tzu treats contempt as a strategic liability. The general who knows the enemy as well as he knows his own forces fights from a position the enemy cannot match. The general who does not is gambling.

The Terrain
The Third Party

Sun Tzu devotes three of his thirteen chapters to ground. He classifies it: accessible, entangling, temporizing, narrow passes, precipitous heights, distances. He classifies situations created by ground: dispersive, facile, contentious, open, ground of intersecting highways, serious, difficult, hemmed-in, desperate. The terrain is not passive scenery. It shapes what is possible, and the general who has not studied the ground he will fight on has already lost the engagement before it begins.

The Spy
The Source of All Advantage

The subject of the final chapter, and Sun Tzu's argument is that it should perhaps have come first. He distinguishes five kinds — local spies, inward spies (officials of the enemy state turned), converted spies (the enemy's own spies turned back), doomed spies (sent to carry false information at the cost of their lives), and surviving spies. Without intelligence, every other principle in the book is guesswork. With it, the general fights battles whose outcome he already knows.
