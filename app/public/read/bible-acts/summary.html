Acts of the Apostles a guided tour

c. 80–90 CE · New Testament · Christian origins narrative

A small group of disciples in a locked room in Jerusalem become, in three decades, a movement that reaches Rome. This is the only narrative we have of the first generation of Christianity.

The book in brief

Acts of the Apostles is the only narrative we have of the first generation of Christianity. It picks up exactly where the Gospel of Luke ends — the disciples gathered in Jerusalem after the resurrection — and carries the story forward three decades, to Paul under house arrest in Rome, awaiting trial before Caesar, preaching the kingdom of God openly and unhindered. No other book of the New Testament does this. Without Acts, we would have only Paul's letters and the gospels to reconstruct the earliest church, and the reconstruction would be guesswork.

The book is organized around a single verse, spoken by the risen Jesus just before the ascension: you shall be my witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth (1:8). Twenty-eight chapters later, the gospel is in Rome. The structure is the argument. Luke is not writing biography or theology in the abstract — he is writing a narrative of a movement spreading across the Roman empire, blocked at every turn and getting through anyway. The last word of the book in Greek is akōlytōs: unhindered.

Acts 1 of 28
Acts 1

The ascension and the waiting

Chapter 1 is a prologue and a hinge. Luke addresses Theophilus again, recaps the gospel's ending, and reports forty days of post-resurrection appearances in which Jesus speaks about the kingdom of God. The programmatic verse is 1:8 — you shall be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth — which is the table of contents for all twenty-eight chapters. The ascension follows: a cloud takes Jesus out of the disciples' sight, and two men in white ask them why they are standing there looking into the sky. The disciples return to Jerusalem and wait in an upper room. The chapter closes with the election of Matthias to replace Judas, restoring the Twelve to full number before the Spirit arrives. The method — prayer, lots, the casting of a double-criterion (must be a witness of the resurrection) — is the last formal act of the pre-Pentecost church.

Acts 2

Pentecost and the first sermon

Pentecost is the structural and theological engine of the whole book. Read the narrative in verses 1-13 carefully for its physical details — the sound like rushing wind, the tongues as of fire, the sudden multilingualism — and then read Peter's sermon in verses 14-36 as the first Christian sermon: it interprets Pentecost through Joel, interprets the resurrection through Psalm 16, and ends with a direct accusation and a direct invitation. The crowd's question (what shall we do?) and Peter's answer (repent and be baptized) establish the pattern every later chapter will repeat. The chapter ends with a summary of the early Jerusalem community — holding all things in common, breaking bread house to house — which serves as a baseline against which later complications will be measured. The tower of Babel inversion is intentional: where Genesis 11 scattered languages as judgment, Pentecost gathers them into mutual intelligibility.

Acts 3

A lame man walks at the temple gate

Chapter 3 is the first miracle narrative in Acts, and its structure follows a pattern the book will repeat: the sign, the crowd, the sermon, and the opposition (which comes in chapter 4). Read the healing carefully — I have no silver and gold, but what I have I give you: in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, rise up and walk — and notice that Peter immediately redirects the crowd's amazement away from himself and John (why do you stare at us, as though by our own power or piety we have made him walk?) toward Jesus. Peter's sermon in the portico of Solomon is the second major sermon in Acts, and it makes the same structural move as the Pentecost sermon: events interpreted through scripture (Moses in Deuteronomy 18, Abraham in Genesis 22), direct accusation (you delivered up and denied him), and direct invitation (repent and turn back, that your sins may be blotted out). The man leaping and praising God through the temple precincts is one of the most vivid scenes in the book.

Acts 4

Before the council: we must obey God

Chapter 4 is the first formal opposition scene in Acts, and it establishes a pattern: arrest, interrogation, the apostles' refusal, release, prayer, and the Spirit's confirmation. Pay close attention to the Sanhedrin's predicament described in verses 13-17: the healed man is standing right there with them, the healing cannot be denied, and all they can do is command silence and hope the movement dies. Peter's answer to the command is precise — whether it is right in God's sight to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge; for we cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard — and becomes the locus classicus for principled civil disobedience. The chapter's second half pivots to the community's response to the release: a prayer that quotes Psalm 2 and asks not for safety but for boldness, and a second summary of the community holding all things in common, this time with Barnabas named as a specific example of someone selling property and laying the proceeds at the apostles' feet.

Acts 5

Ananias, Sapphira, and the second arrest

Chapter 5 is one of the most dramatically concentrated in the book. The Ananias and Sapphira episode (verses 1-11) is the shadow side of the Barnabas summary that ended chapter 4: where Barnabas gave everything openly, this couple gives part while claiming to give all. The stakes are announced by Peter: you have not lied to men but to God. Both die. Luke notes that great fear came upon the whole church and upon all who heard these things — the community's integrity is being established through a kind of founding terror. The rest of the chapter is a second arrest cycle with significant escalation: more healings (people carrying the sick into the streets, hoping Peter's shadow would fall on them), a second arrest by the Sadducees, overnight release by an angel, the discovery at dawn that they are teaching in the temple again, a second appearance before the Sanhedrin, and Gamaliel's famous speech — if this plan is of human origin it will fail; if it is of God, you cannot overthrow it, and you might even be found to be opposing God. The apostles are flogged and released, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonour for the name.

Acts 6

The seven are appointed; Stephen accused

Chapter 6 is transitional but efficient. The first crisis it handles — Hellenist widows being neglected in the daily food distribution — is introduced matter-of-factly and resolved cleanly: the Twelve propose that seven men of good repute and full of the Spirit be appointed to oversee this service, freeing the apostles for prayer and the ministry of the word. The seven are named; Stephen and Philip are the two who will drive the subsequent narrative. Then Luke immediately introduces the conflict that will consume chapters 7 and 8: Stephen is seized by members of diaspora synagogues in Jerusalem who cannot refute him and so suborn false witnesses. The charges — he speaks against Moses, against the law, against the temple — are the most serious possible, and the note that Stephen's face was like the face of an angel as he stood before the council is Luke's signal that what follows is to be read as a theophanic scene.

Acts 7

Stephen's speech and the first martyrdom

Chapter 7 is the theological and dramatic climax of the Jerusalem section. The speech (verses 1-53) is carefully structured: Abraham (2-8), Joseph (9-16), Moses (17-43), the tabernacle and temple (44-50), and the direct accusation (51-53). The structural logic is that in each generation Israel rejected the deliverer God sent (Joseph, Moses) and that the temple they now defend as sacred replaced the moveable tabernacle that God prescribed. The speech is not a defense but a prosecution. Stephen ends by calling his hearers stiff-necked, uncircumcised in heart, always resisting the Holy Spirit — the identical resistance his ancestors showed. The vision of the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God (the only use of that title in Acts outside the gospels) triggers the stoning. Read the deaths-scene carefully for two details: Stephen prays exactly as Jesus prayed from the cross (Lord, receive my spirit; Lord, do not hold this sin against them), and the young man Saul, holding the cloaks of the executioners, is introduced for the first time.

Acts 8

Philip in Samaria and on the desert road

Chapter 8 is the first chapter of the middle section — Judea and Samaria — and it works by juxtaposition. The Samaria mission (verses 4-25) is the first preaching to a group explicitly outside Israel's boundaries. It is complicated by Simon Magus, a sorcerer who believes and is baptized but then tries to purchase the Spirit's power with money — which gives English the word simony. Peter's rebuke of Simon is one of the sharpest in the book: your heart is not right before God; repent of this wickedness. Then the episode pivots entirely (verses 26-40): Philip is sent by an angel to a desert road south of Jerusalem, where an Ethiopian official is reading Isaiah 53 in his chariot. The episode condenses the gospel's method into one exchange: Philip asks whether the official understands what he is reading; he asks how he could without a guide; Philip explains Isaiah 53 as a prophecy of Jesus; the official is baptized at a pool on the road and goes on his way rejoicing while Philip is immediately transported to Azotus. The Ethiopian is the first African convert in the narrative.

Acts 9

Saul on the Damascus road

Chapter 9 contains the first telling of Paul's conversion (the other two are in chapters 22 and 26), and it is worth reading all three consecutively to see how Luke shapes each one for its audience. This narrated version (verses 1-19) is the fullest: the light, the voice (Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?), the blindness, Ananias's reluctance and his obedience, the scales falling from Saul's eyes, the baptism. The chapter then fast-forwards through several years of activity to establish Paul as a preacher who immediately becomes a target of the same persecution he had inflicted on others. The chapter's second half (verses 32-43) returns to Peter: healings at Lydda (Aeneas, bedridden eight years) and Joppa (Tabitha/Dorcas, raised from the dead), where Peter stays with a tanner named Simon — details that position him for the encounter with Cornelius that will come in chapter 10.

Acts 10

Peter, Cornelius, and the great sheet

Chapter 10 is the structural pivot of the first half of Acts and one of the most carefully constructed chapters in the New Testament. Read it as a diptych: two simultaneous visions (Cornelius at the ninth hour in Caesarea, Peter at noon in Joppa), then the convergence (the men from Cornelius arrive as Peter is still wondering about his vision), then Peter's speech in Cornelius's house, then the Spirit's interruption before the speech is finished, then the baptism, then the overnight discussion. The great sheet vision — let down from heaven full of all kinds of animals, the voice commanding Peter to kill and eat, Peter's refusal, the voice's reply: what God has cleansed, you must not call common — is explicitly not primarily about food laws; the chapter itself tells us what it means (verse 28). Every detail matters. The fact that Cornelius is not a Jewish proselyte but a God-fearer (sympathetic outsider) makes the episode more, not less, significant: the Spirit falls on people who have not been circumcised, completing the logic the Ethiopian eunuch episode in chapter 8 began.

Acts 11

Peter's report to Jerusalem; Antioch

Chapter 11 has two movements. In verses 1-18, Peter faces criticism in Jerusalem from the circumcised believers (those who insisted on Jewish law observance) and is required to explain himself. His explanation is essentially a retelling of chapter 10, condensed, with one small addition: the Spirit told him as the men from Cornelius arrived that John had baptized with water but they would be baptized with the Holy Spirit. The critics fall silent and glorify God: then to the Gentiles also God has granted repentance that leads to life. The episode is important because the Jerusalem church is established as endorsing the Cornelius baptism before the question is formally debated in chapter 15; what happens there is a regularization of what Peter has already done. The chapter's second half (verses 19-30) shifts to Antioch, where a new phase begins: Greek-speaking Gentiles are being evangelized, not just God-fearers; Barnabas is sent from Jerusalem to oversee the community; he recruits Saul from Tarsus; the disciples are first called Christians in Antioch; and Agabus prophesies the famine under Claudius, prompting a collection for Jerusalem.

Acts 12

Herod, the angel, and Peter's escape

Chapter 12 is the last major Peter chapter in Acts, and it works as a deliberate counterpoint to the Herod material. Herod kills James the brother of John — the first apostolic martyrdom in the book, dispatched in a single verse with no speech and no miracle — and arrests Peter to repeat the result. Luke is precise about the date (Passover) and the guard arrangement (four squads of four soldiers each, bound to Peter in pairs). The escape is described in detail: the angel's appearance, the chain falling off, the gates opening by themselves, the narrator's note that Peter thought he was seeing a vision and only when the angel left him in the street did he realize it was real. The community is praying for him; when he arrives at the house of Mary the mother of John Mark, they don't believe the servant girl who reports it and tell her she's seeing his angel. Peter has to keep knocking until they finally open the door. Herod's death in verses 20-23 — struck by an angel for accepting divine honours from a crowd in Caesarea, eaten by worms — closes out the first and only Herodian villain in Acts. Verse 24 resets the narrative: the word of God increased and multiplied.

Acts 13

The first missionary journey begins

Chapter 13 is the beginning of the long Pauline section, and it opens with a scene worth pausing over: the Spirit speaks during a worship service in Antioch — while they are worshipping and fasting — and sets Barnabas and Saul apart for a specific but unnamed work. They are sent out by the church with prayer and fasting. The Cyprus episode (verses 4-12) introduces Sergius Paulus, an intelligent proconsul who wants to hear the word, and Bar-Jesus the sorcerer who opposes them — and introduces the name Paul for the first time in the narrative, replacing Saul at the moment of his first clear Gentile encounter. The sermon in the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch (verses 16-41) is Paul's first major speech in Acts and the longest until his speech to the Ephesian elders in chapter 20: a retelling of Israel's history from Egypt to David, then the claim that Jesus is David's heir and that his resurrection fulfils Psalm 2 and Isaiah 55. The Gentile response in verses 44-49 is the first explicit scene of Paul turning from the synagogue to the Gentiles after Jewish resistance, quoting Isaiah 49 as authorization.

Acts 14

Iconium, Lystra, and the return

Chapter 14 completes the first missionary journey and its most memorable episode is the Lystra scene (verses 8-20). A man lame from birth is healed — the healing echoes the healing at the temple gate in chapter 3 — and the crowd in Lystra (which speaks Lycaonian, not Greek) concludes that the gods have come down in human form. The priest of Zeus prepares oxen and garlands for a public sacrifice. Barnabas is Zeus, Paul is Hermes (because he is the chief speaker). Paul and Barnabas tear their garments — the Jewish gesture of horror at blasphemy — and rush into the crowd to stop the sacrifice, delivering in the process the shortest missionary speech in Acts (verses 15-17), which is notably different from the synagogue sermons: it addresses pagans who have no knowledge of Israel's God or scriptures, argues from the natural order (rain, fruitfulness, joy in your hearts), and asks them to turn from these vain things to the living God. Then Jews arrive from Pisidian Antioch and Iconium, win over the crowd, and stone Paul. He is left for dead outside the city, rises, and re-enters. He and Barnabas retrace their route, strengthening disciples, appointing elders, and return to Antioch to report everything God has done and how he has opened a door of faith to the Gentiles.

Acts 15

The Jerusalem Council

Chapter 15 is the structural centre of Acts and one of the most important chapters in the New Testament. Read it carefully as a narrative of a formal deliberative process: the problem is stated (men from Judea teaching that circumcision is required for salvation), the delegates are sent to Jerusalem, there is much debate, Peter speaks (recalling Cornelius and warning against yokes), Paul and Barnabas speak (signs and wonders among the Gentiles), James speaks (citing Amos 9 and giving the verdict). Then watch the precise terms of the letter: the council does not merely permit Gentile converts to remain uncircumcised; it acknowledges that requiring circumcision would be burdening them with a yoke that even Israel could not bear. James's four requirements — avoid food sacrificed to idols, blood, what is strangled, and sexual immorality — are concessions to the sensibilities of Jewish communities the Gentile churches will live alongside, not conditions of salvation. The chapter ends with a personal dispute (Paul and Barnabas part ways over John Mark) that humanizes the narrative after the grand deliberative scene.

Acts 16

The Macedonian call; Lydia; the jailer

Chapter 16 is one of the richest in Acts. The Timothy episode (verses 1-5) introduces a figure who will become Paul's most significant associate; Paul has him circumcised — apparently to avoid giving needless offence in synagogue settings, which complicates the easy read of chapter 15. The Macedonian vision (verses 6-10) is abrupt: the Spirit forbids them to enter Bithynia; a man from Macedonia appears in a vision at night; they immediately cross into Europe. This is also the first we-passage: the narrator shifts to first person plural (we sought to go into Macedonia), suggesting the author joined the party at Troas. Lydia (verses 11-15) is the first European convert and her story is told with economy and warmth: the Lord opens her heart, she insists on hosting them, the verb implies she put some pressure on them until they agreed. The exorcism of the slave girl (verses 16-22) and the jail episode (23-40) follow: the earthquake, Paul and Silas singing hymns at midnight, the jailer's question (what must I do to be saved?), the household baptism before dawn, the Roman citizenship card played to secure an apology from the magistrates.

Acts 17

Thessalonica, Beroea, and Athens

Chapter 17 covers three cities and demonstrates the range of Paul's rhetorical repertoire. In Thessalonica (verses 1-9), he argues from scripture in the synagogue that the Messiah had to suffer and rise from the dead. In Beroea (verses 10-15), the synagogue members receive the word eagerly and examine the scriptures daily — Luke calls them noble. Athens (verses 16-34) is the set-piece. Paul is provoked by the idols and provokes controversy in the Areopagus marketplace; some Epicurean and Stoic philosophers bring him before the Areopagus council to explain his teaching. The Areopagus speech (verses 22-31) is the most famous missionary speech in Acts and the one that most carefully avoids Jewish scripture, addressing instead the Athenians' own religious practice (the altar to the Unknown God), quoting their own poets (Epimenides and Aratus), and arguing from creation to the resurrection. The response is the most muted in Acts: some mock, some want to hear more, and some believe — including Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris.

Acts 18

Corinth and the return east

Chapter 18 is relatively measured in tone after the drama of chapter 17. The Corinth material (verses 1-17) is notable for several details: the mention of Priscilla and Aquila, Jewish tentmakers expelled from Rome under Claudius, who become Paul's most significant lay associates and reappear later in the chapter correcting Apollos in Ephesus; a reassuring vision (do not be afraid, I have many people in this city) that keeps Paul in Corinth long enough to stay eighteen months; and the Gallio episode, which is historically important because Gallio's proconsulship is independently dateable to 51-52 CE, giving New Testament scholars a fixed chronological anchor for Paul's movements. Gallio dismisses the Jewish case against Paul: if it is a matter of your law, settle it yourselves; I will not judge these things. He drives them from his tribunal, and when the crowd turns on the synagogue ruler Sosthenes and beats him, Gallio takes no notice. The chapter ends with Paul returning east — Ephesus, Caesarea, Antioch — and Apollos introduced as a learned Alexandrian who knows only the baptism of John and is corrected by Priscilla and Aquila.

Acts 19

The Ephesus years and the riot

Chapter 19 is the longest account of Paul's settled ministry in any city and falls into two parts. The first (verses 1-20) establishes the depth of Ephesus: the twelve disciples who know only John's baptism receive the Spirit when Paul lays his hands on them; he teaches in the synagogue for three months, then withdraws to the lecture hall of Tyrannus for two years, so that all who lived in Asia, both Jews and Greeks, heard the word. Extraordinary miracles follow (even handkerchiefs that had touched Paul's skin were brought to the sick); seven sons of a Jewish priest named Sceva try to use Paul's name as a formula and are beaten by the demonised man they try to exorcise; the incident becomes known and many come, confessing their magical practices and burning their books, the value of the books placed by Luke at fifty thousand silver coins. The second part (verses 21-41) is the riot: the silversmith Demetrius rallies the guild — if this teaching succeeds, the trade in Diana's silver shrines will collapse and the great goddess Diana will be despised — and the city descends into uproar, filling the theatre with confused shouting for two hours. The town clerk calms the crowd by pointing to the proper legal channels, and Paul departs for Macedonia.

Acts 20

Eutychus raised; farewell at Miletus

Chapter 20 is the most elegiac in Acts. The Troas episode (verses 7-12) is compact and vivid: a midnight service, Paul's speech going on so long that a young man named Eutychus falls asleep in the window and falls three floors to his death; Paul goes down, embraces him, and says do not be alarmed, for his life is in him; they return upstairs and continue until dawn. The Miletus speech (verses 18-35) is the only speech in Acts addressed entirely to believers rather than to mission audiences, and it is the most personal thing Paul says in the book. He reviews his ministry at Ephesus — how he served the Lord with all humility and with tears and with trials — and announces that he is going to Jerusalem not knowing what will happen to him there, except that the Spirit testifies in every city that imprisonment and afflictions await him. The speech ends with the only quotation of a saying of Jesus not found in any gospel: it is more blessed to give than to receive. The elders weep, embrace Paul, and accompany him to the ship.

Acts 21

Arrival in Jerusalem; Paul arrested

Chapter 21 is the beginning of the long arrest sequence that occupies the rest of the book. The journey to Jerusalem is punctuated by warnings: at Tyre the disciples tell Paul through the Spirit not to go; at Caesarea the prophet Agabus ties his own hands and feet with Paul's belt and says the Spirit predicts the Jerusalem Jews will bind the owner of this belt and hand him over to the Gentiles. The community weeps; Paul says: what are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart? I am ready not only to be imprisoned but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus. They say: the will of the Lord be done. The Jerusalem welcome from James and the elders is warm but immediately complicated: they tell Paul that tens of thousands of Jewish believers have heard he teaches Jews in the diaspora to abandon Moses, and they propose he take four men under a Nazarite vow and pay for their purification in the temple to demonstrate publicly that he keeps the law. He agrees. Near the end of the seven days, Jews from Asia recognise him in the temple, accuse him of bringing Greeks into the temple, and stir up the whole crowd. Paul is seized and dragged outside; the temple doors are shut; a Roman commander arrives with soldiers and takes Paul into custody. Paul asks to address the crowd, and the commander, learning he speaks Greek, gives him permission.

Acts 22

Paul's speech to the Jerusalem crowd

Chapter 22 is the second telling of Paul's conversion (the first is in chapter 9, the third in chapter 26). This version is addressed to the crowd that has just tried to kill him and is spoken in Aramaic, which quiets them. Compare this telling to chapter 9: some details are the same (the light, the voice, the blindness), some are elaborated (Ananias is here described not merely as a disciple but as a devout man according to the law, well spoken of by all Jews — Paul is adapting the story for his Jewish audience), and the commission to go to the Gentiles is given here directly by Jesus in a temple vision rather than through Ananias. The triggering word that ends the speech and restarts the riot is Gentiles (verse 21): up to that point the crowd listens; the moment Paul says Jesus commissioned him to go far away to the Gentiles, they demand his death. The Roman commander then decides to examine Paul by flogging; Paul declares his Roman citizenship; the flogging stops and the commander is alarmed. The chapter ends with Paul brought before the Sanhedrin.

Acts 23

Before the Sanhedrin; transfer to Caesarea

Chapter 23 is structurally a thriller: the Sanhedrin hearing (verses 1-10), the night vision in the barracks (verse 11), the assassination plot (verses 12-22), and the extraordinary military escort to Caesarea (verses 23-35). The Sanhedrin scene is brief but tactically decisive: Paul identifies himself as a Pharisee and raises the issue of the resurrection of the dead, splitting the council between Pharisees (who hold the resurrection) and Sadducees (who do not). The two factions begin to argue so violently that the commander has Paul removed by force. The night vision (the Lord stood by him) echoes the reassurance visions in chapters 18 and 27 and establishes the pattern of divine confirmation at each critical juncture. The assassination plot is discovered through Paul's nephew, whose identity and existence are otherwise entirely unknown. The commander writes a self-exculpatory letter to Felix the governor — carefully omitting that he had almost had a Roman citizen flogged — and sends Paul to Caesarea with seventy cavalry and two hundred soldiers, an escort whose scale signals how seriously the commander takes the threat.

Acts 24

Before Felix in Caesarea

Chapter 24 is the first of three formal hearings in Acts — before Felix (24), before Festus (25), and before Agrippa (26) — and it establishes the pattern the others follow: Jewish accusation, Paul's defence, Roman incomprehension of the internal religious question, and a procedurally inconclusive result. The advocate Tertullus frames the charge in terms Romans would care about: Paul is a troublemaker who stirs up riots throughout the world and is a ringleader of the Nazarene sect who tried to desecrate the temple. Paul's defence is careful and precise: he has not disputed in the synagogue or stirred up a crowd; he simply went to worship and purify himself; the real issue is the resurrection of the dead. Felix adjourns the case pending the arrival of Lysias the commander. Then Luke gives an unexpected glimpse of Paul's two years in custody: Felix summons him frequently and listens to him, but is also hoping for a bribe; when Felix leaves office, wanting to do the Jews a favour, he leaves Paul in prison.

Acts 25

Before Festus; appeal to Caesar

Chapter 25 is a pivot chapter. The new governor Festus, inexperienced and politically pressured, tries to accommodate the Jerusalem authorities by suggesting Paul be tried there — where an ambush is waiting — and Paul plays the one card that changes everything: I appeal to Caesar. Luke describes the decision's impact with rare directness: Festus confers with his council and says the appeal is granted: to Caesar you shall go. The appeal is the legal mechanism that ensures the remainder of the book happens in Rome, and it is exercised not under duress but as a deliberate choice. The chapter's second half introduces Herod Agrippa II and his sister Bernice, who arrive in Caesarea and provide Festus the expert Jewish audience he needs to write a coherent report to accompany the prisoner to Rome. Festus's problem is plain: he cannot send a prisoner to the emperor without specifying the charges, and none of the charges he can identify are Roman charges — the dispute is about someone named Jesus who died but whom Paul asserts is alive.

Acts 26

Before Agrippa: the third conversion account

Chapter 26 is the third and final telling of Paul's conversion (compare chapters 9 and 22) and the most rhetorically elaborate. This audience — Agrippa the Jewish king, Bernice, Festus, the military tribunes, the prominent men of the city — is the most distinguished Paul has in the book, and Luke gives him the longest and most polished speech of the final section. The additions in this version are notable: Paul's commission from Jesus on the Damascus road is given directly and at length (I am sending you to the Gentiles, to open their eyes, to turn them from darkness to light), with no reference to Ananias at all; and the missionary mandate is framed explicitly as the fulfilment of what Moses and the prophets said would happen. The dramatic climax is the exchange with Festus (who says Paul's great learning is driving him mad) and then with Agrippa (who says in a little while you would persuade me to be a Christian). The chapter ends with Agrippa and Festus agreeing privately: this man is doing nothing to deserve death or imprisonment. Agrippa adds: this man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar.

Acts 27

The shipwreck on Malta

Chapter 27 is one of the most detailed sea narratives in ancient literature and is written in the first person plural (the we-passage resumes at verse 1), which gives it its immediacy. Read it as a sustained piece of nautical prose: the cargo, the contrary winds, the fair harbours, the centurion's decision to ignore Paul's warning and sail on from Crete, the Euroclydon (the violent northeastern storm that overtakes them), the jettisoning of cargo and tackle, the fourteen days without sun or stars, the crew's plan to abandon ship on the pretext of laying anchors from the bow. Paul's role throughout is not miraculous but managerial and prophetic: he warns before the storm, reassures during it (an angel has told him no lives will be lost), prevents the crew from abandoning ship, urges everyone to eat. The grounding on a shoal and the breaking of the ship are described with precision. The centurion prevents the soldiers from killing the prisoners; everyone swims or floats to shore on planks. The count — all 276 escaped — is given by Luke with the formality of a ship's log. The island is Malta.

Acts 28

Malta, Rome, and the word unhindered

Chapter 28 is the ending the book has been building toward, and it rewards slow reading. The Malta episode (verses 1-10) is compact: the viper on Paul's hand, the islanders waiting for him to swell and fall dead, his shaking it off into the fire, the healing of Publius's father, which leads to every sick person on the island coming and being cured. Then the journey to Rome (verses 11-16), notable for the small scene at the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns where brothers come to meet Paul and he thanks God and takes courage — one of the most humanly affecting moments in the whole book. The meeting with the Rome Jewish community (verses 17-28) is Paul's final major scene: he explains his situation, they report they have heard only rumours about this sect, they set a day for a longer conversation, he speaks all day from morning until evening about the kingdom of God and Jesus, some are convinced and some disbelieve, he quotes Isaiah 6 (go to this people and say, hearing you will hear and not understand) and says the salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles. The closing two verses (30-31) give the famous abrupt ending.

Pentecost and the Spirit

The narrative engine of Acts is the Holy Spirit, and the engine starts in chapter 2. The disciples in Jerusalem on Shavuot, tongues of fire, languages from across the empire — and three thousand baptized in a day. Luke is making a theological argument that will run for twenty-eight chapters: the apostles are not the protagonists of their own story.

From Jerusalem to Rome

Acts 1:8 is the table of contents for all twenty-eight chapters. The narrative does exactly what the verse says: Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, the end of the earth. The last word of the book in Greek is akōlytōs — unhindered. Luke ends on that word because that is the point.

Peter and Paul

Acts is built around two great figures in deliberate symmetry. Peter carries the first half (chapters 1–12); Paul carries the second (13–28). The order is theologically careful: Peter opens the door to the Gentiles first. Paul walks through it.

The Jerusalem Council

Chapter 15 is the structural centre of Acts and the most consequential meeting in early Christian history. The question: must Gentile converts be circumcised? The answer changes everything.

The abrupt ending

Paul has survived a hurricane, a shipwreck, a snake bite, and two years of Roman custody. He is now in Rome, awaiting trial before Caesar. The trial never comes. The book ends mid-story. The last word is unhindered. The choice is deliberate.

Key figures

The 5 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Peter
Apostle, protagonist of Acts 1–12

The protagonist of the first half and the bridge between Jesus and the Gentile mission. Preaches the first Christian sermon at Pentecost, defies the Sanhedrin, is delivered from Herod's prison by an angel. The structural pivot is his vision of the great sheet at Joppa and his baptism of Cornelius the Roman centurion — the first Gentile baptism, performed by the leading Jerusalem apostle, that makes the Pauline mission of the second half possible.

Paul
Apostle, protagonist of Acts 13–28

Appears first as Saul the Pharisee, consenting to the stoning of Stephen. Converted on the road to Damascus — a story Luke tells three times — he becomes the most important figure in the expansion of the gospel into Asia Minor, Macedonia, Greece, and Rome. Arrested in Jerusalem, he spends two years under guard in Caesarea, appeals to Caesar as a Roman citizen, survives shipwreck on Malta, and arrives in Rome. The book ends with him there, awaiting a trial that never appears in the narrative.

Stephen
First martyr

One of the seven appointed to serve the community in Jerusalem. His speech before the Sanhedrin in chapter 7 — the longest in Acts, retelling the entire Hebrew Bible as a pattern of Israel's resistance to God's messengers — ends with his execution by stoning. The young Saul holds the cloaks of those who throw the stones. Stephen's death triggers the scattering that begins the wider mission.

Cornelius
Roman centurion, the founding Gentile

A centurion of the Italian Cohort at Caesarea, a devout man who feared God and prayed continually. His vision in chapter 10 sends men to fetch Peter. Peter's vision of the great sheet of unclean animals runs in parallel. When Peter arrives and preaches, the Spirit falls on Cornelius's household before the sermon is finished — the founding precedent that the Jerusalem Council will cite when settling the question of Gentile inclusion.

Lydia
First European convert

A businesswoman from Thyatira living in Philippi, a dealer in expensive purple cloth and a worshipper of God. She meets Paul in chapter 16 by the river outside the city, where women have gathered for prayer on the sabbath. The Lord opens her heart to listen. She is baptized along with her household and immediately offers her house as a base for the mission. The church at Philippi meets in her house; Paul's letter to that church, written from prison years later, is the most affectionate he ever sent.
