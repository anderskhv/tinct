Romans 14

A specific dispute in the Roman church about food and holy days becomes Paul's extended lesson in the limits of individual conscience and the primacy of the community's peace.

Summary

Romans 14 addresses what appears to be a real and specific dispute within the Roman community. Some members — the weak in faith, in Paul's framing — eat only vegetables and observe certain days as holy; others — the strong — eat anything and treat every day as the same. The strong are probably Gentile Christians or Jewish Christians who have moved away from observance; the weak are likely Jewish Christians who retain food laws and sabbath observance. Paul does not say one side is theologically right and the other wrong in a way that requires a ruling; he says they should stop despising and judging each other. The one who eats should not despise the one who abstains, and the one who abstains should not pass judgment on the one who eats, for God has welcomed both.

The chapter's governing principle is accountability to God, not to one another. We will all stand before the judgment seat of God; each will give an account of himself. Therefore do not pass judgment on one another; instead, decide never to put a stumbling block or hindrance in the way of a brother. Paul's position is that all food is clean — he sides with the strong on the substance — but that the strong have a responsibility that goes beyond their own clean conscience. If your brother is grieved by what you eat, you are no longer walking in love. The kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit. The chapter ends with the individual principle: whatever does not proceed from faith is sin. Each person must act from conviction, but the strong must bear the weakness of those for whom conviction has not yet reached the same conclusion.
