Romans 4

Paul takes the founding figure of Jewish identity and demonstrates that faith — not circumcision, not law — is what made Abraham righteous before God.

Summary

Romans 4 is an extended piece of scriptural exegesis focused on Genesis. Paul's argument is that the great ancestor of the Jewish people, Abraham, was declared righteous by God on the basis of faith — Genesis 15:6, and he believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness — and that this happened before either of the other two distinctives of Israel's identity was in place. Abraham was not yet circumcised when Genesis 15 takes place; circumcision comes in Genesis 17. And the Mosaic law was given four centuries after Abraham. The timing is not incidental in Paul's reading. It is decisive. Abraham was reckoned righteous as a Gentile, as an uncircumcised man who had no law to keep. He is therefore the father not only of the circumcised but of all who share his faith — Gentile and Jew alike — on exactly the same basis.

The second half of the chapter draws out the analogy between Abraham's faith and Christian faith. Abraham's specific act of trust, as Paul reads it, was believing that God could give him a child through the dead womb of Sarah and his own body as good as dead — he did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, now as good as dead (for he was about a hundred years old), or when he considered the barrenness of Sarah's womb. He grew strong in faith as he gave glory to God, being fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised. The God Abraham trusted is the God who gives life to the dead and calls into existence things that do not exist. And Christians trust the same God for the same kind of impossible act — the resurrection of Jesus from the dead. Faith was counted to Abraham as righteousness; the same will be counted to those who believe in the God who raised Jesus.
