Romans 16

Twenty-six names, a scribe's own greeting, a woman described as apostle, and the final benediction — the human face of the early Pauline mission.

Summary

Romans 16 opens with the commendation of Phoebe. Paul commends to the Roman church their sister Phoebe, a deacon — diakonos — of the church at Cenchreae, asking them to welcome her in the Lord and help her in whatever she may need, for she has been a benefactor of many and of Paul himself. Phoebe is the carrier of the letter; she travels to Rome on her own business and delivers the long parchment, probably reading it aloud to the assembled community. Her commendation by Paul is the beginning of the letter's closing, and her role in its transmission is as significant as Tertius's role in its composition. The greeting list that follows is the longest in any Pauline letter — twenty-six people named, several with brief characterizations. Prisca and Aquila, who risked their lives for Paul. Epaenetus, the first convert in Asia. Mary, who worked hard. Andronicus and Junia, in prison with Paul, senior in faith, prominent among the apostles.

The name Junia at verse 7 is one of the most consequential points of translation in the letter. The Greek form Iounian was taken from the fourth century onward by most interpreters as a feminine name — Junia, a woman described as prominent among the apostles. Beginning in the medieval period and accelerating in the early modern era, the name was masculinized to Junias, a name unattested anywhere else in antiquity, by translators who found a female apostle theologically impossible. Modern scholarship, examining the Greek usage and the patristic record, has returned overwhelmingly to the feminine reading. The greeting list continues, including Tryphaena, Tryphosa, and Persis — women who worked hard in the Lord. At verse 22, Tertius the scribe briefly surfaces: I Tertius, who wrote this letter, greet you in the Lord. The warning against divisive people in verses 17-20 is a brief sharp interruption in the greetings. The letter closes with a doxology of four and a half verses, sealing the whole argument of the letter with a final act of praise: to the only wise God, through Jesus Christ, be glory forever and ever. Amen.
