Summary

Romans 13:1-7 is the most debated passage in the practical chapters of Romans. Paul instructs every person to be subject to the governing authorities, because there is no authority except from God — all existing authority has been instituted by him. The one who resists the governing authority resists God's appointment, and resistance will bring judgment. The governing authority is God's servant for the good of the one who does right; it bears the sword to execute God's wrath on the wrongdoer. Therefore pay taxes, owe respect, give honor where honor is due. The passage was written for a small and vulnerable community in Rome in the 50s CE, and Paul's concern is the peace of a minority group in the imperial capital. The verses have been used, across nineteen centuries, to justify obedience to regimes Paul himself could never have endorsed, and they have been argued over by every generation of Christian political thought since Augustine.

Verse 8 pivots to a principle that governs the whole practical section: owe no one anything, except to love one another, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law. Paul then summarizes the second table of the Decalogue — you shall not commit adultery, you shall not murder, you shall not steal, you shall not covet — and any other commandment, in the single word: love your neighbor as yourself. Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfilling of the law. The chapter closes with eschatological urgency: the hour has come to wake from sleep; salvation is nearer than when we first believed; the night is far gone; the day is at hand. Lay aside the works of darkness; put on the armor of light; make no provision for the flesh to gratify its desires. The practical instruction is grounded, at every point, in the expected return of Christ.
