Romans a guided tour

c. 57 CE · New Testament · Pauline Epistle

The most carefully argued letter ever written. Paul dictates sixteen chapters to a scribe in a port city in Greece, and sends them ahead of himself to a church he has never visited — to explain the gospel he has been preaching for twenty years.

The book in brief

Romans is the longest and most carefully argued letter in the New Testament. Paul writes it from Corinth in the winter of 56-57 CE, near the end of what scholars call his third missionary journey. He is about to take a collection to Jerusalem, expects trouble there, and plans afterward to travel west — past Rome to Spain. Romans is the letter he writes ahead of that visit, partly to introduce himself to a church he did not found, partly to enlist their support, and mostly to set out the gospel he has been preaching for twenty years in its most sustained form.

He does not write it himself. Romans 16:22 is one of the most quietly remarkable verses in the New Testament: I Tertius, the writer of this letter, greet you in the Lord. Tertius is a professional scribe taking dictation over multiple sessions; the letter is delivered by Phoebe, a deacon of the church at Cenchreae, travelling to Rome on her own business. The structure is unusually clear for Paul: chapters 1-4 diagnose the universal problem and prescribe justification by faith; chapters 5-8 trace the consequences for believers; chapters 9-11 wrestle with the question of Israel; chapters 12-16 translate the theology into practice. Every serious reader of the Western tradition has had to reckon with it.

Romans 1 of 16
Romans 1

Paul introduces his gospel to Rome

Chapter 1 is the opening of a long letter to a community Paul did not found and has not yet visited. He introduces himself by his calling — apostle, set apart for the gospel — and then the gospel itself: the Son of David by descent, declared Son of God by the resurrection. Verses 16-17 are the thesis of the entire letter: the gospel is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek, and in it the righteousness of God is revealed through faith for faith. The chapter then pivots into its first sustained movement: a diagnosis of the Gentile world. The wrath of God is revealed, Paul writes, against all ungodliness. His argument is that the knowledge of God is available through creation — his invisible attributes have been clearly perceived since the creation of the world — and that the human turn to idolatry is therefore without excuse. The long closing section of the chapter lists the moral consequences Paul associates with that turn: a catalog of human wrongs, the longest single list of this kind in his letters.

Romans 2

The Jewish reader is not exempt

Chapter 2 is the turn in Paul's argument. Having described the Gentile world's rejection of the knowledge of God, he now addresses the reader who applauded that description — you who judge, he writes, you condemn yourself, because you the judge practice the very same things. The indictment of chapter 1 was universal from the start, but Paul keeps that universality hidden through chapter 1 and springs it in chapter 2. The chapter then works through the logic of divine impartiality: God shows no partiality; there is no difference between Jew and Greek before the judgment seat. Paul raises the question of the Gentiles who have never heard the Mosaic law and concludes that the law written on the heart — the moral intuition that produces conscience — is enough to bring them within the scope of divine judgment. The chapter ends with a direct challenge to the Jewish interlocutor: circumcision counts only if you obey the law; the uncircumcised Gentile who keeps the law's requirements is more circumcised, in the sense that matters, than the circumcised man who breaks it.

Romans 3

All under sin; righteousness through faith

Chapter 3 is the hinge of the letter's first movement. Paul begins by answering objections: if the Jews' unfaithfulness does not nullify God's faithfulness, what advantage does the Jew have? Considerable, he says — they were entrusted with the oracles of God. But their unfaithfulness does not dissolve the problem; it only demonstrates that God is faithful and every human being a liar. He then gathers the evidence from the Psalms and Isaiah — a catena of quotations establishing that all are under sin, none righteous, none seeking God. The verdict is delivered at 3:19-20: whatever the law says it says to those under the law, so that every mouth may be stopped and the whole world held accountable to God. Then the pivot: But now. Verses 21-26 are the most concentrated statement of the gospel anywhere in Paul — righteousness through faith, redemption through Christ, propitiation by his blood, the demonstration of God's justice in justifying the one who has faith in Jesus. The chapter closes with the question Paul will answer in chapter 4: does justification by faith overthrow the law? By no means — it upholds the law.

Romans 4

Abraham: father of all who believe

Chapter 4 is Paul's exegetical demonstration of justification by faith, built entirely from the figure of Abraham. The argument is precise and depends on the sequence of events in Genesis. Abraham was reckoned righteous at Genesis 15:6 — and he believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness — before he was circumcised (Genesis 17) and before the Mosaic law was given (Exodus 20, four hundred years later still). The sequence is not accidental in Paul's reading; it means that Abraham's righteousness was counted to him as a man of faith, not as a circumcised man or a law-keeper. He is therefore the father of all who believe — Gentile and Jew alike — on the same basis: trust in God's promise. The chapter then draws the analogy to Christian faith. Abraham believed God who gives life to the dead, specifically that God would give him a son despite his age and Sarah's barrenness. Christians believe in the God who raised Jesus from the dead. The structure of faith is the same: trust in a life-giving God against visible impossibility. Faith was counted to Abraham as righteousness; the same will be counted to those who believe in the resurrection.

Romans 5

Peace with God and the Adam-Christ parallel

Chapter 5 opens the second major movement of the letter (chapters 5-8), which draws out the consequences of the justification argued in chapters 1-4. The opening is lyrical: since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we also have obtained access to this grace in which we stand, and we rejoice in hope of the glory of God. Paul then works through a chain of consequences — tribulation produces endurance, endurance character, character hope, and hope does not put us to shame because the love of God has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit. The chapter then pivots to the Adam-Christ parallel, which Paul introduces from verse 12 onward. Adam and Christ are the two heads of two humanities: through the trespass of one, death spread to all; through the righteousness of one, life comes to all. The parallel is typological and structural — Adam's act in one direction, Christ's in the other — and the rhetorical point is that the gift in Christ is not merely equivalent to the damage of Adam but exceeds it. Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.

Romans 6

Dead to sin, alive in Christ

Chapter 6 opens with Paul's most pointed rhetorical question: Shall we continue in sin so that grace may abound? The question follows logically from the last verse of chapter 5, where he said grace abounds where sin increases, and it is the objection he expects. The answer is immediate and physical: By no means! How can we who died to sin still live in it? The death he has in mind is baptism — the ritual in which the believer is immersed, identified with Christ's death, and raised with him to new life. Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried with him and united with him in a death like his, so that we might also be united with him in a resurrection like his. The chapter works out the implications: the old self was crucified with him; the body of sin is brought to nothing; the believer is dead to sin and alive to God. The language of slavery runs through the chapter: you were once slaves of sin, but you have become slaves of righteousness. The shift of allegiance is total. The wages of sin is death, Paul concludes; the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.

Romans 7

The wretched man and the divided self

Chapter 7 addresses the role of the law in the new life. Paul has argued in chapters 5-6 that the believer has died with Christ and is no longer under sin; now he must explain what role the law plays. The law itself is holy and righteous and good, he insists. But the human encounter with the law is not straightforward: the commandment you shall not covet, far from defeating desire, actually sharpened it, because sin seizes the opportunity provided by the prohibition. Sin used the law to produce death in him. From verse 14, the chapter shifts into a sustained first-person present-tense lament. I am of the flesh, sold under sin. I do not understand my own actions; for I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate. I can will what is right, but I cannot do it. The passage runs for a dozen verses, climaxing in the cry: Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death? Then the turn: Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord. Chapter 7 ends with the division still in view — with my mind I serve the law of God, but with my flesh I serve the law of sin — and chapter 8 provides the answer.

Romans 8

No condemnation; nothing can separate us

Chapter 8 is the climax Paul has been building toward since chapter 1. It opens with the most celebrated sentence in the letter: There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. The chapter then works through what the Spirit means for the new life: the mind set on the Spirit is life and peace; the believer has received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry Abba, Father; the Spirit himself testifies with our spirit that we are children of God, and if children then heirs — heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ. Creation itself groans, waiting for the revealing of the children of God. The believer groans too, waiting for the redemption of the body. The Spirit intercedes with groanings too deep for words. Then the predestination sequence: those God foreknew he also predestined, called, justified, glorified. Then the great rhetorical climax from verse 31: If God is for us, who can be against us? He who did not spare his own Son — how will he not also with him graciously give us all things? Who shall bring any charge? Who is to condemn? Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? A list of ten adversaries, and the answer: in all these things we are more than conquerors. Nothing in all creation shall be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.

Romans 9

The anguish of Israel and divine election

Chapter 9 opens the three-chapter meditation on Israel — the hardest sustained argument in the letter. Paul begins with one of the most striking personal statements in his letters: he has great sorrow and unceasing anguish in his heart, and he could wish himself accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of his brothers, his kinsmen according to the flesh. Then the catalog of Israel's privileges: adoption, glory, covenants, law, worship, promises, the patriarchs, and the Messiah himself, according to the flesh. The problem is immediate: most of Israel has not embraced the gospel. Has God's word failed? Paul's first answer is from within scripture: not all who are descended from Israel are Israel; the promise has always operated through election — Isaac not Ishmael, Jacob not Esau, before either was born or had done anything good or bad. The election is from God's free purpose, not from human works or descent. The chapter then presses into the hardest question: Is God unjust? No — he says to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I have mercy. He hardens whom he wants, as he hardened Pharaoh. The objection — why then does he still find fault? — is raised and not fully answered; instead, Paul invokes the sovereignty of the potter over the clay, and ends with the inclusion of Gentiles (as Hosea foretold) and the remnant of Israel (as Isaiah foretold).

Romans 10

Israel has heard; how shall they hear without a preacher?

Chapter 10 continues the argument of chapter 9 but shifts from divine sovereignty to human responsibility. Paul's desire and prayer is for Israel's salvation; he grants them a genuine zeal for God but argues it is not according to knowledge. They have sought to establish their own righteousness and have not submitted to God's righteousness, which is Christ — the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes. The great statement of the chapter is the word of faith Paul proclaims: if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. For there is no distinction between Jew and Greek — the same Lord is Lord of all, bestowing his riches on all who call on him. Then the famous chain of questions: How are they to call on one in whom they have not believed? And how to believe in one of whom they have never heard? And how to hear without someone preaching? And how to preach unless they are sent? The missionary logic follows from the gospel logic. The chapter ends by returning to Israel: Moses and Isaiah both foretold that Israel would hear and not believe, and that the Gentiles who were not seeking God would find him. But Paul ends not with condemnation but with the outstretched divine hands: all day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people.

Romans 11

The olive tree and the mystery of Israel

Chapter 11 brings the three-chapter meditation on Israel to its resolution. Has God rejected his people? Paul's answer is immediate and personal: By no means! He himself is an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham. As in Elijah's day, when God told the prophet that seven thousand had not bowed to Baal, there is at the present time a remnant chosen by grace. The rest were hardened — as the scriptures predicted. But their stumbling has not been a fall; it has been the occasion of Gentile inclusion, and Paul uses the position to make an argument about mercy. If Israel's trespass means riches for the Gentiles, how much more will their fullness mean. The great image of the chapter is the olive tree. The natural branches (Israel) have been broken off because of unbelief; the wild olive branches (Gentiles) have been grafted in. But those natural branches can be grafted back in — indeed, it will be easier; God is able. The Gentiles are warned against arrogance: do not boast over the branches, for you do not support the root, the root supports you. Then the mystery: a partial hardening has come on Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in, and in this way all Israel will be saved. The chapter ends with the great doxology: O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable his judgments, how inscrutable his ways. The argument has reached the point where argument gives way to wonder.

Romans 12

Living sacrifice; the body of Christ

Chapter 12 opens the practical movement of the letter with the word therefore, gathering everything from chapters 1-11 into a single imperative. Present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God — this is your spiritual worship. Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind. The bodily, communal, and intellectual dimensions are all present in the opening two verses. Then Paul develops the community image: we are one body in Christ, and each member belongs to all the others, with different gifts given according to grace — prophecy, service, teaching, exhortation, giving, leading, mercy. The chapter then moves into a rapid sequence of ethical exhortations: let love be genuine; hate what is evil; hold fast to what is good; love one another with brotherly affection; outdo one another in showing honor; be fervent in spirit; contribute to the needs of the saints; practice hospitality. The second half of the chapter addresses the treatment of enemies: bless those who persecute you; bless and do not curse; weep with those who weep; associate with the lowly; never be wise in your own sight; repay no one evil for evil; do not take revenge; instead, if your enemy is hungry, feed him — for in so doing you will heap burning coals on his head. Overcome evil with good.

Romans 13

Governing authorities and the debt of love

Chapter 13 contains the verses that have generated more controversy in the history of Romans' reception than any other passage in the letter. Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and the authorities that exist have been instituted by God. He who resists the authorities resists what God has appointed, and those who resist will incur judgment. The context is Rome in the mid-50s, under Nero in his early relatively peaceful years, and the community addressed is a small minority in the imperial capital who have every incentive to keep a low profile. The counsel of public peace for a vulnerable community has been read across the tradition as a general theology of the state, with consequences that Paul could not have anticipated. The chapter then moves to the summary of all law in love your neighbor as yourself. Love does no wrong to a neighbor — therefore love is the fulfilling of the law. The chapter ends with an eschatological urgency: salvation is nearer than when we first believed; the night is far gone, the day is at hand — put on the armor of light, put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh.

Romans 14

The strong and the weak: welcome one another

Chapter 14 addresses a concrete dispute in the Roman church. There are those Paul calls the weak in faith — who eat only vegetables and observe certain days as special — and those he calls the strong — who eat anything and regard every day as the same. Neither position is described as heretical; this is a dispute about practice within the community, probably relating to Jewish food laws and Sabbath observance. Paul's counsel is directed primarily at the strong: welcome those who are weak in faith, but not for quarrels over opinions. The strong have the right on their side — all food is clean — but the right is not the highest consideration. Nothing is unclean in itself, but it is unclean for anyone who thinks it unclean. If your brother is grieved by what you eat, you are no longer walking in love. The chapter works through the logic of mutual accountability before God: we will all stand before the judgment seat of God, and each of us will give an account of himself. Therefore do not pass judgment on one another anymore. The closing principle: whatever does not proceed from faith is sin. The strong should act from conscience, but where the conscience of the weak is violated, the act is itself wrong.

Romans 15

The Gentile mission and Paul's plans for Spain

Chapter 15 continues directly from chapter 14. We who are strong ought to bear with the failings of the weak, and not to please ourselves. The example is Christ himself — who did not please himself but bore reproach for God's sake, citing Psalm 69. The whole scripture was written for our instruction, that through endurance and through the encouragement of the scriptures we might have hope. Then the prayer: may the God of endurance and encouragement grant you to live in such harmony with one another, in accord with Christ Jesus, that together you may with one voice glorify God. Christ has become a servant to the circumcised — the Jews — in order to confirm the promises to the patriarchs, and so that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy. Four quotations from Psalms, Deuteronomy, and Isaiah follow, each predicting Gentile inclusion. Then Paul's own role: he has been given grace to be a minister of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles, a priestly servant of the gospel. He has completed his work from Jerusalem to Illyricum — modern-day Yugoslavia — and now plans to come to Rome on his way to Spain, after first delivering the Jerusalem collection. He asks for the community's prayers for his trip to Jerusalem, where he expects trouble from the unbelievers.

Romans 16

Phoebe, Junia, Tertius, and the doxology

Chapter 16 is the longest closing greeting in any of Paul's letters. He begins by commending Phoebe, deacon of the church at Cenchreae and the carrier of the letter, to the Roman community. Then the greetings begin: Prisca and Aquila, who risked their necks for Paul's life; Epaenetus, the first convert in Asia; Mary, who worked hard; Andronicus and Junia, who are prominent among the apostles and were in Christ before Paul — Junia, almost certainly a woman, has been translated as the masculine Junias by interpreters uncomfortable with a female apostle; modern scholarship has largely restored the feminine. The list continues for twenty-six people, several of them women (Mary, Tryphaena, Tryphosa, Persis, Julia, the sister of Nereus). At verse 22, Tertius the scribe inserts his own greeting. Paul then adds a warning against those who cause divisions and obstacles contrary to the teaching — a brief, sharp note that stands out in the middle of the greetings. The letter ends with a doxology — now to him who is able to strengthen you according to my gospel and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery that was kept secret for long ages but has now been disclosed — the most sustained doxological sentence in the letter, and the seal of everything Paul has argued since verse 16 of chapter 1.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Thesis at 1:16-17

Paul states the thesis of the entire letter in two verses. Everything that follows is an unpacking of those verses: salvation, righteousness, faith, Jew, Greek, gospel, power — named in the order he will treat them.

Justification by Faith

The doctrine for which Romans is best known. Paul argues from chapter 3 through chapter 4 that no one can be justified by works of the law — and that Abraham himself was reckoned righteous before he was circumcised.

Jew and Gentile in One Community

The hardest single problem for Paul. The gospel fulfils Israel's promises — yet most of Israel has not received it. Romans 9-11 is his most sustained answer: three anguished chapters ending in a doxology.

Romans 7 and the Divided I

One of the most personal passages in any of Paul's letters and one of the most contested. The I who cannot do what it wants has been argued over for two thousand years — and quoted by every later writer who needed to describe weakness of will.

Living Sacrifice — The Practice of the Gospel

Chapter 12 begins with the word therefore. Everything from here is the working-out of what the theology means in practice: present your bodies, love one another, the governing authorities, the strong and the weak.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Paul
Author, Apostle to the Gentiles

Born in Tarsus, a Roman citizen, raised as a Pharisee, converted on the road to Damascus in the mid-30s. Writes Romans from Corinth near the end of his third missionary journey. The letter is the most carefully argued of his surviving writings and his most considered statement of the gospel he has preached for twenty years.

Tertius
Scribe

The professional scribe who took down Paul's dictation and produced the physical letter. He inserts his own greeting at 16:22 — I Tertius, the writer of this letter, greet you in the Lord — one of the most quietly remarkable verses in the New Testament.

Phoebe
Deacon, carrier of the letter

A deacon of the church at Cenchreae, the eastern port of Corinth. Paul commends her to the Roman church at the start of chapter 16. She carried the letter to Rome, and probably read it aloud at its first reception there.

Abraham
Father of faith

Not a contemporary of Paul but the figure on whom the demonstration of justification by faith in chapter 4 turns. Paul argues that Abraham was reckoned righteous before he was circumcised — making him the father not only of the circumcised but of all who believe.

The I of Romans 7
Divided self

The first-person voice that emerges at 7:14 in one of the most contested passages in Paul. I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate. Who the I is — Paul before conversion, Paul as believer, Adam, Israel, humanity — has been argued for two thousand years.

Junia
Apostle (Romans 16:7)

Named at 16:7 as outstanding among the apostles — a woman, which caused translators for centuries to masculinize the name to Junias. Modern scholarship has returned to the female form. She is the only woman in the New Testament identified as an apostle.
