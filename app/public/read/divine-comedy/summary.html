The Divine Comedy

1320 CE · by Dante Alighieri

The book in brief

The Divine Comedy is a poem in three parts — Inferno, Purgatorio, Paradiso — that recounts a vision Dante claims to have had during Holy Week of the year 1300, in which he travels through Hell, climbs the mountain of Purgatory, and ascends through the spheres of Heaven to a vision of God. He is guided through Hell and Purgatory by the Roman poet Virgil, and through Paradise by Beatrice, the Florentine woman he had loved at a distance since childhood and who had died at twenty-four.

It is one hundred cantos in three parts of thirty-three each, plus an introductory canto, written in the verse form Dante invented for it: terza rima, three-line interlocking rhymes that pull the reader forward without rest. He wrote it in Italian — not Latin — at a time when serious poetry was supposed to be written in Latin; this single decision did more than any other to make Italian a literary language. He composed it during nineteen years of exile from Florence, his city having banished him under sentence of death in 1302, and he never returned.

The poem is at once a personal vision (Dante is a character in it, his sins are addressed, his salvation is at stake), a theological summa (it dramatizes the moral order of medieval Christianity in the most concrete terms ever attempted), a political treatise (it sorts contemporary Italian politics into circles of damnation and ascent), and a love poem to a woman who had been dead for ten years when Dante began writing. T.S. Eliot called it, with Shakespeare, one of the two great poets of the modern world. Borges said no other book in any language was its equal. Reading it is to read a poem that contains, in some way, almost everything Western literature would later try to do separately.

Why it still matters

The Argument for Italian

Dante's choice to write the Comedy in Italian, in 1300, was a decision against centuries of intellectual habit. Serious work — theology, philosophy, formal poetry — was written in Latin. The vernaculars were for love songs and informal speech. By writing the most ambitious poem of the age in Tuscan Italian, Dante did two things at once. He made the vernacular respectable as a vehicle for high thought. And he made Tuscan, specifically, the prestige form of Italian — the form that would, six centuries later, become the basis of standard modern Italian. No other writer in any European language has had this kind of effect on the language he was writing in. To read the Divine Comedy in Italian is to read the founding document of Italian as a literary language. To read it in any language is to be reading the work that proved the local tongue could carry, in poetry, the full weight of what Latin had carried in prose.

Hell as a Map of the Modern Conscience

Dante's Inferno is the most influential single work in the literary imagination of evil. The structure — circles descending in increasing severity, sins classified by their motive (incontinence, violence, fraud), each sin punished by an exact contrapasso designed to embody what it really was — has shaped the way readers think about wrongdoing ever since. Almost no later treatment of moral failure does not, somewhere, work in the vocabulary Dante established. The image of fraud as deeper than violence, the idea that betrayal is the worst of all sins, the conviction that punishment should mirror the sin: all of these are Dante's contributions to the moral imagination of the West. Even readers with no theological commitments find Inferno organizing their intuitions about evil. It is the book that taught Western culture how to picture damnation, and the picture has held for seven hundred years. Modern people still see Hell, when they imagine it at all, with Dante's eyes.

Beatrice, and Love as the Engine of Salvation

At the center of the Comedy is a woman Dante had probably exchanged about a dozen sentences with in his lifetime. He saw Beatrice Portinari first when he was nine and she was eight; she died when she was twenty-four, married to someone else. By the time Dante began writing the Comedy a decade later, she had become for him the figure of grace — the love that, given the chance to grow into adoration of God, lifts the soul through Heaven. The radical theological claim of the Comedy is that human love, properly directed, is itself the road to the divine. Beatrice does not represent God; she leads to him. The vision of Paradise is built around her. This idea — that the love of one human being can be the route by which a soul approaches the absolute — has been one of the most consequential in Western literature. Every later treatment of romantic love as spiritually serious is, in some way, downstream of Beatrice.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The audacity of placing your enemies in Hell.

Dante condemned real people — popes, politicians, rivals — to specific torments while they were still alive. I want to track who he put where and why, and ask what it means to claim God's judgement as your literary device.

Beatrice as the force that moves everything.

She is the woman who sends Virgil to rescue Dante in the dark wood. She is the guide through Paradise. She is, for Dante, the face of divine love. I want to understand why a real woman he barely knew becomes the universe's organising principle.

The Inferno as a map of the self.

The poem begins with Dante lost in a dark wood at midlife. Every circle of Hell he descends through is a category of human failure. I want to read the descent as interior — what it says about what Dante feared, regretted, and was tempted by.

The architecture of moral philosophy.

Every location in the Comedy is designed — sins punished by contrapasso (the punishment mirrors the crime), virtues rewarded by proximity to God. I want to follow the logic of that structure and understand the complete moral system Dante built into the geography of the afterlife.

Key figures

Dante
The Pilgrim

The poet of Florence, exiled in 1302, dead in Ravenna in 1321 — and the protagonist of his own poem. The Dante who walks through Hell, Purgatory, and Heaven is younger and frailer than the poet who is writing about him. He faints. He weeps. He misjudges. He has to be corrected, often sharply, by his guides. Dante's own decision to put himself in his masterpiece, fully visible with all his faults, is one of the formal innovations of the poem.

Virgil
Reason as Guide

The Roman poet of the Aeneid, Dante's literary master, his guide through Hell and Purgatory. As a virtuous pagan he cannot enter Heaven; he lives, with Homer and Plato, in the first circle of Hell, where the absence of God is itself the punishment. His relationship with Dante is one of the great teacher-student bonds in literature: patient, sometimes severe, deeply tender. When he leaves the poem at the top of Mount Purgatory, the reader feels the loss as keenly as Dante does.

Beatrice
Divine Love

Beatrice Portinari, a Florentine woman Dante had loved at a distance since seeing her at the age of nine. She died at twenty-four, married to someone else. In the Comedy she is the soul who prays Virgil into sending help to the lost Dante, descends into limbo to make the request, and waits in the Garden of Eden at the top of Mount Purgatory. From there she guides him through the spheres of Heaven. She is the engine of his salvation, and Dante's greatest creation.

Francesca da Rimini
The Most Sympathetic Damned Soul

A noblewoman condemned to Hell's second circle for adultery, who tells her story so beautifully that Dante weeps and faints. She is the Inferno's most famous encounter — and Dante's most honest admission that Hell and sympathy can coexist. The poem's whole problem with damnation is concentrated in this scene: the reader pities her, Dante pities her, and yet the moral structure of the cosmos requires that she be where she is.

Ulysses
The Restless Voyager

The Greek hero of the Odyssey, encountered in the eighth circle of Hell among the false counselors. Dante does not damn him for his Trojan tricks; Dante damns him for the voyage past the limits set for human exploration that Dante invents for him. Ulysses tells the story of leaving Ithaca again, sailing past the Pillars of Hercules, and being drowned within sight of Mount Purgatory. The episode is one of the most ambivalent in the poem — Dante clearly admires Ulysses's reach even as he condemns it.

Lucifer
The Frozen Center

At the bottom of Hell, frozen in a lake of ice up to his chest, three-faced, six-winged, perpetually weeping. In each of his three mouths he chews on a great traitor: Judas Iscariot, Brutus, Cassius. Dante's Lucifer is not a roaring rebel; he is dumb, immobile, pitiable, and trapped — the final horror of evil understood not as energy but as paralysis. The image is one of the great theological inventions of the poem: the proper end-state of a will turned permanently away from God.
