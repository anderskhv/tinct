Job a guided tour

c. 6th–4th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Writings · Wisdom

A righteous man in the land of Uz loses everything in a single afternoon — and then refuses, for thirty-five chapters, to accept any of the explanations his friends offer. The answer, when it comes, is God speaking out of a whirlwind about Behemoth and Leviathan.

The book in brief

Job opens with a wager in heaven. Ha-satan — the adversary, a member of the divine council, not the medieval devil — challenges God to test whether Job's piety is disinterested. Within two chapters Job has lost his children, his livestock, and the health of his own body. He sits in the ashes outside the city, scraping himself with a potsherd, while three friends come and sit with him in silence for seven days. Then Job opens his mouth, and one of the greatest poems ever composed begins.

From chapter 3 to chapter 31 the book is a sustained poetic dialogue: Job protests, each friend answers, Job answers back — three rounds of this, with mounting intensity. The friends articulate the orthodoxy of their tradition: God is just, the righteous prosper, the wicked suffer, therefore Job must have sinned. Job rejects it. He insists, with bitterness and beauty in equal measure, that his suffering does not fit the pattern, that the friends are physicians of no value, and that he wants his day in court. The Voice from the Whirlwind arrives in chapter 38 and speaks for four chapters — not to answer the question, but to ask seventy others. Job lays his hand upon his mouth. The book is the longest sustained protest against unmerited suffering in any ancient literature, and the argument it leaves unresolved has been going on for two and a half thousand years.

Job 1 of 42
Job 1

Job's world and its undoing

Chapter 1 opens the prose frame in which the entire poem is set. We are introduced to Job of the land of Uz — blameless, upright, wealthy beyond most men of the east, the father of seven sons and three daughters. The camera then shifts to heaven, where the sons of God present themselves before the Lord and ha-satan, the adversary, comes among them. The Lord invites him to consider Job; the adversary answers with the wager: 'Doth Job fear God for nought?' The Lord agrees to the first test. Within a single chapter, four messengers arrive in quick succession. The Sabeans have taken the oxen and asses. Fire from heaven has consumed the sheep. The Chaldeans have taken the camels. A wind from the wilderness has struck the house where Job's sons and daughters were feasting and all of them are dead. Job rises, tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground, and says: 'The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord.'

Job 2

Boils, a bitter wife, three friends

Chapter 2 opens with a second scene in heaven that mirrors the first almost exactly — the sons of God, the Satan, the same exchange — before the stakes are raised. The Lord grants permission for the Satan to touch Job's body, withholding only his life. Job is struck with sore boils from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head. He sits outside the city in the ashes, scraping himself with a potsherd — the image of a man who has moved beyond the social world. His wife speaks the one sentence she is given: 'Dost thou still retain thine integrity? Curse God, and die.' Job answers that one receives good and evil alike from God's hand. The narrator confirms again: 'In all this did not Job sin with his lips.' Then three friends — Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, Zophar the Naamathite — hear of his disaster, come from their countries, agree to meet, and sit with him on the ground seven days and seven nights without speaking. 'For they saw that his grief was very great.' The silence is where the book pauses before Job opens his mouth in chapter 3.

Job 3

The curse on the day of birth

Chapter 3 is the opening aria of the poetic dialogue, and it announces at once that the Job of the poem is not the patient Job of the prose frame. He curses the day of his birth — 'Let the day perish wherein I was born, and the night in which it was said, There is a man child conceived.' He calls on those who curse days, skilled in rousing Leviathan, to make that day dark. He wishes the stars of its morning had gone out. He asks why he was not stillborn, why knees received him and breasts nursed him. He lists those who now lie together in the grave: kings, counsellors, princes, the wicked, the weary. He envies the slave whose master cannot reach him. He asks why light is given to a man whose way is hid, who is hedged in by God. The poem ends with the bleak inventory of what has come to him: sighing is his meat; his roarings are poured out like water; that which he feared has come upon him; he has no quietness, no rest, no peace — only trouble.

Job 4

Eliphaz: a word in secret

Eliphaz the Temanite speaks first, and he begins with the tact of a man who has respect for Job and also has an argument he cannot keep to himself. He reminds Job that he himself has instructed many and comforted the grieving; now that trouble has come to him, should he lose heart? Then Eliphaz makes his central claim: the innocent do not perish; those who plough iniquity reap it. He anchors this not in scripture but in personal experience. And then he reports a vision: a thing was secretly brought to him, a voice passed his ear in the deep silence of the night. A spirit glided before his face; the hair of his flesh stood up. The spirit spoke: 'Shall mortal man be more just than God? Shall a man be more pure than his maker?' Mortals are charged with error even by God, and mortal life — crushed before the moth, between morning and evening — is correspondingly fragile. The vision gives Eliphaz's theology a source beyond tradition: it has come to him through personal revelation.

Job 5

Eliphaz: seek God and be restored

Chapter 5 is the second half of Eliphaz's opening speech. Having established that the wicked perish and the innocent are sustained, he now tells Job what he should do: commit his cause to God, who does great and unsearchable things. God raises the low and puts down those who mourn in safety. He frustrates the devices of the crafty. The poor are saved by God's hand from the sword and from the mouth of the mighty. Then comes the central passage of the speech: 'Happy is the man whom God correcteth; therefore despise not thou the chastening of the Almighty.' The logic is that affliction is divine discipline, and if Job will accept it as such, and turn to God, the restoration that follows will be comprehensive — protection from famine, from death in war, from the beasts of the field, from the stones of the field. His descendants will be like the grass of the earth; he will die in a good old age. This is the friends' doctrine in its most attractive form: suffering is correction, correction leads to restoration, and the man who accepts both is finally blessed.

Job 6

Job: my grief outweighs the sea

Job's first reply begins with a statement of the weight of his grief: if it were laid in the balances against the sand of the sea, it would be heavier. He has not cried out without cause. He then makes a striking claim: he would not refuse the consolation of death — 'it would be my consolation' to be crushed by God, to have God let loose his hand and cut him off. He is not suicidal in the modern sense; he is articulating the extremity of his situation by saying that annihilation would be preferable to his present condition. He challenges the friends: what wrong has he done? Show him, and he will be silent. But their reproofs are useless, like wind. A man in despair should have the devotion of his friends. He compares them to a wadi — a seasonal stream that is full of water in winter and vanishes in summer when the traveller most needs it. They have seen a dreadful thing and are afraid of it. He asks only that they tell him where he has gone wrong; speak plainly, don't deceive him with empty comfort.

Job 7

Job addresses God directly

Chapter 7 shifts Job's address from his friends to God himself — the first direct address to the divine in the dialogues. He begins with the condition of human life in general: it is like the life of a hired man, whose days are numbered, who longs for the end of his shift. He has months of emptiness; his nights are full of tossing until the dawn. When he looks for relief it does not come. His skin is broken and full of worms. He asks: what is man that God magnifies him, that God visits him every morning and tries him every moment? He is asking Psalm 8's question ('What is man that thou art mindful of him?') but inverting its sense: the attention that the psalmist celebrates as honour, Job experiences as surveillance and persecution. He wishes God would look away. He asks, in direct address, why God has made him his target. Why cannot God forgive his transgression, if he has one? He will soon be dead — God will seek him in the morning and he will not be there.

Job 8

Bildad: the ancients knew better

Bildad the Shuhite speaks second, and his manner is crisper and less warm than Eliphaz's. He opens with reproach: 'How long wilt thou speak these things? and how long shall the words of thy mouth be like a strong wind?' He makes the retribution argument in its most direct form: God does not pervert justice. If Job's children sinned, they were delivered into the hand of their transgression. If Job will seek God earnestly, God will restore him. Then Bildad grounds this not in vision, as Eliphaz did, but in ancestral tradition. The fathers of wisdom knew these things, and their wisdom is reliable: 'For we are but of yesterday, and know nothing.' The ancients observed that papyrus without marsh and reed without water wither and die before any other plant; so the paths of those who forget God. But those who are perfect shall be strengthened; God will not cast away a blameless man. The chapter ends with the promise that Job's present condition will look small compared to the flourishing that awaits him if he repents.

Job 9

Job: how can a man contend with God?

Chapter 9 is one of the most searching legal-philosophical passages in the book. Job begins by agreeing with Eliphaz's question: 'I know it is so of a truth; but how should man be just with God?' He agrees that God is almighty, that the mountains move and the stars go out at God's word, that no one can resist God or ask what he is doing. But then Job presses the logical consequence: if that is so, then how could a trial between Job and God be fair? If Job called and God answered, he would not believe that God would hear him in earnest — God crushes Job without cause and multiplies his wounds. He cannot serve his own case. He is blameless, he says — 'it is all one; therefore I say he destroyeth the perfect and the wicked.' God is beyond subpoena. There is no daysman, no arbiter, who can lay his hand on both parties. Job needs a mediator between himself and God and there is none. The chapter ends with Job stating plainly that he is afraid of God's afflictions and that God is not righteous toward him.

Job 10

Job: why did you make me to suffer?

Chapter 10 is a sustained address to God that moves between the intimacy of a creator-creature relationship and the bewilderment of a man who cannot understand why his maker has turned against him. Job's soul is weary of his life. He will speak freely and ask God: why dost thou contend with me? Is it good to oppress and to despise the work of thine own hands? Did God's hands make him and fashion him, pour him out as milk and curdle him as cheese, clothe him with skin and flesh, fence him with bones and sinews — and now the same hands destroy him? Job asks whether God has eyes of flesh, whether God sees as a man sees. He was created; God knew he would sin if he was given the chance; and yet God made him anyway and now condemns him. He asks why he was brought out of the womb at all — let him alone long enough to take comfort before the dark comes. The chapter ends with one of the book's bleakest geographical images: the land of darkness and the shadow of death, where there is no order and the light is as darkness.

Job 11

Zophar: God knows your hidden sin

Zophar the Naamathite is the third friend to speak and the most blunt. He opens with indignation at Job's long speeches: should a man full of talk not be answered? Should a man who claims to be pure in God's eyes go unchallenged? Zophar's central argument is that God's wisdom is deeper than Job can measure — deeper than heaven, deeper than Sheol, longer than the earth and broader than the sea. If God passed by and shut someone in, who can hinder him? God knows vain men; he sees wickedness. Then Zophar makes the move that is the sharpest of all: 'Know therefore that God exacteth of thee less than thine iniquity deserveth.' He is not merely arguing that Job has sinned; he is arguing that Job's actual punishment is merciful relative to what his sin warrants. The chapter closes with the promise that if Job will put away iniquity and spread his hands toward God, he will be restored — his life brighter than the noonday, his hope secure. The wicked, by contrast, shall not escape. It is the retribution doctrine at its most severe.

Job 12

Job: the wisdom of birds and beasts

Chapter 12 opens the longest continuous speech Job makes in the first cycle. He begins with biting sarcasm: 'No doubt but ye are the people, and wisdom shall die with you.' He knows everything they know; he is not inferior to them. He is the man his neighbours once came to, and now he is a laughingstock. He asks them to consult the animals: ask now the beasts, and they shall teach thee; the fowls of the air, the fish of the sea. These creatures will confirm that the hand of the Lord has made all this. Then Job moves to a catalogue of what God does — and it is not a catalogue of justice. God leads counsellors away captive, makes judges fools, looses the bond of kings, overthrows the mighty, removes the speech of the trusty, and pours contempt on princes. He leads nations astray; he makes nations great and destroys them. He takes away understanding from the chiefs of the people of the earth and makes them wander in a wilderness where there is no way. The catalogue is a demonstration that raw divine power is not the same thing as moral order — and that the evidence of the natural world, if read honestly, supports his argument rather than the friends'.

Job 13

Job demands his day in court

Chapter 13 is the most forensically direct of Job's speeches so far. He begins with a claim about knowledge: 'mine eye hath seen all this, mine ear hath heard and understood it; what ye know, the same do I know also.' He will not be instructed in what he already knows. Then he turns on the friends with increasing sharpness: they are forgers of lies, physicians of no value. Would they speak wickedly for God and talk deceitfully for him? Would they accept his person and contend for God? God will search them out. 'Will not his excellency make you afraid?' Then Job pivots, with what feels like a sudden decision, to speak directly to God. He will take his life in his hand — even if God slay him, he will maintain his own ways before God. He will speak and God will answer. He then addresses God with a request: withdraw thine hand from me, and let not thy dread make me afraid; then call thou, and I will answer — or let me speak, and answer thou me. He wants a fair hearing in which neither party intimidates the other. He is ready to present his case.

Job 14

Man that is born of a woman

Chapter 14 closes the first cycle of speeches, and Job's tone shifts from forensic argument to elegy. 'Man that is born of a woman is of few days, and full of trouble. He cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down; he fleeth also as a shadow, and continueth not.' He asks God to look away from man for a season, as one looks away from a hired labourer through the day. Then Job entertains, briefly and beautifully, a thought about the afterlife: if a tree is cut down, it will sprout again from its stump; but when a man dies, where is he? He lies down and rises not till the heavens are no more. Job then imagines — as a conditional, a wish — that God would hide him in Sheol, appoint a time and remember him, and call him back. But then he turns and closes the thought: no, the waters wear away the stone; the hope of man perishes. The chapter ends with a lament on the body's decay and the strangeness of a God who makes children honorable and the man knows it not, who afflicts him while he mourns.

Job 15

Eliphaz returns: ancient guilt

The second cycle begins with Eliphaz speaking again, and his tone is markedly harder. He opens by accusing Job's own words: 'Thine own mouth condemneth thee, and not I.' Job's speech has undermined piety; it has encouraged others to do the same. He returns to the question of human purity before God, but now he grounds it differently: 'What is man, that he should be clean? and he which is born of a woman, that he should be righteous?' The ancients had a vision of human sinfulness — even the heavens are not clean in God's sight. This is not merely Eliphaz's observation; it is presented as the accumulated wisdom of the ages. He then describes the fate of the wicked man at considerable length: travail all his days, the sound of the destroyer always in his ears, darkness and terror, the day of consumption ready to devour him. The wicked man shakes his fist against God and runs at him with a thick-bossed shield. The chapter closes with the retribution doctrine restated for the second cycle, with increased emphasis on the primordial and universal character of human sinfulness as the warrant for it.

Job 16

Job: a witness in heaven

Chapter 16 is Job's reply to Eliphaz's second speech, and it opens with one of the great reversals in the dialogues: 'I have heard many such things; miserable comforters are ye all.' If they were in his place and he were in theirs, he could speak the same speeches, shake his head at them, and strengthen them with his mouth — but he would not. He then describes what God has done to him with devastating physical specificity: God has torn him in his wrath, gnashed upon him with his teeth, sharpened his eyes against him; enemies gaped upon him with their mouth; God delivered him to the ungodly; God broke him asunder and held him by the neck and dashed him to pieces; God set him up as his mark. Then comes the pivotal move: 'Also now, behold, my witness is in heaven, and my record is on high.' Even as he calls out to God for justice, he claims that something in heaven will speak for him. The language is deliberately ambiguous — it might be God himself, it might be a figure distinct from God — but it anticipates the redeemer claim of chapter 19 and is the first moment in the dialogues where Job imagines a vindication that transcends the friends' framework.

Job 17

My spirit is broken

Chapter 17 continues Job's second-cycle speech and moves further into exhaustion and isolation. His spirit is broken; his days are extinct; the graves are ready for him. He is made a byword of the people; they spit in his face. His eye has grown dim from grief; all his members are as a shadow. The upright, he says, are astonished at this — which is an implicit challenge to the friends' interpretation. Upright men see Job's suffering and are not able to explain it by any simple formula; they are astonished, which is the mark of encountering something that exceeds their categories. He accuses those around him — perhaps the friends, perhaps others — of laying snares for him. He then turns to contemplation of death: if he hopes for Sheol as his house, if he has spread his bed in the darkness, then even the hope of seeing his situation resolved in this life is gone. He closes with a brief ironic turn: 'Where is now my hope?' Even wisdom's consolations — rest, hope — belong to Sheol with him.

Job 18

Bildad: the fate of the wicked

Bildad's second speech is a sustained rhetorical portrait of the wicked man's end. He opens with exasperation: 'How long will it be ere ye make an end of words?' He accuses Job of tearing himself in anger, and asks whether the earth is to be forsaken for Job's sake. Then he launches into a detailed catalogue of the snares that await the wicked. The snare catches him by the heel; the robber waits for him. His strength is hunger-bitten; calamity is ready at his side. It devours the strength of his skin; the firstborn of death devours his limbs. His confidence is rooted out of his tent; it brings him to the king of terrors. In his tent nothing remains; brimstone is scattered upon his habitation. His roots dry up beneath and his branches are cut off above. His remembrance perishes from the earth; he has no son or nephew. Those who come after shall be astonished at his day. This is the wicked man's fate. Bildad does not name Job. But in the context of the dialogue, the description is unmistakable in its application.

Job 19

I know that my redeemer liveth

Chapter 19 is the emotional and theological climax of the dialogues. Job begins by reproaching the friends: how long will ye vex me and break me in pieces? He has been wronged — whether by God or circumstance — and even if he acknowledges that, it is his problem, not theirs to exploit. He then enumerates, in a careful and mounting list, the full scope of his social abandonment: God has fenced him in; his brethren, kinsfolk, familiar friends, guests in his house, maids, manservant, wife, young children — all have forsaken him or look at him strangely. Even young children despise him. All the men of his intimate council abhor him. Then comes the pivot: 'Oh that my words were now written! oh that they were printed in a book! That they were graven with an iron pen and lead in a rock for ever!' He wants his protest to be permanent. And then: 'For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth; and though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God.' The redeemer claim crystallises everything the book has been moving toward — an advocate, a vindicator, who exists beyond the dialogue's impasse.

Job 20

Zophar: the triumph of the wicked is short

Zophar's second speech is his most sustained and least patient. He opens with urgency: his thoughts cause him to answer, and he cannot be still. Job has put him to shame. He launches at once into the central motif: the triumphing of the wicked is short and the joy of the hypocrite is but for a moment. The wicked man rises high — to the heavens, his head touching the clouds — but perishes like his own dung. Those who have seen him will say, where is he? He flies away like a dream; he is chased away as a vision of the night. His children seek the favour of the poor; his hands restore his goods. His bones are full of the sin of his youth. The evil he has hidden under his tongue, which he cherished under the palate, is turned in his bowels and is the gall of asps within him. He has swallowed riches and must vomit them up; God casts them out of his belly. He will not rejoice in the produce of his trade. He has violently oppressed the poor, seized a house he did not build, and his prosperity will not endure. The chapter ends with the confident assertion that this is the portion of a wicked man from God, and the heritage appointed to him by God.

Job 21

Job: the wicked prosper — look at the evidence

Chapter 21 is Job's most sustained empirical argument. He opens by demanding that the friends hear him — 'suffer me that I may speak; and after that I have spoken, mock on.' He then makes an argument the friends have not been able to answer: look at the wicked. The wicked man's seed is established, his offspring before his eyes; his children dance; he has the ox, the ass, the sheep, the flock. He enjoys life and goes down to Sheol in peace. They say to God, 'Depart from us; we desire not the knowledge of thy ways' — and nothing happens. 'How oft is the candle of the wicked put out? and how oft cometh their destruction upon them?' Not often. Job grants that some judgment may fall on their children — but he dismisses this: the man ought to be punished himself, not his children; the man himself ought to see his destruction. The friends might say that the wicked man's house is burned. Job answers: 'Have ye not asked them that go by the way?' Ask the travellers; they will tell you the wicked man escapes in the day of destruction. He closes with the retribution formula turned against itself: when the mourners carry the wicked man to his grave, even the clods of the valley are sweet to him.

Job 22

Eliphaz's third speech: Job accused directly

Chapter 22 opens the third and final round of the dialogue. Eliphaz, who began with the gentlest reproach in chapter 4, now accuses Job point-blank: he must have stripped the poor naked, withheld water from the thirsty, refused bread to the hungry, sent widows away empty. These are not vague charges — they are the specific sins the wisdom tradition associates with catastrophic divine punishment. Eliphaz is not reporting what he has seen. He is reasoning backward from the punishment to infer the crime, which is exactly the inferential logic Job has been contesting throughout the dialogue. The chapter closes with an earnest plea for Job's return to God, promising that if Job acquaints himself with God and removes wickedness, the Almighty will be his defence and he will have gold in abundance. The invitation is sincere; the diagnosis it rests on is, the book will eventually say, wrong.

Job 23

Job: if I could only find him

Chapter 23 is one of the most searching speeches in the dialogue. Job does not answer Eliphaz's charges because he does not accept that they apply to him. Instead he turns entirely inward and describes the experience of trying to find God and failing. He knows what he would say if granted an audience: he would lay his case in order before God, fill his mouth with arguments, and understand the words God would answer. He is confident he would not be condemned. But he cannot find God. He goes forward, backward, left, right — God is not there. The chapter then turns darker: God knows where Job is, and when God has tried him, he will come forth as gold. But God is not arbitrary — he performs what is appointed for Job, and many such things are with him. This is not consolation; it is a description of entrapment. The chapter closes with Job in darkness, not knowing what to do with this God who terrifies him and who will not be found.

Job 24

Why does God not set times for judgment?

Chapter 24 widens the frame from Job's personal case to the problem of social injustice at large. If the doctrine of retribution were true, the wicked would suffer and be seen to suffer. Job looks at the world and does not see this. He describes, in vivid social observation, the crimes of the powerful: removing landmarks, stealing flocks, driving away donkeys, depriving orphans of their donkeys and taking the widow's ox. He then describes the poor who live in the cold, who glean the vineyard of the wicked, who lodge in the rock for want of shelter. The wicked, meanwhile, exploit the poor and murder in the dark. Job asks why God does not set an appointed time — a day of reckoning — if the moral order is real and operative. The chapter does not resolve its own question. It is a sustained observation that the world does not look the way retribution theology says it should look. Job is doing empirical theology, and the empirical observation is that the wicked are not visibly punished.

Job 25

Bildad's brief third speech: how can man be just?

Chapter 25 is the shortest chapter in the dialogue section and the last speech attributed to a named friend. Bildad does not answer Job's charges from chapter 24. He retreats to a theological claim about the vast distance between God and humanity. God has dominion and fear; he makes peace in his high places. His troops are numberless; his light rises on everyone. Can any man be justified before God? Even the moon is not bright and the stars are not pure in his sight. How much less man, who is a worm, and the son of man, who is a maggot? The argument is not an answer to Job's empirical case. It is an appeal to the infinite distance between the divine and the human — a claim that Job's demand for a hearing is simply inappropriate given the gulf between the two parties. The brevity of the speech, and the fact that Zophar does not speak a third time at all, signals that the dialogue has reached an impasse. The friends have run out of things to say.

Job 26

Job mocks the friends and praises God's power

Chapter 26 opens with withering irony. How hast thou helped him that is without power? How hast thou saved the arm that hath no strength? How hast thou counselled him that hath no wisdom? Job is addressing someone, and the most natural reading is Bildad — whose six-verse speech on divine transcendence is dismissed as useless help to a helpless man. Job then delivers his own meditation on divine power and cosmic order, one that is more expansive and more beautiful than anything the friends have managed. He describes the dead trembling below, the grave lying uncovered before God, the north stretched over empty space, the earth suspended upon nothing, the waters wrapped in thick cloud, the horizon marked on the face of the waters at the boundary of light and darkness, the pillars of heaven trembling at God's rebuke, the sea calmed by God's hand and the serpent slain. The chapter ends with Job's own admission: these are but the outer fringe of his works, and how faint a whisper do we hear of him. Job can praise divine power and mystery as well as the friends can — and refuses to let their invocation of it become an argument against his case.

Job 27

Job swears by God he will not yield

Chapter 27 is a formal oath, one of the most striking rhetorical moments in the dialogue. Job swears by God's life — the God who has taken away his right and vexed his soul — that he will not speak wickedness or utter deceit, and that he will hold fast his integrity as long as he breathes. He will never concede that his friends are right. His conscience is clear, and he will not abandon that claim. The oath is paradoxical: Job is swearing by the God he is disputing with. He invokes God as the authority for his oath at the same moment that he is challenging God in court. This is not contradiction. It is the position of someone who believes in the God he is accusing — believes in his justice, believes in his authority — and is willing to put his life on that belief by swearing by God against the friends who are invoking the same God. The chapter then shifts, in a passage that has puzzled commentators, to a description of the fate of the wicked that sounds like the friends' theology — suggesting that some verses may have been displaced or that Job is here appropriating the friends' idiom against them.

Job 28

The poem on wisdom: where is it found?

Chapter 28 is widely regarded as the most formally distinct chapter in the book. Most scholars treat it as a set-piece hymn on wisdom, possibly by a different hand, inserted at this point in the dialogue. Whether it belongs to Job, to the narrator, or to neither is debated. Structurally it falls between the dialogue proper and Job's final monologue, and it reads as a pause in the argument. The chapter opens with a meditation on human mining: men dig in the earth for silver, gold, iron, copper, sapphires. They have searched out all things; they can find what is hidden in the darkest places. But wisdom — where shall it be found? And where is the place of understanding? The sea says it is not in me; the deep says it is not in me. It cannot be bought with gold or silver or rubies. God understands its way; only he knows its place. And the chapter's answer: the fear of the Lord is wisdom, and to depart from evil is understanding. This is the traditional wisdom answer, and its presence here creates a deliberate irony — it is exactly what the friends have been saying all along, and it is also, potentially, what Job already knows and already does.

Job 29

Job recalls his former life

Chapter 29 opens Job's final monologue, which runs through chapter 31. He is no longer addressing the friends. He is speaking into the air, as if taking an inventory of what has been taken from him. He begins with the former days, when God's candle shone upon his head and he walked through darkness by God's light. He describes the household intimacy — when God was with him, when his children were about him, when his steps were washed with butter and the rock poured out rivers of oil. Then he describes his public life. When he went out to the gate of the city, young men saw him and hid themselves, old men rose up and stood, princes refrained from talking, nobles held their peace. His words fell on them as the dew on the grass. He was the eyes of the blind, feet of the lame; he was a father to the poor; he broke the fangs of the wicked. He thought he would die in his nest and multiply his days as the sand. His bow was renewed in his hand. The description is not nostalgia for luxury. It is the portrait of a fully realized moral life — the life the friends have been claiming he must never have had, because suffering of this scale requires wrongdoing of matching scale.

Job 30

Job now mocked by the lowest men

Chapter 30 is the counterpart to chapter 29 — the but now to the when of the former days. The contrast is brutal and precise. Men whose fathers Job would have refused to set with the dogs of his flock now make songs about him and are his byword. They are young men of no account, whose fathers were driven out of the land, who lived in desert caves and bushes, who brayed like animals. These men now mock him: they abhor Job, they flee from him, they spit on his face. His honour is pursued as by wind; his prosperity has passed like a cloud. God has cast him down and made him their target. He cries out but is not answered; he stands up and is not heard. God has become cruel to him; he attacks him with mighty hand and with tempest. Job is now in pain — his bones are pierced in the night, his gnawing pains take no rest, his garment is disfigured, God has flung him into the mire. The chapter ends with lament: Job went to the poor expecting good, and evil came; he looked for light and dark came; he mourned openly, as one who is desolate and without sun.

Job 31

Job's oath of innocence

Chapter 31 is Job's closing statement — the end of the dialogue section and the culmination of the entire case he has been building since chapter 3. It is structured as a series of conditional oaths: if I have done this, then let that punishment fall on me. He has not looked with lust at a virgin; if he had, let him reap what another sows. He has not walked in falsehood or hasted to deceit; if he has, let him be weighed in an even balance. He has not rejected the cause of his servants when they contended with him. He has not withheld bread from the poor, nor eaten alone without sharing, nor caused orphans to suffer, nor seen anyone perish for want of clothing. He has not made gold his hope or trusted in riches. He has not rejoiced at the ruin of his enemy or rejoiced that evil found him. He has not hidden his sin as Adam did. He has not mistreated his land or its workers. Item by item, the life he has described in chapter 29 is here sworn to. And then the final challenge: if only someone would hear me! Here is my signature; let the Almighty answer. Let my adversary write his indictment. The oath of innocence is complete. Job has nothing more to say.

Job 32

Elihu's anger and introduction

Chapter 32 introduces a character who has not previously appeared in the book: Elihu the son of Barachel the Buzite, of the family of Ram. He has been present throughout the dialogue but holding his peace out of deference to the friends' seniority. Now he is angry — angry at Job for justifying himself rather than God, and angry at the three friends because they have found no answer and yet have condemned Job. He waited while the elders spoke, trusting that great age would teach wisdom. It did not. There is a spirit in a man, he argues, and the inspiration of the Almighty gives understanding. The great are not always wise, nor do the aged always understand judgment. He has waited long enough, and he is full of matter, like new wine in bottles. He will speak, and he will not show partiality to any man or give flattering titles to anyone. He is explicit that flattery would put him in danger of being taken away by his maker. He will speak what he knows.

Job 33

Elihu: God speaks through dreams and pain

Chapter 33 is Elihu's first substantive argument. He claims that Job has said God does not answer and that the divine council is silent. But that is wrong, Elihu argues: God does speak, not in the way Job expects, but in two modes. First, in dreams and visions of the night, when deep sleep falls upon men, God opens men's ears, seals their instruction, in order to turn them from their deeds and cover men's pride. Second, in pain and sickness — God chastens men with pain upon their beds, so that their life abhors bread and their soul dainty meat. In this affliction a mediating angel, a interpreter, one among a thousand, may come to show a man his uprightness. If the man responds, the angel becomes his intercessor; God becomes gracious to him; the man's flesh is renewed, he comes to God with joy, God has restored the man's righteousness to him. Elihu is arguing that suffering is instructional and that Job's error is in refusing the lesson. He invites Job to answer if he can. This is the theology of suffering as divine pedagogy — different from the friends' retribution, but still committed to a moral economy of affliction and response.

Job 34

Elihu defends God's justice

Chapter 34 is Elihu's second speech, addressed to 'wise men' and 'men of knowledge' — meaning the friends as well as Job — and it is more polemical than chapter 33. Elihu quotes Job's own words back at him: Job has said he is righteous but God has taken away his judgment; that the man who consorts with evildoers profits more than the righteous. Elihu finds this scandalous. His argument for divine justice has two parts. First, God cannot be unjust because he holds all life in his hand — if he withdrew his spirit, all flesh would perish together and man would return to dust. A creator who sustains all life cannot be arbitrary in the way Job implies. Second, God is like a king who does not favour princes and who regards the rich and poor alike, because all are his work; therefore he can strike down the mighty without inquiry. The chapter's argument is more vigorous than the friends' but philosophically continuous with theirs: God is just by definition, and Job's challenge is impious. The chapter closes with Elihu's strongest condemnation of Job: he adds rebellion to his sin, he multiplies his words against God.

Job 35

Elihu: your righteousness cannot affect God

Chapter 35 develops a distinctive argument: the transcendence of God is so great that human righteousness or wickedness cannot touch him. Job has implied that it would profit him nothing to have been righteous, since God treats him the same regardless. Elihu's response is theological: look at the heavens — they are higher than you. Your wickedness cannot harm God, and your righteousness cannot benefit him. The argument is not that morality is irrelevant; it is that God's motive for caring about human morality cannot be self-interest. When men cry out because of oppressors, God does not answer because of the pride of evil men. They do not say, 'Where is God my maker?' They make empty cries for relief but not genuine supplication. Elihu argues that Job's cries have not been heard because they have been complaints rather than prayer, demand rather than worship. He accuses Job of speaking in vanity and multiplying words without knowledge. The chapter is brief and its argument is pointed: the premise that God should have been affected by Job's righteousness depends on a misunderstanding of what God is.

Job 36

Elihu: God is mighty and just in his teaching

Chapter 36 opens Elihu's fourth and final speech. He asks patience for a moment longer — he has yet things to say on God's behalf, and he will fetch his knowledge from afar and ascribe righteousness to his maker. His theology of suffering as instruction is now developed more fully. God is mighty but does not despise any; he does not preserve the wicked alive but gives right to the poor. He puts kings on the throne and removes them; when he afflicts the righteous, he opens their ear to instruction, and if they turn and serve him they finish their days in prosperity. If they do not obey, they perish by the sword and die without knowledge. Elihu warns Job specifically: do not turn to iniquity in the bitterness of your suffering; your wealth and the ransom on offer cannot keep you from your affliction. The chapter then pivots toward a hymn to God's works in nature — the rain, the thunder, the spread of the clouds — which anticipates the whirlwind speech that is about to come. Elihu is, in effect, warming up the argument that God's power in creation speaks to his authority over human life.

Job 37

Elihu's hymn to the storm

Chapter 37 is the climax of Elihu's speeches and their most beautiful section. His heart, he says, trembles and leaps from its place when it hears thunder. God thunders wondrously with his voice; great things he does which we do not understand. He commands the snow to fall, the great rain, the sealing of every man's hand so that all men may know his work. The beasts go to their lair and remain in their dens. From his chamber comes the whirlwind, cold out of the north, ice formed by the breath of God. He loads the cloud with moisture and the clouds scatter his lightning. They turn round and round as he steers them, to accomplish all that he commands. By his breath frost is given, and the broad waters are frozen. Does Job know how God lays his command on these clouds, how the light of his cloud spreads? Do you know this, Job? Job has been asked before if he understands; now the question is addressed to him directly, as a formal preparation for the divine interrogation. Elihu ends by insisting that God is clothed in terrible majesty and that mortal men should fear him. Whether Elihu's speech here partially pre-empts the whirlwind or makes space for it is a matter of how the speeches are edited together.

Job 38

God speaks from the whirlwind: the foundations

Chapter 38 is the beginning of one of the great passages of world literature. The LORD answers Job out of the whirlwind — suddenly, without preamble — and the voice is not the voice of the friends' theology or of Elihu's self-assurance. It is the voice of the creator addressing the creature. The first question is the frame for everything that follows: Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare if thou hast understanding. Who laid the cornerstone when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy? Who shut up the sea with doors when it burst forth from the womb? Have you ever commanded the morning since your days began? Have you entered the springs of the sea or walked in the recesses of the deep? Do you know the ordinances of the heavens or establish their rule on the earth? Who has put wisdom in the dark clouds, or given understanding to the mind? The questions do not answer Job's complaint. They redirect it. The scale of what God is doing — and what Job is not doing and cannot do — is the answer, or the frame within which any answer must be received.

Job 39

God's speech: the wild creatures

Chapter 39 continues the whirlwind speech into its most extended passage of nature writing. God asks Job whether he knows the time when the wild mountain goats give birth on the rocky heights, or when the deer bears her fawns. God attended to these things; Job was not there. Then the wild ass, who scorns the city, hears not the driver, ranges the mountains for his pasture — free and undomesticated in a way no human economy can contain. The wild ox will not serve Job; he will not stay by the manger; his great strength is useless to Job. God describes the ostrich, who leaves her eggs in the earth and does not know that a foot may crush them or that the wild beast may break them: God has made her forget wisdom. And yet when she lifts herself up, she laughs at the horse and his rider. Then the war horse: hast thou given the horse his strength, clothed his neck with thunder? He paws in the valley, he rejoices in his strength, he saith among the trumpets Ha ha, and he smelleth the battle afar off. The hawk flying south, the eagle building its nest on the cliff. The chapter places Job in a world of wild creatures that have never been under his governance and were never made for his use. They are beautiful, strange, and entirely outside the moral economy the friends and Job have both been arguing within.

Job 40

Job silenced; God speaks of Behemoth

Chapter 40 contains two distinct movements. The first is brief: God pauses and asks whether Job who contended with the Almighty will reprove him. The man who corrected God should answer. Job's reply is the shortest speech in the dialogue: Behold, I am of small account; what shall I answer thee? I lay my hand upon my mouth. Once I have spoken, and I will not answer. This is not the full repentance of chapter 42. It is a placing of the hand on the mouth — a recognized ancient gesture of having said too much, of silencing oneself before superior authority. The second movement begins God's second speech, which opens with a formal challenge: Gird up thy loins now like a man. Do you have an arm like God's? Will you condemn God so that you may be in the right? Then begins the description of Behemoth — usually identified as the hippopotamus or a mythological creature derived from it — which is presented as the chief of God's works, eating grass like an ox, his tail swaying like a cedar, his bones like tubes of bronze, the first of the works of God. Behold, Behemoth, which I made as I made you — the equality of creature is explicit, but the creature's power is beyond Job's governance. No one can approach Behemoth with a sword.

Job 41

Leviathan: the sea-dragon beyond all power

Chapter 41 is the climax of the whirlwind speeches. God asks whether Job can draw out Leviathan with a fishhook, press down his tongue with a cord, play with him as a bird, put him on a leash for his daughters. Can Job make a covenant with Leviathan? Will the creature make many supplications to Job and speak soft words? Will the merchants bargain over him? The irony is sharp: the creature who is entirely out of human power to approach, much less to trade in, is displayed as the measure of the gulf between God and Job. Then the description of Leviathan himself: his scales are his pride, shut up together as with a close seal. His sneezes flash forth light; his eyes are like the eyelids of the dawn. Out of his mouth go burning torches; sparks of fire leap out. Smoke goes out of his nostrils. His breath kindles coals and a flame goes out of his mouth. His heart is hard as stone, hard as a lower millstone. When he raises himself, the mighty are afraid. The sword, spear, dart, javelin — none of these avail against him. He makes the deep boil like a pot; he makes the sea like a pot of ointment. He is king over all the sons of pride. The speech ends. The wild creation has been surveyed. Job has been shown the creature whose existence is beyond his governance and whose very nature puts the scale of Job's complaint in a different register.

Job 42

Job's second answer; epilogue; restoration

Chapter 42 is the book's last chapter and one of its most argued-over. Job's second response to the whirlwind is eight verses: I know that thou canst do all things and that no purpose of thine can be thwarted. He quotes back the question God asked him — who is this that hideth counsel without knowledge? — and confesses that he spoke of things too wonderful for him, things he did not know. He had heard of God by the hearing of the ear; but now his eye seeth thee, and therefore he despises himself and repents in dust and ashes. The epilogue that follows is prose. God rebukes the friends and tells them to bring sacrifices for Job to offer on their behalf, because they have not spoken of God the thing that is right, as his servant Job has. Job prays for the friends and is heard. Then God restores Job's fortunes, gives him twice what he had before, seven new sons and three new daughters — named, beautiful, and given inheritance — and Job lives a hundred and forty years, sees four generations of descendants, and dies old and full of days. The book ends here. Whether this ending is consolation, problem, or both is the question every reader carries away.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The wager in the prologue

The book opens with two scenes in heaven that commit it, before Job ever speaks, to a precise argument. The Satan's challenge is that all religion is bribery — that piety dissolves the moment the rewards are removed. The book exists to test that proposition.

The friends' theology against Job's experience

The three friends are not strawmen. They articulate, with increasing severity, an orthodoxy that is coherent on its own terms. The book is the demonstration that there are situations in which a correct theology does not suffice.

The silence of God

From chapter 3 through chapter 37, God does not speak. The book stages, on a scale not attempted again in the Bible, the experience of religious silence — and earns the right, by the length of the wait, to ask what the answer means when it finally comes.

The Voice from the Whirlwind

When God finally speaks, the answer is not an explanation. It is a tour of the wild creation — seventy questions about the foundations of the earth, the constellations, the wild ass, the war horse, Behemoth, and Leviathan.

The restored ending and what it leaves unsettled

The book closes with a prose paragraph that has divided readers for two and a half thousand years. Job is restored twofold. New children are born. Job dies old and full of days. The ending resolves the plot while leaving everything that matters permanently open.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Job
the sufferer of the land of Uz

A man from the land of Uz, east of Israel — not an Israelite. Introduced as tam ve-yashar, complete and upright. Father of seven sons and three daughters, owner of prodigious wealth, the greatest of all the men of the east. Loses everything in a single afternoon. From chapter 3 forward he is the speaker of the most sustained protest against unmerited suffering in any ancient literature — theologically literate, demanding, refusing his friends' explanations, insisting on his innocence until the Voice arrives. When the Voice finally comes, he answers twice and lays his hand upon his mouth. The figure on whom every later argument about innocent suffering has had to position itself.

The Three Friends
Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar

Three sages from the wisdom traditions of the surrounding peoples — not Israelites. Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, Zophar the Naamathite. They sit with Job in silence for seven days, then spend twenty-five chapters articulating the doctrine of retribution with increasing severity: God is just, the righteous prosper, the wicked suffer, therefore Job must have sinned. The book takes care to make the reader feel the force of what they say. In the epilogue God rebukes them, not Job.

Elihu
the young interlocutor

Appears for the first time in chapter 32, younger than the friends, with a six-chapter speech that has no parallel in the rest of the book. He waited because of deference; now his anger is kindled against Job for justifying himself rather than God, and against the friends for failing to answer Job. He develops a theology of suffering as instruction. He does not appear in the prologue or the epilogue and is not rebuked. Whether his speeches advance the argument or interrupt it is one of the perennial questions of Job interpretation.

The Satan
ha-satan, the adversary

Ha-satan in the Hebrew, 'the adversary' or 'the accuser,' with the definite article, not yet the proper name of the medieval devil. A member of the divine council who comes before the Lord with the other sons of God. His function is closer to that of a divine prosecutor. He proposes the test — 'Doth Job fear God for nought?' — and exits the narrative after chapter 2. He does not return. The later identification with the figure of evil is not native to this book.

The Voice from the Whirlwind
God answering out of the storm

The figure who speaks in chapters 38 to 41. Comes out of the whirlwind, asks Job seventy questions about the wild creation, and never directly addresses the question Job has been asking for thirty-five chapters. Speaks the longest sustained piece of nature poetry in the Hebrew Bible. Job, when the speech ends, lays his hand upon his mouth and repents in dust and ashes — not of the sins the friends charged him with, but of speaking without understanding the scale of what he was addressing.

Job's Wife
the one line she is given

She has only one line: 'Dost thou still retain thine integrity? Curse God, and die.' The line has been read every way it can be read — as temptation, as mercy, as the rage of a woman who has lost the same children Job has lost and is given less screen time to grieve them. The book does not adjudicate. She is one of the great unspoken figures in the Hebrew Bible, and the line she does speak is one of the lines no pious reading can domesticate.
