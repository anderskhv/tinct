Job 33

Elihu argues that God does speak to humans — through dreams, through suffering, through the preserving angel — and that Job has missed this.

Summary

Elihu invites Job to listen without fear, declaring that he too is formed from clay and that Job need not be afraid of him. He then charges Job with misrepresenting God: Job has said that God treats him as an enemy, that he is in bonds of affliction, that he cries out but receives no answer. But God does speak, Elihu argues. He speaks in dreams, in the night visions, when deep sleep falls on men and they slumber in their beds. In this state God opens men's ears and seals their instruction, turning them from their deeds and covering their pride to keep the soul from the pit. The mechanism of instruction is quiet and interior, not the thunderous courtroom hearing Job has been demanding.

God also speaks through pain. When a man is chastened with pain on his bed so that he cannot eat, an interpreting angel may come — one of a thousand, an intermediary — to declare what is right for the man, to be gracious to him, to say, Deliver him from going down to the pit; I have found a ransom. If this happens, the man's flesh is renewed; he is restored; he comes before God with joy; God has restored to him his righteousness. Elihu is proposing a different theodicy from the friends'. He is not arguing that Job is being punished for specific sins. He is arguing that Job's suffering is communicative — that God is trying to reach him through it — and that Job's error is in treating the silence as absolute when it is not. Whether this advance is sufficient is left for the reader to judge.
