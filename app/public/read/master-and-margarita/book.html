1. Never talk to strangers

“...So who are you, then?” — “I am a part of that force which forever wants evil and forever does good.” Goethe, Faust

At the hour of a hot spring sunset, two citizens appeared at Patriarch’s Ponds. The first of them—about forty, dressed in a grayish summer suit—was short, dark-haired, plump and bald; he carried his respectable pork-pie hat in his hand, and his neatly shaven face was adorned with glasses of supernatural size in black horn-rimmed frames. The second—a broad-shouldered, reddish, tousle-haired young man with a checked cap pushed to the back of his head—wore a cowboy shirt, crumpled white trousers and black canvas shoes.

The first was none other than Mikhail Alexandrovich Berlioz, editor of a thick literary magazine and chairman of the board of one of Moscow’s largest literary associations, known for short as Massolit; his young companion was the poet Ivan Nikolayevich Ponyrev, who wrote under the pen name Bezdomny.

Reaching the shade of the lindens, which were just turning green, the writers first of all made a dash for a gaily painted kiosk with the sign “Beer and Soft Drinks.”

Yes, we must note the first oddity of that terrible May evening. Not only at the kiosk but along the whole avenue running parallel to Malaya Bronnaya Street, there was not a single person. At that hour, when it seemed there was no strength left even to breathe, when the sun, having baked Moscow through, was sinking in a dry haze somewhere beyond the Garden Ring, nobody came under the lindens, nobody sat down on a bench; the avenue was empty.

“Give us some Narzan,” Berlioz asked.

“There isn’t any Narzan,” the woman in the kiosk replied, and for some reason took offense.

“Is there any beer?” Bezdomny inquired in a hoarse voice.

“The beer’s coming this evening,” the woman replied.

“Then what is there?” asked Berlioz.

“Apricot soda, only it’s warm,” said the woman.

“Well, let’s have it, let’s have it, let’s have it!..”

The apricot soda produced a rich yellow foam, and the air began to smell of a barbershop. Having drunk, the writers immediately started hiccuping; they paid and sat down on a bench facing the pond, with their backs to the Bronnaya.

Here a second oddity occurred, concerning Berlioz alone. He suddenly stopped hiccuping, his heart thumped and for an instant dropped away somewhere, then came back, but with a blunt needle lodged in it. What was more, Berlioz was seized by a fear so groundless, yet so strong, that he wanted to run from Patriarch’s that very moment without looking back.

Berlioz looked around in anguish, not understanding what had frightened him. He went pale, wiped his forehead with his handkerchief, and thought: “What’s the matter with me? This has never happened before... my heart’s acting up... I’m overworked... Maybe it’s time to send everything to the devil and go to Kislovodsk...”

And then the sultry air thickened in front of him, and out of this air a transparent citizen of the strangest appearance was woven. On his little head, a jockey’s cap; a skimpy little checked jacket, also made of air... The citizen was seven feet tall but narrow in the shoulders, incredibly thin, and his face, kindly note, was a jeering one.

Berlioz’s life had been arranged in such a way that he was not used to extraordinary phenomena. Turning paler still, he goggled and thought in dismay: “This cannot be!..”

But, alas, it was, and the long citizen, whom one could see right through, swayed before him to the left and to the right without touching the ground.

At this, terror so overpowered Berlioz that he shut his eyes. And when he opened them, he saw that it was all over: the mirage had dissolved, the checked figure had vanished, and with it the blunt needle had popped out of his heart.
