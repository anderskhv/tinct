Crito

399 BCE · by Plato

The book in brief

Crito is a short dialogue in which Socrates, condemned to death by an Athenian jury and held in prison awaiting the return of the sacred ship from Delos, is visited at dawn by his old friend Crito. Crito has come with an escape plan — money has been raised, a place of exile arranged, the prison guard bribed. He begs Socrates to leave. He argues that staying is a betrayal of his sons, of his friends who will be blamed for failing to save him, of his own life. Socrates listens patiently. Then he refuses. The rest of the dialogue is the argument by which he refuses: that the only question is whether escape is just, that he has lived seventy years under the laws of Athens and accepted their benefits, and that to flee now would be to break a contract with the city that has made him who he is.

The dialogue was written by Plato around the 380s BCE, set in the spring of 399 BCE, and runs about 7,500 words — among the shortest of Plato's works. It is the second of the four prison dialogues. Euthyphro stages Socrates on the way to court; Apology is his defense speech; Crito is the night before execution; Phaedo is the execution itself. The four together are the founding texts of Western philosophy as a way of life — and Crito is the moment in that sequence when the choice between living and being just becomes literal.

The argument Socrates gives — that the citizen owes the law a kind of obedience he cannot withdraw without injustice — is the founding statement of social-contract thinking. Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls all eventually have to answer it. The dialogue is also one of the most personally exposed pieces of writing in the canon: a man given the chance to live, choosing not to, and explaining why.

Why it still matters

The Founding Text of Social Contract

Crito is the first sustained argument in Western thought that a citizen has a contract with the city — a contract whose obligations cannot be unilaterally withdrawn. The Laws of Athens, speaking through Socrates, claim to have raised him, educated him, given him every chance to leave if he disagreed with them, and concluded that by staying he tacitly consented to be bound by them. Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls, and every modern theorist of political obligation eventually has to engage with the argument. To read Crito is to read the document that put the question on the table for the next 2,400 years. The radicalism of it is easy to miss because the framework is now so familiar — but in 399 BCE no one had ever made the case quite this way before.

Justice as the Only Question

Crito argues from every other consideration — money, friendship, family, reputation, the practical. Socrates rules them all out. The only question, he insists, is whether escape would be just. Living, dying, what people think, what one's sons need — none of these can override the question of justice. The clarity of that move is part of why Crito remains philosophically provocative. Socrates is not making a sentimental claim. He is arguing that justice is a separate kind of consideration, not commensurable with the others. Modern ethics has been arguing about whether that move is right, and what its costs are, ever since. The dialogue is a short, exact statement of one of the most extreme positions in moral philosophy.

Practicing What He Preached

Throughout the early dialogues Socrates argues that one should never do what is unjust, even to save one's life — that the unjust life is the unlivable one. In Crito, that argument is no longer abstract. He has been condemned. He has the chance to escape. He chooses not to, on the same grounds he has been giving others for forty years. The dialogue is, among other things, the spectacle of a philosopher being asked whether his philosophy actually applies to himself. Socrates says it does. The choice he makes a few hours later, in Phaedo, follows directly from this conversation. Modern readers come away divided about whether the consistency is admirable or pathological — but the argument has not stopped haunting people who claim to have principles.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The argument for staying.

Crito has every practical reason on his side: friends, money, escape, exile, sons left behind. Socrates listens to all of it and refuses. Trace the argument by which he does — and ask whether it would convince you in his place.

The Laws of Athens speak.

Halfway through the dialogue Socrates lets the Laws of Athens speak through him — addressing him directly, asking what he would say to them if he fled. The personification is one of the strangest moves in Plato.

Justice over survival.

Socrates does not say flight is illegal. He says it would be unjust, and that the only thing one should care about is whether an action is just. Test the strength of that claim — is it a useful principle, an extreme one, or simply Socrates being himself?

The most personal philosophy.

This is not abstract ethics. It is a man on the night before his execution, arguing himself into accepting it. Read for the gap between argument and choice — what does it cost Socrates to be right?

Key figures

Socrates
The Condemned

Seventy years old, in prison in Athens, sentenced to drink hemlock by an Athenian jury. He has been waiting for weeks while the sacred ship from Delos sailed and returned — Athenian law forbade execution while the ship was abroad. Crito brings news that the ship will arrive tomorrow and offers him a way out. Socrates refuses. The dialogue is the argument by which he does — calmly, philosophically, on his last night alive.

Crito
The Friend

A wealthy Athenian, contemporary of Socrates, devoted to him for decades. He has bribed the guard, raised money, found a city that will take Socrates in. He has come at dawn because the ship is almost there. He is not a philosopher; his arguments are pragmatic and emotional. He fears for Socrates's sons, for his own reputation, for his friend's life. The dialogue is, in part, the spectacle of his philosophical patience being slowly outmaneuvered.

The Laws of Athens
The Speaking City

Roughly halfway through the dialogue Socrates lets the personified Laws speak in their own voice. They address him directly, asking what he would say to them if he fled. They remind him that they raised him, educated him, married his parents, fed his children. They argue that to escape now would be to destroy the city's claim on its citizens. The speech is one of the strangest rhetorical moves in Plato — and the founding text of the city-as-contract argument.

The Many
The Public

Crito invokes them constantly: what will people say, what will the many think, what will they conclude about Crito and his friends if Socrates dies when he could have escaped. Socrates spends the early part of the dialogue dismantling Crito's appeal to public opinion. The Many are wrong about most things, he argues, and the question is not what they think but what is just. The contrast between the Many and the One Wise Man runs through the whole dialogue.

The Expert
The Standard

The hypothetical figure Socrates introduces to make the case against Crito's appeal to public opinion. In matters of physical training, you listen to the trainer, not the crowd. In matters of medicine, you listen to the doctor. In matters of justice — the most important of all — you listen to the one who actually knows. The figure is mostly absent from the action of the dialogue, but the argument depends on him.

Crito's Sons
The Orphans

Crito's most powerful argument against staying: Socrates's own sons will be left without a father, and Crito's responsibility for them and for his own children weighs on him. They never appear, never speak. They are the human cost of the philosophical position Socrates is taking. Plato lets Crito name them, and lets Socrates not respond directly — the argument moves on, and the sons recede, but the dialogue does not pretend they were not raised.
