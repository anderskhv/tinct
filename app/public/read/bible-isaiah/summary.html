Isaiah a reading guide

8th–5th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Latter Prophets

In the year King Uzziah died, a man saw the Lord seated on a throne, high and lifted up, and the train of his robe filled the temple. The book that begins there spans four centuries, three authors, two catastrophes, and a vision of redemption that has not stopped echoing.

The book in brief

Isaiah is the longest and most theologically influential of the Hebrew prophetic books. Its sixty-six chapters span four centuries of Israelite history and at least three distinct compositional layers: First Isaiah (chapters 1–39), associated with the 8th-century BCE prophet Isaiah ben Amoz, active in Jerusalem under four kings and during the Assyrian crises; Second Isaiah (40–55), composed during the Babylonian exile of the 6th century, opening with comfort, comfort my people; and Third Isaiah (56–66), addressed to the post-exilic community struggling to rebuild. The Suffering Servant, Immanuel, the Holy One of Israel, the new heavens and a new earth — the book's central images have shaped Jewish and Christian imagination for over two thousand years.

The three layers were bound by editors into a single sustained vision in which Israel's judgement, exile, and restoration are stages of one divine purpose. The vision in the temple in chapter 6 — the seraphim crying holy, holy, holy, the coal from the altar, Isaiah's sending — is the theological centre of First Isaiah. Chapter 53, the Suffering Servant, is the most quoted Hebrew Bible text in Christianity and one of the most argued-over passages in biblical scholarship. Chapter 65, behold I create new heavens and a new earth, is the climax of Third Isaiah and the source of Revelation 21:1. Handel built the Messiah mostly out of Isaiah. The KJV version of this book is among the great monuments of English prose.

Isaiah 1 of 66
Isaiah 1

The Great Indictment

Isaiah opens not with a narrative but with a lawsuit. God summons the cosmos itself — heavens and earth — as witnesses against Judah, a rhetorical move rooted in ancient Near Eastern treaty forms. The indictment is precise: Israel has rebelled against the Holy One of Israel, exchanging covenant fidelity for empty ritual. The city of Jerusalem, once faithful, is now a harlot; its rulers are companions of thieves; the widow and orphan go unheard. Yet the passage turns on a single pivot — the famous invitation that has echoed through three thousand years of theological argument: 'Come, let us reason together, says the Lord. Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow.' The God who indicts is also the God who offers, and the conditions of the offer are entirely moral: learn to do good, seek justice, correct oppression. The chapter closes with a vision of Jerusalem restored as Zion — the city that was faithful will be faithful again, the ore will be purified of its dross. The whole arc of the book — judgement, invitation, restoration — is contained in the opening chapter.

Isaiah 2

Swords into Plowshares

Chapter 2 holds two visions in sharp juxtaposition. The first is one of the most celebrated passages in all prophetic literature: in the last days the mountain of the Lord's house will be established as the highest mountain, and all nations will stream to it, learning God's ways and beating their swords into plowshares and their spears into pruning hooks — nation shall not lift sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more. The same passage appears in Micah 4:1-4, which suggests it was a shared tradition, perhaps a liturgical text from the Jerusalem temple. The vision is radically universalist — all nations, not just Israel, come to Zion. It is also radically pacifist — warfare itself is abolished as the nations choose another form of conflict resolution. Then the chapter turns 180 degrees. The same God who is exalted in the nations' worship is about to humble every human pride. The day of the Lord will come against everything lofty: high mountains, tall trees, great ships, proud towers. Every eye that is haughty will be brought low. The idols will be thrown to the moles and bats as people flee into the clefts of the rock. The juxtaposition — universal peace followed by universal judgement — is characteristic of Isaiah's prophetic vision.

Isaiah 3

When Leadership Collapses

Chapter 3 is an oracle of social disintegration. The Lord is about to remove from Jerusalem every prop that holds a society upright: the supply of bread and water, the military officer, the judge, the prophet, the elder, the captain, the counsellor, the expert enchanter. What remains? Children will be their princes, and infants will rule over them — the ultimate image, in Isaiah's ancient world, of social collapse. People will oppress one another, the young will be insolent to the old, the base to the honourable. The specific target of the chapter's longest section is the ruling class of Jerusalem, the elders and princes who have eaten up the vineyard (the people) and ground the faces of the poor. Then the oracle turns to the women of Zion — a sustained satirical catalogue of luxury: anklets, headbands, crescent ornaments, pendants, bracelets, veils, tiaras, perfume boxes, amulets. All of it will be replaced with sackcloth, branding, baldness. The men who wore these ornaments on their behalf will fall by the sword. The chapter is one of Isaiah's most concrete social critiques, rooted in the specific conditions of 8th-century Jerusalem at the height of its material prosperity.

Isaiah 4

Branch and Fire

Chapter 4 is brief — six verses — but pivotal, forming the hinge between the harsh social critique of chapter 3 and the allegory of chapter 5. The opening verse completes the image of chapter 3's catastrophic male mortality: so many men of Jerusalem will die that seven women will seize one man, asking only to be called by his name — the social disgrace of widowhood and childlessness is worse, in their context, than sharing a husband. Then the oracle pivots entirely. 'In that day the Branch of the Lord shall be beautiful and glorious.' The remnant who survive in Zion will be called holy; their names will be written among the living. The Branch — a term that will recur in later messianic contexts — will be the beauty and glory of the survivors. Then the theophanic imagery returns: God will create over all of Zion, over all her assemblies, a cloud by day and smoke and the shining of a flaming fire by night. The echo of the wilderness wandering is deliberate — the pillar of cloud and fire that guided Israel in the desert will return over the restored city. A canopy will cover it for shade from the heat by day and for refuge and shelter from storm and rain. The chapter is structurally a miniature of the whole book: catastrophe, remnant, restoration, divine presence.

Isaiah 5

Song of the Vineyard

Chapter 5 contains one of the most famous literary set-pieces in the Hebrew Bible: the song of the vineyard. The prophet begins in a lyric register, inviting the audience to hear a love song he will sing about his beloved and his vineyard. The vineyard is planted on a fertile hill, dug, cleared of stones, planted with choice vines, furnished with a watchtower and a winepress. But when the vinedresser came for the harvest, the vineyard had produced nothing but wild grapes — bitter, useless fruit. The audience is then invited to judge: what more could the vinedresser have done? The answer: nothing. So the vineyard will be abandoned, its hedge removed, its wall broken, left to be trampled and overgrown, a waste. Then the disclosure: the vineyard is the house of Israel, the pleasant planting is the men of Judah. And the wild grapes have a name — bloodshed where justice was expected, an outcry where righteousness was expected. The chapter continues with a sequence of six woes against specific social sins — accumulating land, drunkenness and revelry that crowds out attention to the Holy One's work, calling evil good, being wise in one's own eyes — climaxing with the threat of the Assyrian invasion: a nation that will come swiftly, roaring like lions, carrying prey away with none to rescue.

Isaiah 6

Holy, Holy, Holy

Chapter 6 is the theological centre of the entire book — the call narrative that is placed not at the beginning but after five chapters of oracles, as if to say: you now know what the prophet was commissioned to do; here is how the commission came. In the year King Uzziah died (about 740 BCE), Isaiah sees the Lord seated on a throne, high and lifted up. The train of his robe fills the temple. Above him stand seraphim — six-winged, each covering face and feet and flying — crying to one another in antiphon: holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory. The doorposts shake at the sound; the house fills with smoke. Isaiah's response is immediate: woe is me, I am ruined. Then one of the seraphim flies to him with a coal from the altar, touches his lips, and declares his guilt removed. Then comes the divine voice: whom shall I send? And Isaiah answers: here am I, send me. The commission is the most paradoxical in the prophetic literature: go, tell the people, but make their ears heavy and their eyes shut, so that they do not understand, lest they repent and be healed. Isaiah asks how long. Until cities are desolate and the people are removed far away. Yet a tenth will remain — and even that tenth will be burned. Only a stump will be left. But the stump: the holy seed is its stump.

Isaiah 7

The Immanuel Sign

Chapter 7 opens in the middle of the Syro-Ephraimite crisis of 735-732 BCE. Rezin of Damascus and Pekah of Israel have allied against Judah; they are marching on Jerusalem to replace Ahaz with a puppet king. The house of David shook, the text says, like trees before the wind. Isaiah is sent to meet Ahaz at the conduit of the upper pool, his son Shear-jashub (a remnant shall return) at his side — the son's name is itself a sign of the double edge of prophetic judgment. The message: do not fear these two smoking firebrands, Rezin and Pekah. Their plan will not stand. The famous double condition follows: if you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all. Ahaz is offered any sign he likes; he refuses, piously citing the commandment not to test God. Isaiah's response is sharp: the refusal is itself a burden on God. So God will give a sign unsolicited: a young woman (Hebrew: almah) is pregnant and will bear a son and call his name Immanuel — God with us. Before the child knows to refuse evil and choose good, the two threatening kings will be gone. But the same time frame brings worse: the Assyrian, whom God will whistle for like a fly from Egypt, will cover the land, strip it bare, and turn vineyards into briars.

Isaiah 8

Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz

Chapter 8 continues the Syro-Ephraimite crisis with a second sign: Isaiah is instructed to write a name on a large tablet — Maher-shalal-hash-baz (the spoil hastens, the prey hurries) — with reliable witnesses present to certify the date. He then becomes the father of a son by 'the prophetess' (his wife), and the son receives this name. Before the child can say 'father' or 'mother,' Damascus and Samaria will be taken by the king of Assyria. The speed of the sign outpaces the Immanuel sign: no time for the discrimination of good and evil, only the infant babble of the child's first words. The chapter then develops the political theology of the crisis more explicitly. Because the people have rejected the waters of Shiloah — the gentle stream, the quiet trust in God — the Lord is bringing the great river: the king of Assyria and all his glory will overflow and sweep through Judah, reaching to the neck. Yet it will not drown Jerusalem: God is with us. The prophet then addresses a warning to all who would plot against Judah: the counsel that will stand is not human strategy but the Lord of hosts. He closes with a personal statement of vocation — he will wait for the Lord, hope in him, bind up the testimony among his disciples — and a rebuke of those who consult the dead rather than the living God: to the Torah and to the testimony; if they do not speak according to this word, there is no dawn for them.

Isaiah 9

A Child Is Born

Chapter 9 is the lyric peak of the first nine chapters and contains one of the most quoted oracles in the entire prophetic literature. The opening movement reverses the darkness imagery that closed chapter 8: the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light; those who lived in the land of deep shadow, on them light has shone. The cause of the joy is multiple — harvest, victory, the breaking of the oppressor's rod — and then resolves into a single focus: for unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given. The titles given to the child have echoed through every Christian celebration of the nativity since Handel: Wonderful Counsellor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. His government will be upon his shoulder; of the increase of his government and peace there will be no end. He will reign on David's throne and over his kingdom, establishing it with justice and righteousness from this time forth and forever. In its 8th-century setting the oracle is probably addressed to the birth or accession of a Davidic king — some scholars suggest Hezekiah. But the titles exceed anything any historical king achieved, which is why the oracle has been consistently read in Jewish and Christian traditions as pointing forward to a figure still to come. The chapter closes with a refrain repeated through the following chapter: God's anger has not turned away, his hand is stretched out still — judgement continues despite the light of promise.

Isaiah 10

Assyria, the Rod of Wrath

Chapter 10 is a sustained reflection on the theological problem of using a pagan empire as an instrument of divine judgment. The opening woe is directed against unjust legislators — those who write oppressive decrees, turn aside the needy from justice, make widows their prey. When the day of visitation comes, to whom will they flee? Then the oracle turns to Assyria itself. Assyria is the rod of God's anger, the staff of his fury — sent against a godless nation, against the people of his wrath, to take spoil and seize plunder. But Assyria does not understand this; Assyria thinks it is the sovereign agent of its own conquests, a purely self-sufficient engine of power: 'By the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom, for I have understanding.' This is the theological problem at the heart of the chapter: if God uses an agent that does not know it is being used, and that agent then claims credit for its own actions, what then? The answer: when God has finished using Assyria for his purposes, he will punish the king of Assyria for the fruit of his arrogance — Assyria will be cut down like a forest by an axe. Then a great reversal: the remnant of Israel will no longer lean on Assyria but on the Holy One of Israel in truth. The chapter closes with a vivid military geography of an invader approaching Jerusalem from the north, city by city, before the Lord himself destroys the invader.

Isaiah 11

The Root of Jesse

Chapter 11 is the lyric summit of the first section of Isaiah and one of the most beautiful texts in the Hebrew Bible. The dynastic image is precisely calibrated: the tree of Jesse (David's father, not David himself) has been felled — the great Davidic monarchy has been cut down — but from its stump a shoot comes up, a branch grows from its roots. The spirit of the Lord rests on him: the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the Lord — three pairs, corresponding to the six activities of the ideal king. He will not judge by what his eyes see or decide by what his ears hear, but with righteousness for the meek and justice for the poor. He will strike the violent with the rod of his mouth and kill the wicked with the breath of his lips. Then the vision of the peaceable kingdom — one of the most iconic passages in all of prophetic literature: the wolf will dwell with the lamb, the leopard will lie down with the kid, the calf and the lion and the fatling together, a little child leading them. The nursing child plays over the hole of the asp; the weaned child puts its hand on the adder's den. None will hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain, for the earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord as the waters cover the sea. The chapter closes with a second exodus: the scattered remnant of Israel will be gathered from all the corners of the earth, the highway lifted up, the nations streaming to Zion's root.

Isaiah 12

Song of Trust

Chapter 12 is the shortest chapter in the first twelve, and functions as the doxological close of the first great movement of the book — the oracles of judgment and promise concerning Judah and Jerusalem. It has the structure of a psalm: in the first verse, the individual voice of Isaiah (or the community) speaks to God: 'I will give thanks to you, O Lord, for though you were angry with me, your anger turned away.' The ground of thanksgiving is the turned anger — the same anger that had been announced so relentlessly in chapters 1-11, with its refrain 'his hand is stretched out still.' Here that hand is withdrawn. The name that will be proclaimed among the nations in verse 4 is the same divine name that gives the chapter its theological weight: the Holy One of Israel is great in your midst — the very title that has run through the whole of First Isaiah as the ground of both judgment and hope. The chapter is brief enough to be read as a single lyric breath, and that brevity is itself the point. After the long, complex, historically dense oracles of chapters 1-11, the book pauses for a song. Draw water from the wells of salvation — the image of Siloam and the gentle waters returns from chapter 8, now as restoration rather than warning. The chapter has been used as a canticle in both Jewish and Christian liturgy.

Isaiah 13

The Burden of Babylon

Chapter 13 opens the great series of oracles against the nations that spans chapters 13-23, and it begins with the largest target: Babylon. The oracle is titled a 'burden' (Hebrew: massa, a technical term for a weighty prophetic oracle, often rendered 'oracle against'). Isaiah summons a horde of warriors — the Lord's consecrated ones — from the ends of the heaven, the weapons of his indignation, to destroy the whole land. The day of the Lord is coming, the chapter declares — the same eschatological phrase that appeared earlier, now applied to the destruction of an empire rather than just of Judah. The stars of heaven and their constellations will not give their light; the sun will be dark at its rising; the moon will not shed its light. The earth will be shaken out of its place; the heavens will tremble. The human suffering is described without softening: the men of Babylon will be as rare as fine gold, the infant will be dashed to pieces, women ravished. Babylon will be like Sodom and Gomorrah when God overthrew them. It will never be inhabited or lived in for all generations; the Arab will not pitch his tent there, nor will shepherds make their flocks lie down there. Ostriches and goats and jackals will dwell in the ruined houses. The oracle is addressed to the 8th-century audience but envisions the destruction of Babylon by the Medes, which took place in 539 BCE.

Isaiah 14

Fallen from Heaven

Chapter 14 opens with the promise that drives the preceding chapter: the Lord will have compassion on Jacob and will again choose Israel; they will be settled in their own land, and foreigners will join them. Then comes one of the most extraordinary literary set-pieces in the prophetic literature: the taunt song (Hebrew: mashal, which can mean both proverb and taunt) over the fallen king of Babylon. The whole earth is at rest and quiet; the trees of Lebanon and the cedars rejoice over the fall of the feller. Sheol, the realm of the dead, stirs to meet the fallen king; the shades of all the world's dead kings rise to greet him: 'You too have become as weak as we! You have become like us!' And then the fall is described in cosmic terms that have entered the Western imagination: 'How you are fallen from heaven, O Day Star, son of Dawn! How you are cut down to the ground, you who laid the nations low! You said in your heart, I will ascend to heaven; I will raise my throne above the stars of God; I will sit on the mount of assembly in the far reaches of the north; I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will make myself like the Most High.' But instead: brought down to Sheol, to the far reaches of the pit. The image of Lucifer (Latin for Day Star, the basis of the name in Christian tradition) derives from this passage.

Isaiah 15

Moab Weeps in the Night

Chapter 15 is the first of a two-part oracle against Moab (chapters 15-16), Israel's neighbour to the east, across the Dead Sea. The oracle opens with extraordinary speed: in a single night, Ar of Moab is laid waste, Kir of Moab is laid waste. The destruction is not narrated but asserted — the night has already happened. The reaction is described in the characteristic language of mourning in the ancient Near East: heads shaved bald, beards cut off, sackcloth on their backs, weeping in streets and on housetops. The major towns of Moab are listed one by one — Heshbon, Elealeh, Jahaz, Zoar, Eglath-shelishiyah, Luhith — and each is associated with specific mourning or flight. The waters of Dimon will be full of blood. The list reads like a tour of Moabite geography in the register of lamentation. What is unusual about the oracle is the prophet's own emotional register: he is not triumphant but grief-stricken. 'My heart cries out for Moab; her fugitives flee to Zoar.' The prophetic voice that has pronounced judgment on Judah and now on the nations is not immune to the suffering it announces. Isaiah here foreshadows the later tradition of lament over fallen enemies that will reach its fullest expression in the book of Lamentations.

Isaiah 16

Send Lambs to the Lord

Chapter 16 continues the Moab oracle and develops it in a direction that is unusual in the international oracles: an invitation. The refugee Moabites are urged to send tribute to Jerusalem, to seek shelter under the shadow of the throne of Zion. Then an extraordinary conditional vision: if a king reigns in steadfast love, in faithfulness, and in justice, his throne will be established — a Davidic ideal that is offered as the ground on which Moab's refugees might trust. Then the oracle pivots to grief. The prophet mourns the destroyed vineyards of Sibmah — the vines whose branches once spread as far as Jazer, that reached to the sea, that extended to the desert. The vintage song will cease; no treader treads out wine in the presses. Isaiah weeps for these things as he would weep for Jazer. The emotional register of chapter 15 continues: this is a prophet who does not separate the announcement of judgment from the experience of what the judgment means for real vineyards and real people. Then the oracle closes with a dateline: within three years, as the years of a hired worker, the glory of Moab will be brought into contempt, the remnant very small and feeble. The specificity of the dateline — three years — places the oracle within the prophet's own credibility.

Isaiah 17

The Burden of Damascus

Chapter 17 opens with the oracle against Damascus (Syria) that has been impending since the Syro-Ephraimite crisis of chapters 7-8. The announcement is stark: Damascus will cease to be a city and will become a heap of ruins — a prophecy fulfilled when Tiglath-pileser III destroyed Damascus in 732 BCE. The oracle then shifts to Ephraim (the northern kingdom of Israel), which was allied with Damascus in the crisis: Ephraim's fortresses will fail, the kingdom of Damascus will fall, the remnant of Syria will be like the glory of Israel — reduced. The oracle of verses 4-6 is one of the finest examples of the harvest gleaning image in the prophetic literature. The glory of Jacob will be made thin, like the leanness of a man ill; like the harvest of grain in the Valley of Rephaim, only gleanings will remain; like the gleaning of olives when the harvest is done — two or three berries in the top of the uppermost bough, four or five on the branches of the fruit tree. The specificity is agricultural and precise. The oracle then pivots to restoration: in that day, humanity will look to its Maker and trust in the Holy One of Israel. The chapter closes with an oracle against the nations who attack — the roaring of many peoples, the rushing of mighty waters — and then God rebukes them, and they flee like chaff before the wind.

Isaiah 18

Land of Whirring Wings

Chapter 18 is one of the shorter and more cryptic of the national oracles, directed against 'the land of whirring wings' beyond the rivers of Cush — commonly identified as Ethiopia or the Sudanese kingdom of Cush, which in the late 8th century BCE was the ruling dynasty of Egypt (the 25th dynasty, known as the Cushite or Ethiopian pharaohs). Cushite envoys had apparently been visiting Jerusalem seeking an anti-Assyrian alliance — they are described arriving by the sea on vessels of papyrus, swift messengers. The oracle is addressed to the messengers themselves: go back to your people, the tall and smooth-skinned nation feared near and far, whose land the rivers divide. The instruction to them is to wait: the Lord will be quiet, looking from his dwelling like clear heat in sunshine, like a cloud of dew in the heat of harvest. He will wait until just before the harvest, then cut off the shoots, lop the spreading branches. The birds of prey of the mountain and the beasts of the earth will feed on the Assyrian dead. And then: at that time, gifts will be brought from that tall and smooth-skinned people to the Lord of hosts — to Mount Zion. The oracle is unusual in that it ends with the submission of the distant nation to Zion rather than its destruction.

Isaiah 19

Egypt Will Know the Lord

Chapter 19 is the richest and most theologically unexpected of the national oracles. It opens with judgment on Egypt — the classic enemy of Israel's whole history. The Lord rides on a swift cloud to Egypt; the idols tremble at his presence; the hearts of the Egyptians melt. He stirs up Egyptians against Egyptians — civil war. He hands Egypt over to a hard master (probably an Assyrian ruler). The Nile dries up; the fishermen mourn; the workers of flax and the weavers are confounded; the wise counsellors of Pharaoh give foolish advice. Egypt is brought low. Then — after the extended judgment — the oracle pivots in a direction that is almost without precedent in the prophetic literature. In that day there will be five cities in Egypt that speak the language of Canaan and swear allegiance to the Lord. In that day there will be an altar to the Lord in the midst of Egypt and a pillar to the Lord at its border. Egypt will cry out to the Lord because of oppressors, and God will send them a saviour. God will make himself known to Egypt, and the Egyptians will know the Lord. Then the climax: blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance. The three great empires of the biblical world — Egypt, Assyria, Israel — are gathered into a single blessing, a threefold covenant formula applied universally.

Isaiah 20

The Naked Prophet

Chapter 20 is one of the most unusual in the prophetic literature: a sign-act, a piece of embodied prophecy in which the prophet himself becomes the message. In the year Sargon II of Assyria sent his commander against Ashdod (711 BCE), the Lord commanded Isaiah to remove his sackcloth and his sandals. Isaiah obeyed, walking naked and barefoot — three years. The statement is simple and startling: the prophet of the most theologically sophisticated oracle collection in the Hebrew Bible walked through the streets of Jerusalem unclothed for three years as an enacted parable. The explanation is given explicitly: the prophet is a sign and a portent for Egypt and Cush. As he has walked naked and barefoot, so will the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptian and Cushite captives — young and old, naked and barefoot, with buttocks uncovered, to the shame of Egypt. And then the political application: those who trusted in Cush and boasted in Egypt will be dismayed. The alliance that was supposed to save Judah from Assyria will itself be defeated by Assyria. The question 'how will we escape?' is the question Isaiah has been preparing his audience to ask — and the answer is the one the political theology of chapters 1-19 has been developing: only by trust in the Holy One of Israel, not by military alliance.

Isaiah 21

Fallen, Fallen Is Babylon

Chapter 21 contains three oracles, the first and most famous of which is the 'burden of the wilderness of the sea' — a title that commentators have associated with Babylon's swampy Chaldean environment. The vision comes as whirlwinds sweeping from the Negeb desert — a terrifying atmospheric opening. The prophet is overwhelmed: 'My loins are filled with anguish; pangs have seized me, like the pangs of a woman in labour.' He is so distressed by what he sees that he cannot straighten; he is dismayed by what he hears. Then the oracle is given to him directly: 'Go, set a watchman; let him announce what he sees.' The watchman, in his tower, sees riders coming — horsemen, riders on donkeys, riders on camels — and then cries: 'Fallen, fallen is Babylon! And all the carved images of her gods he has shattered to the ground.' This verse (21:9) is the source of the repeated Revelation refrain 'Babylon is fallen, is fallen' (Revelation 14:8, 18:2). Then a brief oracle against Edom (Dumah, a wordplay on the Edomite name) and an oracle against Arabia — the Dedanites camping in the forest, the Kedarites bringing water to the thirsty. Within a year (as the years of a hired worker) the glory of Kedar will be gone.

Isaiah 22

Valley of Vision

Chapter 22 turns from foreign nations to Jerusalem itself — the 'valley of vision,' a name that evokes the city as the place where prophetic vision is received. The oracle opens with a puzzling celebration: what has happened that Jerusalem's people are all on the housetops, full of tumult and riot? The answer the prophet gives is not triumph but shame: the defenders of the city fled without fighting; they were captured without a bow being drawn. This appears to be the crisis of 701 BCE, the Assyrian siege of Jerusalem under Sennacherib, after which the Assyrian army withdrew — but the prophet sees the celebration as premature and misplaced. The Lord called for weeping and mourning; instead there was feasting and drinking, 'for tomorrow we die.' This is Isaiah's sharpest condemnation of Jerusalem's spiritual blindness: to celebrate survival without acknowledging the deliverance as God's gift, or to face the remaining threat with feasting rather than repentance. Then comes a personal oracle against Shebna, the steward of the palace — a named individual attacked for carving himself a rock-cut tomb above his station. He will be cast away like a ball into a wide land. The oracle against Shebna introduces Eliakim, the future steward, on whom will be hung the key of David — and all the weight of his father's house.

Isaiah 23

Oracle against Tyre the sea-merchant

Chapter 23 is the last of the oracles against foreign nations that fill chapters 13–23. Tyre, the great Phoenician port city on the Lebanese coast, was the commercial hub of the ancient Near East — its merchants were princes (verse 8) and its trade-ships reached the ends of the known world, from Tarshish (probably in Spain) to Egypt and Mesopotamia. The oracle announces that Tyre will be laid waste for seventy years (verse 15), after which she will take up her trade again — the prophet compares the restored city to a forgotten prostitute returning to her business. The oracles against the nations in chapters 13–23 address Babylon (13–14), Moab (15–16), Damascus (17), Egypt and Cush (18–20), Babylon again (21), Edom and Arabia (21), Jerusalem's valley of vision (22), and finally Tyre. Together they form a sustained declaration that the Holy One of Israel is Lord not only of Judah but of every nation, and that the same God who judges Jerusalem judges the empires. Tyre's particular indictment is its pride of wealth and its trust in commerce rather than in God. The seventy years of its desolation will parallel the seventy years of Judah's exile — a point later readers noted.

Isaiah 24

The earth laid waste — Isaiah's Apocalypse opens

Chapters 24–27 — sometimes called the Isaiah Apocalypse — represent a major shift in the book. The oracles against individual nations that filled chapters 13–23 give way to a cosmic vision of universal judgement. Chapter 24 opens with God laying waste the earth, turning it upside down, and scattering its inhabitants. The language is eschatological in a way that exceeds the historical oracles: the earth mourns and withers, the world languishes, the heights of heaven and the kings of the earth are punished together. The covenant has been broken — the same covenant that undergirds all creation, not merely the Sinai covenant with Israel — and the earth staggers like a drunkard and is moved like a hut. Only a remnant will remain, as the gleaning of an olive harvest, crying glory to the Righteous One from the west and from the islands of the sea. The chapter ends with voices of praise from the ends of the earth, but the prophet answers: I pine away, I pine away, for the treacherous deal treacherously. The mood is awe-laden and uncertain — the final judgement and the final redemption are both in view, but neither has yet arrived.

Isaiah 25

The mountain feast — death swallowed up for ever

Chapter 25 is one of the lyric peaks of the Isaiah Apocalypse. After the devastation of chapter 24 the prophet breaks into praise: O Lord, you are my God, I will exalt you, I will praise your name. He has made the fortified city a ruin, the palace of foreigners a city no more. Then comes the vision of the great banquet: on this mountain the Lord of hosts will make for all peoples a feast of rich food, a feast of aged wine — fat things full of marrow, aged wine well refined. On this mountain God will destroy the shroud that is spread over all peoples, the covering that is cast over all nations, and he will swallow up death for ever. The Lord God will wipe away tears from all faces. The reproach of his people he will take away from all the earth. This is extraordinary language for any ancient text. The swallowing up of death — the annihilation not just of suffering but of mortality itself — goes beyond the restoration of a city or even a people. The chapter ends with a song of contempt for Moab and trust in the God who has overthrown it. But the centre is the feast and the destruction of death — passages that will echo in Revelation 7 and 21, in Paul's First Corinthians 15 (death is swallowed up in victory), and in the Christian doctrine of resurrection.

Isaiah 26

The city of God and the whisper of resurrection

Chapter 26 is a sustained hymn of trust placed in the mouth of the redeemed community — a song to be sung in the land of Judah on the day of final vindication. The strong city has salvation for its walls and its ramparts; the nation that keeps faith and whose mind stays on God, God keeps in perfect peace. The chapter's theology of trust is precise: you will keep in perfect peace the mind that is stayed on you, because it trusts in you — a verse that has become one of the most memorised in the book. The chapter also contains the most explicit hint of resurrection in the pre-exilic prophetic material: your dead shall live, O Lord; their bodies shall rise. You who dwell in the dust, awake and sing for joy. The verse is debated — some interpreters read it as metaphorical, referring to the national restoration of Israel rather than the literal resurrection of individuals — but it has been foundational for the Jewish doctrine of bodily resurrection (tekhiyat ha-metim) and for Christian belief. The chapter ends with the call to enter one's chambers and wait a little while, until the wrath has passed, and the assurance that the Lord comes out from his place to punish the inhabitants of the earth for their iniquity.

Isaiah 27

Leviathan slain — Israel gathered from river to river

The final chapter of the Isaiah Apocalypse opens with a mythological image: on that day the Lord will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent, and he will kill the dragon that is in the sea. Leviathan is the chaos-monster of ancient Near Eastern mythology, the symbol of the powers hostile to creation and to God. The image declares that the ultimate threat — not merely Assyria or Babylon but the deep disorder underlying all empires — will be destroyed by divine fiat on the last day. Then comes the parable of the pleasant vineyard: I, the Lord, am its keeper; every moment I water it; I guard it night and day so that no one harms it. The vineyard that burned in the vineyard song of chapter 5 is now tended and protected. When the fortified city becomes a forsaken pasture, the people come there to graze their flocks and break off its branches; when its boughs dry up, women come and make a fire of them. There is no understanding about this people; therefore he who made them will not have compassion on them. Then the turn: in that day, from the Euphrates to the Brook of Egypt, Israel will be gathered one by one. The great trumpet sounds, and those lost in Assyria and driven out to Egypt will come and worship the Lord on the holy mountain at Jerusalem.

Isaiah 28

Woe to Ephraim's drunkards — the cornerstone in Zion

Chapter 28 opens the sequence of six woe-oracles (chapters 28–33) addressed to the political and religious leadership of Judah and to the northern kingdom. The first woe targets Ephraim — the northern kingdom, about to fall to Assyria in 722 — whose proud crown of drunkards will be trampled underfoot like a first-ripe fig. Then the oracle turns to Jerusalem: its priests and prophets reel with wine, they stagger with strong drink, they err in vision, they stumble in judgement. God's people will hear only precept upon precept, line upon line, here a little, there a little — and this mechanical, uncomprehending instruction will become the means of their stumbling. Against the false security of Jerusalem's rulers, who say we have made a covenant with death and with Sheol a mutual agreement, Isaiah sets the promise of God: Behold, I am the one who has laid in Zion a stone, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, a sure foundation. The one who believes will not be in haste. This cornerstone verse (28:16) is one of the most heavily interpreted in the Hebrew Bible — it is quoted in Romans 9:33, 10:11, and 1 Peter 2:6 in connection with Jesus Christ, and it has shaped the architectonic imagery of both Jewish and Christian theology. The covenant with death will be annulled; the overflowing scourge will sweep through.

Isaiah 29

Woe to Ariel — the city that cannot hear its own scroll

The name Ariel — meaning hearth of God, and used here for Jerusalem — gives the oracle its uncanny mood. The city will be besieged; she will moan like a ghost out of the dust, and her speech will whisper out of the dust. Yet God himself will suddenly turn and fight for her: in an instant, as in a dream, the multitude of all the nations that fight against Ariel will vanish. Then the prophet's deepest critique emerges. The Lord says: because this people draws near with their mouth and honours me with their lips, while their hearts are far from me — and their fear of me is a commandment taught by men — I will again do amazing things with this people, wonderful and marvellous, and the wisdom of their wise men will perish and the understanding of their discerning men will be hidden. The critique of formal religion without inward reality, of lips that move while hearts remain elsewhere, is one of Isaiah's most powerful. It is quoted by Jesus in the Gospels (Matthew 15:8–9, Mark 7:6–7) in the controversy over the traditions of the elders. The chapter closes with a reversal: the deaf will hear the words of the scroll, the eyes of the blind will see. The poor will rejoice in the Holy One of Israel.

Isaiah 30

Woe to the Egypt-alliance — in quietness is your strength

The dominant political crisis behind Isaiah 30–31 is the anti-Assyrian coalition that Judah considered joining in the late 8th century, one that sought Egyptian military support against Sennacherib. Isaiah's opposition to this alliance is total. Those who go down to Egypt for help, who rely on horses and trust in chariots because they are many and in horsemen because they are very strong, but do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the Lord — they will be shamed. Egypt's help is worthless and empty; Isaiah coins a name for Pharaoh: Rahab who sits still — the chaos-monster that does nothing. The oracle continues with an instruction to write the word in a book, because this is a rebellious people, children who will not hear the instruction of the Lord. Yet after the indictment comes the most beautiful passage in First Isaiah's woe-cycle: in returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and trust shall be your strength. But you were unwilling. The people refuse and say they will flee on horses — and so their enemies will pursue. But God waits to be gracious; he will arise to show mercy, for the Lord is a God of justice. Isaiah 31

Woe to horse-trusters — the Lion of Judah will shield Jerusalem

Chapter 31 is the companion piece to chapter 30, completing the woe against the Egypt-alliance. The oracle opens with precision: Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses, who trust in chariots because they are many and in horsemen because they are very strong, but do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the Lord. The argument then takes a theological turn: the Egyptians are human and not God; their horses are flesh and not spirit. When the Lord stretches out his hand, the helper will stumble and the one helped will fall, and they will all perish together. Then two contrasting images of God defending Jerusalem. First: as a lion or a young lion growls over its prey and is not frightened by a multitude of shepherds — so the Lord of hosts will come down to fight on Mount Zion. Second: like birds hovering, so the Lord of hosts will protect Jerusalem; he will protect and deliver it, he will spare and rescue it. The images are elemental and non-architectural — not a fortress or a shield, but a predator and a hovering parent-bird. The chapter ends with a call to return: return to him from whom you have deeply revolted. Isaiah 32

The righteous king and the outpouring of spirit

Chapter 32 offers a brief vision of what governance looks like when it works. A king will reign in righteousness and rulers will rule in justice; each will be like a hiding place from the wind, a shelter from the storm, like streams of water in a dry place, like the shadow of a great rock in a weary land. The king as shelter, as water, as shade — these images of what just rule provides to the vulnerable are among the most concrete in the book. Then follows the longest sustained address to women in First Isaiah: the complacent daughters of Jerusalem who feel secure are warned. In little more than a year you will shudder, for the vintage will fail. Beat your breasts for the pleasant fields, for the fruitful vine. The land will be overgrown with thorns and briers. Then the reversal: the spirit will be poured out on us from on high, and the wilderness will become a fruitful field. Justice will dwell in the wilderness, and righteousness abide in the fruitful field. The effect of righteousness will be peace; the result of righteousness, quietness and trust for ever. The chapter holds the social vision — just king, protected poor, the land producing — and the pneumatological vision — the outpouring of spirit — together.

Isaiah 33

Woe to the destroyer — the king in his beauty

The sixth and final woe in the cycle (chapters 28–33) is addressed to a generic destroyer and treacherous dealer who has not yet been destroyed or betrayed himself — a likely reference to the Assyrian threat rather than a specifically named enemy. The oracle moves through prayer, vision, and promise. The prophet prays: O Lord, be gracious to us; we wait for you. Be our arm every morning, our salvation in the time of trouble. Then the vision: God has arisen and the nations scattered; the spoil of the nations has been gathered like the gathering of caterpillars. Zion is called God's dwelling; there the Lord in majesty will be with them, a place of broad rivers and streams where no war-galley can sail. The key closing image: your eyes will see the king in his beauty; they will behold a land that stretches afar. The king in his beauty — an image of the Messianic king, or more immediately of Hezekiah or God himself — seen from a land whose borders have been expanded rather than contracted. The chapter closes with the Lord as judge, lawgiver, and king who will save: he will divide spoil, and even the lame will carry off plunder. The resident of Zion will say they are not sick; the people who dwell there will be forgiven their iniquity.

Isaiah 34

The sword of the Lord upon all nations — Edom become wilderness

Chapter 34 is among the most violent in the prophetic canon — a sustained vision of divine warfare against all nations, focusing on Edom as the representative enemy. The Lord is enraged against all the nations and has doomed them to destruction; their slain will be cast out, and the mountains will flow with their blood. The stars of heaven will dissolve, the sky will roll up like a scroll, and the starry host will fall like leaves from a vine. Then the focus narrows to Edom: the Lord has a sword that is sated with blood; it has descended on Edom for judgment. What follows is a haunting catalogue of desolation. Edom's streams will be turned to pitch, its dust to brimstone; no one shall pass through it for ever and ever. The creatures that will inhabit it are the animals and birds associated with desolation in the ancient Near East: the hawk, the owl, the raven, the wild ox, the wild goat, the jackals, the ostriches, the hyena, the lilith (the night monster of ancient demonology). Thorns will grow up over its palaces; the haunt of jackals will become a habitation for ostriches. No nobles will be there to call it a kingdom. The book of the Lord declares it; none of these things will fail. Read alongside chapter 35, which immediately follows, the desolation of Edom becomes the dark foil for the blossoming of the wilderness of God's people.

Isaiah 35

The wilderness blossoms — the highway of holiness home

Chapter 35 is one of the most lyrical chapters in the entire prophetic corpus and the most concentrated expression of restoration hope in First Isaiah. After the desolation of Edom in chapter 34, the contrast is total. The wilderness and the dry land shall be glad; the desert shall rejoice and blossom like the crocus; it shall blossom abundantly and rejoice with joy and singing. The glory of Lebanon, the majesty of Carmel and Sharon — the most fertile landscapes of the ancient land — will be given to it. Isaiah 36

Sennacherib at the walls — the Rabshakeh speaks

Chapters 36–39 are the historical narrative heart of First Isaiah, and chapter 36 is among the most dramatic single chapters in the prophetic books. In the fourteenth year of Hezekiah, Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the fortified cities of Judah and took them — and then sent his field commander, the Rabshakeh, to Jerusalem with a great army. The Rabshakeh stands at the conduit of the upper pool on the highway to the Fuller's Field — the same location where Isaiah had met Ahaz during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis thirty years earlier (7:3) — and demands to speak with Hezekiah. Three of the king's officials come out to meet him. The Rabshakeh's speech is remarkable: he systematically dismantles every possible source of Judah's confidence. Don't trust Egypt — it's a broken reed. Don't trust the Lord — Hezekiah removed the Lord's high places and said you must worship only at this altar in Jerusalem. If you're trusting military force, make a bet: I'll give you two thousand horses if you can find riders for them. He even claims a divine commission: Did I come up without the Lord? The Lord said to me, go up against this land. Then, when the officials ask him to speak in Aramaic (not Hebrew, so the people on the wall cannot understand), he refuses and addresses the people directly, offering them surrender terms — everyone will eat from their own vine and fig tree until he comes and takes them to a land of grain and wine. Hezekiah's officials come to him with their clothes torn.

Isaiah 37

Hezekiah's prayer — the angel in the night

Chapter 37 is the resolution of the Sennacherib crisis and one of the most theologically compressed chapters in the Hebrew Bible. When the Rabshakeh's speech is reported to Hezekiah, he tears his clothes, covers himself in sackcloth, and goes to the temple. He sends officials to Isaiah. Isaiah's first response is calm: do not be afraid because of the words you have heard; I will send a spirit upon Sennacherib and he will hear a rumour and return to his own land and fall by the sword there. But Sennacherib, withdrawing from Jerusalem for a time, sends a letter: Do not let your God deceive you. Have the gods of the nations delivered their lands? Have the gods of Hamath, Arpad, Sepharvaim? No. Hezekiah takes the letter, goes to the temple, and spreads it before the Lord. Then the prayer — perhaps the most concentrated royal prayer in the Hebrew Bible: O Lord of hosts, God of Israel, who is enthroned above the cherubim, you are the God, you alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth; you have made heaven and earth. It is true that the Assyrian kings have laid waste all the nations. But they were only wood and stone — not gods. Now, O Lord our God, save us, that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you alone are Lord. The prayer is answered: Isaiah delivers a long oracle against Sennacherib. Then the narrative: that night the angel of the Lord went out and struck down a hundred and eighty-five thousand in the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib returned to Nineveh and was killed by his own sons while worshipping Nisroch his god.

Isaiah 38

Hezekiah's illness — the shadow on the sundial

Chapter 38 relates an episode that, in the chronology of 2 Kings 20, precedes the Sennacherib crisis but is placed here as a narrative coda. Hezekiah is told by Isaiah: Set your house in order, for you will die and not recover. The king turns his face to the wall and weeps bitterly, praying that the Lord would remember his faithfulness. Isaiah has not yet left the middle court when the Lord's word comes to him: go back and tell Hezekiah that he will live fifteen more years, the city will be delivered from Assyria, and a sign will confirm it — the shadow on the sundial of Ahaz will go back ten steps. The sign is extraordinary: a reversal in the movement of a shadow, meaning in effect a reversal of time, a guarantee encoded in the physical world. Hezekiah's writing — a psalm of convalescence embedded in the narrative — is one of the few royal psalms outside the Psalter. It records the experience of coming near to death: I said in the middle of my days I must depart; I go to the gates of Sheol; I shall look on man no more among the inhabitants of the world. The living, the living — they give thanks to you, as I do today. The sharp line between life (praise) and death (silence before God) that runs through Psalms 88 and 115 is here given a biographical context: the king who nearly went silent learns again to praise.

Isaiah 39

The Babylonian envoys — everything will go to Babylon

Chapter 39 is the hinge of the entire book — the narrative bridge between First Isaiah (chapters 1–39) and Second Isaiah (chapters 40–55). The envoys of Merodach-baladan king of Babylon have come to congratulate Hezekiah on his recovery; Hezekiah, flattered, shows them everything in his house — all the silver and gold, spices, precious oil, armour, everything in his storehouses. Nothing was left that Hezekiah did not show them. Isaiah asks: where have these men come from? From Babylon. What have they seen? Everything. Isaiah's oracle is therefore simple and devastating: Behold, the days are coming when all that is in your house and that which your fathers have stored up until this day shall be carried to Babylon. Nothing shall be left. And of your sons who will come from you, whom you will father, some will be taken away, and they will become eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon. Hezekiah's response has puzzled readers: Good is the word of the Lord that you have spoken. For there will be peace and security in my days. This reads as either political realism (the disaster is distant, not immediate), or a failure of imagination, or a kind of complacency that the narrative presents without comment. The oracle sets the table for Second Isaiah: what comes next — comfort, comfort my people — is addressed to the people who went to Babylon.

Isaiah 40

Comfort, comfort — Second Isaiah opens

Chapter 40 is the opening of Second Isaiah and one of the most celebrated chapters in all of scripture. The setting has shifted by a century and a half from the Hezekiah narrative: the exile to Babylon has happened, the temple has been destroyed, Jerusalem is in ruins. Into that world the prophet speaks: Comfort, comfort my people, says your God. Speak tenderly to Jerusalem, and cry to her that her warfare is ended, that her iniquity is pardoned, that she has received from the Lord's hand double for all her sins. Then a voice cries: In the wilderness prepare the way of the Lord; make straight in the desert a highway for our God. Every valley shall be lifted up; every mountain and hill be made low; the uneven ground shall become level; the rough places a plain. The glory of the Lord shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together. The voice of the Lord says: cry. What shall I cry? All flesh is grass, and all its glory is like the flower of the field. The grass withers, the flower fades — but the word of our God will stand for ever. The second half of the chapter rises to a sustained celebration of God's incomparable power: who has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand and marked off the heavens with a span? He sits above the circle of the earth, its inhabitants are like grasshoppers. He brings princes to nothing; the rulers of the earth become emptiness. They who wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run and not be weary; they shall walk and not faint.

Isaiah 41

Be not afraid — God's servant Israel held by the right hand

Chapter 41 opens with a dramatic courtroom scene: the nations are summoned to present their case and renew their strength. Who has stirred up one from the east whom victory meets at every step, who gives up nations before him and makes him trample kings underfoot? Isaiah 42

The First Servant Song — a bruised reed he will not break

Chapter 42 opens with the First Servant Song (verses 1–9), one of the most influential passages in the prophetic books. Behold my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my Spirit upon him; he will bring forth justice to the nations. He will not cry aloud or lift up his voice, or make it heard in the street; a bruised reed he will not break, and a faintly burning wick he will not quench; he will faithfully bring forth justice. He will not grow faint or be discouraged till he has established justice in the earth; and the coastlands wait for his law. The passage presents a mode of power that is entirely unlike any political or military power in the book. The servant does not shout, does not raise his voice in the street, does not break the already broken or snuff out the already nearly extinguished. The contrast with every empire in the preceding chapters — Assyria, Babylon, the nations who trample kings — is total. God then speaks directly: I am the Lord; I have called you in righteousness; I will give you as a covenant for the people, a light for the nations, to open the eyes that are blind, to bring out the prisoners from the dungeon. The chapter continues with a new song of praise to the Lord from the ends of the earth — sea creatures, coastlands, the desert and its cities, the inhabitants of Sela. Then a declaration that God has been silent for a long time but now cries out, breathes hard, gasps, and acts.

Isaiah 43

When you pass through the waters — you are precious in my sight

Isaiah 44

Bel bows, Nebo stoops — idols are half firewood

Chapter 44 develops two contrasting arguments that run through Second Isaiah: the power of the God who gives his spirit freely to his people, and the comprehensive absurdity of idolatry. The spirit-gift opens the chapter: I will pour water on the thirsty land and streams on the dry ground; I will pour my Spirit upon your offspring and my blessing on your descendants. Then the mock trial of idols — one of the most sustained pieces of theological satire in the Hebrew Bible. The idol-maker plants a cedar and an oak and a cypress; he cuts the wood; half of it he burns in the fire to warm himself and bake his bread and cook his meat. Over the other half he makes a god, fashions an idol, bows before it, worships it, prays to it: deliver me, for you are my god. He feeds on ashes; a deluded heart has led him astray; he cannot deliver himself or say, is there not a lie in my right hand? The satire is extended and deliberate — the prophet wants the listener to see and feel the complete absurdity of worshipping something one has just manufactured from the same tree one cooked dinner with. The chapter then moves to the declaration of Cyrus (verse 28): who says of Cyrus, he is my shepherd, and he shall fulfil all my purpose, saying of Jerusalem, she shall be built, and of the temple, your foundation shall be laid. The naming of Cyrus — a Persian king who had not yet risen to power when these words were first heard — is one of the most contested passages in the debate about prophetic prediction.

Isaiah 45

Cyrus, the anointed of God

Chapter 45 is the theological summit of Second Isaiah. God addresses Cyrus directly in the second person — I have called you by your name, I have surnamed you, though you did not know me. The title given to Cyrus (mashiach, anointed) is the same used of Israelite kings. The prophet anticipates the obvious objection — why should God use a pagan conqueror? — and preempts it with the most concentrated statement of divine sovereignty in the book: I form light and create darkness, I make weal and create woe; I the Lord do all these things. The argument is not theodicy but doxology: God's freedom to act outside the expected channels is the very ground of the promise. The chapter closes with the vision of all nations streaming to the one God, acknowledging that there is no other. Readers should notice how the address to Cyrus frames the address to Israel: the same God who calls Cyrus is the one Israel can trust.

Isaiah 46

Gods carried vs. God who carries

Chapter 46 opens with a vivid satirical image: Bel and Nebo, the chief gods of Babylon, are loaded onto beasts for transport as Cyrus's armies approach. They stoop, they bow, they cannot save the burden. The contrast the prophet draws is precise and cutting. Babylon's gods must be carried; Israel's God carries. He has borne Israel from the womb, borne it since birth; even to old age and grey hairs he will carry it. The chapter then pivots to theodicy: from the beginning I declared the end, and from ancient times things not yet done. The argument — that God's prediction of Cyrus fulfils the ancient promises — is the prophet's ground for trust. Remember this and be assured, hold it firm in your memory. The chapter is short but dense; readers should linger on the central contrast between borne and bearing as the key to Second Isaiah's theology of divine faithfulness.

Isaiah 47

The fall of Babylon foretold

Chapter 47 is a taunt-song against Babylon, one of the most sustained pieces of prophetic satire in Second Isaiah. The city is personified as a queen — virgin daughter of Babylon, sit in the dust — and addressed directly: get down, sit in the darkness. Her confidence in her sorceries and her enchantments, her claim that I am, and there is no one besides me, mirrors the divine self-declaration of chapter 45 and is here exposed as false pride. She trusted in her wickedness and said no one sees me. Her wisdom and knowledge led her astray. The prophets who once told her the future cannot now save her. Evil will come upon her; she will not know how to charm it away. Disaster will fall upon her; she will not be able to put it off. Ruin will come suddenly, when she does not know it. The chapter is a sustained reversal: the queen becomes the slave, the mistress becomes the servant, the city that boasted of her permanence faces sudden collapse.

Isaiah 48

Israel: refined but not consumed

Chapter 48 closes the first major sub-section of Second Isaiah with a complex argument about Israel's history and the grounds of the coming redemption. The tone is sharper than the preceding comfort chapters: God accuses Israel of stubbornness, of swearing by God's name but not in truth, of being an obstinate people — the neck is an iron sinew, the brow is bronze. The reason God announced events beforehand, the chapter explains, was precisely because he knew Israel would attribute them to their idols rather than to him. The former things I declared; then suddenly I acted. Yet despite this persistent failure, the promise stands — not for Israel's merit but for God's reputation: for my own sake, for my own sake, I do it. The chapter closes with the great line that becomes a hallmark of the exilic promise: there is no peace, says the Lord, for the wicked. Readers should notice how the chapter holds together radical realism about Israel's failures with equally radical divine commitment.

Isaiah 49

The Servant's mission to the nations

Chapter 49 opens the second great Servant Song. The Servant speaks in his own voice: the Lord called me from the womb, from the body of my mother he named my name. He was prepared as a polished arrow kept in a quiver. Then the crucial expansion: he says to me, you are my servant, O Israel, in whom I will be glorified (verse 3). But then the counter-movement — it is too small a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob; I will give you as a light to the nations, that my salvation may reach to the ends of the earth (verse 6). The Servant is first identified with Israel and then given a mission to Israel, which forces interpreters to hold the two meanings simultaneously. The chapter continues with God's answer to Zion's complaint that God has forsaken her: can a woman forget her nursing child? Even if she did, I will not forget you. See, I have inscribed you on the palms of my hands. The chapter closes with the vision of the nations bringing Israel's scattered children home.

Isaiah 50

The third Servant Song: the open ear

Chapter 50 opens with a brief argument about the exile — it was not God who divorced Israel or sold her for debt, but Israel's own iniquities that separated them. Then comes the third Servant Song (verses 4-9), the most intimate of the four. The Servant speaks as a disciple: the Lord God has given me the tongue of those who are taught, that I may know how to sustain the weary with a word; morning by morning he wakens — wakens my ear to hear as those who are taught. The ear is opened, and the Servant does not rebel. He gave his back to those who struck him, his cheeks to those who pulled out the beard; he did not hide his face from disgrace and spitting. He knows he will be vindicated; he sets his face like flint and asks who will contend with him. The chapter is a meditation on costly obedience — the Servant as the one who listens, accepts suffering, and trusts in vindication. It is brief but crucial for understanding the fourth Servant Song that follows in chapters 52-53.

Isaiah 51

Look to Abraham, look to the rock

Chapter 51 is an extended call to trust addressed to a community that has forgotten its own founding story. Listen to me, you who pursue righteousness, who seek the Lord: look to the rock from which you were cut, look to the quarry from which you were dug. Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you. The argument is analogical and historical: from one couple came a great people; from the same source of divine creativity will come the consolation of Zion. The desolate places will be made like Eden, the wilderness like the garden of the Lord. The chapter continues with the assurance that the strength of God's arm is available for the present moment as it was in creation and at the exodus — the one who dried up the sea, who made a way in the depths for the redeemed to pass over. It closes with the vision of God's own hand removing the cup of wrath from Jerusalem and putting it into the hand of Jerusalem's oppressors.

Isaiah 52

Awake, O Zion — and the fourth Servant Song begins

Chapter 52 opens in the register of triumphant proclamation: awake, awake, put on your strength, O Zion; put on your beautiful garments, O Jerusalem. The chapter then turns to history — Israel was enslaved in Egypt, was mocked in Assyria — and declares that the redemption to come will be recognised by the nations who have not heard. The herald's feet on the mountains are beautiful, carrying good news, peace, salvation, the return of the Lord. The watchmen on the walls will see it and sing. Then, at verse 13, without introduction or transition, the fourth Servant Song begins: behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up and shall be exalted. His appearance was marred beyond human semblance; kings shall shut their mouths at him. The chapter ends mid-song — the full passage runs through to 53:12. The abrupt beginning, with no scene-setting and no named character, gives the song its characteristic quality of confronting the reader without warning. Who is this? is the reader's first question, and the chapter leaves it hanging.

Isaiah 53

Despised and rejected, yet bearing our griefs

Chapter 53 is the centre of the Servant Songs and one of the most consequential single chapters in any religious literature. The speaking voice shifts: it is no longer God addressing Israel but apparently the nations — or some collective — looking back on the Servant in retrospect. We esteemed him not. He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief. We accounted him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, bruised for our iniquities; the chastisement of our peace was upon him, and with his stripes we are healed. The identification of the Servant's suffering as vicarious — for us, not for himself — is the chapter's theological innovation. He was brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth. He made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death. Then the divine address at the close: out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied. The great shall be given to him as a portion because he bore the sin of many.

Isaiah 54

Enlarge your tent — the barren one sings

Chapter 54 follows immediately upon the death and vindication of the Servant with a burst of lyric joy addressed to Zion as a woman who had been barren and forsaken. Sing, O barren one, who did not bear; break forth into singing and cry aloud, you who have not been in labour. Enlarge the place of your tent and let the curtains of your habitations be stretched out; do not hold back. The language is deliberately paradoxical: the one who has not borne is told to enlarge the nursery. Then the grounding: For your Maker is your husband — the Lord of hosts is his name; the Holy One of Israel is your Redeemer. The chapter addresses the shame of the exile directly: for a brief moment I abandoned you, but with great compassion I will gather you. In overflowing anger for a moment I hid my face from you, but with everlasting love I will have compassion on you. The comparison to Noah is precise: as I swore that the waters of Noah should no more go over the earth, so I have sworn that I will not be angry with you any more.

Isaiah 55

Ho, everyone who thirsts — the open invitation

Chapter 55 is the climax and conclusion of Second Isaiah, and among the most celebrated chapters in the entire prophetic literature. It opens in the voice of a market crier, but the goods being offered are not for sale: ho, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters; and he who has no money, come, buy and eat. Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price. Why do you spend your money for that which is not bread, and your labour for that which does not satisfy? The invitation is universal — everyone, without qualification — and the bounty is free. The chapter then pivots to the covenant with David: I will make with you an everlasting covenant, my steadfast, sure love for David. The nations will be drawn to Israel's witness. The call to the wicked to return is pressing: seek the Lord while he may be found. And then the great theological statement about divine transcendence and divine word: my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways — as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways. As rain comes down and does not return without watering the earth, so my word shall not return to me empty.

Isaiah 56

Foreigners and eunuchs gathered in

Chapter 56 opens Third Isaiah (chapters 56-66) and immediately signals a new situation and a new set of questions. The exilic assurance of Second Isaiah gives way to the contested reality of the post-exilic community — who belongs? who is excluded? The chapter responds to these questions by radically expanding the circle of inclusion. Two categories excluded by Deuteronomic law (Deuteronomy 23) are directly addressed: the foreigner who has joined himself to the Lord and the eunuch who holds fast to the covenant. Both are told: you have a place. To the eunuchs who keep the sabbath, hold fast to the covenant, and choose what pleases God, I will give in my house and within my walls a monument and a name better than sons and daughters. To foreigners who join themselves to the Lord: I will bring them to my holy mountain and make them joyful in my house of prayer. My house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples. The chapter closes with a sharp contrast: the watchmen — the leaders of Israel — are blind, ignorant, and silent shepherds who serve only themselves.

Isaiah 57

The high and lofty one dwells with the contrite

Chapter 57 divides sharply between judgement on idolaters and comfort for the lowly. The first section (verses 1-13) is a sustained accusation against those in the restored community who have returned to the fertility cult worship that the earlier prophets condemned — they make beds under every green tree, they sacrifice children in the valleys. The prophetic voice is scathing: upon a high and lofty mountain you have set your bed; you have gone up there to offer sacrifice. Then the comfort shifts at verse 14: prepare the way, remove every obstruction from my people's way. And the theological pivot: for thus says the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy: I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, to revive the spirit of the lowly and to revive the heart of the contrite. This pairing — the transcendent and the intimate dwelling-place — is one of the most condensed theological statements in the book. The chapter closes with the now-familiar refrain: there is no peace for the wicked.

Isaiah 58

True fasting vs. loosening the bonds of injustice

Chapter 58 is one of the sharpest ethical chapters in Third Isaiah, addressing a community that is performing the correct religious acts — fasting, prayer, sabbath observance — but whose daily life contradicts them. They fast and then quarrel and strike with a wicked fist; they bow their heads like a reed and spread sackcloth and ashes beneath them. Is this the fast I choose? The divine answer specifies an alternative: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, to break every yoke. To share bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house. If you do these things, then your light shall break forth like the dawn. The chapter closes with a positive vision of sabbath-keeping as delight rather than burden — if you call the sabbath a delight and the holy day of the Lord honourable, if you honour it rather than going your own ways or pursuing your own business, then you shall take delight in the Lord and he will make you ride on the heights of the earth.

Isaiah 59

Sin as a wall between God and Israel

Chapter 59 is a sustained lament over the state of the post-exilic community, structured as accusation, confession, and divine response. The prophet opens by deflecting a common theological complaint: it is not that God cannot act — his arm is not shortened, his ear is not heavy — but that Israel's iniquities have made a separation between them and God. The accusation is elaborated in violent imagery: their feet run to evil, they hasten to shed innocent blood, their thoughts are thoughts of iniquity. Justice is turned back, righteousness stands far away, truth has stumbled in the public squares. The confession in the middle of the chapter is remarkable: we hope for light but see only darkness; we hope for brightness but walk in gloom. We are like the dead among those with life; we growl like bears, we moan like doves. When justice was absent, God saw it and was displeased that there was no one to intercede. So his own arm brought salvation and his righteousness upheld him — he put on righteousness as a breastplate. The chapter closes with the vision of the Redeemer coming to Zion, accompanied by the promise of the spirit upon his people forever.

Isaiah 60

Arise, shine — your light has come

Chapter 60 is the great vision of restored Zion as the light of the world, the fullest positive statement of Third Isaiah's eschatological hope. Arise, shine, for your light has come, and the glory of the Lord has risen upon you. For behold, darkness shall cover the earth and thick darkness the peoples, but the Lord will arise upon you and his glory will be seen upon you. The contrast between Zion's light and the world's darkness is the chapter's structural centre: the nations will come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your rising. The vision expands into a catalogue of the wealth of the nations streaming to Zion — gold from Sheba, the flocks of Kedar, the ships of Tarshish, the servants of foreign kings. The sun will no longer be needed for daylight, for the Lord will be Zion's everlasting light. The chapter closes with the assurance that the community, once small and despised, will become mighty; the least will become a clan and the smallest a mighty nation. I am the Lord; in its time I will hasten it.

Isaiah 61

The spirit of the Lord upon me

Chapter 61 opens with one of the most familiar sentences in the prophetic tradition: the spirit of the Lord God is upon me, because the Lord has anointed me to bring good news to the poor; he has sent me to bind up the broken-hearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives and the opening of the prison to those who are bound; to proclaim the year of the Lord's favour, and the day of vengeance of our God. The speaker is not named — perhaps the Servant figure of Second Isaiah in a new key, perhaps the prophet himself, perhaps the restored community — but the commission is comprehensive: physical healing, social liberation, restoration of mourners, rebuilding of ancient ruins, a new name (righteousness, planted by the Lord), a role as priests and ministers of God. The vision of restoration is communal and physical: they will rebuild the ancient ruins, raise up the former devastations, repair the ruined cities. The chapter closes with the image of the bridegroom and bride, decked in beauty — as a young man marries a young woman, so your sons will marry you; and as the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so your God will rejoice over you.

Isaiah 62

You shall be called by a new name

Chapter 62 is a sustained intercession on behalf of Zion, spoken by either the prophet or God himself (the text is deliberately ambiguous), committing to continual prayer until Zion's restoration is complete. The chapter's centre is the new name. You shall no longer be called Forsaken, and your land shall no longer be called Desolate; but you shall be called Hephzibah, my delight is in her, and your land Beulah, married. As a young man marries a young woman, so your sons shall marry you; and as the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so your God shall rejoice over you. The new name is the sign of the new relationship; to be re-named is to be re-constituted. The chapter closes with the command to prepare the way for the people — go through, go through the gates, prepare the way for the people, build up the highway, clear it of stones, lift up a signal over the peoples. Behold, the Lord has proclaimed to the ends of the earth: say to the daughter of Zion, behold, your salvation comes.

Isaiah 63

The warrior from Edom and the prayer for return

Chapter 63 opens with one of the most startling images in the prophetic tradition: a figure approaches from Edom, robed in splendour, striding in the greatness of his strength. Who is this? The answer is: I, speaking in righteousness, mighty to save. His garments are red, like one who treads in the wine press — and he has been treading it alone. There was no one to help, so his own arm brought salvation. The image is of divine judgement executed in utter solitude — no human ally, no nation to share the work. It is one of the most violent images in the book. The chapter then turns at verse 7 to a completely different mode: a prayer of communal lament and appeal to divine memory. The prophet recalls God's past acts of steadfast love — he was their saviour; in all their affliction he was afflicted; he bore them and carried them all the days of old. The prayer becomes increasingly urgent as it proceeds: where is your zeal and your might, the stirring of your inner parts and your compassion? Look down from heaven; do not hold back.

Isaiah 64

Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down

Chapter 64 continues the prayer of lament that began in chapter 63, and the prayer reaches its emotional climax. Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down, that the mountains might quake at your presence — as when fire kindles brushwood and fire causes water to boil. The appeal to theophany — the visible coming of God — draws on the oldest tradition of divine self-disclosure (the imagery of fire, earthquake, and mountain is the imagery of Sinai). The prayer then makes a remarkable theological move: all our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment; we all fade like a leaf, and our iniquities take us away like the wind. The community's prayer is grounded not in its own merit but in its need. But now, O Lord, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand. Do not be exceedingly angry, O Lord, and do not remember iniquity forever. Behold, look, we are all your people. The chapter closes with the picture of Zion as a wilderness, the holy cities as desert, the temple burned and desolate.

Isaiah 65

New heavens and a new earth

Chapter 65 is God's answer to the long prayer of chapters 63-64 — and the answer is more complex and more comforting than simple restoration. God speaks first in reproach: I was ready to be sought by those who did not ask for me; I was ready to be found by those who did not seek me. I said, here am I, here am I, to a nation that did not call on my name. The tension between the faithful and the unfaithful within post-exilic Israel is acute: my servants shall eat, but you shall be hungry; my servants shall drink, but you shall be thirsty; my servants shall rejoice, but you shall be put to shame. Then the great vision: behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered. No more the sound of weeping in Jerusalem, no more the child who lives only a few days, no more the one who does not live out his years. They shall plant vineyards and eat their fruit; they shall build houses and inhabit them. The wolf and the lamb shall feed together, the lion shall eat straw like the ox. None shall hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain.

Isaiah 66

All flesh shall come to worship

Chapter 66 is the final chapter of the book of Isaiah and one of the most complex closings in the prophetic tradition. It opens with the image of God's cosmic throne — heaven is my throne and earth is my footstool; what is the house you would build for me? — which undercuts any theology that places God at human disposal through temple building. The next section attacks empty sacrifice: he who slaughters an ox is like one who kills a man; he who offers a lamb, like one who breaks a dog's neck. Then the great burst of comfort to those who tremble at God's word: Zion will give birth suddenly, before her labour pains, like the earth giving birth in a single day. Can a woman forget the child she bore? God will comfort like a mother comforts her child. The nations will bring the scattered of Israel home in their arms and on their backs. Then the new heavens and the new earth are established, and all flesh shall come to worship before God, from new moon to new moon, from sabbath to sabbath. The very last verse is the horror: those who rebelled against God, their worm shall not die, their fire shall not be quenched, and they shall be an abhorrence to all flesh. The book ends there — with both realities held open.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Three Isaiahs and the Book as One

The most important fact a modern reader of Isaiah needs to know is that the sixty-six chapters are not all from one author or one century. The differences are real. The unity is also real. Both things matter.

The Vision in the Temple

Chapter 6 is the founding theological moment of the book. Isaiah sees the Lord seated on the throne, the seraphim cry holy three times, a coal touches his lips, and he is sent. The chapter is one of the most concentrated accounts of religious experience in any literature.

The Suffering Servant

The four Servant Songs in Second Isaiah (42, 49, 50, 52–53) depict a figure who suffers, is rejected, and through whose suffering many are made righteous. Who the Servant is has been one of the longest-running interpretive questions in the history of biblical scholarship.

The Holy One of Israel

The most distinctive divine title in the book — occurring about twenty-five times, and only rarely elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible. The title binds two ideas that sit in tension: God's radical otherness and his covenanted commitment to a particular people.

New Heavens and a New Earth

The closing chapters of Third Isaiah culminate in one of the most extraordinary visions of the future in any ancient text. Chapter 65 is the source of Revelation 21:1 and of the entire Christian and Jewish tradition of the world to come.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Isaiah ben Amoz
Prophet of Jerusalem

The 8th-century BCE prophet whose name the book bears. Active in Jerusalem from about 740 to 700, during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah — a member of the aristocracy with access to the royal court, father of two sons with symbolic prophetic names (Shear-jashub, Maher-shalal-hash-baz). He is the prophet of First Isaiah (chapters 1–39); the later sections come from disciples and successors who continued his tradition through the Babylonian exile. Tradition holds he was martyred under Manasseh, sawn in half — alluded to in Hebrews 11:37.

Hezekiah
King of Judah

King of Judah from about 715 to 686 BCE, the central royal figure of First Isaiah. Religiously reforming and politically embattled, he is the king who in 701 faces the Assyrian invasion under Sennacherib. The narrative in chapters 36–37 describes the crisis: the Assyrian commander mocks Judah's trust in their God; Hezekiah spreads the enemy's letter before the Lord and prays; Isaiah's oracle announces deliverance. Hezekiah is one of the most fully drawn kings in the Hebrew Bible and stands as the type of the king who trusts the Holy One of Israel rather than political alliance.

The Suffering Servant
Mysterious figure of Second Isaiah

The figure who appears in the four Servant Songs of Second Isaiah (42, 49, 50, 52–53). Identified as Israel in 49:3, yet given a mission to Israel in 49:5–6 that complicates the simple identification. He is chosen, beloved, given the spirit, sent to the nations as a light; rejected, despised, afflicted, wounded, killed; through his suffering many are made righteous. Jewish tradition reads him primarily as the figure of Israel; Christian tradition reads him as a prefiguration of Christ. Modern scholarship reads him as a polysemous figure working out a new theology of redemptive suffering.

Cyrus the Great
Anointed Persian king

The Persian king who conquered Babylon in 538 BCE and permitted the Jewish exiles to return to their land. Named directly in Isaiah 45:1 — "Thus says the Lord to his anointed, to Cyrus" — in a passage that must have been written during or in anticipation of the exile, over a century after the historical Isaiah. The fact that a pagan king is called God's anointed is one of the most theologically striking moments in Second Isaiah and is central to the argument that Isaiah 40–55 is exilic material.

Sennacherib
Assyrian king

King of Assyria from 705 to 681 BCE, the great enemy of First Isaiah. His invasion of Judah in 701 — during which he took forty-six walled cities but did not take Jerusalem — forms the centre of the historical narrative section (chapters 36–37). His own annals record shutting Hezekiah up "like a bird in a cage in Jerusalem" without recording the city's fall. He returns to Nineveh and is later assassinated by his own sons, confirming Isaiah's earlier oracle.

The Holy One of Israel
God as named in the book

The distinctive divine title that occurs about twenty-five times in Isaiah and only rarely elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible. The title concentrates the book's theology into four words: God is holy — radically other, transcendent — and is the Holy One of Israel — covenanted to a particular people, acting in their history. The title runs across all three compositional layers. In First Isaiah it appears in judgement; in Second Isaiah in comfort and redemption; in Third Isaiah in restoration. It is the theological hinge between the language of awe (the seraphim in chapter 6) and the language of intimate care (the shepherd in chapter 40).
