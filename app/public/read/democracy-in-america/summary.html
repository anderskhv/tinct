Democracy in America

1835 CE · by Alexis de Tocqueville

The book in brief

Democracy in America argues that the steady leveling of social ranks — what Tocqueville calls the equality of conditions — is the master fact of modern history, and that the United States is the place to study its consequences before they arrive everywhere else. The book describes American institutions — townships, juries, voluntary associations, the federal structure, the press, the bar — and argues that their specific shape is what allows the country to be both democratic and stable. It then turns, in volume two, to the deeper effects of equality on manners, religion, family, art, and ambition. The argument is descriptive in form and prophetic in intent: Tocqueville is writing for France, and his question is whether the conditions of American liberty can be reproduced where the social ground is different.

Tocqueville traveled in the United States for nine months in 1831 with his friend Gustave de Beaumont, ostensibly to study the American penitentiary system on commission from the French government. He was twenty-five, a young aristocrat from a Norman family that had lost members to the Terror, trying to understand the political world that was replacing the one his class had ruled. Volume one was published in 1835 and made him famous across Europe. Volume two, more abstract and pessimistic, appeared in 1840. The two volumes are different books in tone — the first amazed, the second worried — and the worry is what gives the work its lasting analytical force.

It remains the most penetrating book ever written about the United States by a foreigner, and the most precise diagnosis of democratic life produced in the nineteenth century. Tocqueville's vocabulary — tyranny of the majority, individualism, soft despotism, self-interest rightly understood — entered political analysis and never left.

Why it still matters

Democracy Described From Outside

Tocqueville was a French aristocrat looking at a young democracy with neither enthusiasm nor contempt. The outside angle gave him distance to see what Americans took for granted — voluntary associations, religious practice as a civic habit, social mobility as the default expectation, the township as a school of self-government, the jury as a school of justice. He could also see what was harder to see from inside: the conformist pressure of public opinion, the restless materialism of a society without inherited rank, the way American religion supported democratic mores precisely by remaining apart from politics rather than competing with it. Most foreign observers of America have been either dazzled or appalled. Tocqueville is neither, which is why his observations have aged better than almost anyone else's. The book is a model of what a comparative analysis can do when the analyst has done the comparison honestly and refused to flatter either side.

The Tyranny of the Majority

Tocqueville's argument is that democracies can be more oppressive than monarchies because the pressure to conform comes not from the state but from public opinion, and public opinion has no opposition party. A king can be defied by a noble; a church can be defied by a heretic; an aristocracy can be defied by a rival aristocracy. The majority in a democratic society has no rival institution to defy. The dissenter is left isolated, mocked, and eventually silent — and the silencing requires no police, no censorship, no overt act of coercion at all. The argument has been confirmed and reinvented in every era of democratic life since: in nineteenth-century debates about religious dissent, in twentieth-century analyses of mass society and totalitarianism, in twenty-first-century discussions of social media and the platform economy. The mechanism Tocqueville described has only become more efficient as the channels through which majority opinion reaches the individual have multiplied.

Soft Despotism

In volume two Tocqueville describes a future in which a benevolent state takes on more and more responsibility for citizens' lives — providing security, regulating commerce, managing welfare, guiding education, organizing leisure — until the citizens, who never feel coerced, gradually lose the capacity to govern themselves at all. He calls this soft despotism, and he distinguishes it from every previous form of tyranny: it does not torture, does not execute, does not exile. It merely arranges, and the people it arranges around eventually forget how to do without it. The diagnosis has been contested ever since: critics argue Tocqueville mistook the conditions of civilized life for the conditions of servitude. Supporters argue he saw the welfare state coming a hundred years before it arrived. The argument is alive in every contemporary debate about the proper scope of government, and it is alive because the question Tocqueville posed has not been answered.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The tyranny of the majority.

Democratic conformity can crush independent thought more completely than any tyrant, precisely because it carries no visible force. Social pressure, not censorship, silences the dissenter. Tocqueville tracks how majority opinion becomes its own kind of despotism — invisible, ambient, almost impossible to resist.

Mild despotism and the administrative state.

Volume 2, Part IV, Chapter 6 is one of the most prescient passages in the history of political philosophy. Tocqueville describes a future soft tyranny — not a brutal dictatorship but a tutelary state that keeps citizens in "perpetual childhood", managing their affairs while they quietly give up the will to govern themselves. Read for this passage alone.

Equality as the engine of everything.

Tocqueville calls the march toward equality of conditions a "providential fact" — something so deep in history that it amounts to a divine decree. Every other feature of democratic life flows from it: restlessness, materialism, individualism, the tyranny of opinion. Track equality as the generating force behind all the book's other arguments.

Associations as the antidote.

If equality atomises people and the majority crushes individual voices, what holds democratic society together? Tocqueville's answer is voluntary associations — civic organisations, townships, free press, independent courts. These are the institutions that train democratic citizens and distribute power. Read for his argument about what democracy needs in order not to devour itself.

Key figures

Alexis de Tocqueville
THE OBSERVER

A young Norman aristocrat, twenty-five at the time of his American journey, descended from a family that had been guillotined and imprisoned during the Terror. He went to America to understand the political form he believed was coming for France whether the French wanted it or not. The voice in the book is patient, ironic, and aware of its own distance — neither democratic enthusiast nor reactionary, which is what made the analysis last.

Gustave de Beaumont
THE COMPANION

Tocqueville's traveling partner and lifelong friend. The two men were sent together on commission to study American prisons; Beaumont produced the official report on the penitentiary system, and a novel about American slavery, while Tocqueville produced the book on democracy. Beaumont's letters and notebooks supplied much of the underlying observation, and his more direct attention to the slavery question shaped Tocqueville's most uncomfortable chapters.

The New England Township
THE SCHOOL OF LIBERTY

Tocqueville's favorite institution and the unit of analysis he returns to most often. The town meeting, in which propertied citizens deliberate and decide local affairs, trains the habits — argument, compromise, attention to the public business — that scale up to make a republic possible. He argues that the township is to liberty what primary school is to literacy: the place the basic competence is acquired.

The Tyranny of the Majority
THE DEMOCRATIC DANGER

Tocqueville's most famous warning: in a democracy, the majority's opinion becomes its own form of despotism. Without censors or police, it silences dissent through social pressure so complete that the isolated individual eventually stops thinking against it at all. Tocqueville thought this was a more thorough enslavement of mind than any monarchy had achieved — because it left the body free.

Mild Despotism
THE TUTELARY STATE

The new and subtle tyranny Tocqueville foresees in volume two — not brutal, not violent, but suffocating. A vast and tutelary power provides for citizens, regulates their affairs, manages their anxieties, and keeps them in perpetual childhood until they have surrendered the capacity for self-government. He thought this might be democracy's natural end state if civil society did not resist it.

Individualism
THE NEW WORD

Tocqueville coined the term in its modern sense — not healthy self-reliance but a quiet withdrawal from public life, in which each person retreats into family and a small circle of friends and lets the wider world govern itself. He considered it one of democracy's most dangerous tendencies, because it dissolves the intermediate bodies that stand between the individual and the state, leaving the citizen alone with a power he cannot resist.
