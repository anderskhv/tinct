Treasure Island a guided tour

1883 · The founding adventure novel

A boy finds a treasure map in a dead sailor's chest. The map leads to a Caribbean island — and to Long John Silver, who recruited the old pirate crew for the voyage without the honest men knowing. Jim Hawkins is the narrator, but Silver is the problem the book sets and never quite resolves.

The book in brief

Treasure Island is the story of Jim Hawkins, an innkeeper's son who finds a treasure map in a dead sailor's chest. The map leads to the Caribbean island where Captain Flint buried his fortune — and to Long John Silver, the ship's cook who recruited Flint's old crew for the voyage without the honest men aboard knowing it. Jim sails with Squire Trelawney and Dr. Livesey, discovers the mutiny before it begins, and spends the next two weeks surviving on the island through a series of decisions that, for a boy of fourteen, require a level of nerve the adults around him do not always manage. The book ends with the treasure found and Silver escaped — the only character whose fate the narrator cannot account for, and cannot forget.

Stevenson wrote the book in the summer of 1881 at his stepson Lloyd's request, working from a hand-drawn map of an imaginary island. It was serialized in Young Folks magazine in 1881–82 and published as a book in 1883. It is the founding adventure novel — the book that established what the form would look like for the next century. Every adventure story since — the desert island, the buried treasure, the double-crossing pirate, the boy who outperforms the adults — is in conversation with it. Stevenson himself knew what he was making: "no woman in the story, no love interest, no need of psychological machinery." The interest is action and character, and the character who dominates both is Silver.

Jim narrates in retrospect, from adulthood, writing at the request of the men who were there. The retrospective frame gives him full knowledge from the beginning — the early chapters plant details whose significance only becomes clear later, controlled entirely by Jim's narrative intelligence. There is one structural interruption: Chapters 16 through 18 are narrated by Dr. Livesey, giving the reader the events at the stockade during Jim's solo adventures elsewhere. When Jim's voice returns, the contrast is precise — a professional adult's tactical account against an adolescent's felt experience. The two registers are Stevenson's argument about how differently the same events look from inside and outside extremity.

Chapter 1

The old sea-dog arrives

Jim Hawkins begins his narrative: Squire Trelawney and Dr. Livesey have asked him to write down the whole story of Treasure Island. He goes back to the beginning — a tall, heavy sailor who arrived at the Admiral Benbow inn one grey morning, his sea-chest on a handbarrow, a sabre cut white across his cheek. He called himself "the captain," paid for his board, and spent his days scanning the horizon from the clifftop. He hired Jim to watch for "a seafaring man with one leg." He drank rum. He told sailors' stories in the taproom. He terrified everyone including Jim's ailing father. He sang "Fifteen men on the dead man's chest" in a cracked old voice.

Chapter 2

Black Dog at the inn

Winter. Jim's father is clearly dying. A pale stranger with two fingers on his left hand — Black Dog — comes to the inn asking for "the captain." Billy Bones comes downstairs; the two retire together; Jim hears raised voices. Black Dog runs out with blood on his arm, Bones in furious pursuit. Bones collapses on the road, cursing. Dr. Livesey is sent for and diagnoses a stroke, warns Bones off rum, and is ignored.

Chapter 3

The black spot delivered

A blind man named Pew arrives at the inn, takes Jim's arm to be guided, and forces him to lead him to the captain. Pew slips something into Billy Bones's hand — the black spot, a circle of black on one side, a deadline written on the other: "You have till ten tonight." Bones has six hours to produce something he has. He tells Jim it is the map. He tries to get up and escape; his heart gives out. He dies on the inn floor.

Chapter 4

The sea-chest opened

Jim tells his mother everything. They must get into the chest before Bones's shipmates return — they are owed money for his lodging and mean to take it. They go back to the inn, light a candle, and open the chest. It holds the usual sailor's property — clothes, tobacco, a quadrant — and at the bottom a bundle of paper and a sealed packet. They take the packet and a handful of coins, counting out exactly what Bones owed them. Then they hear Pew's tapping stick on the road and run.

Pew ridden down

Revenue riders arrive at the inn, summoned by the hamlet's postmaster. The pirates scatter into the darkness. Pew, left behind by his shipmates in the confusion, is ridden down by one of the horses on the road and killed. The riders search the area but find nothing more. Jim gives the sealed packet to the Revenue officer, Mr. Dance, who takes him to see Dr. Livesey and the squire.

Chapter 6

The map revealed

At the squire's house, Dr. Livesey opens the sealed packet. It contains a detailed account of Flint's finances and a map of an island — latitude, longitude, soundings, anchorages, and three red crosses marking locations, with a note in a different hand: "Bulk of treasure here." Squire Trelawney immediately plans an expedition: he will hire a ship in Bristol, Livesey will come as the ship's doctor, Jim as cabin boy. Livesey agrees but warns the squire: keep this secret. The squire agrees enthusiastically and with no apparent intention of managing it.

Chapter 7

I go to Bristol

Weeks pass while the doctor goes to London and the squire works at Bristol. Then the squire's letter arrives — he has hired a schooner, the Hispaniola; found a crew with the invaluable assistance of "a one-legged sailor" who has been his go-between; and is ready to sail. Jim travels to Bristol and meets the squire. Everything is ready. Silver has hired the crew.

Chapter 8

First meeting with Silver

Jim finds the Spy-glass tavern and meets Long John Silver — the most vivid figure he has ever encountered. Tall, one leg cut off at the hip, managing the crutch with birdlike ease, affectionate, funny, full of stories. Silver greets Jim with warmth, calls for the boy, makes him feel immediately at home. Jim is enchanted. Then he sees Black Dog — the man who visited Billy Bones — sitting in the corner. Silver chases Black Dog out and explains him away so convincingly that Jim's suspicions dissolve. He walks back to the squire convinced Silver is the finest man he has met.

Chapter 9

Captain Smollett's warning

Jim and Trelawney row out to the Hispaniola. Captain Smollett appears almost immediately, irritated by everything. He tells the squire, directly and without much diplomacy, that he does not like the crew and that the mission — destination, purpose, even the name of the island — is known throughout the ship. He had been promised a hand-picked crew of his own choosing; he got the crew Silver found. He demands the arms and powder be moved under the cabin. The squire is furious. Livesey privately thinks Smollett may be right.

Chapter 10

The voyage out

The ship sails. Silver is the life of the voyage — running the galley, managing the crew, singing with them in the evenings. He calls Jim "Hawkins" and "my son" and Jim, who knows about Black Dog, cannot manage to stay suspicious of him for more than a few minutes at a time. The mate Mr. Arrow turns out to be a hopeless drunk and disappears overboard one night — no one is very sorry, though no one can say exactly what happened to him. Silver takes over day-to-day management of the crew.

Chapter 11

The apple barrel

Jim, restless, goes to the apple barrel for a late apple and finds it almost empty — he has to climb in to reach the last ones. He cannot get out. Silver arrives with two of the crew and talks freely, assuming the barrel is empty. Jim hears everything: Silver was quartermaster under Captain Flint, has spent years planning this, has been recruiting the crew since Bristol. Once they have the treasure, the captain and the squire and the doctor will be killed. Silver has a fortune already stashed in banks; this is one last score. Jim hides in the barrel until the island is sighted and the crew rushes on deck.

Chapter 12

Council of war

In the captain's cabin, Jim tells the whole story. The honest party calculates their odds: they have seven loyal men against Silver's nineteen (including the captain, the squire, the doctor, Jim, and three crew members they believe are trustworthy). They must reach the island — the crew expects it — and must not show their hand until they can improve their position. The captain decides to let the pirates go ashore first, separate them from the ship, and act from there.

Chapter 13

Jim goes ashore

The island from on deck looks grey and unwelcoming. The crew is restless; the captain lets them go ashore in the boats, keeping six men aboard. Jim, on impulse, jumps into one of the pirate boats just before it shoves off. He runs into the jungle when they beach. He explores the island alone while Silver, some distance away, kills a loyal sailor who refuses to join the mutiny.

The first blow

Jim, alone in the interior, crosses a marsh and open sandy ground. He is uneasy and alert — he has just watched Silver kill a man. A scream from the direction of the shore tells him another of the loyal sailors has been killed. He presses on rather than going back. Chapter 15

Ben Gunn

A figure runs from tree to tree ahead of Jim — faster than he is, circling to cut him off. Jim turns and faces it. It is a sun-blackened man dressed in goatskins, with long matted hair and beard. He falls on his knees. He is Ben Gunn, a former pirate left on the island by Captain Flint three years ago as punishment. He wants cheese. He has been doing something with the treasure in his three years alone. He asks if there is anyone he could meet before meeting Long John.

Chapter 16

The doctor's narrative — the ship

Dr. Livesey narrates. Aboard the Hispaniola, the honest party — the captain, the squire, the doctor, and the loyal crew — wait while the pirates are ashore. Two shots from the shore signal murders; the atmosphere turns dangerous. The captain decides they cannot stay on the ship. They load the jolly-boat with arms and supplies and head for the stockade on the map. They get ashore with difficulty.

The jolly-boat's last trip

The jolly-boat is overloaded. The cannon shot from the ship falls short. They reach the stockade. The doctor narrates with his characteristic precision — this happened, then that happened, then the other thing. No embellishment. Jim's absence is noted.

End of the first day's fighting

The pirates attack in force. A skirmish at the edge of the wood; the pirates use the trees as cover. A loyal sailor is shot. The doctor, in his first action as a combatant, fights with his sword. The attack is repulsed but at cost. The stockade holds.

Chapter 19

Jim reaches the stockade

Jim, with Ben Gunn, makes his way through the jungle toward the British flag he can see above the trees. Ben Gunn stops at the edge — he will not meet the squire until he is sure of his welcome — and Jim goes in. He finds the honest party: tired, diminished, the captain wounded. He tells them about Ben Gunn. He tells them everything. Smollett makes a note of his good service.

Chapter 20

Silver's embassy

Under a white flag, Silver approaches the stockade with one man. He proposes a deal: hand over the chart, live safely under Silver's protection, and Silver will either leave them on the island with provisions or put them ashore in England. Smollett refuses on every point. He does not trust the offer, does not like the terms, and would rather fight from a position he knows than accept terms from a man who offered them. Silver returns to his party.

Chapter 21

The stockade attack

The pirates attack in force: scaling the walls, coming from three directions at once. The fight is brief and violent. Three of the honest party are killed; so are five pirates. The stockade holds. Captain Smollett is wounded. The surviving pirates retreat. Smollett takes stock of what is left: six honest men, the captain wounded, the stockade intact.

Chapter 22

Jim's sea adventure begins

After the stockade attack, Jim slips away from the stockade without permission — his third unauthorized act. He has the idea of finding the Hispaniola and cutting her anchor cable, sending her adrift where she cannot be used by the pirates. He finds Ben Gunn's hide in the rocks and the coracle — a small, round, home-built boat — and waits for darkness.

Chapter 23

The ebb-tide and the coracle

The coracle is nearly impossible to steer — it spins sideways, goes in every direction except forward, and is controlled only by the tide. By luck, the tide is running toward the Hispaniola. Jim bumps against her anchor cable in the dark, cuts it, and the ship begins to drift. He clings to her cable as she moves.

Chapter 24

The cruise of the coracle

Jim wakes at dawn, drifting off the southwest end of Treasure Island. He cannot control the coracle. He drifts all morning, close to the cliffs, until a change in the breeze sends him toward the island and he paddles desperately for shore. He reaches land in time to avoid the rocks. Chapter 25

I take the Hispaniola

Jim pulls himself aboard. Two men are on deck: one dead, Israel Hands sitting against the bulwarks, wounded and weak. Between them they make a deal: Jim will sail the ship if Hands tells him how. Jim hauls down the Jolly Roger and replaces it with nothing. He begins to sail for the North Inlet.

Chapter 26

Israel Hands

Jim sails the Hispaniola toward the North Inlet, Hands advising from the deck. He notices too late that Hands has been inching toward a dirk — the knife the dead man dropped. Hands charges. Jim runs up the rigging. Hands follows. Jim shoots him at the crosstrees and Hands falls to the sea. Jim is pinned to the mast by the dirk.

"Pieces of eight"

Jim returns to the stockade at night and climbs in without waking what he assumes are his friends. In the darkness he hears a familiar voice: "Pieces of eight!" It is the parrot. The pirates have taken the stockade while Jim was away. Jim is captured by Silver.

Chapter 28

In the enemy's camp

Jim is in Silver's camp: six pirates, the stockade, provisions, and Jim as a prisoner. Silver protects him from the crew's desire to kill him on the spot. Silver has his reasons: he knows the tide is turning against him, knows the honest party must have something he doesn't, and knows Jim is more useful alive. He tells Jim he is between the devil and the deep sea. Jim tells Silver everything he has done.

Chapter 29

The black spot again

The pirates formally deliver the black spot to Silver: he is deposed on four charges. Silver reads the charges, refutes them one by one, and produces the chart — given to him by Dr. Livesey — as his trump card. The chart changes everything: the pirates want the gold and need Silver's leadership to find it. He is reinstated by unanimous vote. Jim watches Silver manage the crew with something close to admiration.

Chapter 30

On parole

Dr. Livesey comes to the stockade to treat the sick pirates — part of the deal he made with Silver when he gave up the stockade. He treats them efficiently and without comment. Silver allows Livesey a private word with Jim. Livesey tells Jim, with suppressed agitation, that Ben Gunn's information has changed the situation — not what, not how, only that Jim must stay with Silver and watch. Silver has given Jim his parole and Jim keeps it.

Flint's pointer

Silver leads the pirates inland with the map. Jim goes with them, Silver's nominal prisoner. Chapter 32

The voice among the trees

As the pirates move toward the tall tree, a voice comes from the trees above them singing "Fifteen men on the dead man's chest." It sounds exactly like Captain Flint. The pirates stop, terrified. Silver identifies it as a practical trick — some living man doing it — and marches on. The voice follows them, adapts to their responses, names their own dead. Silver keeps moving.

The fall of a chieftain

The pirates reach the tall tree and the location the map describes. They dig. The pit is empty — someone has been here before them and taken everything. The crew turns on Silver immediately. Chapter 34

And last

The gold — which Ben Gunn moved to his cave over three years — is transported to the Hispaniola and loaded over several days. The three surviving pirates are left on the island with provisions; taking them to England would mean a hanging they haven't earned. Silver is allowed to stay aboard until they reach a port in Spanish America, where he disappears overnight with a small portion of the gold. Jim, back in England, says he has been troubled by nightmares and does not think he will go to sea again.

Key themes

4 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The problem of Silver

Silver is the reason the book endures. He is not the villain of popular mythology — snarling, obviously evil. He is charming, generous, strategically brilliant, and genuinely fond of Jim. He is also entirely willing to murder anyone who stands between him and the gold. Stevenson refuses to resolve the contradiction.

The boy who acts alone

Jim's three unauthorized decisions — hiding in the apple barrel, cutting the Hispaniola adrift, confronting Israel Hands alone on the rigging — are the structural spine of the novel. Each is a moment where Jim does something none of the adults instructed, and each one changes the course of events.

The pirate code

The pirates are not anarchic. They have articles, a captain elected by vote, and a formal instrument of deposition — the black spot. Understanding how the code works makes the mutiny and its failure much clearer.

Ben Gunn — the man outside the system

The marooned sailor, left on the island three years by Captain Flint, is pathetic, comic, and the agent of the only event that finally matters. People dismissed as useless are sometimes doing the most important work.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Jim Hawkins
The narrator

An innkeeper's son, perhaps fourteen, who finds the treasure map in Billy Bones's sea-chest. Tells the story in his own words from adulthood, which means he has full knowledge from the beginning and plants details whose significance only becomes clear later. Makes three major unauthorized decisions — each one decisive. Not a born hero in any stylized sense: frightened, occasionally wrong, honest about both. What makes him the center is not his courage but his capacity to act when the adults have run out of moves.

Long John Silver
The cook — and the pirate captain

One-legged, parrot-equipped, endlessly charming, and the most dangerous man in the book. Served as quartermaster under Captain Flint before recruiting the old crew for this voyage under the squire's nose. Reads every room, adjusts every performance, and is loyal to himself alone while appearing loyal to whoever is listening. Likes Jim genuinely — and would also kill him without hesitation if it served the purpose. Escapes at the end with a portion of the treasure, and Jim admits he does not expect to see him again.

Captain Smollett
The honest captain

The captain hired by Squire Trelawney, and the one adult on the honest side who behaves like a professional throughout. Distrusts the crew from the beginning and says so; is overruled; is right. His tactical competence in the stockade defense is the reason the honest party survives until the treasure is found. Not a warm man — Jim finds him cold and censorious — but the one whose judgment holds when everyone else's has failed.

Dr. Livesey
The physician

The local doctor who receives the treasure map and organizes the expedition. Methodical, brave without theatrics, and the author of the strategic move that saves the honest men's lives in the second half: trading the stockade to Ben Gunn for what Gunn knows. Treats the wounded pirates after the stockade battle — a detail Stevenson plants to distinguish Livesey from moral simplicity. He is not a man who hates his enemies.

Ben Gunn
The marooned sailor

A former pirate left on the island three years earlier by Captain Flint as punishment for losing track of part of the treasure. His three years alone have produced the single action that decides the novel: he found the treasure, moved it, and has been guarding it in secret. He is pathetic, comic, and — in the book's final reversal — the agent of the only event that matters.

Squire Trelawney
The organizer

The wealthy country squire who funds the expedition after reading the treasure map. Generous, enthusiastic, and completely unable to keep a secret — a flaw that is almost fatal. He hired every man on the ship, including Silver and the old pirate crew, and hired them with Silver's help. His cheerful confidence is the book's running irony: the man who made the voyage possible is the one who nearly sank it before it started.
