Treasure Island — themes & analysis

Four threads through the novel

Treasure Island invented the adventure novel. What keeps it alive is not the treasure but the moral problem it poses in the shape of one particular person.

In this analysis
  1. The problem of Silver
  2. The boy who acts alone
  3. The pirate code
  4. Ben Gunn — the man outside the system

1 · The problem of Silver

charming, loyal, lethal, self-serving — in the same breath

Long John Silver is the central problem of Treasure Island, and Stevenson knew it. He called Silver the most successful creation he had ever made — more successful than the book that contains him — and the success is built on a refusal to resolve the question of what Silver actually is. He is not the villain in the conventional sense. He does not sneer, does not hate his enemies personally, does not wish harm for its own sake. He is a man of extraordinary social intelligence who adapts his presentation to his audience with what feels like genuine warmth.

The Silver that Jim sees over meals in the ship's galley — the clever, affectionate mentor with the parrot and the crutch and the stories — is not entirely a performance. Silver likes Jim. The liking is real. And Silver kills without hesitation. He murders Tom the honest sailor in a field in front of Jim because Tom refuses to join the mutiny. He leads the pirate party with tactical intelligence and personal courage. He is willing, at any point in the book, to turn Jim over to his crew if the price is right, and willing to betray his crew to the honest party if the price is right. He is loyal to himself alone.

Stevenson's original inspiration was W.E. Henley, his friend, who was missing a leg and whose physical vitality made the disability almost invisible. The biographical origin matters because it explains why Silver is physically formidable rather than pitiful — the crutch is a weapon, the one-leggedness a limitation Silver has turned into something close to an advantage. The parrot is partly an extension of the theatrical self-presentation that makes Silver the most memorable person in every room he enters.

What Jim feels about Silver at the end — that he does not expect to see him again and is, in some part of himself, glad of it — is one of the most carefully calibrated emotional endings in Victorian adventure fiction. It is not relief. It is not grief. It is the complex feeling of a person who has been genuinely educated by someone who would have sold him without a second thought, and who knows it, and still cannot quite bring himself to wish the man ill. That feeling is what makes Silver a great character rather than merely a vivid one.

Where to follow it: Ch. 8 (Silver first appears at the Spy-glass), Ch. 11 (Jim hears Silver's plan in the apple barrel), Ch. 20 (Silver's embassy to the stockade), Ch. 28 (Jim in the enemy's camp — Silver protects him), Ch. 34 (Silver disappears with the gold).

2 · The boy who acts alone

"I had got myself into a mess I couldn't escape from"

The structural argument of Treasure Island is built into its plot mechanics. Jim Hawkins makes three major unilateral decisions, each unauthorized, each decisive. First: hiding in the apple barrel and overhearing Silver's plan. Second: cutting the Hispaniola adrift in the night and sailing her around the island alone. Third: walking into Silver's camp and delivering himself as a prisoner on principle. Each decision horrifies the adults around him when they learn of it. Each one saves the expedition.

Stevenson's account of how nerve actually works is more honest than most adventure fiction is willing to offer. Jim does not act from heroic calculation. He hides in the apple barrel because he is eating an apple and Silver's party arrives before he can get out. He cuts the Hispaniola adrift because the ship is dragging toward the pirates and he cannot think of what else to do. He walks into Silver's camp because he has already done it and there is no way to undo it. The decisions are not premeditated. They emerge from situations of genuine extremity in a person who has not yet learned to be afraid of the right things.

The contrast with the adults is precise. Squire Trelawney funded the expedition and nearly sank it by hiring Silver's men. Captain Smollett commanded the ship with professional competence and was right about the crew from the beginning — and was overruled. Dr. Livesey is the steadiest mind among the honest party and makes the strategic move that saves them in the second half. None of them makes a decision on the island that changes the situation as fundamentally as Jim's three unauthorized acts. Stevenson is not arguing that children are better than adults. He is arguing that a certain kind of competence — the kind that requires not knowing what you are risking — can only come from someone who has not yet been taught to calculate the odds.

The ending of Jim's account is carefully calibrated. He says he has been tormented by nightmares about the sea and does not think he will go to sea again. He is not celebrating the adventure. He survived it. The nerve that carried him through it was real and was his, and it cost him something he cannot name and cannot recover. That is Stevenson's final argument about what acting alone actually costs, and it is more honest than the genre he founded.

Where to follow it: Ch. 11 (the apple barrel — first unauthorized act), Ch. 22 (Jim decides to cut the ship adrift), Ch. 28 (Jim walks into Silver's camp).

3 · The pirate code

more democratic than the honest party's hierarchy

The pirates in Treasure Island are not simply lawless men. They have articles — a written agreement that every pirate signs before a voyage, specifying the division of loot, the rules of behavior, and the penalties for infractions. The articles define each man's share, what happens if a man is wounded in service, and what crimes are punishable by marooning or death. The pirates refer to the articles repeatedly. They are men operating under a different law.

The most important element of the code is the black spot. This is a formal instrument of pirate democracy: a piece of paper — traditionally cut from a Bible page — with a black spot on one side and, on the other, the accusation or demand. Delivering the black spot to a captain means the crew has voted to depose him or deliver an ultimatum. The captain, on receiving it, is entitled to appeal — to argue his case, to turn the spot over and see the charge — and the crew votes on whether the appeal succeeds. Silver receives the black spot twice in the novel. Both times he talks his way out of it. The code that his crew believes protects them from him is the tool he uses to survive their anger.

What makes Stevenson's treatment interesting is that the pirate code is more democratic than the hierarchy of the honest party. Squire Trelawney makes decisions for the expedition because he paid for it. Captain Smollett commands because he was hired. Dr. Livesey has authority because of his social position. The pirates vote. Silver is their captain because they chose him, and he can be deposed by a majority. The irony is that their democracy makes them more vulnerable to Silver's manipulation, not less: he knows exactly what the vote requires and can perform it every time the count runs against him.

The code also has provisions for marooning — leaving a man on an uninhabited island rather than killing him, which is both merciful and cruel depending on the island. Ben Gunn was marooned by Captain Flint three years before the book begins. His three years alone on the island are the result of the code's mercy, and his solitude produces the single act that matters most in the book's resolution. The code does not protect men from Silver; it makes them predictable to him. Only Ben Gunn, outside the code entirely, operates without Silver's ability to forecast him.

Where to follow it: Ch. 11 (Silver explains the articles in the apple barrel), Ch. 20 (Silver's embassy — the code of parley), Ch. 29 (the black spot delivered to Silver), Ch. 33 (the treasure gone — the code collapses).

4 · Ben Gunn — the man outside the system

"I want nothing so much as cheese"

Ben Gunn is the novel's overlooked figure, which is exactly the point. He appears in Chapter 15 as a wild, sun-baked creature who has been alone on the island for three years — since Captain Flint marooned him for finding and then losing part of the treasure. He talks about cheese constantly. He is afraid of almost everything. He has been alone so long that his speech has gone strange. He is, at first glance, comic relief.

He is also the person who decides the novel. Three years of solitude have not been wasted. Gunn found the skeleton — Flint's pointer — that Silver uses to locate the cache. He found the treasure itself, moved it, and has been living near it in secret while the pirates' entire mission has been organized around its supposed location. When Silver leads his crew to the cache site in Chapter 33 and finds the pit empty, it is because Ben Gunn moved the contents. The whole mutiny — the planning, the voyage, the murders, the stockade attack — has been aimed at an empty hole.

Stevenson plants Gunn carefully. He is on the island when Jim finds him. He has useful information but imparts it obliquely, in the way of a man who has not spoken to a sane person in three years. Dr. Livesey understands what Gunn is telling him and makes the strategic move — giving up the stockade in exchange for Gunn's information — that saves the honest party. The adults understand Ben Gunn before Jim does, which is one of the few places in the novel where adult judgment is ahead of Jim's.

The novel's quiet argument through Ben Gunn is about what solitude produces in a person who uses it rather than merely endures it. He was a pirate before he was marooned; he was a prisoner of the island afterward; he is a free man — or as close to one as the novel offers — by the time the events conclude. He gets a portion of the treasure, spends it in nineteen days, and becomes a gatekeeper. His fate is comic and deliberately so. He is not the novel's hero. He is its wild card, and wild cards are the thing systems cannot plan for.

Where to follow it: Ch. 15 (Ben Gunn appears — "I'm rich, I'm rich"), Ch. 19 (Jim reaches the stockade and finds the honest party), Ch. 33 (the empty cache — what Gunn did with three years), Ch. 34 (Gunn's role revealed).
