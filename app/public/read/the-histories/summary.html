The Histories

440 BCE · by Herodotus

The book in brief

The Histories is Herodotus's account of the rise of the Persian Empire and its wars with the Greek city-states. Nine books long, it begins with the legendary kidnappings — of Io, Europa, Medea, Helen — that supposedly set East and West against each other, then settles into the real history. It tracks the rise of Lydia under Croesus, his war against the Persian Cyrus, the conquests of Cyrus and Cambyses, the consolidation of the empire under Darius. It covers the Ionian revolt, the failed first expedition that ended at Marathon in 490 BCE, and the great invasion under Xerxes in 480 — the burning of Athens, the holding action at Thermopylae, the naval battle at Salamis, and the Greek victories at Plataea and Mycale in 479. Herodotus stops constantly to describe places he has visited, customs he has heard about, animals he has been told exist.

Herodotus was born in Halicarnassus, on the coast of what is now Turkey, around 484 BCE. He traveled extensively in the eastern Mediterranean — Egypt, Babylon, the Black Sea, southern Italy — and asked questions wherever he went. He wrote in Ionic Greek prose around 440 BCE, when most accounts of the past were still in poetry. He gave what he was doing a name that had not been used for it before: historia, inquiry.

Herodotus is the founder of historical writing in the Western tradition. He has been called the father of history; he has been called the father of lies. His method was inclusive — he reports what he was told and what he saw, with caveats when he found something implausible — and the results include phoenixes, gold-digging ants, and rivers of amber, alongside some of the most accurate descriptions of foreign cultures any ancient writer produced. The pleasure of the book is partly the texture of his curiosity. He is interested in everything, and he expects you to be too.

Why it still matters

The Invention of Inquiry

Before Herodotus, accounts of the past were poetry. Homer, the genealogies, the founding myths, the lists of dynasts — all in verse, all blending gods and men. Herodotus invented something different: prose narrative based on inquiry. He traveled to the places. He talked to the people. He compared what they told him with what others said. He distinguished between what he had seen, what he had been told by reliable witnesses, and what he had been told but did not himself credit. The form was so new he had to give it a name. Historia meant inquiry. The discipline that Thucydides would refine, that Polybius and Tacitus would inherit, and that every modern historian still works inside, started here.

A Curious Mind, Recorded

Herodotus is interested in everything. Egyptian mummification techniques. Scythian burial rites that involve smoking hemp in a felt tent. The size of the walls of Babylon. Why the Nile floods in summer when every other river floods in winter. The nature of cinnamon, which he reports comes from giant nests built by birds in inaccessible cliffs and is gathered when the Arabs leave large pieces of meat below for the birds to carry up, breaking the nests with the weight. The book is an early portrait of how a curious mind handles a large unfamiliar world. Reading him is reading the prose of someone who finds humans and the things they do permanently interesting.

The Persian Wars as Drama

The set pieces are some of the most memorable scenes in classical literature. Croesus on the pyre, calling out the name Solon. Solon telling Croesus that no man should be called happy until he is dead. Xerxes weeping over his army. The Spartan envoys at the Persian court asked why they will not bow to Xerxes. The conversation between Xerxes and the exiled Greek king Demaratus before Thermopylae about why free men fight harder than slaves. The naval engagement at Salamis seen from the throne Xerxes had set up on the cliffs to watch. Herodotus is a great storyteller. The fact that he was also the founder of a discipline does not diminish that. His history is also a great book.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Herodotus as the world's first journalist.

He interviewed sources, weighed conflicting accounts, and regularly noted when he found something implausible but chose to report it anyway. I want to read tracking his method as much as his content.

Greece versus Persia as a civilisational argument.

Herodotus isn't just narrating battles — he's arguing that freedom and tyranny produce different kinds of soldiers, different kinds of courage. I want to follow that argument through the whole work.

The digressions as the real point.

Herodotus spends more time on Egypt, Scythia, and Babylon than on many of the battles. I want to read these digressions as a coherent project — an attempt to understand the whole world, not just one conflict.

What Herodotus actually believed.

He reports flying snakes, gold-digging ants, and rivers of amber — but often with explicit caveats. I want to track his sceptical markers and form a view of what kind of historian he was and what he thought he was doing.

Key figures

Herodotus
The Narrator

Everywhere and nowhere — present in every digression and editorial aside, absent as a character. He notes when he visited a place, when he is reporting something he was told, when he finds a story implausible but is including it anyway because the people he asked all said the same thing. The voice that gathered, weighed, and arranged everything in the book. Understanding his temperament — curious, ironic, generous to foreigners, occasionally credulous — is most of the pleasure of reading him.

Croesus
The Cautionary Opening

King of Lydia, the wealthiest man of his time. He asked the oracle at Delphi whether he should attack Persia and was told that if he did, a great empire would fall. He attacked. The empire that fell was his own. Cyrus captured him, was about to burn him on a pyre, and at the last moment was moved by something Croesus said about the Athenian sage Solon. The story sets the moral tenor of the whole book: the unstable equilibrium of fortune, the limits of human knowledge.

Cyrus
Founder of Persia

The first and greatest Persian king. Raised as a shepherd's son, he was discovered to be the heir to the Median throne, conquered the Medes, then Lydia, then Babylon, and built the empire that would invade Greece three generations later. Herodotus admires him. He gives Cyrus the long death of a man who finally overreached — killed against the Massagetae, on the steppe, far from any throne. The same hubris that destroyed Croesus catches up with the man who captured him.

Xerxes
The Great Invader

Cyrus's grandson and the antagonist of the second half of the work. He led the largest army the ancient world had ever seen across the Hellespont in 480 BCE on a bridge of boats. Herodotus gives him both grandeur and folly: weeping at the sight of his forces because in a hundred years none of them will be alive, then ordering the sea to be whipped with three hundred lashes when a storm broke his bridge. He returned to Persia after Salamis. The empire never invaded Greece again.

Themistocles
The Athenian Genius

The architect of the Greek victory at Salamis. He read the Delphic oracle's instruction to trust in wooden walls correctly — as meaning ships, not the Acropolis — and persuaded Athens to spend a silver windfall on building a fleet. When the Persian armada bore down, he tricked Xerxes into committing his ships to the narrow strait of Salamis, where Greek maneuverability mattered more than Persian numbers. Brilliant and unscrupulous in equal measure. He died in exile, in the service of the Persian king he had defeated.

Leonidas
Who Held Thermopylae

The Spartan king who held the narrow pass at Thermopylae against the Persian advance with three hundred Spartans and a few thousand other Greeks. They held for three days; on the third, betrayed by a local who showed the Persians a path around the position, Leonidas dismissed most of his force and stayed with his Spartans and a few others to die where they stood. Herodotus knew the engagement failed to stop Xerxes. He tells it anyway, at length, because he understood what the story would become.
