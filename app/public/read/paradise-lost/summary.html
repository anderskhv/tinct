Paradise Lost

1667 CE · by John Milton

The book in brief

Paradise Lost is a twelve-book epic in blank verse that tells the story of the fall of man and, around it, the fall of Satan. The poem opens not in Eden but in Hell, where the rebel angels lie burning on a lake of fire after their defeat in heaven. Satan rouses them, builds a capital called Pandemonium, and proposes a strategy of revenge against God: not war, which has already failed, but the corruption of the new creature God has just made. He flies up through chaos, talks his way past the gates of Hell, slips into Eden disguised as a serpent, and persuades Eve to eat the fruit of the one forbidden tree. Adam, told what she has done, eats it knowingly to share her fate. The angel Michael shows Adam the future of human history through to the redemption, and the poem ends with Adam and Eve walking out of paradise hand in hand into the fallen world.

Milton dictated the poem in his late fifties and early sixties to a series of amanuenses, including his daughters and a nephew, after he had gone completely blind around 1652. He composed it in his head at night, often dictating thirty or forty lines in the morning. He was also writing as a defeated political revolutionary: he had served as Latin Secretary to Cromwell's Commonwealth, defended the regicide of Charles I in print, and watched the Restoration in 1660 nearly cost him his life. Paradise Lost was published in ten books in 1667 and reissued in twelve in 1674, the year of his death. The price was three shillings. He earned ten pounds for it.

It is the most ambitious poem in the English language. Milton announced his intention as "to justify the ways of God to men," and the poem attempts the hardest theological problem in Christianity head-on: why a good and omnipotent God permits evil and suffering. The argument is honest enough that it cannot quite contain its own materials. Satan is given some of the most powerful speeches in English poetry. Eve's psychology is rendered with a tenderness no previous epic had attempted. Adam's choice to fall with Eve is depicted as moral failure and as the most recognizable kind of love. Almost every long English poem since — Wordsworth's Prelude, Blake's prophetic books, Shelley's Prometheus Unbound, Tennyson's In Memoriam — works in the wake of what Milton did with blank verse, syntax, and ambition in this book.

Why it still matters

Satan as a Charismatic Argument

The first two books of Paradise Lost give Satan some of the most powerful speeches in English poetry: "What though the field be lost? All is not lost," "The mind is its own place, and in itself / Can make a Heav'n of Hell, a Hell of Heav'n," "Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heaven." He is proud, defiant, self-aware, eloquent. By Book IV he is also pathetic, soliloquizing on Mount Niphates with a soul "hot Hell" inside him. Blake said Milton was "of the Devil's party without knowing it." Shelley agreed; C.S. Lewis disagreed; Stanley Fish argued Milton put Satan there to seduce the reader and then expose the seduction. The poem is large enough to hold all four readings. The argument over Satan is one of the longest-running disputes in English literary criticism.

Justifying the Ways of God

Milton's stated aim is to "justify the ways of God to men" — to make sense of why a good God permits the Fall, suffering, death. He attempts the hardest theological problem in Christianity head-on, in twelve books of blank verse, and refuses the easy moves. He will not pretend the Fall was insignificant. He will not pretend Adam and Eve were tricked. He will not pretend the punishment is light. He defends the doctrine of free will against predestination by making Adam's choice fully meaningful, which means fully terrible. Whether the theodicy succeeds is what readers have argued about for three hundred and fifty years. Even the readers most convinced it fails admit that the failure is at the height of the question.

The English Epic

Before Milton, English poetry had no epic of the stature of Homer or Virgil. Spenser had attempted the Faerie Queene and left it unfinished. Milton's choice of blank verse rather than rhyme — defended in the prefatory note as a recovery of "ancient liberty" against "the modern bondage of rhyming" — set the meter that English poetry would use for serious sustained verse for the next three centuries. The syntax he developed, with its long suspended Latinate periods and its delayed verbs, gave English a register it had not previously had. Wordsworth, Keats, Tennyson, Browning, and Stevens all measure themselves against him. T.S. Eliot tried to dethrone him in the 1930s and conceded by 1947 that he could not.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Satan as the most compelling character.

Many readers come away feeling Satan is the real hero of this poem. I want to understand whether Milton intended that, or whether the poem got away from him — and what it means if it did.

The free will problem.

God knew Adam would fall before he created him. So what does it mean that Adam freely chose it? Milton thought this was the central theological puzzle, and he wrote twelve books trying to answer it.

What Milton thought he was doing.

Milton announced his intention as "justifying the ways of God to men." I want to track whether the poem achieves that — or whether it ends up somewhere more complicated and more honest.

The politics in the poem.

Milton lived through the English Civil War, helped justify regicide, and watched the Restoration bring back monarchy. What he put into Satan — and into the Court of Heaven — is not accidental.

Key figures

Satan
The Adversary

Once Lucifer, brightest of the archangels and second only to the Son in heaven. Refuses the elevation of the Son and leads a third of the angels in revolt. Defeated, falls nine days through chaos and lands in Hell. Refuses to repent — "Better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven." Builds Pandemonium, undertakes the journey to Eden alone, and corrupts the new creation as his only available revenge. The most vivid character in the poem. The first half is largely his.

Adam
The First Man

Created by God in Book VIII, named the animals, was given the warning about the tree, was given Eve. Argues with Raphael about astronomy. Argues with Eve about whether to garden separately. Eats the fruit not because Satan deceived him — Satan never speaks to him — but because he refuses to live without Eve after she has eaten. The choice is rendered as moral failure and as a recognizable form of love at the same instant. After the Fall he rages, mourns, and finally accepts the future Michael shows him.

Eve
The First Woman

Created from Adam's rib in Book IV. Wakes by a pool and falls in love with her own reflection before God turns her toward Adam. The most psychologically complex character in the poem. Argues for separating to garden faster. Listens to Satan's flattery in the form of the serpent. Eats the fruit alone, then deliberates whether to share it with Adam, fearing both her own death and his replacement by another woman. Milton renders her interior life with a tenderness no previous epic had attempted.

God the Father
The Sovereign

Foreknows everything that happens in the poem, including the Fall, and permits it because virtue under no temptation is not virtue. His speeches are the most difficult passages of the poem — deliberately so. Milton, an unorthodox Christian who eventually came to deny the Trinity, gives the Father the harder theological work of explaining how foreknowledge does not entail predestination. The Son does the easier work of volunteering to redeem mankind. Many readers find the Father cold; Milton thought that was the price of theological precision.

Raphael
The Angel Sent to Warn

Sent by God in Book V to warn Adam of the danger he is in and to make sure Adam cannot later claim ignorance. Tells him the story of Satan's rebellion and the war in heaven across Books V through VII. The poem's internal storyteller — through Raphael, Milton narrates everything that happened in heaven before the action of the poem proper. Eats with Adam in the garden in one of the strangest scenes in any epic, an angel and a man at table together while Eden is still intact.

Sin and Death
Satan's Children

Allegorical figures Satan meets at the gates of Hell on his way out. Sin sprang full-grown from Satan's head when he first conceived rebellion in heaven; Death is the offspring of Satan's incestuous union with Sin. In Book X, once the Fall is accomplished, Sin and Death build a great causeway from the gates of Hell across chaos to Earth so that they can pass freely into the world. Milton's darkest invention. The image of the highway from Hell to Eden is one of the strangest things in the whole poem.
