Politics

335 BCE · by Aristotle

The book in brief

The Politics is Aristotle's treatise on the nature of the political community — what cities are for, what kinds of regimes are possible, what makes a citizen, what role wealth and virtue and the middle class play in public life. It is the second half of his practical philosophy, the partner volume to the Nicomachean Ethics. The Ethics asks what it is for an individual to live well; the Politics argues that the individual good can only be fully realized in a well-ordered polis, and then sets out to describe what a well-ordered polis looks like. Across eight books Aristotle examines the household, citizenship, the typology of regimes, the causes of revolution, and the design of an ideal city.

Aristotle composed the Politics in Athens during his second period at the Lyceum, in the 330s and 320s BCE. The surviving text is almost certainly lecture notes — terse, sometimes uneven, occasionally repeating itself between books. He worked from a survey of 158 Greek constitutions which he and his students compiled, the Athenian Constitution being the only one to survive. The book is divided into eight books, though scholars still argue about their original order. Book I treats the household; Books II–III examine existing constitutions and define citizenship; Books IV–VI catalog regime types and their pathologies; Books VII–VIII describe the ideal city.

It is the founding work of political science as a distinct discipline. Where Plato's Republic asks what a perfect city would look like in principle, Aristotle asks what cities actually are and how their real types behave. The empirical method founded the way political life has been studied ever since. The book is also where the most influential vocabulary of political theory comes from: the human as political animal, the distinction between rule for the common good and rule for private interest, the doctrine of the middle class as the guarantor of stability, the concept of the citizen as one who rules and is ruled in turn.

Why it still matters

Politics Begins From the Empirical

Aristotle and his students examined 158 actual Greek constitutions before writing this book. Where Plato's Republic asks what an ideal city would look like in principle, Aristotle asks what cities are and how their real types behave under stress. He categorizes them, watches them fail, identifies the causes of failure, and reasons about what helps them last. The empirical approach founded political science as a discipline distinct from political philosophy. Every comparative study of regimes — from Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws to modern work on democratic backsliding — is downstream of this method. He was the first to insist that you cannot answer political questions without first looking at how political life actually works.

The Human as Political Animal

The famous claim — that humans are political animals (zōon politikon) — is from this book. Aristotle's argument is that humans cannot become fully human alone. Speech, reason, and moral judgment are capacities that develop only in shared political life, in deliberation with others over what is just and unjust. The polis is not a constraint on individual freedom but the condition of its full realization. The argument is the foundation of the civic-republican tradition that runs through Cicero, Machiavelli, Rousseau, and Hannah Arendt. It is a direct rebuttal of any political theory that begins with the isolated individual contracting into society — Hobbes, Locke, and the libertarian tradition that descends from them.

The Defense of the Middle Class

Aristotle argues that the most stable regime is one with a strong middle class — neither rich enough to be oligarchic nor poor enough to be revolutionary. The argument is empirical: he watched cities tear themselves apart when wealth concentrated at one end or poverty deepened at the other, and he watched cities hold together when neither extreme could dominate. The wealthy refuse to obey because they are unused to being ruled; the poor revolt because they are unused to being rewarded. The middle, he argues, has both habits. The argument has been rediscovered in every century since, and it is recognizable in contemporary debates about whether democracies can survive sustained economic inequality.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The polis as natural.

Aristotle's claim that man is by nature a political animal — that the state is not a convention we invented but the completion of our nature — is one of the most consequential ideas in the Western tradition. It shapes what he thinks citizenship means, what it means to live well, and why political philosophy matters at all.

Why constitutions fall.

Book V is the most modern part of the Politics: a systematic analysis of revolution, factional conflict, and regime decay. Aristotle catalogs the specific grievances, misjudgments, and structural flaws that bring down oligarchies and democracies — and what rulers can do to slow the process. The patterns he identifies recur across 2,400 years of political history.

The middle-class state.

Aristotle argues that the most stable constitution is one in which the middle class is large and dominant — neither extreme poverty nor extreme wealth, because both produce instability and faction. This is not a sentimental claim. It is an empirical observation drawn from 158 constitutions. His argument for polity as the best practical regime rests entirely on it.

Citizenship: who belongs?

Book III opens with a question that has never been fully settled: who is a citizen? Aristotle's answer — someone who shares in deliberation and judgment — seems simple, but its implications are enormous. It excludes slaves, women, mechanics, and resident aliens. Wrestling with where he draws the line, and why, is the only honest way to read his political theory.

Key figures

The Polis
The Natural Community

Not merely a city or a state but the highest form of human association — the community that makes the good life possible. Aristotle argues the polis is natural, not conventional: family becomes village becomes polis as the natural completion of what human beings are. Without it, a human cannot fully be what a human is. The famous line — that man is a political animal — is a claim about biology and ends, not a slogan about civic engagement.

Polity (Politeia)
The Best Practical Constitution

Aristotle's name for the mixed constitution that blends democratic and oligarchic elements around a dominant middle class. Aristocracy and kingship are better in the abstract; polity is the best a real city can realistically achieve. Most of his constitutional recommendations in Books IV–VI are aimed at helping cities approximate it — not by adopting an ideal blueprint but by tilting whichever flawed regime they currently have toward stability.

The Citizen
The Contested Category

Defined in Book III as one who shares in deliberation and judgment — who rules and is ruled in turn. The definition is functional rather than legal: a citizen is someone who actively participates in the political life of the city, not merely a resident or a free person. Aristotle's definition excludes slaves, women, artisans, and resident aliens, and he is candid that different constitutions answer the question differently. The hinge of the whole work.

Revolution (Stasis)
The Decay of States

Book V's subject: factional conflict, coup, and constitutional breakdown. Aristotle analyzes the specific triggers — perceived injustice, extreme inequality, personal ambition, ethnic and economic division, the demagogue who flatters the people, the oligarch who provokes them. He gives examples from dozens of cities. His taxonomy of how democracies slide into demagoguery and oligarchies into faction is still cited by political scientists studying regime collapse in modern states.

The Middle Class
The Stabilizing Force

Aristotle's most practical political insight: constitutions held together by a large, prosperous middle class are the most stable and the least prone to revolution. Extreme wealth produces arrogance and contempt for the rules; extreme poverty produces resentment and willingness to overturn them. The middle is moderate because it has something to lose and something to gain. Polity is the regime that makes the middle class its governing principle, and Aristotle treats this as an empirical finding rather than a hope.

Slavery and the Household
The Controversial Foundation

Book I grounds political life in the household — master and slave, husband and wife, parent and child. Aristotle argues that natural slavery exists: some people, he claims, are fitted by nature to be ruled rather than to rule. The argument is unconvincing on its own terms — he keeps having to admit exceptions and edge cases — and it is the part of the Politics most modern readers will resist most strongly. Confronting it honestly is necessary to understand both the limits and the strengths of everything that follows.
