2 Samuel a guided tour

c. 6th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Former Prophets

A king at the height of his powers stays home from a war, walks on a rooftop one evening, and sees a woman bathing. Everything in the second half of the book follows from that pause.

The book in brief

2 Samuel is the book in which David becomes king, takes Jerusalem, dances before the ark, sleeps with Bathsheba, has Uriah killed, and watches the consequences of that single evening on the rooftop work their way through the rest of his life. It is the most psychologically exact narrative in the Hebrew Bible — a portrait of a king at the height of his powers brought down, slowly, by what he chose to do one spring when the army was away.

The book opens with the elegy for Saul and Jonathan — how are the mighty fallen — and closes with a census and a plague and an altar on a threshing floor that will one day be the site of the temple. Between those two points are the consolidation of the kingdom, the dynastic promise in chapter 7, the Bathsheba affair in chapter 11, Nathan's parable of the ewe lamb in chapter 12, the rape of Tamar, the rebellion of Absalom, the flight from Jerusalem, the death in the oak, and David's collapse at the news. It has been read for two and a half thousand years as the founding charter of Israelite kingship and as one of the strangest royal portraits ever written. A 21st-century reader picks it up for the literary craft of the Court History, for the political education in how a regime consolidates and then begins to crack, and for the question the book refuses to settle: whether God's promise to David survives David's behaviour, or whether the promise is precisely what survives it.

Samuel 11 of 24
2 Samuel 1

David's lament for Saul and Jonathan

Chapter 1 is the hinge between 1 Samuel and 2 Samuel. The news of Saul's death at Gilboa reaches David at Ziklag through an Amalekite who claims, falsely or tactically, to have administered the killing blow. David has the messenger executed for daring to stretch out his hand against the Lord's anointed, then tears his clothes and mourns. The chapter closes with the elegy known as the Song of the Bow, one of the earliest examples of formal lamentation poetry in Hebrew: 'How are the mighty fallen.' The poem mourns both Saul and Jonathan, making no distinction between the king who hunted David and the friend who loved him. For the student of political history, the chapter establishes how carefully David manages the optics of his accession; for the literary reader, the elegy is the entry point into a book that will take grief with complete seriousness.

2 Samuel 2

David anointed at Hebron; civil war begins

Chapter 2 opens the period of civil war that will occupy much of the first half of the book. David, on divine instruction, moves to Hebron in Judah, where the men of Judah anoint him king over the house of Judah. Meanwhile Abner, Saul's general, installs Saul's son Ish-bosheth as king over the northern tribes at Mahanaim. The confrontation at the pool of Gibeon is the first military engagement of the war: twelve men from each side fight in single combat and kill each other simultaneously, then full battle erupts. Joab's brother Asahel pursues Abner and is killed by him; this establishes the blood debt that Joab will settle later with Abner's life. The chapter ends with the armies disengaging and returning to their respective capitals. For the historian, this chapter traces the fracture between Judah and Israel that will resurface after Solomon's death; for the literary reader, it introduces Joab and Abner as the two generals whose rivalry will shape the coming chapters.

2 Samuel 3

Abner defects; Joab kills him

Chapter 3 is one of the most politically complex chapters in the book. Abner and Ish-bosheth quarrel over a concubine of Saul's — an act that in the ancient Near East carried dynastic implications — and Abner opens negotiations with David, offering to bring all Israel over to him. David's condition is the return of his first wife Michal, Saul's daughter; the reunion has no warmth and Michal's husband Paltiel follows weeping behind her until Abner sends him back. Abner arranges the political groundwork, comes to Hebron to finalise terms, and is sent away in peace. Joab, returning from a raid, hears that Abner was there and was sent away, confronts David privately, goes after Abner in secret, and kills him in the gate at Hebron, ostensibly in blood revenge for Asahel but also because Abner as a figure inside David's court would diminish Joab's position. David's response is formal and public: he tears his clothes, follows the bier weeping, fasts, and composes a lament.

2 Samuel 4

Ish-bosheth is assassinated

Chapter 4 closes the civil war with a brief, ugly episode. Ish-bosheth, already weakened by Abner's death, is killed in his bed at midday by two of his own captains, Baanah and Rechab. They bring his severed head to David at Hebron, expecting to be rewarded as the men who have removed his rival. David's response is the same as his response to the Amalekite messenger in chapter 1: he has them executed. The parallel is explicit and deliberate. The narrative inserts a notice about Mephibosheth, Jonathan's lame son, establishing a character who will return in chapter 9, and then closes the episode. The chapter is short — thirteen verses — and its brevity is part of the argument. The end of the northern kingdom comes not in battle but in the treachery of its own servants, and David's refusal to celebrate or accept the fruit of that treachery is the political posture he has maintained throughout the civil war.

2 Samuel 5

David takes Jerusalem and becomes king of all Israel

Chapter 5 is the pivotal chapter of the book's first half. The elders of Israel come to Hebron, acknowledge David as bone of their bone, and anoint him king of all Israel. He is thirty years old and will reign forty years. His first act as king of all Israel is to capture Jerusalem from the Jebusites — a city in the hills that has remained outside Israelite control. The Jebusites taunt David, saying even the blind and lame could hold the city; David takes it and calls it the city of David. The choice of Jerusalem is politically shrewd: it belongs to no tribe, so it offends none. The chapter also records two victories over the Philistines, with David explicitly waiting for divine instruction before each engagement. The battle of the mulberry trees — in which David is told to wait until he hears the sound of going in the tops of the trees, then move, because that is the Lord going before the army — is one of the strangest military orders in the Bible and one of the most memorable.

2 Samuel 6

The ark comes to Jerusalem; Michal's contempt

Chapter 6 is one of the most dramatically charged chapters in 2 Samuel, covering two episodes that frame the ark's arrival in Jerusalem. First, the abortive attempt: the ark is transported on a new cart, Uzzah reaches out to steady it when the oxen stumble, and is struck dead. The incident terrifies David, who leaves the ark at the house of Obed-edom for three months. When the house of Obed-edom prospers, David brings the ark up the rest of the way to Jerusalem with sacrifices and dancing. The second drama occurs in the gate: Michal, Saul's daughter, sees David leaping and dancing before the Lord and despises him in her heart. After the celebrations, she confronts him, accusing him of uncovering himself before the handmaids as one of the vain fellows shamelessly uncovereth himself. David's response is direct and final: the Lord chose him over her father and his house, and he will be yet more vile than this, and the handmaids will honour him. Michal has no children until the day of her death. The chapter is the book's longest study of what separates David from the house of Saul: he is willing to make himself nothing before God in public, and she is not.

2 Samuel 7

Nathan's oracle: the dynastic promise

Chapter 7 is the theological centre of the entire David narrative and one of the most consequential passages in the Hebrew Bible for later Jewish and Christian thought. David is settled in his cedar house and observes to Nathan that he dwells in cedar while the ark dwells in curtains; he implies he wants to build a temple. Nathan initially agrees. That night the word of the Lord corrects Nathan: David is not to build the house; he should say to David that God has been with him through everything and has made him a great name, but it is God who will build David a house. The wordplay on bayit — building, dynasty, household — is at the centre of the oracle. David's son will build the temple; but David's line will endure forever. 'I will be his father, and he shall be my son.' The promise is unconditional in the oracle's terms, and it becomes the seed of every later messianic expectation: the prophetic books' longing for a restored Davidic ruler, the New Testament's insistence on Jesus's Davidic lineage, and the rabbinic concept of the Davidic messiah.

2 Samuel 8

David's wars and administration

Chapter 8 reads differently from everything around it. It is not narrative but summary: a catalogue of David's victories over the Philistines, the Moabites, Hadadezer king of Zobah, the Syrians, the Edomites. He defeats them all, takes their gold and silver, and dedicates the spoils to the Lord. He then establishes a vassal relationship with Toi of Hamath. The chapter closes with an administrative list: Joab over the army, Jehoshaphat as recorder, Zadok and Ahimelech as priests, Seraiah as scribe, Benaiah over the Cherethites and Pelethites, and David's sons as priests. For the literary reader, the chapter is a chapter of consolidation placed before the crisis of chapter 11 to make the fall from grace as vertiginous as possible. The king who subdues every foreign enemy will not be able to control his own household. The administrative apparatus described in this chapter will survive the crisis; the moral authority will not.

2 Samuel 9

David's kindness to Mephibosheth

Chapter 9 is the chapter the Court History uses to establish one key dimension of David's character before the crisis arrives. He asks whether any of the house of Saul remain to whom he can show kindness for Jonathan's sake — recalling the covenant of friendship made in 1 Samuel. A servant named Ziba, from Saul's household, tells him of Mephibosheth, Jonathan's lame son, living at Lo-debar. Mephibosheth, summoned, prostrates himself in fear — the reflex of a man from a dethroned dynasty waiting to hear his fate. David gives him all the land of Saul his grandfather, gives him Ziba and Ziba's fifteen sons and twenty servants to farm the land, and tells him he will eat at David's table. Mephibosheth returns the bow of gratitude — calling himself a dead dog — and the chapter ends with him eating at David's table as one of the king's sons. The chapter is short and functional: it introduces a character who will matter in the Absalom chapters, and it establishes that David's kindness is real. But its placement just before chapters 10-11 carries a structural irony: this is the last thing David does before the Bathsheba afternoon.

2 Samuel 10

The Ammonite war and Syrian allies

Chapter 10 establishes the military context of the Bathsheba episode. The king of Ammon dies and David sends ambassadors to express condolence to his son Hanun. The Ammonite princes, suspicious of the ambassadors' motives, convince Hanun to humiliate them: their beards are shaved on one side, their garments cut off at the buttocks, and they are sent back. David, hearing of their humiliation, tells them to stay at Jericho until their beards have grown. The Ammonites, realising they have stunk themselves in David's sight, hire Aramean mercenaries. Battle is joined at the city gate; Joab commands the main force against the Arameans, his brother Abishai commands the force against the Ammonites. The Arameans flee and the Ammonites retreat into the city. David gathers all Israel and defeats the Arameans in a second engagement at Helam; their king Shobach is killed. All the kings who were servants of Hadadezer, seeing they are beaten before Israel, make peace with Israel. The chapter ends with the notice that the Ammonites remain besieged in their city — which is where the army is when the next chapter opens and David is not.

2 Samuel 11

David and Bathsheba; the death of Uriah

Chapter 11 is the turning point of 2 Samuel and one of the most consequential chapters in biblical narrative. The prose is brisk, almost clinical. David stays in Jerusalem while Joab besieges Rabbah. One evening he rises from his bed, walks on the roof, and sees a woman bathing. She is beautiful. He sends, inquires, is told she is Bathsheba the wife of Uriah the Hittite. He sends for her, lies with her, and sends her back. She conceives. The cover-up is attempted twice: David summons Uriah from the front, tries to get him to sleep at home; Uriah refuses on grounds of honour, citing the ark and the army still in the field. David gets him drunk; Uriah still sleeps at the king's threshold with the servants. David then sends Uriah back carrying his own death warrant, a letter to Joab instructing that Uriah be placed where the fighting is hottest and then abandoned. Joab arranges it; Uriah dies. Bathsheba mourns. David sends for her, takes her as wife. She bears him a son. The narrator inserts a single editorial sentence: 'But the thing that David had done displeased the Lord.' The chapter is the seed of the entire second half.

2 Samuel 12

Nathan's parable; the child dies; Solomon is born

Chapter 12 contains the most famous confrontation in the book. Nathan comes to David and tells the parable of the poor man's ewe lamb: a rich man with many flocks takes a poor man's single cherished lamb to feed a guest. David's anger blazes; he says the rich man deserves to die and must repay fourfold. Nathan's response, three syllables in Hebrew: 'Thou art the man.' The parable works because it strips the powerful man of his defences before he can deploy them. Nathan delivers the divine sentence — the sword will not depart from David's house, what he did in secret will be done in public to him, with his own wives. David confesses simply: 'I have sinned against the Lord.' Nathan says the Lord has put away his sin, he shall not die; but the child will die. The child falls ill for seven days. David fasts and lies on the ground. When the child dies, his servants are afraid to tell him, but David perceives it from their behaviour, gets up, washes, anoints himself, eats. His servants are astonished; he explains that while the child lived he fasted and wept, hoping God might be gracious, but now the child is dead, why should he fast? He will go to the child, but the child will not return to him. David comforts Bathsheba; she bears another son, named Solomon, whom the Lord loves, and the prophet Nathan names him Jedidiah, beloved of the Lord.

2 Samuel 13

Amnon rapes Tamar; Absalom kills Amnon

Chapter 13 is the first fulfilment of Nathan's sentence in chapter 12. Amnon, David's eldest son, becomes obsessed with his half-sister Tamar, the full sister of Absalom. His friend Jonadab devises a scheme to get Tamar alone with him. Amnon pretends illness, requests that Tamar come and make cakes for him; David sends her. Amnon rapes her when they are alone and then, in one of the most brutal reversals in ancient literature, hates her with great hatred so that the hatred wherewith he hated her was greater than the love wherewith he had loved her and throws her out. Tamar tears her virgin's garment, puts ashes on her head, and goes to live in her brother Absalom's house, desolate. David is furious but does nothing. Absalom says nothing to Amnon good or bad, but hates him. Two years later Absalom arranges a sheep-shearing feast, invites all the king's sons, and has his servants kill Amnon when he is drunk with wine. False reports reach David that all his sons are dead; Jonadab corrects the picture. Absalom flees to his maternal grandfather in Geshur for three years. David mourns for Absalom daily. The chapter is the beginning of the domestic catastrophe that will cost David the loyalty of his kingdom.

2 Samuel 14

Joab engineers Absalom's return

Chapter 14 covers the three years in which David longs for Absalom but will not recall him, and Joab's intervention to change that. Joab perceives that the king's heart is toward Absalom and commissions a wise woman from Tekoa to come before the king with a legal case: her two sons fought, one killed the other, and the clan wants to execute the survivor, leaving her with no heir and no one to carry on the family name. David tells her the man shall not be touched and reinforces the order with an oath. The woman then springs the trap: why then has the king thought such a thing against the people of God? The king is as an angel of God, so is Joab behind all this. The parable has done what Nathan's ewe lamb did: it has drawn a verdict from the king before the king knows whose case he is deciding. David grants Absalom's return but will not see his face. Absalom lives in Jerusalem two years without coming before the king; he then sets Joab's field on fire to force an audience, is received by the king, and David kisses him. The chapter's psychology — a king who cannot discipline the living because he cannot stop mourning the dead and the absent — is one of the most acute observations in the book about what personal grief costs a political leader.

2 Samuel 15

Absalom's revolt; David flees Jerusalem

Chapter 15 is the emotional nadir of the first movement of the Absalom crisis. After the reconciliation of chapter 14, Absalom spends four years building a political faction: he acquires a chariot and horses and fifty men to run before him, positions himself at the gate to intercept those coming to the king for judgment, tells each man his cause is good but there is no deputy of the king to hear it, and kisses any man who does him obeisance. 'So Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.' At the appointed time he requests permission to go to Hebron to fulfil a vow; David assents. Two hundred men go with him in ignorance; messengers run through all Israel announcing that Absalom reigns in Hebron; Ahithophel, David's own counsellor, is among the conspirators. Word reaches David and he decides to flee with his household, leaving ten concubines to keep the house. The exodus from Jerusalem is one of the great passages of the book: David barefoot, head covered, weeping, going up the Mount of Olives; the Levites carrying the ark whom David sends back, saying if God brings him back in his favour the ark will be there for him to return to; Hushai the Archite, David's friend, whom David sends back into Jerusalem to frustrate Ahithophel's counsel.

2 Samuel 16

Ziba's accusation; Shimei's curse; Ahithophel's counsel

Chapter 16 covers three encounters as David makes his way out of Jerusalem. First, Ziba, Mephibosheth's servant, meets him with supplies and tells David that Mephibosheth has stayed in Jerusalem expecting to receive the kingdom of Saul back in the chaos of the rebellion. David immediately grants all Mephibosheth's land to Ziba — a hasty judgment made under pressure that the book will revisit in chapter 19. Second, a Benjaminite named Shimei comes out cursing David, throwing stones and dust, calling him a bloody man and a man of Belial, saying the Lord is repaying him for the house of Saul he has usurped. Abishai wants to take Shimei's head; David forbids it, saying perhaps the Lord has bidden him curse, and no one can ask why. It is one of the most remarkable things David says in the book: an acknowledgment that the curse may be grounded in divine justice. Third, Absalom arrives in Jerusalem, takes counsel with Ahithophel, and, at Ahithophel's advice, sleeps with David's ten concubines on a rooftop in public — fulfilling Nathan's prophecy in chapter 12. Ahithophel's counsel is treated as the word of God by both Absalom and David.

2 Samuel 17

Ahithophel's advice rejected; Hushai frustrates the plan

Chapter 17 is the intelligence chapter of the Absalom revolt and turns on a moment of competitive counsel. Ahithophel proposes to Absalom that he pursue David immediately that night with twelve thousand men; David is tired and demoralised, the people with him will flee, and only David need die. The proposal is so sound tactically that the narrative says Absalom and all the elders of Israel are pleased with it. Then Hushai is called in to respond, and his counter-counsel — wait, gather all Israel from Dan to Beersheba like sand on the seashore, lead them yourself and overwhelm David wherever he is found — plays to Absalom's vanity and buys time. Absalom chooses Hushai's plan; the narrative remarks that the Lord had purposed to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel. The intelligence is sent to David through Zadok and Abiathar's sons, though a woman hides them in a well from Absalom's search party before they can deliver it. Ahithophel, seeing that his counsel is not followed, goes home, sets his house in order, and hangs himself. David crosses the Jordan to Mahanaim. Absalom appoints Amasa over his army and crosses the Jordan after David.

2 Samuel 18

The battle in the wood of Ephraim; Absalom's death

Chapter 18 is the emotional climax of 2 Samuel. David arrays his army in three divisions under Joab, Abishai, and Ittai, and wants to go out himself but is persuaded to stay in Mahanaim. Before the army goes out, he charges the commanders in the hearing of all: deal gently for my sake with the young man Absalom. The battle in the wood of Ephraim is a rout of Absalom's forces; the wood devours more people than the sword. Absalom, riding on his mule, passes under the thick branches of a great oak and his head — or his hair — is caught; the mule rides on and he hangs in the air. A man reports this to Joab, who asks why he did not kill him; the man says no reward would be worth killing the king's son after the king's public charge to spare him. Joab takes three darts and thrusts them through Absalom's heart while he still lives. Ten of Joab's armour-bearers strike him. Joab blows the trumpet; the pursuit is stayed. Two runners race to David with the news. David's response to the first runner — is the young man Absalom safe? — and then to the second is the chapter's unforgettable moment: 'O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!'

2 Samuel 19

Joab rebukes David; the return to Jerusalem

Chapter 19 covers David's return to Jerusalem and the political settlements it requires. Joab comes to David weeping for Absalom in his chamber and delivers a speech of remarkable political directness: the king has shamed all his servants today, having made it plain that if Absalom had lived and all his servants had died, it would have been right in his sight. Now arise, go forth and speak comfortably to thy servants, for I swear by the Lord if thou go not forth there will not tarry one man with thee this night. David sits in the gate and the people hear it. The chapter then becomes a series of political settlements as David crosses back over the Jordan: Shimei the curser prostrates himself and begs forgiveness; Abishai wants to execute him; David pardons him. Mephibosheth arrives barefoot and unkempt, claims Ziba deceived David with false accusations, and David splits the land between them — a judgment that satisfies neither. Barzillai the Gileadite, who supplied David at Mahanaim, is offered a place at the Jerusalem court but declines at eighty years old, asking David instead to take his son Chimham. A quarrel erupts between Judah and Israel over who has precedence in accompanying the king back.

2 Samuel 20

Sheba's revolt; Joab kills Amasa

Chapter 20 is the coda to the Absalom crisis. A Benjaminite named Sheba blows a trumpet and calls Israel away from the house of David; the men of Israel follow Sheba, while Judah sticks with the king. David returns to Jerusalem, shuts the ten concubines who had been left in the house away in widowhood for the rest of their lives — fulfilling the logic of Nathan's prophecy and Ahithophel's advice, all the consequences now locked in place — and sends Amasa, whom he had appointed general in place of Joab, to muster Judah in three days. Amasa delays beyond the appointed time; David sends Abishai after Sheba, and Joab goes with him. Joab meets Amasa at the great stone of Gibeon, greets him warmly, takes his beard as if to kiss him, and stabs him in the belly so his bowels fall out. Joab then leads the pursuit of Sheba to Abel Beth-maacah in the far north; the city is besieged. A wise woman inside the city negotiates with Joab; he agrees to withdraw if Sheba is delivered to him. The woman persuades the city, Sheba's head is thrown over the wall, and Joab blows the trumpet. The chapter ends with the restoration of the royal administration.

2 Samuel 21

The Gibeonite famine; David's mighty men

Chapter 21 opens the appendix section of 2 Samuel — chapters 21-24 are not chronological narrative but supplementary materials: two episodes of famine and plague bracketing two poems and two hero lists. The first episode: a famine of three years prompts David to inquire of the Lord, who reveals it is for Saul and his house's massacre of the Gibeonites, a people Israel had sworn not to destroy in the time of Joshua. David asks the Gibeonites what atonement would satisfy them. They ask for seven of Saul's male descendants to be hanged before the Lord at Gibeah. David spares Mephibosheth for Jonathan's sake but hands over two sons of Rizpah (Saul's concubine) and five sons of Merab. They are hanged at the beginning of barley harvest. Rizpah keeps watch over the bodies from barley harvest through the autumn rains, preventing birds and beasts from touching them. David, told of Rizpah's vigil, gathers the bones of Saul and Jonathan from Jabesh-gilead and buries them with the seven executed men in the burial place of Kish. God then answers the prayer for the land. The chapter closes with a series of brief battle notices in which David's mighty men kill Philistine giants.

2 Samuel 22

David's great psalm of deliverance

Chapter 22 is an extended psalm, found also as Psalm 18 in the Psalter, which the editors of 2 Samuel have placed near the end of the book as David's retrospective meditation on his life. It is one of the longest poems in the Hebrew Bible and one of the most intense in its imagery. The Lord is described as rock, fortress, deliverer, buckler, horn of salvation, high tower. The threat from which David is delivered is described as death's cords wrapping round him, floods of ungodly men, the sorrows of hell. God's descent to help him is cosmic in scale: the earth shakes, the foundations of the hills move, smoke goes up from his nostrils, fire out of his mouth, bowing of the heavens, darkness under his feet, a cherub-mounted descent on the winds, arrows and lightning discharged against the enemies. Then, as the poem turns to David's righteousness as the ground of his deliverance — 'the Lord rewarded me according to my righteousness' — the modern reader reared on the Court History finds the assertion almost impossible to square with the David of chapters 11-12. The psalm, whether composed early in David's life or placed here editorially, says what the narrative will not quite say: that God's deliverance is real, that David is innocent enough to assert it, and that the assertion need not be resolved against the story the surrounding chapters have told.

2 Samuel 23

David's last words; the roll of the mighty men

Chapter 23 contains two distinct sections. The first is David's 'last words,' a short poem presented as the final oracle of a dying prophet-king: 'The Spirit of the Lord spake by me, and his word was in my tongue.' The poem speaks of the ruler who rules in the fear of God being as the light of the morning when the sun riseth, even a morning without clouds. It then turns to David's house: his covenant with God is ordered and sure, all his salvation and all his desire. And the sons of Belial shall be as thorns thrust away, consumed with fire. The second section is the list of David's mighty men — the warriors who made him king and kept him there. The list includes Josheb-basshebeth, who killed eight hundred men at one encounter; Eleazar who smote the Philistines until his hand was weary and cleaved to the sword; Shammah who stood his ground in a field of lentils and smote the Philistines when all Israel fled. An anecdote: David longs for a drink from the well of Bethlehem; three of his mighty men break through the Philistine line to bring it; David pours it out as a drink offering to the Lord, refusing to drink what was bought at the risk of these men's lives. Uriah the Hittite appears, last on the list.

2 Samuel 24

The census and the plague; Araunah's threshing floor

Chapter 24 is the second appendix episode bracketing the poems of chapters 22-23, and it closes the book on an unexpected note. The anger of the Lord is kindled against Israel and he incites David to number them — the census. David orders Joab to count all Israel; Joab resists, calling it a sin against the Lord, but obeys. The count is eight hundred thousand in Israel and five hundred thousand in Judah. David's conscience smites him; he confesses his sin and asks the Lord to take away the iniquity. God gives him a choice of three punishments through the prophet Gad: seven years of famine, three months of flight before enemies, or three days of pestilence. David chooses to fall into the hand of the Lord rather than the hand of man, and seventy thousand people die in the plague from Dan to Beersheba. The destroying angel stretches out his hand against Jerusalem; the Lord repents of the evil and says to the angel: it is enough. The angel is at the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite. David sees the angel and pleads that his hand be upon him and his father's house, not upon the people. God tells Gad to tell David to set up an altar on Araunah's threshing floor. David buys the site, builds the altar, offers burnt offerings and peace offerings. The plague is stayed. The threshing floor of Araunah — which is Mount Moriah — will become the site of Solomon's temple.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Bathsheba afternoon — and what it commits the book to argue

Chapter 11 opens with one of the most quietly damning sentences in ancient literature. The king who has fought his way up from a shepherd's field is the king who is, this season, not at the front. From the roof he sees a woman washing herself, and the second half of the book begins.

Nathan's parable — the prophetic confrontation

Chapter 12 contains one of the most famous moments in the Hebrew Bible. The prophet Nathan comes to David and tells him a story. A rich man had many flocks; a poor man had one little ewe lamb. The reversal is one of the cleanest in any literature.

Absalom — and the long shadow

Nathan's sentence — that the sword shall not depart from David's house — is fulfilled not in a single event but over the next ten years, in a sequence of family catastrophes that the book traces with terrible patience.

The dynastic promise — and what survives David

Chapter 7 contains the other pivot of the book, and the one that has shaped Western religious imagination more than any single passage outside the Pentateuch. God refuses to let David build a temple — and promises to build David a house instead.

The realism of the prose

The Court History of David is one of the earliest sustained works of historical prose anywhere. What is remarkable about it, by the standards of any ancient royal narrative, is its freedom from propaganda.

David
King of Israel

The book's subject. Anointed at Hebron after Saul's death, then king of all Israel after the long civil war with the house of Saul. Takes Jerusalem from the Jebusites, brings up the ark with dancing, receives the dynastic promise from Nathan in chapter 7, wins every war he fights. Then, in the spring when kings go out to battle, stays home and sees Bathsheba bathing. The Bathsheba affair, the killing of Uriah, Nathan's confrontation, the death of the child, and the long unwinding through Amnon, Tamar, and Absalom occupy the rest of the book. The strangest royal portrait in ancient literature: a man whose flaws the text refuses to hide and whose election it refuses to take back.

Nathan
Prophet

The court prophet of David's reign, not a wilderness figure but a member of the royal entourage with access to the king. He delivers the dynastic promise in chapter 7 and the parable of the ewe lamb in chapter 12 — the two pivots of the book. Nathan's three Hebrew syllables of indictment, thou art the man, have echoed through every later literature in which an outsider holds a mirror up to a powerful man and refuses to lower it.

Bathsheba
Wife of Uriah, then of David

First introduced in chapter 11 as the woman David sees from the roof. The Hebrew gives her almost no interior life in the chapter that bears her body — she appears, conceives, mourns Uriah, marries the king, bears the child that dies. The narrative will not let the reader settle the question of her agency; the verbs are all David's. She bears David four sons, of whom Solomon is the second to survive. In 1 Kings she will speak, decisively, to secure Solomon's succession.

Absalom
David's son, rebel

David's third son, famous for his beauty and his hair. Avenges the rape of his sister Tamar by having her brother Amnon killed. Flees, returns, spends four years building a faction, has himself proclaimed king at Hebron, drives David from Jerusalem, pursues David's army across the Jordan. Dies caught by his hair in the branches of a great oak in the wood of Ephraim, run through by Joab against the king's orders. David's cry — O my son Absalom — is the book's emotional climax.

Joab
General

David's nephew, commander of his army from the early Hebron years to the end. Brutally effective, politically ruthless, and at least twice the man who does what David is too divided to do himself. He kills Abner, carries out the order to expose Uriah at the wall, kills Absalom in the oak when David has commanded that the young man be dealt with gently, and scolds the king back into the gate at Mahanaim when grief is destroying the army's morale. The book's great study of the realist who serves a king who is not, finally, willing to do what realism requires.

Uriah the Hittite
Soldier, Bathsheba's husband

One of David's mighty men, listed by name in the roll call at the end of the book. A Hittite who has thrown in his lot with Israel and with David's God. The narrator goes out of his way in chapter 11 to make him honourable. Summoned home from the front by a king trying to hide a pregnancy, Uriah refuses to sleep with his wife while the ark and the army are in the field. He carries, unknowing, the letter that orders his death. His shadow in the book is longer than the chapter that contains him.
