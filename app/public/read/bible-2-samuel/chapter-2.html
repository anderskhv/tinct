2 Samuel 2

Two kingdoms form along a fault line that runs through every later crisis in Israelite history: north and south, tribe against tribe.

Summary

David inquires of the Lord whether to go up to any city of Judah and is directed to Hebron. He goes with his two wives, Ahinoam and Abigail, and with his men and their households. The men of Judah anoint him king over the house of Judah at Hebron. His first diplomatic act as king is to send messengers to the men of Jabesh-gilead, praising them for burying Saul and promising them his favour — a careful outreach to a city that had reason to be loyal to Saul. Simultaneously, on the other side of the fault line, Abner the general makes Ish-bosheth, Saul's surviving son, king over Gilead, the Ashurites, Jezreel, Ephraim, Benjamin, and all Israel. The narrative places the two anointings in swift succession without editorial comment, allowing the structural irony to stand alone.

Abner with the servants of Ish-bosheth and Joab with the servants of David meet at the pool of Gibeon and sit on opposite sides of it. Abner proposes a trial by combat: twelve young men from each side. The twenty-four fight, each seizing his opponent by the head and thrusting his sword in his side, and all die together. The place is named Helkath-hazzurim, the field of flints. Full battle erupts, and Abner's forces are beaten back. Asahel, Joab's younger brother, pursues Abner with great speed; Abner warns him off twice, unwilling to kill Joab's brother. When Asahel refuses to turn away, Abner strikes him with the butt of his spear and the blow goes through his body. The armies disengage at sunset, Abner retreating to Mahanaim, Joab returning to Hebron. The chapter closes with the body count: Joab's side lost nineteen men and Asahel; Abner's side lost three hundred and sixty.
