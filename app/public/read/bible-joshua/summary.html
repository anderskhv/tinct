Joshua a guided tour

c. 6th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Former Prophets

Moses is dead. The Jordan is in flood. The people who have wandered for forty years are about to walk into the land that was promised to their great-great-grandfather Abraham. The sixth book of the Hebrew Bible is a crossing, a conquest, an allotment, and a final knife-edged covenant ceremony at Shechem — and one of the most morally difficult books in the canon.

The book in brief

Joshua is the book of the crossing. Moses is dead, the Jordan is in flood at harvest time, and the people who have wandered for forty years are about to walk into the land that was promised to their ancestor Abraham. What follows — twenty-four chapters of conquest, allotment, and a final covenant ceremony at Shechem — has been read for nearly three thousand years as both the fulfilment of the promise and one of the most morally difficult books in the Hebrew canon. Both readings are honest. The book has shaped Jewish, Christian, and modern political imaginations of land, conquest, and possession in ways that have not stopped reverberating.

Joshua reaches its present form during or just after the Babylonian exile in the 6th century BCE, as part of what scholars call the Deuteronomistic History — the long narrative running from Joshua through 2 Kings, edited under the influence of Deuteronomy and reading the eventual loss of the land as the consequence of long covenant failure. The traditional view assigns the book to Joshua himself; the modern critical view sees a composite work drawing on older traditions recast by exilic editors with a coherent theological program. Whether the conquest happened on the scale the book describes is one of the most contested questions in biblical archaeology, with the textual claim of a unified campaign poorly matched by the archaeological record of the late Bronze Age. A 21st-century reader picks Joshua up because the questions it raises about land, promise, conquest, and the moral cost of inheritance have not gone away.

Joshua 1 of 24
Joshua 1

The Commissioning of Joshua

Chapter 1 is a document of transition — the longest and most formal commissioning speech in the Hebrew Bible outside of Deuteronomy. God speaks to Joshua three times using near-identical language: be strong and courageous. The repetition is liturgical, establishing a formula that will echo through the book and into later Jewish and Christian piety. Joshua instructs the officers to prepare the people to cross the Jordan within three days. He reminds the Reubenites, the Gadites, and the half-tribe of Manasseh that their land is already settled east of the Jordan, but their warriors must cross and fight alongside their kin until the land is secured. They respond with an oath of loyalty: as we obeyed Moses in all things, so will we obey you — only be strong and courageous. The chapter ends on this pledge. The book has set up its central character, its central command, and its central question: whether the people can sustain the obedience they are promising.

Joshua 2

Rahab and the Spies

Chapter 2 is the book's first narrative set-piece and one of its finest. Joshua sends two men quietly to spy out Jericho. They go to the house of a woman named Rahab — the text calls her a zonah, a prostitute or innkeeper — built into the city wall. The king of Jericho hears that Israelite spies have come and sends messengers demanding she hand them over. She has hidden them under stalks of flax on her roof and sends the messengers chasing toward the Jordan. Then she goes back up to the spies and tells them what the city knows: the Lord has given you this land. Our hearts have melted. We have heard what the Lord did at the Red Sea and to the two Amorite kings. The Lord your God is God in heaven above and on earth below. She asks them to spare her and her family in exchange. They agree, and give her a scarlet cord to tie in her window as the signal. She lets them down by a rope through the window. They return to Joshua with their report: the Lord has given all the land into our hands; moreover all the inhabitants melt in fear because of us.

Joshua 3

The Crossing of the Jordan

Chapter 3 is the crossing — the founding act of the new condition. Joshua commands the officers to tell the people: when you see the ark of the covenant of the Lord your God being carried by the Levitical priests, then you shall set out. The people are to maintain a distance of about a thousand cubits between themselves and the ark, so that they can see it clearly and know which way to go. God tells Joshua that day he will begin to exalt him in the sight of all Israel, so they will know that as he was with Moses, so he will be with Joshua. The key instruction: the priests who bear the ark of the Lord shall stand still in the Jordan. The water from upstream will stop and pile up in a heap. When the feet of the priests touch the water — the Jordan is in flood, the text emphasises — the water stops at a city called Adam. The priests stand in the middle of the dry river bed. All Israel passes over. The moment explicitly reprises the Red Sea crossing and completes the pattern begun in Exodus. Moses split water; Joshua stops water. The generation that came out of Egypt died in the wilderness; their children are now walking on dry ground into the land.

Joshua 4

Twelve Stones at Gilgal

Chapter 4 is the liturgical completion of the crossing. While the priests still stand in the middle of the Jordan with the ark, God commands Joshua to choose twelve men — one from each tribe — to carry twelve stones from where the priests stand to the camp at Gilgal. Joshua does this and sets the stones up as a memorial. He explains their purpose precisely: when in time to come your children ask their parents, what do these stones mean, then you shall let your children know that Israel crossed over the Jordan here on dry ground. The monument is engineered to produce a catechetical question. It is a teaching device built of river stones, designed to last for generations and to ensure the event is narrated forward. The crossing concludes: the priests come up out of the Jordan carrying the ark, and as soon as their feet touch the dry ground, the waters of the Jordan return to their place and overflow all its banks as before. The river closes. The forty years of wandering have ended. The book also notes that this news went out to all the kings of the Amorites and all the kings of the Canaanites, and their hearts melted, confirming what Rahab told the spies.

Joshua 5

Circumcision and Passover at Gilgal

Chapter 5 is entirely ritual: no fighting, no military movement, only the performance of two founding rites that have been in abeyance during the wilderness years. First, God commands Joshua to make flint knives and circumcise the Israelites again — the generation born in the wilderness had not been circumcised. This is done at a place called Gibeath-haaraloth, Hill of the Foreskins, and the people stay in camp until they have healed. God declares: today I have rolled away from you the disgrace of Egypt. The place is called Gilgal, from the Hebrew root for rolling. Then the people keep the Passover on the fourteenth day of the month — the first Passover in the land. The day after, they eat some of the produce of the land, and the manna stops. The manna that began in Exodus 16 and sustained the people for forty years ceases the moment there is something else to eat. Then Joshua, near Jericho, looks up and sees a man standing before him with a drawn sword in his hand. Joshua asks him: are you for us or for our enemies? Neither, the man says — I am the commander of the army of the Lord. Joshua falls on his face. The chapter ends with Joshua receiving instructions: remove your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy.

Joshua 6

The Fall of Jericho

Chapter 6 is the most famous chapter in Joshua and one of the most distinctive siege accounts in ancient literature. God's instructions to Joshua are ceremonial rather than military: march around the city once a day for six days, with seven priests bearing seven rams' horn trumpets before the ark. On the seventh day, march around the city seven times, with the priests blowing the trumpets. When they give a long blast on the trumpet, the people shall shout, and the wall will fall down flat. There is no undermining, no battering, no starvation. The city is taken by procession, trumpet, and sound. The text then records the execution of the instructions exactly, with careful attention to the seven priests, the seven days, and the seven circuits on the seventh day. The people shout, the walls fall, and the army goes straight in. The city is put under the herem — the entire population and all animals are destroyed by the sword. Only Rahab and her household, gathered inside her house as agreed, are saved. The silver and gold and bronze and iron go to the treasury of the house of the Lord. The city is burned. Joshua curses anyone who rebuilds Jericho: at the cost of his firstborn shall he lay its foundation, at the cost of his youngest son shall he set up its gates.

Joshua 7

Achan's Sin and the Defeat at Ai

Chapter 7 is the dark hinge of the conquest narrative. The army of Israel, still camped at Gilgal after Jericho, sends scouts to the small town of Ai. The scouts return confident: only about three thousand men need to go up, the town is small. Three thousand go up. They are routed. Thirty-six are killed; the rest flee. The hearts of the people melt. Joshua tears his clothes and falls on his face before the ark. His lament — why did you bring us over the Jordan only to destroy us? — echoes the Israelites' complaints against Moses in the wilderness. God's response is sharp: Israel has sinned. Someone has taken from the devoted things. Until the offender is found and the devoted things destroyed, God will not be with them. A lengthy selection process follows — tribe by tribe, clan by clan, household by household — until the lot falls on a man named Achan. He confesses: he saw a beautiful Babylonian robe, silver, and gold at Jericho, coveted them, and buried them under his tent. The plunder is dug up and verified. Achan, his household, his animals, and all his possessions are taken to the Valley of Achor and stoned and burned. Over them they raise a great heap of stones.

Joshua 8

The Taking of Ai and the Altar at Ebal

Chapter 8 is in two halves. The first is the second attempt on Ai, now conducted by military stratagem rather than divine miracle. God tells Joshua: take the whole army up; I have given into your hand the king of Ai, his people, his city, and his land. You shall do to Ai and its king as you did to Jericho and its king — only the livestock and spoil you may take as booty. Joshua positions thirty thousand soldiers in ambush behind the city at night. He and the main force advance on Ai in the morning. The king of Ai sees them, attacks, and pursues Joshua's force, which feigns retreat. The ambush springs. The ambush force enters the city and sets it on fire. The men of Ai look back and see the smoke. They are caught between two forces. All twelve thousand inhabitants are killed. Joshua hangs the king on a tree until evening, then takes him down and throws his body at the city gate. The second half of the chapter is unexpected: Joshua builds an altar on Mount Ebal, makes offerings, writes a copy of the law of Moses on stones, and reads aloud the whole law — every word of the blessing and the curse — to the entire assembly including women, children, and resident aliens.

Joshua 9

The Gibeonite Deception

Chapter 9 is the book's great cautionary tale about decision-making. All the kings west of the Jordan form a coalition to fight Joshua; but the inhabitants of Gibeon take a different approach. They load their donkeys with worn-out sacks, worn-out patched wineskins, worn-out patched sandals on their feet, and worn-out clothing. They take dry crumbly bread. They go to Joshua at Gilgal and say: we are your servants and have come from a far country. Make a treaty with us. Joshua and the leaders of Israel are suspicious: perhaps you live among us — we cannot make a treaty with you. The Gibeonites produce the evidence: our wineskins were new when we filled them, look how cracked they are; our bread was still hot when we packed it, look how dry it is. The leaders of Israel, the text notes pointedly, did not ask the counsel of the Lord. They make a covenant with the Gibeonites and swear an oath in the name of the Lord. Three days later they discover that the Gibeonites live nearby — in Gibeon, Chephirah, Beeroth, and Kiriath-jearim. They cannot destroy them because of the oath. The congregation murmurs against the leaders. The Gibeonites are made hewers of wood and drawers of water for the assembly and for the altar of God.

Joshua 10

The Long Day at Aijalon

Chapter 10 is the climax of the southern campaign and contains the most spectacular miracle in the book. Five Amorite kings, alarmed by Gibeon's treaty with Israel, send a coalition army against Gibeon. The Gibeonites send to Joshua: do not abandon your servants. Joshua marches all night from Gilgal and arrives at Gibeon by surprise. God says: I have given them into your hand; not one of them shall stand before you. The Lord throws the coalition into a panic; Israel strikes them down. As they flee down the Beth-horon road, the Lord throws large hailstones on them — more die from hailstones than from Israelite swords. Then Joshua speaks to the Lord in front of all Israel: Sun, stand still at Gibeon, and Moon, in the valley of Aijalon. The sun stands still and the moon stops until the nation took vengeance on its enemies. This is recorded, the text says, in the Book of Jashar. The sun stopped in midheaven and did not hurry to set for about a whole day. Then the five kings, who have hidden in a cave at Makkedah, are brought out. Joshua kills them and hangs their bodies on five trees until evening. The chapter ends with Joshua taking a series of southern cities in rapid succession: Makkedah, Libnah, Lachish, Eglon, Hebron, Debir.

Joshua 11

The Northern Campaign

Chapter 11 is the final major military chapter of the conquest narrative. King Jabin of Hazor assembles a northern coalition of kings, a vast army with horses and chariots as many as the sand on the seashore. God tells Joshua: do not be afraid because of them; by this time tomorrow I will hand over all of them slain to Israel. Do to their horses what I tell you: hamstring them. Burn their chariots with fire. Joshua attacks them suddenly at the waters of Merom and God hands them over. He hamstrings the horses and burns the chariots — an instruction that prevents Israel from using the captured military technology and maintains their dependence on God rather than on superior weapons. Hazor, the greatest of all those kingdoms, is burned. Its king is killed. Joshua takes all the territory from the south to the far north. The chapter closes with a summary of the entire conquest: Joshua took all that land, the hill country and all the Negeb and all the land of Goshen and the lowland and the Arabah and the hill country of Israel and its lowland. He struck all their kings and put them to death. Joshua made war a long time with all those kings. There was not a city that made peace with the Israelites except the Hivites of Gibeon. The Lord's doing is insisted upon: it was the Lord who hardened their hearts to come against Israel in battle so that they would be utterly destroyed.

Joshua 12

List of Defeated Kings

Chapter 12 is a king-list, one of the ancient Near Eastern documentary genres that the Deuteronomistic historian incorporates into the narrative. It begins with the two kings defeated east of the Jordan during Moses's leadership — Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan, whose defeat is narrated in Numbers — then continues with thirty-one kings defeated west of the Jordan under Joshua, named one by one. The format is stripped to the minimum: the king of Jericho, one; the king of Ai, one; the king of Jerusalem, one; the king of Hebron, one. Each is a single entry in a count that climbs to thirty-one. The chapter has no narrative momentum; it is a list. Its function is archival and rhetorical. After the rapid narrative of the conquest, the list slows everything down and makes the reader attend to each name. Thirty-one kingdoms. Thirty-one kings. The repetitive one, one, one is the drumbeat of completion. It is also the boundary between the conquest narrative and the allotment narrative: chapters 1-11 took the land; chapters 13-21 will distribute it. Chapter 12 is the archive that stands between the two.

Joshua 13

Territories Yet to Be Taken

Chapter 13 opens the second major section of the book — the allotment of the land — with a striking note: Joshua is old and advanced in years, and God tells him there is still very much land to be possessed. The territories not yet taken are listed: Philistia, Geshur, the Avvim, the Geshurites and Maachathites. This is an honest acknowledgement that the conquest narrative's claim of completeness in chapters 11-12 was idealised or forward-looking. Chapter 13 then records the allotments given east of the Jordan, which Moses had already distributed: Reuben received the Arnon plateau, Gad received the Gilead, and the half-tribe of Manasseh received Bashan. The Levites received no territorial inheritance among them, because the Lord God of Israel is their inheritance — a principle stated twice and elaborated in subsequent chapters. The chapter ends with the note that Moses had not given anything to the half-tribe of Manasseh east of the Jordan, because the Lord God of Israel is their inheritance. The allotment section begins, in other words, not with the new gifts west of the Jordan but with the confirmation of what Moses had already given, placing Joshua's work in continuity with his predecessor's.

Joshua 14

Caleb Claims Hebron

Chapter 14 introduces the western allotments, which are distributed by lot before God at Shiloh, with Eleazar the priest and Joshua presiding. Then comes the personal appeal that is the chapter's real focus: Caleb son of Jephunneh comes to Joshua at Gilgal and reminds him of the promise Moses had made when Caleb returned from spying out the land forty-five years earlier. Moses promised: the land on which your foot has trodden shall be an inheritance for you and your children forever, because you have wholeheartedly followed the Lord. Caleb presses his claim with a remarkable statement of physical vigour: I am still as strong today as I was the day Moses sent me; my strength now is as my strength was then, for war and for going and coming. He asks for Hebron — the hill country where the sons of Anak live, the giants who had terrified the first generation of spies. Give me this hill country of which the Lord spoke on that day. Joshua blesses him and gives him Hebron as his inheritance. Then comes the explanation of why: Hebron became the inheritance of Caleb because he wholeheartedly followed the Lord God of Israel. The chapter ends there. Caleb, eighty-five years old, takes the most daunting territory on the map.

Joshua 15

Judah's Allotment

Chapter 15 is the longest and most geographically detailed allotment chapter in the book, tracing Judah's territory with a precision that scholars have used to identify specific sites in the southern Levant. The southern boundary runs from the southern end of the Dead Sea along the Wilderness of Zin to Kadesh-barnea and out to the Mediterranean. The eastern boundary is the Dead Sea. The northern boundary is a detailed line through specific towns, valleys, and hills running near Jerusalem. Then the cities list: 36 cities in the Negeb, 14 in the lowland, 11 in the hill country, 6 in the wilderness. The chapter interrupts itself for the story of Caleb driving out the three Anakites from Hebron, then offering his daughter Achsah in marriage to whoever takes Kiriath-sepher. His nephew Othniel takes it and wins Achsah. She asks her father for a blessing and receives springs of water along with her land. The chapter closes with a notable admission: the Jebusites who lived in Jerusalem, the people of Judah could not drive out. The Jebusites live with the people of Judah in Jerusalem to this day. Jerusalem, the city that will become the capital under David, is not in Israelite hands.

Joshua 16

Ephraim's Allotment

Chapter 16 is brief. The allotment of Joseph — the two tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh, descended from Joseph's two sons — begins here and extends through chapter 17. Chapter 16 covers Ephraim's territory specifically: the boundaries run from the Jordan near Jericho up through the hill country of Beth-el and west to Gezer and the Mediterranean. The chapter notes that the towns belonging to Ephraim are mixed into the territory of Manasseh, establishing that the tribal boundaries were not neat and complete but overlapping. It closes with a troubling admission: Ephraim did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer; instead the Canaanites have lived within Ephraim to this day but have become forced labourers. This pattern — Canaanite inhabitants remaining within tribal territories, subjected to forced labour rather than destroyed — will repeat across the allotment chapters and will be the central problem with which the book of Judges opens. The contrast between the herem mandate and the actual practice of subjugation and coexistence is not hidden by the text but acknowledged repeatedly.

Joshua 17

Manasseh's Allotment

Chapter 17 deals with the allotment of the half-tribe of Manasseh west of the Jordan. It begins with a special provision: the daughters of Zelophehad — Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah — come before Eleazar and Joshua and invoke the ruling Moses made in Numbers 27: their father had no sons and died in the wilderness for his own sin, not for joining Korah's rebellion, so they are to receive an inheritance among their father's brothers. They receive an inheritance among their father's brothers. This is one of the few moments in the allotment chapters where the disposition of land to women is recorded. Then the ten clans of Manasseh receive their territory. But again the admission: Manasseh could not take possession of Megiddo, Taanach, Dor, and Ibleam — the Canaanites had the will to live in that land. When Israel grew strong, they put the Canaanites to forced labour, but did not utterly drive them out. The people of Joseph then come to Joshua and complain that they have been given only one lot while they are a great people. Joshua tells them: go up to the forest, and clear ground there for yourselves in the land of the Perizzites and the Rephaim, since the hill country of Ephraim is too narrow for you.

Joshua 18

The Survey at Shiloh

Chapter 18 marks a shift in procedure. The whole assembly of the Israelites gathered at Shiloh and set up the tent of meeting there. Seven tribes had not yet received their inheritance. Joshua rebukes them: how long will you be slack about going in and taking possession of the land that the Lord, the God of your ancestors, has given you? He institutes a formal survey: choose three men from each tribe and I will send them out to go up and down the land, write a description of it in seven divisions, and bring it here to me. They go, survey the land, and record it in a book with seven divisions. They come back to Joshua at Shiloh. Joshua casts lots for them before the Lord at Shiloh, and distributes the land to the Israelites according to their divisions. Then the allotment of Benjamin is given in detail: the northern boundary, the eastern boundary running down to the Dead Sea, the western boundary, the southern boundary running up to Jerusalem. Benjamin's territory sits between Judah to the south and Ephraim to the north, and includes Jericho and Bethel.

Joshua 19

Allotments for Six Remaining Tribes

Chapter 19 completes the tribal allotments. Simeon receives territory within Judah's portion — Judah's allotment was too large and Simeon's is carved from it. Zebulun receives a northern territory centred on the Jezreel valley. Issachar receives the land around the valley east of Megiddo. Asher's territory runs along the Mediterranean coast in the north. Naphtali receives the territory around the Sea of Galilee. Dan is allotted a coastal territory in the west, but then a complication: the Amorites press the people of Dan back into the hill country and do not allow them to come down into the plain. Dan then migrates north, conquers the city of Leshem, and renames it Dan. The conquest narrative has acknowledged from chapter 13 that not all the land was taken; now the allotment section records that not all the allotments were even occupied. After all the tribes have received their portions, the Israelites give Joshua an inheritance in their midst at his own request: Timnath-serah in the hill country of Ephraim, the town he asked for. He rebuilt the town and settled in it. The land divisions close here. The chapter ends: these are the inheritances that Eleazar the priest and Joshua son of Nun and the heads of the ancestral houses distributed by lot to the Israelites at Shiloh before the Lord.

Joshua 20

The Cities of Refuge

Chapter 20 is brief but legally significant. God commands Joshua to designate the cities of refuge that Moses had spoken of in Deuteronomy: cities to which any Israelite or resident alien who kills a person without intent and without prior enmity can flee. The city's elders must admit the manslayer and give him a place to live. If the avenger of blood pursues him, the elders must not hand him over, because he struck down the neighbour without prior enmity. The manslayer shall remain in the city until he has stood in judgement before the congregation and until the death of the high priest who is in office at the time. Then he may return to his own town. Six cities are designated: Kedesh in Galilee, Shechem in Ephraim, Kiriath-arba (Hebron) in Judah — these three west of the Jordan — and Bezer in Reuben, Ramoth in Gilead, and Golan in Bashan — three east of the Jordan. The six cities are distributed spatially so that no part of the settled territory is far from one of them. The chapter closes with the declaration that these cities were designated for all the Israelites and for the strangers residing among them — the institution applies to non-Israelites as well.

Joshua 21

Levitical Cities and the Promise Fulfilled

Chapter 21 completes the allotment by distributing the Levitical cities. The heads of the Levitical families come to Eleazar and Joshua at Shiloh and invoke the command of Moses: the Lord commanded that cities be given to us to live in, with their pasture lands for our livestock. Lots are cast for the Levites, and they receive forty-eight cities in total, distributed through all the tribal territories. The Kohathites receive thirteen cities from Judah, Simeon, and Benjamin; the remaining Kohathites receive ten cities from Ephraim, Dan, and the half-tribe of Manasseh. The Gershonites receive thirteen cities from Issachar, Asher, Naphtali, and the half-tribe of Manasseh in Bashan. The Merarites receive twelve cities from Reuben, Gad, and Zebulun. The Levites are thus distributed spatially through the entire territory of Israel, providing priestly and teaching presence in every region without any one tribe bearing the entire burden of their support. The chapter then closes with what may be the theological climax of the allotment section: not one of all the good promises that the Lord had made to the house of Israel had failed; all came to pass.

Joshua 22

The Altar by the Jordan

Chapter 22 is the most narratively suspenseful chapter in the allotment section. The Reubenites, Gadites, and the half-tribe of Manasseh are released by Joshua to return to their territories east of the Jordan with a benediction and the instruction to keep the commandments of Moses. When they reach the Jordan, they build there a large, imposing altar. The rest of Israel hears of this and the whole assembly of Israel gathers at Shiloh to march against them. First they send a delegation: Phinehas the priest and ten chiefs. Why have you broken faith with the God of Israel? Did not Achan's sin bring guilt on all Israel? The eastern tribes explain: we did not build it for burnt offerings or for sacrifices. We built it out of fear — fear that in time to come your children might say to our children, what have you to do with the Lord, the God of Israel? The Lord has made the Jordan a boundary between us and you. We built this altar as a witness between us that we serve the Lord. The delegation is satisfied. The altar is named: a witness between us that the Lord is God. War is averted.

Joshua 23

Joshua's First Farewell

Chapter 23 is the first of Joshua's two farewell addresses, addressed to the elders, heads, judges, and officers of Israel rather than to the whole assembly. Joshua is old and advanced in years. He begins with what the Lord has done: you have seen all that the Lord your God has done to all these nations for your sake, for it is the Lord your God who has fought for you. He then turns to the future: Be very steadfast to keep and do all that is written in the book of the law of Moses, turning aside from it neither to the right nor to the left. Do not mix with these nations remaining among you. Do not invoke the names of their gods. The Lord your God will push them back before you and drive them out, just as he promised, until no one is left. But then the warning: if you turn back and attach yourselves to the survivors of these nations remaining among you, and intermarry with them, know for certain that the Lord your God will not continue to drive out these nations before you. They shall be a snare and a trap for you, a scourge on your sides and thorns in your eyes. Then the gravest warning of all: just as all the good things that the Lord your God promised concerning you have been fulfilled for you, so the Lord will bring upon you all the bad things, until he has destroyed you from this good land that the Lord your God has given you.

Joshua 24

Covenant at Shechem — Choose This Day

Chapter 24 is the literary and theological climax of the book. Joshua gathers all the tribes of Israel to Shechem. He speaks first as God's voice — a recitation of history from Abraham's call beyond the Euphrates through the patriarchs, the bondage in Egypt, the plagues, the Exodus, the wilderness, and the gift of the land. Then the demand: revere the Lord and serve him in sincerity and faithfulness. Put away the gods your ancestors served. Choose this day whom you will serve — whether the gods from beyond the river, the gods of Egypt, or the gods of the Amorites in whose land you now live. As for me and my house, we will serve the Lord. The people respond: we will serve the Lord. Joshua presses them with a startling counter: you cannot serve the Lord, he is a holy God, a jealous God, he will not forgive your transgressions or your sins. They insist. He makes a covenant with them, records it in the book of the law of God, and sets up a great stone under the oak in the sanctuary of the Lord as a witness. Then Joshua dismisses the people to their inheritances. He dies at one hundred and ten and is buried at Timnath-serah. The bones of Joseph, carried from Egypt, are buried at Shechem. Eleazar dies and is buried in the hill country of Ephraim.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

Crossing the Jordan

The Jordan is in flood at harvest time. When the priests carrying the ark step into the water, the flow is cut off and the people cross on dry ground. The parallel with the Red Sea crossing is exact and intentional — the book is announcing that the deliverance from Egypt is now being completed.

Jericho and the walls

The instructions God gives Joshua for the siege of Jericho are unlike any military instructions in ancient narrative. No battering ram. No tunnel. A procession with an ark and seven horns, and on the seventh day the wall falls down flat. The strangest siege in literature is also the book's purest theological statement: the land is being given, not taken.

Achan and the hidden plunder

After Jericho's miraculous fall, thirty-six Israelites are killed in flight from tiny Ai. The reason: one man named Achan hid a Babylonian mantle, two hundred shekels of silver, and a wedge of gold under his tent. The scene is the book's argument about collective integrity — one hidden disobedience contaminates the whole.

The allotment of the land

Almost half of Joshua is taken up with the apportionment of the land among the twelve tribes. To a modern reader, these chapters can look like a tax assessor's report. They are also the theological climax: the promise to Abraham, six hundred years earlier — to your offspring I will give this land — is being made specific, boundary stone by boundary stone.

The covenant at Shechem

Chapter 24 is the literary climax of the book. Joshua gathers all Israel at Shechem, recites the whole history of God's acts from Abraham to the crossing, and issues a challenge that has been carved into church entrances for two thousand years: as for me and my house, we will serve the Lord.

Key figures

The 5 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Joshua
General and successor to Moses

Son of Nun, of the tribe of Ephraim, Moses's assistant since the wilderness years, and one of only two of the original twelve spies who came back from Canaan urging the people to go in. Commissioned at the start of the book to lead the people across the Jordan. Strong, decisive, faithful, and — as the book carefully shows — entirely dependent on the Lord for the victories that follow. He dies at one hundred and ten and is buried in the territory the people gave him. The book's final note about him is that Israel served the Lord all the days of Joshua, and all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua and had known all the work that the Lord had done for Israel.

Rahab
Woman of Jericho who sheltered the spies

A woman of Jericho who runs an inn or a brothel — the text uses the word zonah, which can mean either — built into the city wall. The two Israelite spies come to her house. She hides them under stalks of flax on her roof, misdirects the king's men, and extracts a promise of safety for her family when the city falls. The scarlet cord in her window marks her household for preservation. She becomes part of Israel, and according to the genealogy in Matthew's Gospel is an ancestor of David and of Jesus. Hebrews 11 includes her among the heroes of faith. Among all the figures in Joshua, she is the most quietly elevated.

Caleb
The faithful spy who waited forty-five years

Son of Jephunneh, of the tribe of Judah, the other of the original twelve spies who came back from Canaan urging the people to go in. Forty-five years later, in chapter 14, he comes to Joshua at Gilgal and reminds him of the promise Moses made: I am still as strong this day as I was on the day Moses sent me, my strength now is as my strength was then for war and for going and coming. Give me this hill country. Joshua blesses him and gives him Hebron. He drives out the three sons of Anak who lived there. Caleb is the embodiment in the book of the promise long deferred and still kept.

Achan
The man whose hidden plunder stalled the conquest

Of the tribe of Judah, the man whose hidden plunder from Jericho brings disaster on the army at Ai and through whom the book stages its insistence on the integrity of the herem. After the rout at Ai, the lot falls on him; he confesses; the plunder is dug up from under his tent; he and his household are stoned and burned in the Valley of Achor — the valley of trouble. The book uses his story as a parable of how a single hidden disobedience can stall the whole project of inheritance. His name becomes proverbial in later Hebrew literature.

Eleazar
High priest alongside Joshua

Son of Aaron, the high priest who serves alongside Joshua throughout the book. He carries the breastplate with the urim and thummim, the sacred lots used to determine God's will at decisive moments. He stands with Joshua at the apportionment of the land, which is done by lot before the Lord at Shiloh. He is the priestly counterpart to Joshua's military and civil leadership. He dies in the closing chapter, just after Joshua, and is buried in the hill country of Ephraim.
