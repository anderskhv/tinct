Hebrews 1

The most admired sentence in New Testament Greek opens a systematic argument: the Son is greater than every previous mediator, beginning with the angels.

Summary

Hebrews 1 is both prologue and thesis. The opening four verses establish the contrast that will structure the entire epistle: the revelation given through the prophets was genuine but partial and sequential; the revelation given in the Son is final and definitive. The elevated christology of these verses — the Son as the one through whom God created the world, as the radiance of the divine glory, as the one who upholds all things by his powerful word — places Hebrews alongside John's prologue as one of the two highest christological statements in the New Testament. The uniqueness of Hebrews is that this high christology is immediately connected to the once-for-all sacrifice: after making purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high.

Verses 5–14 open the comparison with angels through a catena of scriptural citations. Why begin with angels? Because in second-temple Jewish tradition, angels were the mediators of the law at Sinai. The author wants to argue not merely that Christ is exalted but that his mediation is categorically greater than the mediation that produced the Mosaic covenant. The seven citations — drawn from Psalms 2, 97, 104, 45, 102, and 110, and from 2 Samuel 7 — accumulate into a portrait of the Son as the one whom God begot, before whom the angels worship, whose throne is forever, and whom God addressed as 'sit at my right hand.' The angels, by contrast, are winds and flames of fire, sent on assignment. The structural contrast is stark and deliberate.

Hebrews 1Chapter 1 opens with the thesis of the entire epistle compressed into a single long sentence: at many times and in many ways God spoke to the fathers through the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken through his Son — heir of all things, radiance of God's glory, exact imprint of his nature, sustainer of the universe.
  2. Hebrews 2The chapter opens with the first of Hebrews' warning passages (2:1–4): pay careful attention, lest we drift away — for if the word declared by angels proved binding, what escape is there for those who neglect so great a salvation? The argument then pivots to the incarnation.
  3. Hebrews 3Chapter 3 introduces the comparison with Moses.
  4. Hebrews 4Chapter 4 develops the concept of rest from the Psalm 95 citation.
  5. Hebrews 5Chapter 5 defines the qualifications for high priesthood in the Hebrew tradition — chosen from among human beings, able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward since he himself is beset with weakness, offering sacrifice for his own sins as well as the people's — and then applies the pattern to Christ.
  6. Hebrews 6Chapter 6 opens with a brief exhortation to move past elementary Christian doctrine — repentance, faith, baptism, laying on of hands, resurrection, eternal judgment — and go on to maturity.
  7. Hebrews 7Chapter 7 is the Melchizedek chapter, the exegetical centrepiece of Hebrews.
  8. Hebrews 8Chapter 8 opens with what the author calls the main point of what he has been saying: we have a high priest who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven, a minister in the holy places, in the true tent that the Lord set up, not man.
  9. Hebrews 9Chapter 9 describes the arrangement of the earthly tabernacle — the lampstand and the table, the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies with the ark — in precise detail, then explains its limitations.
  10. Hebrews 10Chapter 10 opens by extending the argument of chapter 9 to the entire sacrificial system: the law has only a shadow of the good things to come, not the true form itself.
  11. Hebrews 11Chapter 11 is the great chapter on faith and one of the most carefully constructed rhetorical set-pieces in the Bible.
  12. Hebrews 12Chapter 12 turns the long argument toward the readers.
  13. Hebrews 13Chapter 13 shifts register from the sustained theological argument to practical closing instructions.
