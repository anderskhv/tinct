Hebrews a guided tour

c. 60-90 CE · New Testament · Homily/Epistle

The most polished Greek in the New Testament and the most carefully constructed argument. A thirteen-chapter sustained meditation on the proposition that what God has done in Christ is greater than every previous mediation — angels, Moses, the priesthood, the temple. It ends with the great cloud of witnesses watching the present generation run the same race.

The book in brief

Hebrews is the most stylistically distinct book of the New Testament. Its Greek is the most polished, its rhetorical structure the most carefully built, its argumentation the most sustained. It is also the most anonymous. The traditional attribution to Paul, which prevailed in the Latin West from the fourth century onward, is rejected by virtually all modern scholarship — the vocabulary is not Pauline, the style is not Pauline, and the writer places himself in the second generation of Christians, which is hard to reconcile with Paul's own emphatic claim to be an apostle by direct call from the risen Christ. Origen, writing in the early third century, summarized the situation with a phrase that has been quoted ever since: as for who actually wrote the letter, only God knows.

The book is a sustained argument, sometimes called a sermon or a treatise, in which the writer makes the case that Jesus Christ is greater than every previous mediation between God and humanity. The structure runs through a series of comparisons: greater than the angels, greater than Moses, greater than the Levitical high priesthood, greater than the temple sacrifices. Chapter 11 is the great catalogue of faith — Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Moses — running through the entire history of Israel as a story of those who endured by trust in what they could not yet see. Chapter 12 turns to the readers: since you are surrounded by such a great cloud of witnesses, run with endurance the race that is set before you.

Hebrews 1 of 13
Hebrews 1

The Son above the angels

Chapter 1 opens with the thesis of the entire epistle compressed into a single long sentence: at many times and in many ways God spoke to the fathers through the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken through his Son — heir of all things, radiance of God's glory, exact imprint of his nature, sustainer of the universe. After making purification for sins, the Son sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high. The comparison with angels follows in verses 5–14, worked out through a chain of citations from Psalms, 2 Samuel, and Deuteronomy: to which angel did God ever say 'You are my Son'? To which angel did he say 'Sit at my right hand'? The angels are ministering spirits; the Son is the heir. The author is establishing a structural pattern — previous mediation versus definitive mediation — that will repeat through every subsequent comparison in the book.

Hebrews 2

The pioneer made perfect through suffering

The chapter opens with the first of Hebrews' warning passages (2:1–4): pay careful attention, lest we drift away — for if the word declared by angels proved binding, what escape is there for those who neglect so great a salvation? The argument then pivots to the incarnation. The Son who is greater than the angels nonetheless became lower than the angels for a time, taking on flesh and blood so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone. The theological logic is precise: because the children share in flesh and blood, the pioneer of salvation had to share in that condition — and be made perfect through suffering — in order to deliver those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery. The chapter ends with the image that will be central to the epistle: Christ as the merciful and faithful high priest who has himself been tempted and therefore can help those who are being tempted.

Hebrews 3

Greater than Moses, a hardened heart

Chapter 3 introduces the comparison with Moses. The author is careful to honour Moses — he was faithful in all God's house — but the structural argument is precise: a builder has more honour than the house built. Moses was a servant; the Son is the builder who has authority over the house. The warning that follows draws on Psalm 95: today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion, in the day of testing in the wilderness, where your fathers put me to the test. The wilderness generation — who saw God's works for forty years and yet whose hearts went astray — becomes the negative example. They failed to enter the rest because of unbelief. The chapter ends with an urgent hortatory refrain: exhort one another every day, as long as it is called today, so that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin.

Hebrews 4

The rest that remains for God's people

Chapter 4 develops the concept of rest from the Psalm 95 citation. The author makes a careful argument: Joshua brought the people into Canaan, but Psalm 95 was written after Joshua's time, which means the rest spoken of in the psalm is not merely geographical — there must be a rest that remains. The promise of entering God's rest, the author argues, has been available from the foundation of the world (God rested on the seventh day) but requires faith to enter. The famous parenthesis in verses 12–13 — the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and spirit, discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart — grounds the urgency in the nature of the word itself. The chapter closes with the two most pastorally central verses in the epistle: we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, and since he has been tempted as we are yet without sin, let us with confidence draw near to the throne of grace.

Hebrews 5

Qualified from human frailty, Melchizedek introduced

Chapter 5 defines the qualifications for high priesthood in the Hebrew tradition — chosen from among human beings, able to deal gently with the ignorant and wayward since he himself is beset with weakness, offering sacrifice for his own sins as well as the people's — and then applies the pattern to Christ. But Christ's appointment was not by Levitical lineage; it came through the divine declaration of Psalm 110 ('you are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek') and through what he suffered. In the days of his flesh, the author writes, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications with loud cries and tears to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence. Although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered. The chapter ends with a sharp pastoral rebuke: the readers, who ought by now to be teachers, still need milk rather than solid food. They are not ready for the Melchizedek material — but the author is about to give it to them anyway.

Hebrews 6

The most severe warning, and God's oath

Chapter 6 opens with a brief exhortation to move past elementary Christian doctrine — repentance, faith, baptism, laying on of hands, resurrection, eternal judgment — and go on to maturity. Then comes the most severe of the warning passages: it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift and shared in the Holy Spirit, and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm. The passage has generated more theological debate than almost any other in the New Testament. The author then immediately shifts register: though we speak in this way, yet in your case, beloved, we feel sure of better things — things that belong to salvation. The chapter closes with a meditation on God's promise and oath to Abraham, and on the hope set before us as an anchor for the soul, firm and secure, entering the inner place behind the curtain where Jesus has gone as forerunner.

Hebrews 7

Melchizedek and the eternal priesthood

Chapter 7 is the Melchizedek chapter, the exegetical centrepiece of Hebrews. The author works through the Genesis 14 account systematically: Melchizedek blessed Abraham, Abraham paid him a tithe, his name means 'king of righteousness' and 'king of peace,' and Genesis gives no genealogy — without father or mother or genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, he remains a priest continually. The argument then moves to Psalm 110: the messianic king is declared a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek, not after the order of Aaron. This means a change of priesthood, and a change of priesthood requires a change of law. The Levitical priesthood brought nothing to perfection — if it had, why would another priest arise, of a different order? But Christ holds his priesthood permanently, because he continues forever, and consequently is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them. The chapter ends with the description of the priest who has been appointed not by the law of a fleshly commandment but by the power of an indestructible life.

Hebrews 8

The better covenant in a heavenly sanctuary

Chapter 8 opens with what the author calls the main point of what he has been saying: we have a high priest who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven, a minister in the holy places, in the true tent that the Lord set up, not man. The earthly sanctuary was a copy and shadow of the heavenly one — Moses was told to make everything according to the pattern shown him on the mountain. If the first covenant had been faultless, there would have been no need for a second. The author then cites Jeremiah 31 at length — the longest Old Testament citation in the New Testament. Jeremiah announces a new covenant with the house of Israel, one in which God will put his laws into their minds and write them on their hearts, will be their God as they will be his people, and will remember their sins no more. The citation does most of the work: if God announced through Jeremiah that a new covenant was coming, the old was already in the process of becoming obsolete — and what is obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away.

Hebrews 9

The once-for-all sacrifice and eternal redemption

Chapter 9 describes the arrangement of the earthly tabernacle — the lampstand and the table, the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies with the ark — in precise detail, then explains its limitations. Under the old order, the priests entered the first section regularly, but only the high priest entered the inner sanctuary, and only once a year, with blood he offered for himself and for the unintentional sins of the people. The Holy Spirit was indicating through this that the way into the holy places was not yet open. But when Christ appeared as high priest, he entered once and for all through the greater and more perfect tent, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, and secured eternal redemption. The argument extends: if the blood of goats and bulls sanctified for the purification of the flesh, how much more will the blood of Christ purify the conscience from dead works. The chapter ends with the first of two comparisons to death and inheritance: just as it is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment, so Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, to save those who are eagerly waiting for him.

Hebrews 10

The law a shadow, the sacrifice complete

Chapter 10 opens by extending the argument of chapter 9 to the entire sacrificial system: the law has only a shadow of the good things to come, not the true form itself. The annual repetition of the sacrifices is itself the evidence of their inadequacy — if they had actually taken away sins, they would have ceased. By contrast, when Christ offered one sacrifice for sins forever, he sat down at the right hand of God. The Jeremiah 31 promise is cited again: I will remember their sins no more — and where there is forgiveness, there is no longer any offering for sin. The warning passage that follows (10:26–31) is severe: if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, but a fearful expectation of judgment. It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God. The chapter ends with an extended exhortation: draw near with a true heart, hold fast the confession without wavering, stir up one another to love and good works, and recall the former days when you endured hard struggles with suffering — for you have need of endurance.

Hebrews 11

The great cloud of witnesses

Chapter 11 is the great chapter on faith and one of the most carefully constructed rhetorical set-pieces in the Bible. The definition in verse 1 — faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen — is the closest the New Testament comes to a formal definition of faith. The anaphoric by-faith refrain then sweeps through the history of Israel: Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham (who went out not knowing where he was going, and who was as good as dead yet fathered a son), Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses (who chose to be mistreated with the people of God rather than to enjoy the fleeting pleasures of sin), the exodus generation at the Red Sea and at Jericho, Rahab, and then the great sweep of the named and unnamed — Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, the prophets, those who stopped the mouths of lions and those who were sawn in two, of whom the world was not worthy. The chapter closes with an observation the author has been building toward: all these, though commended for their faith, did not receive what was promised — since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect.

Hebrews 12

Run the race, endure the discipline

Chapter 12 turns the long argument toward the readers. The image of the cloud of witnesses gathered from chapter 11 becomes the audience before whom the present generation runs its race. Look to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of faith, who for the joy set before him endured the cross, despising the shame. The meditation on discipline follows: the author cites Proverbs 3 — the Lord disciplines those he loves — and argues that the readers' suffering is evidence of their adoption as sons, not of divine abandonment. Endure suffering as discipline; God is treating you as sons. The negative example is Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal and afterward, though he sought the blessing with tears, could not regain it. The chapter ends with the last warning passage: the readers have come not to the smoking mountain of Sinai that could be touched, but to the heavenly Jerusalem, to the city of the living God, to innumerable angels, to the assembly of the firstborn, to the spirits of righteous men made perfect. See that you do not refuse him who is speaking — for if those did not escape who refused him who warned on earth, much less will we escape if we reject him who warns from heaven. Our God is a consuming fire.

Hebrews 13

Closing exhortations and benediction

Chapter 13 shifts register from the sustained theological argument to practical closing instructions. Let brotherly love continue. Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers — for thereby some have entertained angels unawares. Remember those in prison. Let marriage be held in honor and the marriage bed undefiled. Keep your life free from love of money; be content with what you have, for he has said 'I will never leave you nor forsake you.' Remember your leaders who spoke the word of God to you. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever. Do not be led away by diverse and strange teachings. The author refers to having no lasting city here but seeking the city that is to come. Those who come from Italy send you greetings — the phrase that has prompted discussion of the epistle's destination for two thousand years. The epistle closes with the great benediction: may the God of peace who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, the great shepherd of the sheep, by the blood of the eternal covenant, equip you with everything good that you may do his will. And a final amen.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Son's Superiority

The opening four verses of Hebrews are one of the most carefully constructed sentences in the New Testament, and they state the thesis of the entire book. The Son is the radiance of the glory of God — and greater than every previous mediator in a series of comparisons that runs for seven chapters.

Melchizedek

The figure of Melchizedek appears in only two places in the Hebrew Bible — Genesis 14 and Psalm 110. Hebrews makes him the structural figure of the central christological argument of the book. The reasoning in chapter 7 is one of the most ingenious pieces of biblical exegesis in early Christian literature.

The Cloud of Witnesses

Chapter 11 of Hebrews is the great chapter on faith, and it is one of the most carefully constructed catalogues in the Bible — the entire history of Israel retold as a sequence of figures who endured by trust in what they could not yet see.

The Warning Passages

Hebrews contains some of the most severe warnings in the New Testament — four concentrated passages that share a common structure: if those who disobeyed the previous revelation faced consequences, how much more those who turn away from the greater?

The Great High Priest

Hebrews is the only New Testament book that develops the figure of Christ as high priest systematically. The development is one of the most influential pieces of theology the early church produced — and its most pastorally important moment is a single verse at 4:15.

Key figures

The 4 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

The Son
Great High Priest, heir of all things

The central figure of the book and the subject of its sustained christological argument. The Son in Hebrews is the heir of all things, through whom also God created the world; the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature; the one who upholds the universe by the word of his power. He is also the great high priest who has been tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin — a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek, who has offered once and for all the sacrifice of himself and entered the heavenly sanctuary not made with hands.

Moses
Faithful servant in God's house

The figure used in chapter 3 as the comparison through which the Son's superior status is established. The writer honours Moses — he was faithful in all God's house as a servant — while establishing that the Son is greater as the builder of the house itself. Moses is also one of the great figures in the catalogue of faith in chapter 11, commended not just for his office but for his faith.

Melchizedek
Priest of Salem, king of righteousness

The mysterious figure from Genesis 14 — king of Salem and priest of God Most High — and the structural figure of the central christological argument of the book. Significant because he predates the Levitical priesthood; because his genealogy is not given in Genesis; because Abraham paid him a tithe; and because Psalm 110 addresses the messianic king as a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. From these four observations the writer builds the entire argument that the christological priesthood is of a different and superior kind from the Levitical.

Abraham
Patriarch, recipient of the promise

The anchor figure of the epistle's argument about faith and promise. Abraham paid tithes to Melchizedek (chapter 7), received and trusted God's oath (chapter 6), and appears in the great catalogue of chapter 11 with Sarah as the two central figures of the faith that endures before receiving the promise.
