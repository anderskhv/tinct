Lamentations — chapter by chapter

All 5 poems, in order — from the widow weeping in the night to the community's unanswered petition.

Lamentations is short — readable in under an hour — and the order matters. Each poem is a different formal experiment in how to hold grief: different voice, different acrostic structure (or its deliberate absence), different theological angle. Read them in sequence and the cumulative effect, ending in chapter 5's open question, is the book's argument.

The city speaks. Jerusalem personified as a weeping widow asks the passerby to stop and witness her grief. The acrostic form holds the elegy from aleph to taw.

Lamentations 2

God is named as the agent of the destruction. Chapter 2 refuses all evasion: the temple is gone, the prophets are silent, the elders sit in the dust, and God himself has bent his bow.

Lamentations 3

The triple acrostic at the heart of the book. The man who has seen affliction deliberately recalls one conviction in the depths of suffering: God's mercies are new every morning.

Lamentations 4

The most concrete chapter in the book. The famine of the siege is recorded without abstraction — the children, the nursing mothers, the darkened gold, the broken city from the inside.

Lamentations 5
