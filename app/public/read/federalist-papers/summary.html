The Federalist Papers

1788 CE · by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison & John Jay

The book in brief

The Federalist Papers argue, paper by paper, that the proposed United States Constitution should be ratified — and that the alternative, the existing Articles of Confederation, will collapse into either disunion, foreign domination, or domestic tyranny. The 85 essays explain the document clause by clause, defend its specific design choices against the objections of the Anti-Federalists, and develop a working theory of how a large commercial republic can remain free. The papers are at once practical journalism and high political philosophy: addressed to New York newspaper readers in 1787, they have outlasted every other document of the ratification debate as the canonical commentary on American constitutional design.

The essays appeared in New York papers between October 1787 and August 1788 under the pseudonym Publius — the name of one of the founders of the Roman Republic. Hamilton wrote 51, Madison 29, Jay 5; the authorship of a few papers is still disputed. The three men were unequal partners. Jay fell ill early. Hamilton drove the project. Madison, who had spent the summer in Philadelphia helping draft the Constitution itself, supplied the most theoretically ambitious arguments — including Federalist 10 on faction and Federalist 51 on checks and balances. The Anti-Federalists, who opposed ratification under their own pseudonyms (Brutus, Cato, the Federal Farmer), supplied the opposition the papers were written against.

It is the founding generation's clearest account of what they were trying to build and why. The papers are unusually candid about trade-offs — Madison openly admits the design assumes that men are not angels — and unusually concrete about failure modes. The Supreme Court still cites them. Almost every later constitutional theory, in the United States and abroad, has had to come to terms with the arguments laid out in these eighty-five essays.

Why it still matters

The Constitution Explained by Its Authors

Hamilton and Madison helped write the Constitution. The Federalist is what they meant by it — the trade-offs, the worries, the design choices, the failure modes the structure was built to avoid. No other founding document in modern political history is accompanied by such candid contemporary commentary by its authors, written in the heat of the ratification fight and intended to persuade actual voters who could see the arguments being made by both sides in the same papers. The Federalist is why American constitutional argument is unusually grounded: when a justice or a scholar wants to know what a clause was meant to do, they have eighty-five essays of contemporaneous explanation written by the men who put the clause there. Other founding moments — the Russian, the French, the Chinese — produced revolutionary slogans and post-hoc justifications written by people who had not been in the room. The American founding produced the Federalist Papers, and the document and the commentary have been read together ever since.

Federalist 10 — The Theory of the Extended Republic

Madison's argument in Federalist 10 is the single most influential piece of American political analysis ever written. Classical political theory, from Aristotle through Montesquieu, had assumed republics must be small to remain virtuous, and that a large territorial state was incompatible with self-government. Madison inverts the assumption: a large republic is more stable than a small one, precisely because it contains many factions, none of which can dominate the rest. The remedy for the diseases of faction is more faction. The argument has shaped democratic theory ever since: it underlies pluralist political science, federalist design in dozens of countries that copied the American model, and most modern thinking about why diverse societies can be more stable than homogeneous ones. Madison wrote it in eight days, for a newspaper, at the age of thirty-six. It has held up better than almost any political theory of comparable ambition.

A Country Talking Itself Into Existence

The papers were written in real time as the Constitution was being debated. They are not retrospective explanation; they are persuasion under pressure, addressed to undecided New York voters whose ratification was anything but assured. The reader sees the founding generation arguing — with their fellow citizens, with each other, with the British tradition they were partly leaving behind, with the classical authors they all had read. The voice changes from paper to paper. The arguments correct themselves across the sequence. Hamilton and Madison address each other's positions without admitting that they are doing so. Reading the papers in order is the closest thing in political literature to watching a country think itself into being. Almost every later constitutional founding — in France, in Germany, in postwar Japan, in post-apartheid South Africa — has been measured against the standard set by these eighty-five essays.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The faction problem.

Madison's great insight in No. 10: faction is not a disease you can cure without killing the patient. Free people will always organize around interests, and those interests will always conflict with the public good. The solution is not moral reform — it is designing a republic large enough that no single faction can dominate. Read the papers asking: does the Constitution actually solve the faction problem, or only manage it?

Checks and balances.

No. 51 contains one of the most honest sentences in political philosophy: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." Madison is not appealing to the virtue of statesmen — he is designing a system that works even when everyone in it is self-interested. Each branch of government is given both the motive and the means to resist the others. Read asking how that design has held up across two centuries of strain.

Judicial review.

Hamilton's argument in No. 78 is that an independent judiciary holding the Constitution above ordinary law is not a threat to democracy but its necessary guardian. Courts are the "least dangerous branch" — they hold neither sword nor purse. Read No. 78 alongside the history of Marbury v. Madison and ask whether Hamilton's argument was right, or whether he underestimated what the judiciary would become.

Voice and authorship.

Three very different men — Hamilton (combative, nationalist, former artillery captain), Madison (bookish, analytical, Virginia planter), Jay (diplomatic, reserved, eventual Chief Justice) — wrote as one "Publius." They agreed on ratification but disagreed about almost everything else. Hamilton wanted a strong executive; Madison feared concentrated power. The scholarly dispute over which of them wrote certain papers lasted 200 years. Read asking what unified "Publius," and what it costs an argument to suppress the disagreements inside it.

Key figures

Alexander Hamilton
THE URGENT NATIONALIST

Wrote 51 of the 85 papers, the most prolific contributor and the most combative. A former artillery captain and Washington's wartime aide-de-camp, born out of wedlock on Nevis, Hamilton believed a strong national government was not a threat to liberty but its precondition. His papers on the executive (Nos. 67–77) and the judiciary (Nos. 78–83) are the most influential. He would become the first Secretary of the Treasury and be killed in a duel with Aaron Burr in 1804.

James Madison
THE ANALYTICAL ARCHITECT

Wrote roughly 29 papers, including the two most famous — No. 10 on faction and No. 51 on checks and balances. A Virginia planter and constitutional scholar who had spent the years before Philadelphia studying every historical republic he could read about, Madison believed the Constitution's structure was the answer to problems no moral appeal could solve. He would become the fourth President and is the figure most scholars credit with the document's design.

John Jay
THE DIPLOMATIC VOICE

Wrote only five papers — Nos. 2–5 and No. 64 — before falling seriously ill. A diplomat and jurist who had negotiated the Treaty of Paris that ended the Revolutionary War, Jay focused on foreign policy: why a united nation is less likely to provoke wars, less likely to be manipulated by foreign powers, and more likely to honor its treaties. He would become the first Chief Justice of the Supreme Court.

Publius
THE UNIFIED VOICE

The pseudonym Hamilton, Madison, and Jay shared, taken from Publius Valerius Publicola, one of the founders of the Roman Republic after the expulsion of the Tarquins. The choice was strategic: a single authoritative voice rather than three men with visible disagreements. The fiction held well enough that scholars spent two centuries debating who wrote which papers. Publius argues for ratification with a consistency the three real authors did not always share in private.

The Anti-Federalists
THE OPPOSITION

The writers and politicians the Federalist Papers were written against — Brutus (probably Robert Yates), Cato (probably George Clinton), Patrick Henry, the Federal Farmer. Their charge was that the proposed Constitution would consolidate power, swallow the states, produce an aristocratic Senate, and eventually generate the very tyranny the Revolution had been fought to escape. Their pressure forced the Bill of Rights into existence. They lost the ratification fight and, in many respects, won the long argument.

Federalist No. 10
THE FOUNDATIONAL ESSAY

Madison's argument that the cure for faction is not suppression but multiplication — that a large republic, with many competing interests, is more stable than a small one because no single faction can capture the whole. The essay inverts the classical assumption that republics must be small to remain virtuous. It is the most consequential ten pages in American political theory and the one paper of the eighty-five that almost every later constitutional argument has had to engage.
