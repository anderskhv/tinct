No. 22 — The Same Subject Continued: Other Defects of the Present Confederation (Hamilton)

Hamilton adds four more defects: no commercial regulation, a military system that produces auctioning for soldiers, a one-state-one-vote rule that lets Rhode Island veto Virginia, and no independent judiciary to enforce federal law.

Summary

Hamilton's opening addition to the defect catalogue is the absence of a federal commercial regulatory power — acknowledged by all parties, he says, so he merely notes it for completeness before moving to more contested ground. The practical consequences of the absence are visible everywhere: thirteen different state commercial policies, competing and conflicting, preventing the uniform treatment of foreign trade that would give America negotiating leverage and preventing the free internal commerce that would allow specialisation and growth across the union as a whole.

The military recruitment defect is more technical but equally damaging. Under the Confederation's requisition system, the federal government asks each state to provide a quota of troops. States then compete with each other to attract soldiers by offering higher bounties — a process Hamilton describes as an auction for men. The auction drives up the price of military service, depletes state treasuries without producing adequate forces, and rewards the richer states at the expense of the poorer. The men recruited under competitive bidding are not necessarily the best soldiers; they are the men who held out for the highest price. A national government with the direct power to raise and pay its own army would recruit more efficiently and more equitably.

The equal state vote is Hamilton's most pointed critique in this paper. One state, one vote — regardless of population — means that the nine smallest states, containing perhaps a third of the total American population, can block any measure opposed by the ten largest. It means that Rhode Island, with a few thousand voters, has the same weight in Confederation deliberations as Virginia, with hundreds of thousands. Hamilton calls this a fundamental injustice and an invitation to minority obstruction. He closes with the judicial defect: without a federal judiciary, federal law is interpreted by thirteen different state court systems, each applying its own precedents and procedures, producing thirteen different versions of what the law of the nation means. A supreme federal court is not a luxury; it is the essential mechanism for giving federal law a uniform meaning.
