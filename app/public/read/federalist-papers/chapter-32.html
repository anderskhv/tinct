No. 32 — The Same Subject Continued (Concerning the General Power of Taxation) (Hamilton)

Hamilton shows that federal and state governments can tax the same citizens at once without either destroying the other — concurrent authority, not consolidation.

Summary

Hamilton begins by separating three cases in which federal authority might be exclusive. First, where the Constitution expressly grants an exclusive power to the Union. Second, where it grants a power to the Union and expressly forbids the states from exercising it. Third, where it grants a power to the Union of a nature incompatible with state exercise. Import duties fall into the third category: the Constitution bars states from laying imposts without congressional consent, and the practicalities of regulating foreign commerce require uniformity. But these are the exceptions, not the rule.

For the great mass of taxation — taxes on land, on the incomes of persons, on consumption of domestic goods — no such exclusivity applies. The states retain full authority. The federal government acquires concurrent authority. Both can tax; neither can annul the other's levy. Hamilton argues that the political mechanism preventing abuse is simple: if the federal government taxes too heavily, the states can tax less, and vice versa. Citizens feel both levies simultaneously and will elect representatives to correct excess in either direction. The system is self-regulating through ordinary electoral accountability.

The paper's structural purpose is to rebut the consolidation argument — the claim that federal taxation necessarily swallows state revenue authority. Hamilton's analysis of concurrent authority shows that the proposed Constitution is not a national government in the centralizing sense but a layered system in which two sovereigns, each directly elected by the same citizens, share a common tax base while pursuing different objects. The innovation was unprecedented in the history of federal systems and is what makes the American constitutional design conceptually distinct from both the Confederation it replaced and the unitary states of Europe.
