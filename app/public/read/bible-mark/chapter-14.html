Mark 14

A woman anoints Jesus at Bethany for his burial; the Last Supper is eaten; Gethsemane watches end in sleep; and at the arrest the disciples desert him and flee.

Summary

Mark 14 opens two days before the Passover. The chief priests and scribes are seeking to arrest Jesus by stealth, not during the feast lest there be an uproar. The anointing (14:3-9) interrupts the conspiracy narrative with a flash of extravagance: a woman, unnamed in Mark, pours a jar of pure nard over Jesus' head — nard worth three hundred denarii, nearly a year's wages. Some present are angry at the waste; Jesus defends her: she has done a beautiful thing and anointed my body beforehand for burial. Wherever the gospel is proclaimed, what she has done will be told in memory of her. The contrast with Judas going out immediately to betray him (14:10-11) is Mark's juxtaposition. The Passover preparation scene (14:12-16) repeats the structural pattern of 11:1-6: two disciples sent ahead, a man with a water jar as sign, the question to the owner, the upper room shown. The Last Supper's institution (14:22-25) is spare in Mark: take — this is my body; this is my blood of the covenant; truly I say to you, I will not drink again of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new in the kingdom of God.

Gethsemane (14:32-42) is Jesus' prayer and the disciples' sleep. He takes Peter, James, and John — the same three as the transfiguration — and becomes greatly distressed and troubled. He says to them my soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here and watch. He goes forward, falls on the ground, and prays: Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will. He returns and finds them sleeping. Three times: the same three times as Peter's denials coming in the chapter's second half, the same three as the crowing of the cock. The arrest (14:43-52) is rapid: a crowd with swords and clubs, Judas's kiss as signal, the ear cut off, the fleeing. The enigmatic young man who flees naked (14:51-52) is found only in Mark; he has been read as a literary signature, as a symbol of the abandoned community, and as a figure of the burial linen. The Sanhedrin trial (14:53-65) gives the high priest's direct question and Jesus' direct answer — ego eimi, I am — after witnesses have failed to agree. The answer from Daniel 7 and Psalm 110 is the first time in Mark's gospel that Jesus accepts a christological title in his own voice without qualification or a charge of silence.
