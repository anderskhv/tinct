Mark 13

On the Mount of Olives, Jesus answers the disciples' question about when the temple will fall with a discourse on tribulation, false prophets, cosmic signs, and the Son of Man coming in clouds.

Summary

Mark 13 is a dense apocalyptic discourse, the only one of substantial length in the gospel. Its trigger is the disciples' admiration of the temple (13:1-4), which Jesus answers with the prediction that not one stone will be left on another. The private question from Peter, James, John, and Andrew — tell us when, and what will be the sign? — invites the discourse. The opening section (13:5-13) warns against false messiahs and interprets wars, earthquakes, and famines as the beginning of birth pains. The persecution of disciples is predicted with precision: arrest, beatings in synagogues, trials before governors and kings, family betrayal. The promise is that the gospel must first be proclaimed to all nations, and that whoever endures to the end will be saved. The abomination of desolation (13:14) is a phrase from Daniel 9 and probably refers in its historical register to the desecration of the temple — by Antiochus IV in 167 BCE as Daniel's original context, and by the Roman armies in 70 CE as the gospel's contemporary reference.

The cosmic signs (13:24-27) — the sun darkened, the moon giving no light, stars falling, powers of heaven shaken — are drawn from Isaiah 13, 34, Ezekiel 32, and Joel 2, and describe in prophetic language the collapse of the present order. The coming of the Son of Man in clouds with great power and glory is from Daniel 7:13. The fig tree parable (13:28-31) argues from the observable — when its branches are tender, summer is near — that when you see these things happening you know the end is near. The unknowability section (13:32-37) is the most theologically striking: no one knows, not the angels, not the Son, only the Father. The statement that the Son does not know the hour has generated enormous theological discussion — it is the strongest statement of the kenotic limitation of the incarnate Son in the gospel. The discourse ends with a parable about a man who goes on a journey, leaving his servants in charge, each with their work, and commanding the doorkeeper to stay awake.

  10. Mark 10Chapter 10 is the last chapter before the entry into Jerusalem and covers the final stretch of the road from Galilee south through Judea and Jericho.
  11. Mark 11Chapter 11 marks the gospel's second movement: after eight chapters of Galilean ministry and two of the journey south, the entry into Jerusalem initiates the events that will end at the cross.
