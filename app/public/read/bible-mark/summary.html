Mark a guided tour

c. 65–75 CE · New Testament · Earliest Gospel

The shortest, fastest gospel. The first chapter baptizes Jesus, drives him into the wilderness, and fills Capernaum with exorcisms and healings — all in forty verses. The favourite word is and immediately. The gospel ends at an empty tomb with the women fleeing in terror, having said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid.

The book in brief

Mark is the shortest, fastest, and earliest of the four gospels — sixteen chapters, probably written in the late 60s or early 70s CE, around the time of the First Jewish Revolt and the destruction of the Jerusalem temple. The traditional ascription to John Mark, companion of Peter, is second-century; the author is anonymous. What is not in dispute is the book's particular register: rougher Greek than the other gospels, a favourite word in kai euthus (and immediately), no genealogy and no infancy narrative. The gospel opens at the Jordan River and does not slow down until Gethsemane.

The gospel is structured around the confession at Caesarea Philippi in chapter 8: before it, the question of who Jesus is stays open; after it, the gospel turns toward Jerusalem and the cross. Chapters 14 and 15 — the Passion narrative — are the only place the book slows. The centurion at the foot of the cross confesses truly this man was the Son of God — the only human to do so in the whole gospel. The oldest manuscripts end at 16:8, with the women fleeing the empty tomb in terror, having said nothing to anyone. The longer ending (16:9–20) is a second-century addition; most critical editions bracket it.

Mark 1 of 16
Mark 1

The gospel begins at full speed

Chapter 1 is the gospel's argument in miniature. There is no genealogy, no infancy narrative, no settling-in. The opening line — the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God — is a title, and within three verses John the Baptist is at the Jordan in camel's hair. Jesus arrives, is baptized, and the heavens tear open: the Spirit descends like a dove and the voice names him as beloved Son. Immediately — kai euthus, the gospel's favourite word — the Spirit drives him into the wilderness for forty days. When he returns, John has been arrested and the ministry begins: the time is fulfilled, the kingdom of God is at hand, repent and believe in the gospel. Two pairs of brothers are called from their nets and their mending without delay. At Capernaum, Jesus teaches with authority, not as the scribes; an unclean spirit in the synagogue cries out who you are and is silenced and expelled. Peter's mother-in-law is healed; that evening the whole city gathers at the door; a leper is cleansed and charged to tell no one. The chapter counts eleven uses of kai euthus. The reader has been dropped into the middle of an event already in motion.

Mark 2

Forgiveness, controversy, and the new wine

Chapter 2 introduces the pattern that will run through chapters 2 and 3: five controversy stories in which Jesus acts or allows an action and the scribes or Pharisees object, and Jesus answers with a counter-question or a pronouncement. The paralytic lowered through the roof is healed with the words your sins are forgiven; the scribes call this blasphemy in their hearts, and Jesus' response — which is easier, to say your sins are forgiven, or to say rise and walk? — frames the healing as proof of the Son of Man's authority to forgive. The call of Levi the tax collector follows; Jesus eats with tax collectors and sinners, and the scribes object; he answers that he has come not for the righteous but for sinners. A question about fasting gives him the parables of the bridegroom, the unshrunk cloth, and the new wine in old wineskins: the new thing he is doing cannot be contained in the old forms. The chapter ends with the grain controversy on the Sabbath — the disciples plucking grain as they walk, the Pharisees objecting — and Jesus' pronouncement that the Sabbath was made for people, not people for the Sabbath, and that the Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath.

Mark 3

Plotting, the Twelve, and the Beelzebul controversy

Chapter 3 marks the transition from controversy to conspiracy: after the healing of the withered hand on the Sabbath, the Pharisees immediately hold counsel with the Herodians against Jesus, how to destroy him. The pattern of the messianic secret continues: unclean spirits fall before Jesus and cry out you are the Son of God and he strictly orders them not to make him known. He withdraws to the mountain and appoints twelve — the inner circle named here for the first time, with their two-by-two pairings and the nicknames he gives (Boanerges, sons of thunder, for James and John). A crowd so large that he cannot even eat prompts his family to come and seize him, saying he is beside himself. The scribes from Jerusalem say he has Beelzebul and casts out demons by the prince of demons; Jesus answers with the parable of the divided house — a house divided against itself cannot stand — and argues that no one can enter a strong man's house without first binding the strong man. The chapter ends with the only unforgivable sin in Mark — the blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, whoever ascribes the Spirit's works to an unclean spirit — and with Jesus' mother and brothers outside asking for him; his answer, looking at the circle of disciples around him: whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother.

Mark 4

Parables of the kingdom and the calmed sea

Chapter 4 is the only extended teaching section in Mark's gospel before the apocalyptic discourse of chapter 13, and its placement — between the Beelzebul controversy and the series of healings that follows — is deliberate. Jesus sits in a boat with the crowd on the shore and teaches in parables: the sower, whose seed falls on four kinds of ground; the seed growing secretly, which grows by itself while the farmer sleeps; the mustard seed, smallest of all seeds and largest of all garden plants. To the Twelve and those around him he gives the key: to you has been given the mystery of the kingdom of God; for those outside, everything is in parables, so that they may indeed see but not perceive. The explanations he gives the disciples privately are the interpretive structure: the sower is the word; the four soils are four kinds of hearers. The lamp under the bushel, the measuring of the measure, the hiddenness that will be revealed — these form a cluster of sayings about the nature of the kingdom's announcement. At evening, Jesus and the disciples cross the sea; a great windstorm comes; the disciples wake him with the accusation do you not care that we are perishing?; he rebukes the wind and says to the sea peace, be still; there is a great calm; he turns to them: have you still no faith? They say to each other, who then is this?

Mark 5

Legion, a dead girl, and a hem touched in a crowd

Chapter 5 gives three healings in sequence that together define the outer reach of Jesus' authority: the demoniac, the woman, the corpse. At Gerasa, a man among the tombs who cannot be bound — the chains have been broken, no one has the strength to subdue him — comes running to Jesus; the unclean spirit names him Son of the Most High God. Jesus asks the name and the answer is Legion, for we are many. The demons beg to be sent into a herd of pigs; the pigs rush down the cliff into the sea; the townspeople see the man clothed and in his right mind and ask Jesus to leave. The restored man begs to follow him; Jesus tells him to go home and tell his people what the Lord has done — the only case in Mark's gospel where Jesus commissions a proclamation rather than commanding silence. Across the sea, Jairus, a synagogue ruler, falls at Jesus' feet and begs him to come and heal his dying daughter. En route, a woman with a twelve-year haemorrhage touches his cloak and is healed; he stops the crowd to ask who touched him; the disciples' bewilderment (who in this crowd?) heightens the moment. She comes trembling and tells him everything; he says your faith has made you well, go in peace. News comes: the girl has died. Jesus says do not fear, only believe. He raises her, telling the family to give her something to eat and strictly ordering that no one should know — the secret again, absurdly, for a girl who has just been restored from death.

Mark 6

Nazareth, the Baptist's death, and the five thousand

Chapter 6 alternates between contrasting scales of reception. At Nazareth, the people know too much: he is the carpenter, the son of Mary, and his siblings are known. They take offence at him; he marvels at their unbelief and can do no mighty work there except lay hands on a few sick people. He sends the Twelve out two by two with authority over unclean spirits and instructions for the road — no bread, no bag, no money, one pair of sandals, no extra tunic. Herod hears of Jesus' ministry and the narrative opens backward in time to give the story of John the Baptist's death: Herodias, the dancing daughter, the rash oath, the head on a platter. The story is the only place in Mark's gospel where the narrative slows for a scene in which Jesus is not present, and it is the shadow of the passion narrative — a just man imprisoned by a ruler, executed to honour a public oath. The feeding of the five thousand is the centrepiece of the chapter: five loaves and two fish become enough to feed five thousand men and leave twelve baskets of fragments. After the feeding, Jesus dismisses the crowd, sends the disciples across the sea, and goes to the mountain to pray. He comes to them across the water — walking on the sea — and they are terrified, thinking it a ghost. He gets into the boat; the wind ceases; they are amazed; the gospel says their hearts were hardened, for they did not understand about the loaves.

Mark 7

What defiles, the Syrophoenician woman, and the deaf man

Chapter 7 is the gospel's sustained engagement with the question of purity, and it opens the ministry decisively to Gentiles. The controversy with the Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem over handwashing (7:1-23) is the occasion for the most explicit teaching of the chapter: what goes into a person cannot defile, but what comes out of the heart — evil thoughts, sexual immorality, theft, murder, adultery, coveting, wickedness, deceit, sensuality, envy, slander, pride, foolishness. All these evil things come from within and defile a person. The parenthetical note — thus he declared all foods clean — is the early church's summary of the theological implication, and its weight is felt in Acts 10 and throughout Paul. Jesus then moves from Jewish into Gentile territory, to the region of Tyre, and enters a house seeking obscurity; he cannot be hidden. A Syrophoenician Greek woman comes and asks him to cast the demon from her daughter. His answer — let the children be fed first; it is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs — is the sharpest and most debated exchange in Mark's gospel. She answers: yes, Lord, yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs. He sends her home; her daughter is found lying in bed, the demon gone. The chapter ends with the healing of the man who is deaf and has a speech impediment, at the Decapolis, with the Aramaic command ephphatha — be opened — and the instruction to tell no one, and the crowd's astonishment: he has done all things well.

Mark 8

The second feeding and Peter's confession

Chapter 8 is the pivot of the gospel. It opens with the second feeding — four thousand men, seven loaves, seven baskets of fragments — and immediately the Pharisees come asking for a sign from heaven. Jesus refuses and sighs deeply. In the boat crossing the sea the disciples are worrying about having only one loaf; Jesus delivers the longest string of questions in the gospel: do you not yet perceive or understand? Are your hearts hardened? Having eyes do you not see? Do you not remember? They remember the numbers — twelve baskets, seven baskets — but do not understand. At Bethsaida, a blind man is brought to him; Jesus heals him in two stages, spitting on his eyes, then laying hands on them; the man first sees people as trees walking, then clearly. The two-stage healing is found only in Mark and reads as a figure of the gradual restoration of sight to those who travel with Jesus. The chapter's centre is at Caesarea Philippi: Jesus asks who do people say I am and gets the crowd's guesses — John the Baptist, Elijah, one of the prophets. He asks who do you say I am; Peter answers you are the Christ. Jesus charges them to tell no one — the strictest application of the secret yet — and immediately begins the first passion prediction: the Son of Man must suffer many things, be rejected, be killed, and after three days rise. Peter rebukes him; Jesus rebukes Peter: get behind me, Satan. The chapter ends with the call to take up the cross.

Mark 9

Transfiguration, a boy healed, and the second prediction

Chapter 9 runs from the summit of the transfiguration to the valley of the disciples' argument about who is greatest — from the voice from the cloud naming Jesus as beloved Son to the disciples bringing a possessed child they could not heal. After six days Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain; he is transfigured before them, his clothes becoming radiant white; Moses and Elijah appear and talk with him; Peter, not knowing what to say, proposes three tents; a cloud overshadows them and the voice speaks: this is my beloved Son, listen to him. The charge to tell no one until the Son of Man has risen from the dead produces the disciples' question among themselves about what rising from the dead could mean. Coming down, the scribes are arguing with the other nine; a father brings his son, possessed since childhood, whom the disciples could not cast out; Jesus exorcises the spirit; when asked why they could not, Jesus says this kind comes out only by prayer. The second passion prediction (9:31) follows: the Son of Man is to be delivered into the hands of men and they will kill him, and when killed, after three days he will rise. The disciples do not understand and are afraid to ask. In Capernaum, Jesus asks what they were arguing about on the road; they are silent; they were arguing about who was greatest. He sits down, calls the Twelve, and says if anyone would be first, he must be last of all and servant of all.

Mark 10

Marriage, children, riches, and the third prediction

Chapter 10 is the last chapter before the entry into Jerusalem and covers the final stretch of the road from Galilee south through Judea and Jericho. The controversy on divorce (10:1-12) restates the prohibition against divorce in stronger form than Matthew's version. The blessing of children (10:13-16) follows — the disciples try to prevent people from bringing children to Jesus; he is indignant and says the kingdom belongs to such as these. The encounter with the rich young man (10:17-22) is the most searching conversation about wealth in the gospel: the man keeps all the commandments, Jesus looks at him and loves him, and tells him to go sell all he has and give to the poor and follow; he goes away sorrowful, for he had great possessions. Jesus' remark about the camel and the eye of the needle (10:25) and the saying about what is impossible for humans being possible for God are followed by Peter's reminder that the disciples have left everything. The third and most detailed passion prediction (10:32-34) is placed at the beginning of the ascent to Jerusalem, and the disciples are in fear. James and John immediately request the seats at Jesus' right and left in glory; the other ten are angry; the whole chapter's point is stated in 10:45: the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many. The chapter ends with Bartimaeus, blind by the road, who calls out Son of David have mercy on me, is told to be quiet by the crowd, calls louder, is healed, and follows Jesus on the way.

Mark 11

Entry into Jerusalem and the temple cleared

Chapter 11 marks the gospel's second movement: after eight chapters of Galilean ministry and two of the journey south, the entry into Jerusalem initiates the events that will end at the cross. The entry on the colt (11:1-11) is prepared with careful logistical detail — the village where the colt will be found, the answer to give if challenged — and executed to a crowd's cry of hosanna. But the entry ends quietly: Jesus looks around at everything and, since it is already late, goes out to Bethany with the Twelve. The chapter's governing image is the fig tree cursed and found withered, which sandwiches the temple action in Mark's characteristic way. The cursing of the fig tree (11:12-14) — out of season, but Jesus curses it anyway; the disciples hear — comes before the clearing of the temple (11:15-17). The temple action is not a gentle act of cleansing: he overturns the tables of the money-changers and the seats of those selling doves, and does not allow anyone to carry goods through the temple. The quotation from Isaiah 57 and Jeremiah 7 — my house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations, but you have made it a den of robbers — makes the temple action a prophetic act against the current use of the institution. The finding of the withered fig tree next morning (11:20-25) draws the lesson: faith, prayer, forgiveness. The chapter ends with the authority challenge (11:27-33) and the counter-question about John's baptism.

Mark 12

Parables and traps in the temple

Chapter 12 is the Jerusalem controversy section, the temple equivalent of the Capernaum controversies in chapters 2 and 3. Five exchanges follow one another: the parable of the wicked tenants (12:1-12), the tax question (12:13-17), the resurrection question from the Sadducees (12:18-27), the greatest commandment question (12:28-34), and Jesus' own question about the Son of David (12:35-37). Each exchange is a test or an attempt to trap; each is answered with a counter-question or an unexpected reframe. The parable of the wicked tenants — the vineyard let to tenants who beat and kill the servants sent to collect the fruit, and finally kill the heir — is a transparent allegory of the history of Israel, the prophets, and Jesus. The questioners understand that he has told it against them and want to arrest him but fear the crowd. The tax question produces the most cited response: render to Caesar what is Caesar's and to God what is God's. The Sadducees' question about resurrection — whose wife will she be, she who had seven husbands? — is answered with the claim that in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, and that God is not God of the dead but of the living. A scribe's question about the greatest commandment receives the fullest answer of any question in the chapter: hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one, and love him with all your heart, soul, mind, and strength; and love your neighbour as yourself. The chapter ends with the widow's two coins — more than all the others, for they gave from their abundance, but she from her poverty gave everything she had.

Mark 13

The Olivet discourse on the end

Chapter 13 is the only extended discourse in Mark's gospel outside the parable chapter, and it is placed at the hinge between the temple controversies and the Passion narrative. The occasion is the disciples' admiration of the temple buildings — as they leave, one disciple points to the stones; Jesus answers that not one stone will be left on another. The question on the Mount of Olives is private, from the inner circle: when will these things be, and what will be the sign when all these things are about to be accomplished? The discourse that follows (13:5-37) is the most contested passage in Mark: how much refers to the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, how much to the end of all things, and how much to both at once. The structure is clear: false messiahs and wars (13:5-8), persecution of disciples (13:9-13), the abomination of desolation and the great tribulation (13:14-23), cosmic signs and the coming of the Son of Man (13:24-27), the parable of the fig tree (13:28-31), and the call to watchfulness because no one knows the day or the hour — not the angels, not the Son, only the Father (13:32-37). The word watch appears four times in the final section. The discourse ends as a call to alertness in the face of radical uncertainty about timing and certainty about outcome.

Mark 14

Anointing, supper, Gethsemane, and arrest

Chapter 14 is the longest chapter of the gospel and the point where the narrative finally slows to match the weight of what is happening. The anointing at Bethany (14:1-11) opens the Passion with the juxtaposition of the woman who pours expensive nard over Jesus' head — he interprets it as anointing for burial — and Judas going to the chief priests to betray him. The Passover preparations and the Last Supper (14:12-26) are given in the same preparatory detail as the entry into Jerusalem: the two disciples sent ahead, the upper room found, the table set. At the meal, Jesus predicts the betrayal and then takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it and gives it to them: take, this is my body. He takes the cup: this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many. They sing a hymn and go to the Mount of Olives. Peter's denial is predicted. Gethsemane (14:32-42) is the most intimate scene in the gospel: Jesus falls on the ground, prays that the hour might pass, calls God Abba, Father; the disciples sleep three times. The prayer has been made; he rises; the betrayer is at hand. The arrest (14:43-52) is quick; one of the disciples cuts off a servant's ear; they all flee; a young man in a linen cloth escapes naked. The trial before the Sanhedrin (14:53-65) gives the high priest's question — are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed? — and Jesus' answer: I am, and you will see the Son of Man. Peter's three denials close the chapter.

Mark 15

Pilate, the cross, the cry, the centurion

Chapter 15 is the Passion's centre of gravity. The trial before Pilate (15:1-15) is brief: Pilate's question are you the King of the Jews? receives the same minimal answer as the high priest's — you have said so — and then Jesus answers nothing more, to Pilate's amazement. The Barabbas exchange is a crowd scene: Pilate offers the Passover release to the crowd, the crowd chooses Barabbas, the chief priests stir the crowd to ask for crucifixion, Pilate releases Barabbas and hands Jesus over to be crucified. The soldiers' mocking (15:16-20) dresses him in purple and a crown of thorns and gives him royal salutations before leading him out. Simon of Cyrene is compelled to carry the cross; they bring Jesus to Golgotha, the Place of the Skull. He is crucified at the third hour. The titulus reads the King of the Jews. He is crucified between two criminals who also revile him. From the sixth to the ninth hour there is darkness over the whole land. At the ninth hour the cry of dereliction is given in Aramaic — Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani — transliterated and translated, my God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Someone offers wine; someone thinks he is calling Elijah; then Jesus utters a loud cry and breathes his last. The curtain of the temple tears from top to bottom. The centurion who stands facing him makes the confession that the gospel has been waiting for: truly this man was the Son of God.

Mark 16

The empty tomb and the ending in fear

Chapter 16 is the gospel's ending — or rather, its refusal of a conventional ending. Three women come to the tomb at dawn on the first day of the week: Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome, bringing spices. They have been worrying on the road about who will roll the stone away; they look up and see it is already rolled back. Inside the tomb they find a young man in a white robe, sitting on the right side; they are alarmed. He tells them not to be alarmed; he identifies Jesus as the one who was crucified; he announces the resurrection: he has risen, he is not here; see the place where they laid him. He gives them an instruction: go, tell his disciples and Peter that he is going before you to Galilee; there you will see him, as he told you. The women go out and flee from the tomb; trembling and astonishment have seized them; and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid. The Greek ends there: ephobounto gar. The longer ending (16:9-20), added by a second-century hand, gives the appearance to Mary Magdalene, the appearance to two disciples walking, the appearance to the Eleven with the great commission, the Ascension, and a note about the signs accompanying those who believe. Modern critical editions bracket it and note its absence from the oldest manuscripts. The gospel as Mark wrote it ends at fear.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

And immediately

Mark's gospel runs faster than the other three. The favourite word is kai euthus — and immediately — and it appears more than forty times in sixteen chapters. The first chapter alone uses it eleven times. The speed is the gospel's argument.

The messianic secret

Throughout the gospel, when demons recognize Jesus and shout his identity, he silences them. When he heals, he charges silence. The pattern is deliberate and consistent: the identity cannot be properly understood except through the cross.

The disciples who do not understand

If the demons know exactly who Jesus is, the disciples are with him throughout and do not understand. After the feeding of the five thousand, the gospel says explicitly: they did not understand about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened.

The cross and the centurion

The Passion narrative is the first sustained piece of slow narration in a gospel that runs at speed. The centurion who has been at the foot of the cross for three hours makes the confession the disciples could not make in sixteen chapters.

The empty tomb and the ending at fear

The oldest manuscripts end at 16:8. Three women come to the tomb, find it empty, hear the announcement of resurrection, and flee in terror saying nothing to anyone. The longer ending (16:9–20) is a second-century addition. The gospel Mark wrote ends at the tomb.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Jesus
Driven Messiah

The Markan Jesus is the most urgent of the four gospel portraits — driven into the wilderness by the Spirit, moving from Capernaum to the lake to the surrounding villages and back, often misunderstood, often impatient with the disciples' incomprehension, often touching the unclean (the leper, the menstruant, the corpse). He silences the demons who name him correctly and accepts the title Christ from Peter only with the immediate qualification that the Son of Man must suffer. His authority is unmistakable to the crowds and to the demons, hidden from the disciples, and finally legible at the cross to the centurion. The cry of dereliction at the ninth hour — my God, my God, why have you forsaken me? — is given without softening; the gospel records only the loud cry and the breath.

The Disciples
Those who do not get it

Mark's portrait of the Twelve is the most exposed and least flattering in the four gospels. They are called in chapter 1 and leave their nets immediately; from there they fail to understand the parables, the loaves, who Jesus is, Gethsemane, and at the arrest they all desert him and flee. The gospel uses them as a structural device — every Passion prediction is followed by an episode revealing their incomprehension. The reader is being asked to recognise themselves in the failure.

Peter
First and denying

First disciple called (1:16–18); first to confess Jesus as the Christ at Caesarea Philippi (8:29); immediately rebuked as Satan when he objects to the prediction of suffering. He swears in the upper room that he will not deny Jesus, denies him three times in the courtyard during the trial, and goes out and weeps. The young man at the empty tomb mentions him by name in the announcement to the women: tell his disciples and Peter.

Mary Magdalene
Witness

One of the women who had followed Jesus from Galilee and ministered to him there. Named among those watching from afar at the cross (15:40). She and the other Mary see where the body is laid (15:47). At dawn on the first day of the week she returns to the tomb with the others, finds it empty, hears the announcement of the resurrection, and flees in fear. She is the figure of the faithful witness whose courage held at the cross when the male disciples had fled, and whose voice at the tomb falls silent in fear.

The Centurion
First confessor

The Roman centurion who stood facing Jesus during the three hours on the cross. He sees the manner of Jesus' breath at the ninth hour and says the line that closes the gospel's christological argument: truly this man was the Son of God (15:39). He is the only human being in Mark's gospel to make that confession. He has no name. He is, in the gospel's structure, the figure of the reader who has read through to the cross and is being asked to make the confession the gospel has been waiting for.

Pilate
Procurator

Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judea from 26 to 36 CE. Mark's portrait is brief and tactical: Pilate recognises the chief priests have handed Jesus over out of envy (15:10), tries to release him via the Passover custom, and finally gives the crowd what they demand. In Mark he marvels at Jesus' silence (15:5) and hands him over to be crucified to satisfy the crowd. The Markan Pilate is a Roman administrator solving a political problem; the gospel neither exonerates nor vilifies him beyond the simple narration of what he does.
