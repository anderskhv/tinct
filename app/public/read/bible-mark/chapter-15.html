Mark 15

The trial before Pilate, the crowd's choice of Barabbas, the crucifixion at the third hour, the cry of dereliction at the ninth, and the centurion's confession: truly this man was the Son of God.

Summary

Mark 15 begins at dawn with Jesus bound and delivered to Pilate. The Markan trial before Pilate (15:1-15) is the briefest of the four gospel accounts. Pilate's single question — are you the King of the Jews? — is answered with su legeis, you say so, and then silence: Jesus answers nothing, not even to the charges the chief priests bring, and Pilate marvels. The Barabbas scene (15:6-15) is carefully balanced: Pilate recognises that the chief priests have handed Jesus over out of envy (15:10), tries to release him, but the crowd stirred by the chief priests presses for crucifixion and Barabbas. Pilate, wishing to satisfy the crowd, releases Barabbas and delivers Jesus to be crucified. The mocking by the soldiers (15:16-20) is the fourth mocking sequence in the Passion (after the Sanhedrin, the high priest's servants, and the crowd at the cross). The purple robe and crown of thorns are a parody of the Roman imperial triumph; the salutation hail, King of the Jews is a parody of the imperial salutation.

The crucifixion (15:21-39) is structured by the hours: third hour — crucifixion; sixth to ninth hour — darkness; ninth hour — the cry of dereliction; death. The timed structure gives the cross a liturgical cadence. The cry — Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani — is in Aramaic, transliterated, and then translated; it is the opening verse of Psalm 22, which the early church read typologically as a messianic psalm, but Mark gives it as the raw cry of abandonment, without the psalm's later vindication. The tearing of the temple curtain from top to bottom is simultaneous with the death, and its theological implication — the end of the barrier between God and humanity, between Jew and Gentile — is left implicit by the gospel. The centurion (15:39) stands facing Jesus, sees the manner of his death, and confesses truly this man was the Son of God. He is a Roman, a Gentile, a soldier; he is the first human in the gospel to confess Jesus' identity, and he confesses it at the death. The women who have been watching from afar (15:40-41) — Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joses, and Salome — are the faithful remnant at the cross when the male disciples have fled. Joseph of Arimathea buries the body before sunset.
