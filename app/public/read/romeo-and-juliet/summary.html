Romeo and Juliet

1597 CE · by William Shakespeare

The book in brief

Romeo Montague crashes a party at the Capulet house on a Sunday evening, sees Juliet across the room, and forgets the girl he was sighing over an hour earlier. They speak fourteen lines together — a sonnet, exactly — and kiss before they know each other's names. By morning they have sworn marriage; by noon Juliet's nurse is carrying messages between them; by afternoon Friar Laurence has secretly married them in his cell. That same day Juliet's cousin Tybalt kills Romeo's friend Mercutio in a street fight, and Romeo kills Tybalt in revenge. Romeo is banished. Juliet's father announces she will marry the Count Paris on Thursday morning. Friar Laurence proposes a desperate plan involving a sleeping potion. The plan fails. By Friday morning both lovers are dead in the Capulet vault.

Shakespeare wrote the play in the mid-1590s, early in his career, drawing on Arthur Brooke's narrative poem The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet. He compressed Brooke's nine-month timeline into five days. The verse is lyrical in a way the later tragedies are not — Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, Juliet's "gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds," the shared sonnet at first meeting, the balcony scene — and many of the most-quoted lines in English come from this play.

It has been a stage favorite for four hundred years partly because it understands that this is what young love feels like from the inside: total, immediate, unanswerable. The play is also the founding modern argument that adolescents have an interior life worth taking seriously. Most subsequent love stories are downstream of it, and most love stories that try to slow down adolescent feeling — to make it reasonable — fail in ways Shakespeare's play does not.

Why it still matters

The Original Love-at-First-Sight

Romeo and Juliet meet, kiss, and swear marriage in the same scene. They speak a single shared sonnet between meeting and the kiss, and in performance the audience does not realize the form has happened until the rhymes lock into place. Shakespeare did not invent the love story — he had Arthur Brooke's narrative poem in hand, and behind it Italian sources stretching back through Bandello to Masuccio Salernitano — but he wrote the version that defined the form. Almost every story Western culture now tells about young love that overrides everything — family, sense, time, the future — is downstream of this play. The pattern of stranger-eyes-meet-across-room, of love declared in metaphor before names are exchanged, of the world insisting it is impossible while the lovers insist it is the only thing that is real, is a Shakespearean inheritance, and four centuries of romance fiction, opera, ballet, musical theater, and pop song have not exhausted it.

A Feud Whose Reason No One Remembers

The Capulets and the Montagues hate each other and have forgotten why. The prologue calls it an "ancient grudge"; the play never explains its cause; no character ever cites a specific wrong. The young men of both houses inherit the quarrel ready-made and bleed for it. The opening scene is two Capulet servingmen — Sampson and Gregory — picking a fight with Montague servingmen because their masters' arms hate one another, and they themselves have learned that this is what their lives are for. Mercutio dies for an argument he was not part of. Tybalt dies an hour after Romeo's secret marriage to his cousin. Both lovers die paying for an argument they did not start and do not believe in. Shakespeare is deliberate about this: the feud is structural, not personal. The argument is gone but the violence isn't, and the people too young to have agreed to it are the ones who pay. Modern readers carrying inherited political enmities recognize the pattern immediately.

The Verse Carries the Speed

Shakespeare compressed Brooke's nine-month source poem into five days, and the verse does the work of making that speed feel inevitable rather than careless. Romeo and Juliet share a sonnet — fourteen lines, perfectly rhymed — at first meeting, finishing each other's quatrains as if they have been writing it together their whole lives. The balcony scene piles metaphor on metaphor faster than the listener can track, every comparison topping the last. Juliet's epithalamion — "gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" — runs faster than any speech can comfortably be spoken aloud. Mercutio's Queen Mab swerve from playful conceit into something darker and uncontrollable, Friar Laurence's moralizing couplets that arrive too late, the lovers' shared rhymes inside their separate speeches: every formal choice is engineered to make the audience feel why these two cannot stop. The play is the founding argument in English that the texture of language can be the engine of plot, not just its decoration.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Who is actually responsible?

The prologue blames the stars. The families blame each other. But Romeo and Juliet make choices — fast, reckless, irreversible ones. I want to watch where the real cause of the tragedy lies, and whether Shakespeare lets anyone off the hook.

The fever of it.

This is not a slow romance. Sunday to Friday. Every decision instant, every declaration absolute. I want to feel the compression Shakespeare built into the play and understand why the speed is the point, not a flaw.

Mercutio as the real heart of the play.

He's funnier than Romeo, clearer-eyed than anyone, and dead before the halfway point. I want to track what he represents — wit, reality, friendship — and what the play loses when he goes.

Juliet is smarter than Romeo.

She asks harder questions, moves more carefully, and sees what is actually at stake. I want to read the play watching her choices against his and ask whether she is undone by love or by the world she's trapped in.

Key figures

Romeo
The Romantic

Heir of the Montague house, somewhere around sixteen. He opens the play in love with a woman named Rosaline who never appears, mooning through Verona in a fashionable melancholy. The instant he sees Juliet he forgets Rosaline entirely. He is impulsive, eloquent, genuinely tender, and his instinct is always to act before thinking. He buys poison from a Mantuan apothecary on a few hours' notice and rides through the night to die beside her. The play loves him and punishes him.

Juliet
The One Actually Thinking

Thirteen years old — the play states it explicitly — and in most ways the wiser of the two lovers. She questions, qualifies, and anticipates. "It is too rash, too unadvis'd, too sudden," she says on the balcony, before agreeing anyway. She arranges the marriage, doses herself with the friar's potion, and faces the vault alone. She is undone not by recklessness but by circumstance and the failures of every adult around her. Her final scene, waking in the tomb to find Romeo already dead, is one of the cruelest in Shakespeare.

Mercutio
The Wit

Romeo's closest friend and the play's best speaker. Kinsman to Prince Escalus, neither Capulet nor Montague, he is loyal to Romeo personally rather than to any house. The Queen Mab speech — a torrent of fairy-tale invention sliding into something darker — belongs to him. He sees through romantic illusion, mocks Romeo's lovesickness, and gets mortally wounded under Romeo's own arm trying to break up a fight Romeo would not finish. His death — "a plague o' both your houses" — is the play's pivot. After it, the comedy stops entirely.

Tybalt
The Feud Personified

Juliet's cousin and the Capulets' most aggressive young man. He hates the Montagues purely on principle and needs no personal grievance to draw a sword. When he spots Romeo at the Capulet ball, his uncle restrains him; when he finds Romeo in the street, he forces the duel that kills Mercutio. He is what happens when honor calcifies into identity, when an inherited quarrel becomes a person's only available self. Romeo kills him out of rage minutes after marrying his cousin.

The Nurse
Juliet's Confidante

Juliet's wet-nurse from infancy and the household member who knows her best. Bawdy, garrulous, devoted, and genuinely fond of the girl she helped raise. She carries the messages, helps arrange the secret marriage, and cheers the young couple on. Then, after Tybalt's death and the Paris match, she counsels Juliet to forget Romeo and marry Paris instead — a practical accommodation that Juliet recognizes as a betrayal. The Nurse's failure is one of the quiet tragedies inside the loud one.

Friar Laurence
Good Intentions, Disastrous Results

A Franciscan friar who collects herbs in his cell and reads moral lessons in their properties. He marries Romeo and Juliet in secret, hoping the union will reconcile their houses. When the plan collapses, he supplies the sleeping potion and devises the final scheme — a letter to Romeo that never arrives. He arrives at the vault late, panics, and abandons Juliet there to die. The play's argument against well-meaning adult interference is unsparing: every plan he makes makes things worse.
