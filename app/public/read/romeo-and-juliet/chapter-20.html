Summary

Juliet's chamber, late Tuesday night. The wedding is at dawn. The Nurse has helped her choose her clothes; Lady Capulet has come in to check on her and been sent, with quiet courtesy, away. She is alone now. She lays Romeo's dagger on the table where she can reach it: "if all else fail, myself have power to die." Then she takes the vial from her dress and looks at it. The speech that follows is the most extended interior monologue in the play — a fear-by-fear inventory of every way the plan can go wrong.

What if the potion does not work at all? Then she must marry Paris in the morning. The dagger will save her. What if the Friar has given her real poison — to cover his complicity and save his own honor? She rejects it, then immediately wonders. What if she wakes before Romeo comes? "How if, when I am laid into the tomb, / I wake before the time that Romeo / come to redeem me?" She will choke on the foul air; she will lie among the bones of her ancestors and Tybalt's freshly-buried body; she will pluck a kinsman's bone from the heap and dash her brains out with it.

The speech ends with an image. She sees Tybalt's ghost rising in the vault to chase Romeo. She cries out, drinks, and falls onto the bed. The line as the vial reaches her lips is the play's clearest gesture of complete commitment: "Romeo, Romeo, Romeo, here's drink. I drink to thee." The curtains close around her body. The audience knows what no one inside the Capulet house knows — that Juliet is, by every test the household can apply, dead, and will be for the next forty-two hours.
