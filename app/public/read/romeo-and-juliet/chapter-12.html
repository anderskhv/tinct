Summary

Friar Laurence's cell, early afternoon. Romeo is waiting. The Friar, in private, gives him the warning the play will quote against him at every subsequent crisis: "these violent delights have violent ends, / and in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / which as they kiss consume." The sweetest honey, he says, is loathsome in its own deliciousness; therefore love moderately, long love does so. It is the longest piece of plain advice in the scene, delivered to a sixteen-year-old who has not slept and has spent the past three hours arranging the rope ladder for tonight.

Juliet enters, almost running. She greets her confessor — "good even to my ghostly confessor" — and turns at once to Romeo. The exchange between them is short and heightened: each insists their love is past expression. "They are but beggars that can count their worth," Juliet says; "but my true love is grown to such excess / I cannot sum up sum of half my wealth." Romeo answers in the same key. They are speaking the language of a high lyric tradition that, in the play, neither of them will live to outgrow.

The Friar interrupts. "Come, come with me, and we will make short work, / for, by your leaves, you shall not stay alone / till holy church incorporate two in one." He leads them off-stage into the chapel. The wedding itself happens between scenes — the audience does not see it; the next scene begins in the public square with Mercutio's death. What the audience sees on stage in Scene 12 is fewer than thirty lines. By the end of them, Romeo and Juliet are married. Everything that follows is the consequence of a fact set down in the world.
