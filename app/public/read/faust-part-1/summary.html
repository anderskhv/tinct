Faust, Part One

1808 CE · by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

The book in brief

Faust is an aging scholar who has mastered philosophy, law, medicine, and theology and concluded that none of it has taught him anything worth knowing. The play opens with him alone in his study at midnight, contemplating suicide. Mephistopheles arrives — a devil sent on a wager with God — and offers him the one thing scholarship has not given him: experience. Faust signs the pact in blood and is made young again. He meets Gretchen, a townswoman of fifteen or sixteen, seduces her, kills her brother in a street fight, and abandons her. By the end she has drowned the child she bore him and is awaiting execution.

Goethe worked on Faust for sixty years. He wrote the first fragments in his twenties, completed Part One in 1808, and finished Part Two only months before his death in 1832. The play takes the German chapbook legend of Doctor Faustus — the scholar who sells his soul to the devil — and rewrites it from the ground up. Goethe added the wager in Heaven, the Gretchen tragedy, and the central condition of the pact: Mephistopheles wins only if he can give Faust a moment so satisfying he wishes it would last.

Faust is the foundational text of German literature and one of the most-quoted works in any European language — the source of "Faustian bargain," "two souls in one breast," and "the eternal feminine." It is also strange. The verse shifts register constantly, from doggerel to lyric to philosophical disputation. The devil is funnier than the hero. The hero's grand project ends in a young woman's ruined life. Goethe treats striving and damage as inseparable, and refuses to settle which his protagonist deserves.

Why it still matters

The Faustian Bargain

The phrase "Faustian bargain" comes directly from this play. Whenever a scientist trades ethics for breakthrough, a politician trades principle for office, or a company trades long-term integrity for a quarter's growth, the reference is here. Goethe rewrote the older legend so that Faust is not damned for wanting too much knowledge — Marlowe's Faustus had been damned for that, in 1592 — he is damned, if he is damned, for wanting to stop wanting. The bet with Mephistopheles is specifically that no experience will satisfy him enough to make him say "linger awhile, you are so fair." If he ever does say it, the devil takes him. The formulation collapses the entire economy of temptation: the soul is at stake not in what Faust accepts but in whether he can be made to rest in any of it. That has shaped two centuries of Western thinking about ambition, technology, and cost. Modern debates over AI risk and biotech often reach for the word Faustian without realizing how exact Goethe's version of it is.

Striving as Salvation and Damage

Goethe's Faust is the first major hero in Western literature whose restlessness is treated as both his glory and his sin. Earlier Fausts — Marlowe's Doctor Faustus, the chapbook tradition behind it — were warning stories: the scholar reached too far and was dragged off to hell at midnight. Goethe rewrote the verdict. He lets his Faust keep striving and lets the wreckage stand. Gretchen is destroyed; Faust survives and continues into Part Two. The play does not condemn his hunger and does not absolve it either; the Lord in the Prologue suggests he will be saved precisely because he cannot rest, and the play immediately shows what that costs. That refusal made him the prototype for the modern protagonist whose energy and damage cannot be separated — Captain Ahab, Raskolnikov, Don Draper, the founder figure of contemporary Silicon Valley mythology, every character whose drive is the source of both achievement and harm.

Gretchen's Story

Goethe invented Gretchen. The older Faustus chapbooks have nothing like her — they are all about the scholar and his demons, with the women appearing as conjured succubi or aristocratic seductions. Goethe added an ordinary townswoman to the legend and let her bear the cost of Faust's bargain in plain sight. By the end of Part One she has poisoned her mother by accident with the sleeping draught Mephistopheles provided, lost her brother in a duel Faust fought, drowned the infant she bore Faust, gone mad in prison, and refused the rescue Faust finally comes to offer. Her dungeon scene is one of the most harrowing passages in German literature, and the church scene where the Evil Spirit whispers behind her at Mass while the choir sings the Dies Irae is among the most theatrically frightening Goethe ever wrote. The play pulls its moral seriousness out of the pact and into her cell, and refuses to let the reader treat Faust's striving as separable from what it did to her.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The pact and what’s actually wagered.

Marlowe’s Faustus sells his soul for power. Goethe’s Faust makes a different bet: if Mephistopheles can ever give him a single moment so satisfying that he wants it to last, he loses. Watch what Faust is actually searching for — and whether the play thinks he can find it.

Two souls in one breast.

Faust says it himself early: “Two souls, alas! reside within my breast.” One pulls toward the world and the senses; the other toward spirit and abstraction. The tension never resolves — it is the engine of the play. Notice which soul is speaking in any given scene.

Gretchen as the play’s original move.

The Faust legend before Goethe had no Gretchen. She is his invention, and she is the most original thing in the play: an ordinary person placed in the path of an extraordinary hunger. The tragedy belongs to her, not to Faust. Watch how the play shifts its moral weight as her story takes over.

Mephistopheles as comedy.

The devil in Faust is not terrifying — he is witty, sardonic, and frequently right. He is the best critic in the play. Goethe gives him the funniest lines and the clearest view of human self-deception. Notice how often you agree with him, and what that agreement costs you.

Key figures

Faust
The Restless Scholar

A doctor of every discipline the university teaches and a man who has concluded he knows nothing. He opens the play in despair, reaching first for magic and then for poison. The pact with Mephistopheles is his attempt to break out of the study and into the world. Made young again, he becomes a lover, a fugitive, and finally the cause of Gretchen's destruction — without ever resting in any of those roles.

Mephistopheles
The Spirit That Denies

The devil of the play, sent by Heaven on a wager. He calls himself "part of that power which would do evil ever, yet ever does good." He is sardonic, cultured, brilliantly funny, and almost always the smartest voice in the room. He does not lie outright; he reframes, ironizes, and lets human appetite do the rest. The play gives him its sharpest lines, and the reader keeps catching herself agreeing with him.

Gretchen (Margarete)
The Townswoman

A young woman of fifteen or sixteen, devout and unworldly, who lives with her mother in a small German town. Faust sees her in the street and wants her. Mephistopheles engineers the seduction with jewels, a bribed neighbor, and a sleeping draught. By the play's end Gretchen has lost her mother, her brother, her child, her reason, and her life. Goethe invented her — she is not in the older Faust legend — and the moral weight of Part One settles on her, not on Faust.

Wagner
Faust's Assistant

Faust's devoted student and academic foil. Where Faust has burnt through every book and found them empty, Wagner believes patient scholarship is the highest calling and looks forward to mastering one more text. His earnestness is gentle and his vision is small. The play treats him with affectionate irony — he is what Faust was, before Faust stopped believing it was enough.

Marthe
Gretchen's Neighbor

A widow next door to Gretchen who befriends her and becomes the social cover for Faust's courtship. Mephistopheles flirts with her clumsily and produces a fake death-notice for her absent husband; she takes both in stride. Practical, slightly vain, and quietly worldly, she is the ordinary social fabric the seduction operates through — and the contrast that makes Gretchen's innocence visible.

The Lord
Voice in Heaven

He appears only in the Prologue in Heaven, where he accepts Mephistopheles's wager over Faust's soul. His confidence is total: "A good man, in his dark, bewildered course, will not forget the way of righteousness." He never appears again. The whole tragedy of Part One — Gretchen's ruin included — unfolds inside the space that wager opens, with Heaven watching and not intervening.
