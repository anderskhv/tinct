Jane Eyre

1847 CE · by Charlotte Brontë

The book in brief

Jane Eyre is the autobiography of an orphan girl, told in her own voice from age ten to her marriage. She is raised at Gateshead by an aunt who despises her, then sent at ten to Lowood, a charity school where she nearly dies of typhus and where her one friend Helen Burns dies in her arms. She teaches at Lowood for two years and at eighteen takes a position as governess at Thornfield Hall to a small French ward named Adèle. The master of the house, Edward Rochester, returns home, fascinated by his plain, sharp-tongued governess, and proposes marriage. The wedding is interrupted at the altar by a stranger announcing that Rochester already has a wife — kept locked in the third floor of his own house. Jane refuses to be his mistress. She walks out into the moors with no money and almost dies of exposure before being taken in by the Rivers family. The novel ends only after she has come into a small fortune of her own, refused another marriage that would have erased her, and returned to Rochester on terms he can no longer dictate.

Charlotte Brontë wrote it at home in Haworth, on the Yorkshire moors, while caring for her dying brother and watching her sisters write Wuthering Heights and Agnes Grey at the same parlor table. It was published in October 1847 under the pseudonym Currer Bell. It was an immediate critical and commercial success — three editions in six months — and the question of who Currer Bell actually was occupied literary London for two years. Thackeray, to whom Brontë later dedicated the second edition, sat up all night to finish it.

The first-person voice — a poor, plain, intelligent woman insisting on her own moral worth — was new in 1847, and the novel's directness about a woman's interior life still gives it a shock English fiction has rarely equaled.

Why it still matters

A Voice That Insists On Itself

Jane is poor, plain, small, and almost entirely without social leverage, and the novel is built around her insistence that she is the moral equal of anyone she meets. "Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless?" she demands of Rochester in the garden. "You think wrong!" Nothing quite like this voice had been published in English before. Earlier first-person heroines tended toward modesty, exposition, or apology; Brontë's narrator argues, accuses, judges, and contradicts the men who try to define her. The novel is directly addressed to a "reader" who is treated as adult and present — "Reader, I married him" is the most famous instance, but the pattern runs throughout. The voice is the argument the book is making. It is one of the reasons the novel still feels current to first-time readers nearly two centuries after publication.

The Madwoman in the Attic

The discovery of Bertha Mason — Rochester's first wife, kept in the third story of Thornfield for ten years under the care of a paid warden — is among the most discussed reveals in nineteenth-century fiction. She has been read as a gothic plot device, as the dark double of Jane's own buried passions, as a colonial figure whose Caribbean origin and racialized description by Brontë carry their own indictment of British wealth, and as the figure who saves Jane from a bigamous marriage by setting Thornfield on fire. The novel does not let any single reading exhaust her. She is also, simply, what Rochester chose to do with a wife he no longer wanted: lock her up and lie about her. Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar's 1979 book of feminist criticism took its title from Bertha. The conversation she opened is still going.

Marriage On Equal Terms

The novel's conclusion is unusual for its period. Jane refuses to live with Rochester as his mistress when his existing marriage is revealed; she walks out into a rainstorm with twenty shillings to her name. She refuses St. John Rivers's offer to become his missionary wife in India, even though he frames the refusal as a refusal of God. She returns to Rochester only after inheriting twenty thousand pounds from a dead uncle — money that makes her financially independent — and only after Thornfield has burned down, taking Bertha and most of Rochester's fortune with it, leaving him blind and one-handed. The reunion happens on terms Rochester can no longer dictate. The book's argument about what real partnership requires is direct: rough equality of standing, real knowledge of the other person, and the freedom to leave. It is uncompromising in a way fiction had largely not been about marriage before.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Jane's refusal to be diminished.

Every man in this novel tries to define Jane on his own terms — Brocklehurst as sinner, Rochester as possession, St. John as instrument. I want to track how she resists each of them and what it costs her.

The madwoman in the attic.

Bertha Mason is the wife locked in the third floor — passionate, colonial, inconvenient. I want to read the novel asking what she represents and whose story is really being told.

Rochester as romantic hero — or not.

Rochester is compelling, but he deceives Jane, keeps a wife locked away, and nearly commits bigamy. I want to track whether the novel ultimately rewards him or holds him accountable.

Jane's navigation of religion.

Three kinds of religious authority compete for Jane — cold institutional piety, warm self-sacrifice, and burning zealotry. I want to understand what Brontë is arguing through each of them.

Key figures

Jane Eyre
The Narrator

Plain, poor, and small, narrating her own life from ten to thirty. She tells the truth about herself in a way no Victorian heroine before her had been allowed to: about anger, about pride, about wanting to be loved on terms she will not compromise. She walks out into the rain rather than become Rochester's mistress, and walks back only when she has her own money and he has nothing left to bribe her with. She is one of the most distinctive narrators in English fiction.

Edward Rochester
The Master of Thornfield

Dark, restless, ironic, twenty years older than Jane and heir to an estate he never wanted. He is hiding a wife in the third story of his own house. He falls in love with Jane partly because she refuses to flatter him and partly because she sees through him. He proposes, dresses her in jewels she does not want, and almost commits bigamy at the altar. By the end he has been blinded and maimed in the fire that burned Thornfield down. The novel is exact about his failures.

Bertha Mason
The Wife in the Attic

Rochester's first wife: a Creole heiress from Spanish Town, Jamaica, whom he married at twenty-three and quickly came to fear. The novel describes her as mad and violent. She has been locked in the third story of Thornfield for ten years under a hired warden, escaping occasionally to set fires and bite. She is the source of the novel's gothic atmosphere and its deepest moral problem; she is never given her own voice. Jean Rhys's Wide Sargasso Sea would, a century later, attempt to give it back.

St. John Rivers
Duty Without Warmth

The clergyman who finds Jane near death on the moors and turns out to be her unknown cousin. Beautiful, glacial, consumed by a missionary calling to India. He proposes to Jane on the explicit grounds that she would be useful to his work, not because he loves her — he frankly admits he does not. The novel's most frightening suitor, because his demands come wrapped in scripture. The chapter in which Jane comes within an inch of accepting him is the book's hardest test of her resolve.

Helen Burns
The First Real Friend

An older girl at Lowood school who quietly endures floggings for blots on her copybook and unbrushed hair. Stoic, principled, devout, dying of consumption almost from the moment Jane meets her. She teaches Jane that some injustices have to be endured rather than answered, and that resentment is itself a kind of bondage. She dies in the same bed as Jane, holding her hand, in one of the most quoted scenes in Victorian fiction. Helen is based on Charlotte Brontë's older sister Maria, who died of tuberculosis at eleven.

Mrs. Reed
The First Injustice

Jane's aunt by marriage, who took Jane in after her mother died and treated her as a parasite ever afterward. She locks ten-year-old Jane in the red-room — the room where her uncle died — until she has a fit. Years later, she lies to a potential benefactor about Jane's character. Jane confronts her twice: once at ten in a moment of explosive defiance, and once at her deathbed, where Jane chooses not to take revenge. The second confrontation is one of the great scenes in the novel.
