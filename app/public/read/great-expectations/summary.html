Great Expectations

1861 CE · by Charles Dickens

The book in brief

Great Expectations is the story of Pip, an orphan boy raised at his sister's forge in the marshes of Kent. In the first chapter he is accosted in a churchyard by an escaped convict who orders him to bring food and a file. Soon after, he is summoned to the decaying mansion of Miss Havisham, who has stopped every clock at the moment she was jilted on her wedding day. There he meets Estella, the beautiful girl Miss Havisham has raised to break men's hearts. Pip falls in love with her, becomes ashamed of the forge that raised him, and longs to be a gentleman. A London lawyer named Jaggers arrives one day with the news that an anonymous benefactor has settled a fortune on him. Pip moves to London, takes lodgings with Herbert Pocket, and tries to become the gentleman he thinks Estella deserves. The novel is the working out of how completely wrong he was — about the source of the money, about Estella, and about the people he was leaving behind.

Dickens wrote it from October 1860 to August 1861, serializing it weekly in his magazine All the Year Round to rescue the magazine's flagging sales. He was forty-eight, recently separated from his wife, and at the height of his powers. It is the shortest of his major novels and the only one apart from David Copperfield he wrote in the first person. He cut the original ending at Bulwer-Lytton's urging and gave the book the more ambiguous, gentler ending now in print, then was never quite sure he had been right.

It is the most morally serious of his books. Other Dickens novels are bigger and more crowded; this one is tighter, sadder, and harder to forget. It is about the way other people's plans can shape a life without one's knowledge.

Why it still matters

A Working-Class Hero in His Own Voice

Pip is the rare Dickens hero who narrates his own story, and the only one Dickens sustained in the first person across the full length of a major novel apart from David Copperfield. The voice is the achievement: ironic about itself, ashamed of what it used to want, exact about its own snobbery. Reading Pip is closer to inhabiting a single mind from inside than anything else in Dickens's corpus. Other narrators in his fiction tend to be omniscient and confident; this one is haunted. The technique allowed Dickens to do something he had not quite done before — let a reader watch a character become wrong about his own life in real time, then watch the older version of the same character wince at what he had thought. First-person narration would become the dominant form of the novel after 1900; Great Expectations is one of the books that taught the form what kind of pressure it could carry.

What Becoming a Gentleman Costs

Pip spends most of the novel trying to leave the people who raised him. He stops visiting Joe at the forge. He is embarrassed when Joe comes to London and addresses him as "sir." He cannot quite bring himself to write home. Dickens is unflinching about what that distance does to the people Pip has abandoned and what it does to Pip — the small daily cruelties, the way shame poisons even his attempts at decency. When the truth about Magwitch arrives, the gentleman's life he had been building reveals itself as a debt to a transported convict, paid in his own contempt for everyone who actually loved him. The novel is the most honest portrait in Victorian fiction of class mobility as a moral injury, not a triumph. It is still the book to read on what is lost when someone leaves where they came from for somewhere supposedly better.

A Plot You Were Wrong About

For the first half of the novel, Pip — and the reader, and almost everyone in the book — believes that Miss Havisham is his secret benefactor and Estella his intended bride. The structure of the plot encourages this reading. The truth, when Magwitch reappears in Pip's London chambers in chapter thirty-nine, restructures every chapter that came before. The opening encounter on the marshes is no longer a frightening prologue but the foundational debt of Pip's whole adult life. Miss Havisham was using him, but not as he thought. The moral consequence is that Pip's gentlemanly identity was built on a stranger's gratitude rather than a romance, and the gratitude was attached to a man Pip would have been ashamed to know. The novel teaches the reader to read it twice — the second time noticing the clues Dickens planted in plain sight, knowing what Pip didn't.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The fraud of gentility.

Pip wants to be a gentleman more than anything. Dickens spends the novel systematically dismantling what that aspiration actually contains — inherited money, contempt for honest work, the erasure of people who love you. I want to track that argument chapter by chapter.

The unreliable narrator.

Pip tells his own story, but Dickens keeps signalling that Pip doesn't understand it. His snobbery is visible before he can see it; his goodness is visible when he can't. I want to read actively, noting every moment where Pip's self-perception and the actual evidence diverge.

How trauma is transmitted.

Miss Havisham was jilted on her wedding day and has stopped every clock in the house at that moment. She has spent decades turning Estella into a weapon. I want to follow that system carefully — what it does to Estella, what it does to Pip, and whether escape is possible.

The secret that changes everything.

There is a reveal in this novel that reorganises everything that came before it. I want to read toward that moment carefully, tracking every clue and irony Dickens plants — and then understand what it means morally that Pip's gentleman's life was funded by a transported convict.

Key figures

Pip
The Narrator

An orphan boy raised on the Kent marshes by his much older sister and her husband Joe, narrating his own story years later as an adult. He is bright, ashamed of his origins, and desperately susceptible to flattery. The voice that tells the novel is older and wiser than the boy it describes, and the tension between them — what the older Pip understands and what the younger one was doing — is the form of the book. He is educated by the loss of almost everything he wanted.

Estella
Havisham's Weapon

Adopted as a child by Miss Havisham and raised in a closed house to be beautiful, cold, and incapable of love. She tells Pip plainly, again and again, that she has no heart to give him. He refuses to believe her. The novel is exact that she is not responsible for what she has been made, and that her clarity about her own coldness is its own integrity. She marries the brutal Bentley Drummle. By the end, life has changed her.

Miss Havisham
Grief Frozen Into Cruelty

A wealthy spinster jilted at the altar in her youth, who has worn her wedding dress and left the cake on the table for decades, every clock stopped at twenty to nine. She has raised Estella expressly to take revenge on the male sex by making them love a girl who cannot love back. Her tragedy is that her revenge has worked too well: Estella will not love her either. The late scene in which she begs Pip's forgiveness on her knees is one of Dickens's most painful pages.

Abel Magwitch
The True Benefactor

The escaped convict in the opening chapter, terrified and starving, who threatens Pip into bringing him a file and a piece of pork pie. He never forgets the kindness. Transported to Australia for life, he makes a fortune in sheep and devotes it secretly to making Pip a gentleman. When he returns to London illegally, the truth of where Pip's money came from rewrites everything Pip thought about his life. His gradual transformation in Pip's eyes — from monster to embarrassment to dying friend — is the novel's moral spine.

Joe Gargery
The Blacksmith

Pip's brother-in-law, who married Pip's sister and then quietly raised the boy. He is a giant of a man, illiterate, gentle, completely without ambition or pretension. He calls Pip "old chap" with helpless affection. Pip is ashamed of him in London and avoids him for years. Joe nurses him through illness without reproach when he finally returns home broken. He is the standard by which Dickens measures everyone else in the novel, and the simplest thing in it.

Herbert Pocket
The True Friend

Pip's London roommate, a cheerful young man whose financial schemes never come off and whose engagement to Clara Barley stretches across years of patient waiting. He is the boy Pip once briefly fought in Miss Havisham's garden. He is also the only person in London who likes Pip for who he is rather than what he might become. Pip secretly arranges an investment to set Herbert up in business — the one genuinely good thing he does with his money — and Herbert never learns it was him.
