Revelation a guided tour

c. 95 CE · New Testament · Apocalyptic

An exiled man on a rocky Aegean island receives the strangest commission in the Bible. What he writes down will be argued about for two thousand years.

The book in brief

Revelation is the only sustained piece of apocalyptic literature in the New Testament, and to read it without knowing the genre is to read it badly. Apocalyptic — from the Greek apokalypsis, an unveiling — was a Jewish literary mode developed in the second and first centuries BCE in works like Daniel, 1 Enoch, and the Sibylline Oracles. It is addressed to communities under pressure, encoded in strange symbols, and structured to show that what looks like local catastrophe is part of a cosmic story that has already been decided. John writes from Patmos, an Aegean island where he has been exiled for his Christian witness during the persecution under Domitian (mid-90s CE), to seven specific churches on the mainland of Asia Minor.

The book has been read in every conceivable way for nineteen hundred years. It gives the throne in heaven, the Lamb who was slain, the four horsemen, the seven seals, the seven trumpets, the seven bowls, the woman clothed with the sun, the dragon, the beast, the whore of Babylon, the marriage of the Lamb, and the new heaven and new earth. It is the strangest book in the New Testament and the most ambitious. It is still being argued about.

Revelation 1 of 22
Revelation 1

The Vision of the Son of Man

Chapter 1 is the prologue and the first vision in one. John identifies himself, names the seven churches on the mainland, and declares the book's central claim: the one who was and is and is to come is already ruling. Then the vision proper begins. John hears a loud voice like a trumpet behind him and turns to see seven golden lampstands, and among them one like a son of man — a figure from Daniel 7 — whose hair is white as wool, whose eyes are like flames of fire, whose voice is like the sound of many waters, whose face shines like the sun in full strength. The figure's right hand holds seven stars. From his mouth comes a sharp two-edged sword. John falls at his feet as though dead. The figure places his right hand on him and says: do not be afraid. I am the first and the last, and the living one. I died, and behold I am alive for evermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades. The stars are the angels of the seven churches; the lampstands are the churches themselves. Everything that follows is sent to these communities.

Revelation 2

Letters to Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira

Chapter 2 contains four of the seven letters to the churches of Asia Minor. Each letter follows the same basic template: an address to the angel of the church (a heavenly representative, perhaps an overseer), a self-description of Christ drawn from the inaugural vision in chapter 1, a statement of what Christ knows about the church's situation, then commendation or rebuke or both, then a call to repent or hold fast, then a promise to the one who conquers, and a closing exhortation to hear what the Spirit says to the churches. The four churches covered here are Ephesus (praised for endurance, rebuked for losing its first love), Smyrna (praised but facing imminent persecution — ten days of tribulation — and told not to fear what it is about to suffer), Pergamum (praised for holding fast where Satan's throne is, rebuked for tolerating those who hold the teaching of Balaam and the Nicolaitans), and Thyatira (praised for love, faith, and service, rebuked severely for tolerating the woman Jezebel, who teaches and seduces). The letters are concrete community portraits rather than abstract theology.

Revelation 3

Letters to Sardis, Philadelphia, Laodicea

Chapter 3 completes the seven letters. Sardis (3:1-6) is told that it has a reputation for being alive but is in fact dead; only a few in the city have not soiled their garments. Philadelphia (3:7-13) receives an open door that no one can shut, a commendation for keeping Christ's word and not denying his name, and a promise to be kept from the hour of trial coming on the whole world. Laodicea (3:14-22) receives the most famous and most cutting of the seven letters: because the church is neither cold nor hot but lukewarm, Christ will spit it out of his mouth. The church thinks itself rich and prosperous and in need of nothing, but it is in fact wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked. The letter counsels the purchase of gold refined by fire, white garments to clothe the church's nakedness, and salve to anoint its eyes so that it may see. Then comes the most unexpected image in all seven letters: behold, I stand at the door and knock. The one who is rebuking Laodicea for its complacency is outside asking for entry. The chapter, and the first major section of the book, ends with the promise of the throne: the one who conquers will sit with Christ on his throne as he himself has sat with his Father on his Father's throne.

Revelation 4

The Throne Room in Heaven

Chapter 4 marks the transition from the seven letters to the visions proper. The structural signal is unmistakable: after this I looked, and behold, a door standing open in heaven. A voice like a trumpet — the same voice from chapter 1 — calls John to come up here. He is immediately in the Spirit and sees the throne in heaven with someone seated on it. The description of the one on the throne avoids any human figure: John offers only a comparison to the appearance of jasper and carnelian, with a rainbow around the throne like an emerald. Around the throne are twenty-four elders on twenty-four thrones, dressed in white with gold crowns on their heads. From the throne come flashes of lightning and peals of thunder. Before it burn seven torches of fire, which are the seven spirits of God. Before it also stretches a sea of glass, like crystal. And around the throne, on each of its four sides, are four living creatures — full of eyes in front and behind, the first like a lion, the second like an ox, the third with a human face, the fourth like an eagle in flight, each with six wings, each full of eyes. Day and night, without ceasing, they sing: holy, holy, holy is the Lord God Almighty, who was and is and is to come. When they give glory, the twenty-four elders fall before the throne and cast their crowns before it.

Revelation 5

The Slain Lamb Takes the Scroll

Chapter 5 is the theological heart of the entire book. In the right hand of the one on the throne John sees a scroll sealed with seven seals. A mighty angel proclaims with a loud voice: who is worthy to open the scroll and break its seals? No one in heaven or on earth or under the earth is able to open the scroll or to look into it. John weeps much, because no one is found worthy. One of the elders tells him to stop weeping — the lion of the tribe of Judah, the root of David, has conquered, so that he can open the scroll and its seven seals. John turns to see the lion. What he sees instead is a Lamb, standing as though it had been slain, with seven horns and seven eyes, which are the seven spirits of God sent out into all the earth. The Lamb comes and takes the scroll from the right hand of the one seated on the throne. Immediately the four creatures and the twenty-four elders fall down before the Lamb. A new song breaks out — worthy is the Lamb who was slain to receive power and wealth and wisdom and might and honour and glory and blessing — and every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea joins the chorus.

Revelation 6

The Six Seals and the Four Horsemen

Chapter 6 opens the first six of the seven seals. Each of the first four seals releases a rider on a colored horse — the four horsemen of the Apocalypse. The first rider is on a white horse, holding a bow, given a crown, and going out to conquer. The second is on a bright red horse, given a great sword and permitted to take peace from the earth so that people slay one another. The third is on a black horse, holding a balance scale; a voice announces famine prices — a denarius for a quart of wheat, a denarius for three quarts of barley — while commanding that the oil and wine not be damaged (protecting luxury goods while staples become unaffordable). The fourth rider is on a pale horse, and its rider's name is Death, with Hades following close behind; they are given authority over a quarter of the earth, to kill with sword and famine and pestilence and by wild beasts. The fifth seal reveals, under the heavenly altar, the souls of those who have been slaughtered for the word of God, crying out how long until their blood is avenged; they are given white robes and told to rest a little longer. The sixth seal brings catastrophic cosmic disturbance — earthquake, black sun, blood moon, falling stars, every mountain and island moved — and the great and small alike hide and call for the mountains to fall on them.

Revelation 7

The 144,000 and the Great Multitude

Chapter 7 is an interlude between the sixth and seventh seals — a deliberate pause before the final seal is opened, answering the question the sixth seal raised: who can stand? The chapter presents two related visions. In the first, four angels hold the four winds of the earth at the four corners, restraining them from blowing until the servants of God have been sealed on their foreheads. An angel comes from the east with the seal of the living God. The number sealed is 144,000 — twelve thousand from each of the twelve tribes of Israel, listed by name. The list of tribes is slightly unusual: Dan is omitted, Manasseh replaces it, and Levi is included. In the second vision, John sees a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed in white robes and holding palm branches. They cry out in a great voice: salvation belongs to our God who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb. The elders and four creatures fall down in worship. One of the elders explains to John who these are: they are coming out of the great tribulation and have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.

Revelation 8

The Seventh Seal and the First Four Trumpets

Chapter 8 opens the seventh seal and begins the trumpet sequence. When the seventh seal is opened, there is silence in heaven for about half an hour — perhaps the most striking non-event in the book, a rest before the next wave of judgment. Then seven angels who stand before God are given seven trumpets. Another angel with a golden censer offers much incense with the prayers of all the saints before the throne. Then the angel fills the censer with fire from the altar and throws it to the earth, and there are peals of thunder, rumblings, flashes of lightning, and an earthquake. The seven angels prepare to blow. The first trumpet: hail and fire mixed with blood thrown upon the earth — a third of the earth burned, a third of the trees, all green grass. The second: something like a great burning mountain thrown into the sea — a third of the sea becomes blood, a third of the sea creatures die, a third of the ships destroyed. The third: a great star blazing like a torch falls from heaven on a third of the rivers and springs — the star's name is Wormwood, and the waters become bitter, and many people die. The fourth: a third of the sun and moon and stars struck, so that a third of their light goes dark. An eagle cries out three woes for the three remaining trumpets.

Revelation 9

The Fifth and Sixth Trumpets: The Abyss Opened

Chapter 9 contains the fifth and sixth trumpets — the first two of the three woes. At the fifth trumpet, a star fallen from heaven receives the key to the shaft of the bottomless pit; it opens the pit and smoke pours out like the smoke of a great furnace, darkening the sun and the air, and from the smoke come locusts with the power of scorpions. But unlike actual locusts, they are commanded not to harm the grass or any plant or tree — only those who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads, and not to kill them but only to torment them for five months. The pain is like a scorpion's sting; in those days people will seek death and not find it. The locusts are described in elaborate visionary imagery — like horses prepared for battle, wearing golden crowns, human faces, women's hair, lions' teeth, iron breastplates, wings with a sound like many horses rushing into battle, and scorpion tails. Their king is the angel of the bottomless pit, whose name in Hebrew is Abaddon and in Greek Apollyon — Destroyer. At the sixth trumpet, the four angels who have been bound at the great river Euphrates are released to kill a third of mankind. They lead a cavalry of two hundred million whose horses have heads like lions and from whose mouths come fire and smoke and sulfur.

Revelation 10

The Mighty Angel and the Little Scroll

Chapter 10 is an interlude between the sixth and seventh trumpets, parallel in structure to the interlude in chapter 7. A mighty angel comes down from heaven, wrapped in a cloud, with a rainbow over his head, his face like the sun, his legs like pillars of fire, holding a little scroll open in his hand. He sets his right foot on the sea and his left on the land and calls out with a loud voice like a lion roaring; seven thunders sound in response, but when John is about to write what the thunders said, a voice from heaven tells him to seal up what the seven thunders have said and not write it. The angel swears by the one who lives forever and ever that there will be no more delay: when the seventh angel is about to blow his trumpet, the mystery of God would be fulfilled, just as he announced to his servants the prophets. Then John is told to take the scroll from the angel and eat it. It will be sweet as honey in his mouth but bitter in his stomach. So he eats it. It is sweet as honey and then bitter. He is told he must again prophesy about many peoples and nations and languages and kings. The scene is a direct parallel to Ezekiel 2:8-3:3, where the prophet is given a scroll to eat that is sweet as honey before he is sent to speak to Israel.

Revelation 11

The Two Witnesses and the Seventh Trumpet

Chapter 11 continues the interlude and then resolves the trumpet sequence. John is given a measuring rod to measure the temple of God, but is told to leave out the outer court — it is given over to the nations, who will trample the holy city for forty-two months. Two witnesses will prophesy for 1,260 days clothed in sackcloth; they are the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth. They have power to shut the sky that no rain falls, to turn water to blood, and to strike the earth with plagues as often as they wish. When they have finished their testimony, the beast that rises from the bottomless pit makes war on them and kills them; their bodies lie in the street of the great city for three and a half days while the peoples of the earth gloat and make merry and exchange gifts. But after three and a half days a breath of life from God enters them and they stand up; great fear falls on those who see them; they are called up to heaven in a cloud. An earthquake kills seven thousand people. The survivors give glory to the God of heaven. Then the seventh trumpet sounds, and voices in heaven announce: the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever. The twenty-four elders fall and worship.

Revelation 12

The Woman, the Dragon, and the War in Heaven

Chapter 12 is the pivot of the entire book — the structural centre around which everything else in the cosmic drama turns. A great sign appears in heaven: a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet and a crown of twelve stars on her head, pregnant and crying out in labor. Another sign: a great red dragon with seven heads and ten horns and seven diadems on his heads, whose tail sweeps a third of the stars from heaven. The dragon stands before the woman to devour her child when it is born. She gives birth to a male child who is to rule all the nations with a rod of iron; the child is caught up to God and to his throne. The woman flees to the wilderness where she has a place prepared by God for 1,260 days. War breaks out in heaven: Michael and his angels fight against the dragon; the dragon is thrown down to the earth and his angels with him. A loud voice announces his defeat: the accuser of our brothers has been thrown down, who accuses them before God day and night. He has been conquered by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, for they did not love their lives even unto death. The dragon, enraged at his defeat, pursues the woman; she is given wings of an eagle to fly to the wilderness. He sends a flood from his mouth after her; the earth swallows the flood. The dragon goes off to make war on the rest of her offspring, those who keep the commandments of God and hold to the testimony of Jesus.

Revelation 13

The Two Beasts: Sea and Land

Chapter 13 introduces the two beasts that are the dragon's primary agents in the world. The first rises from the sea — ten horns and seven heads, with ten diadems on its horns, and blasphemous names on its heads. It is like a leopard with feet like a bear and a mouth like a lion; the dragon gives it his power and his throne and great authority. One of its heads seems to have a mortal wound that has healed; the whole earth marvels and follows the beast, worshiping the dragon because he gave the beast its authority. The beast is given a mouth to utter haughty and blasphemous words, is given authority for forty-two months, and makes war on the saints and conquers them. The second beast rises from the earth; it has two horns like a lamb but speaks like a dragon. It exercises all the authority of the first beast, makes the earth worship the first beast, performs great signs, deceives those who dwell on the earth, tells them to make an image of the beast, and causes all — small and great, rich and poor, free and slave — to be marked on the right hand or the forehead. Without this mark, no one can buy or sell. The mark is the number of the beast — six hundred sixty-six.

Revelation 14

The Lamb on Zion and the Harvest of the Earth

Chapter 14 provides a series of visions that function as previews of the end — parallel announcements of what is coming before the bowl sequence delivers it. The Lamb stands on Mount Zion with the 144,000, who have the Lamb's name and his Father's name written on their foreheads. They sing a new song before the throne that only they can learn — they have not defiled themselves with women, for they are virgins; they follow the Lamb wherever he goes; they are firstfruits to God and to the Lamb. Then three angels appear in sequence. The first proclaims the eternal gospel to every nation and tribe and language and people — fear God and give him glory, for the hour of his judgment has come. The second announces: fallen, fallen is Babylon the great, who made all nations drink the wine of her sexual immorality. The third declares with a loud voice that those who worship the beast and receive its mark will drink the wine of God's wrath, poured full strength into the cup of his anger, and be tormented with fire and sulfur before the holy angels and before the Lamb. Then a voice from heaven tells John to write: blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on. Then two harvest visions: a son of man with a golden crown and sharp sickle reaps the earth; another angel gathers the grapes of the earth and throws them into the great winepress of the wrath of God, and blood flows from the press, as high as a horse's bridle, for 1,600 stadia.

Revelation 15

The Sea of Glass and the Seven Bowls Prepared

Chapter 15 is the shortest chapter in the book and functions as the preparatory scene for the final and most intense series of judgments. John sees another sign in heaven — great and marvelous — seven angels with seven plagues, which are the last, for with them the wrath of God is finished. Before the throne is what appears to be a sea of glass mingled with fire, and standing beside it are those who had conquered the beast and its image and the number of its name, holding harps of God. They sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb — a hymn that combines Exodus imagery with Psalms vocabulary: great and amazing are your deeds, O Lord God the Almighty; just and true are your ways, O King of the nations. Then the temple in heaven — the tabernacle of the witness — is opened, and from it come the seven angels with seven golden bowls full of the wrath of God who lives forever and ever. The temple is filled with smoke from the glory of God and from his power, and no one is able to enter the temple until the seven plagues of the seven angels are finished.

Revelation 16

The Seven Bowls of Wrath

Chapter 16 is the uninterrupted bowl sequence — the most relentless series of judgments in the book, without the interludes that interrupted the seals and trumpets. The seven bowls are poured in rapid succession. The first: harmful and painful sores on those who bear the mark of the beast and worship its image. The second: the sea becomes like the blood of a corpse, and every living thing in the sea dies. The third: the rivers and springs of water become blood; the angel of waters pronounces this just because they shed the blood of saints. The fourth: the sun is given power to scorch people with fire; they curse God and do not repent. The fifth: the throne of the beast is plunged into darkness; people gnaw their tongues in anguish and curse God. The sixth: the Euphrates is dried up to prepare the way for the kings from the east; three unclean spirits like frogs come from the dragon, the beast, and the false prophet to assemble the kings of the whole world for the battle on the great day of God the Almighty — at a place called Armageddon. The seventh: a voice from the temple says it is done; lightning, thunder, and a great earthquake greater than any since man was on the earth; Babylon is remembered before God; the great city splits into three parts; islands and mountains flee; hailstones of about a hundred pounds each fall from heaven.

Revelation 17

Babylon the Great Whore

Chapter 17 is one of the most explicitly decoded visions in the book: an angel shows John a vision and then explains it. One of the seven bowl-angels carries John away in the Spirit to a wilderness and shows him a woman sitting on a scarlet beast full of blasphemous names, with seven heads and ten horns. The woman is dressed in purple and scarlet, adorned with gold and jewels and pearls, holding a golden cup full of abominations and the impurities of her sexual immorality. On her forehead a name: Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes and of earth's abominations. She is drunk with the blood of the saints and the martyrs of Jesus. The angel explains: the beast that was, and is not, and is about to rise from the bottomless pit carries the woman. The seven heads are seven mountains on which the woman is seated — and they are seven kings, five of whom have fallen, one who is, and one who is yet to come. The beast itself is an eighth who belongs to the seven. The ten horns are ten kings who have not yet received kingdoms but will receive authority as kings for one hour together with the beast. These will make war on the Lamb, and the Lamb will conquer them, for he is Lord of lords and King of kings.

Revelation 18

The Fall of Babylon

Chapter 18 is a sustained lament — the longest single scene in Revelation — mourning the fall of Babylon-Rome in the tradition of the Hebrew Bible's oracles against the nations. A mighty angel comes down from heaven with great authority, and the earth is made bright with his glory; he cries with a mighty voice: fallen, fallen is Babylon the great. She has become a dwelling place of demons and a haunt for every unclean spirit and bird. A voice from heaven calls: come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins. Her plagues will come in a single day. Three groups lament: the kings of the earth who committed immorality with her weep and wail when they see the smoke of her burning; the merchants of the earth weep because no one buys their cargoes anymore — and the cargo list that follows is the most precise inventory of imperial commerce in the New Testament, ending with slaves and human lives; the shipmasters and sailors stand far off and cry as they watch the smoke of her burning. Heaven, saints, apostles, and prophets are called to rejoice over her, for God has given judgment for you against her. A mighty angel takes up a stone like a great millstone and throws it into the sea: so will Babylon the great city be thrown down with violence, and will be found no more.

Revelation 19

The Marriage of the Lamb and the Rider on the White Horse

Chapter 19 provides the positive counterpart to chapter 18's lament. A great multitude in heaven cries hallelujah — the only occurrence of the word in the New Testament — in response to the smoke of Babylon rising forever. The twenty-four elders and four creatures fall down and worship. The voice calls for praise from all God's servants. Then the announcement of the marriage of the Lamb: his bride has made herself ready; she has been given fine linen, bright and pure, for the fine linen is the righteous deeds of the saints. A voice tells John to write: blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb. John falls down before the angel who shows him this and is told sharply to stand up and worship God only — the worship of any figure but God is refused. Then heaven opens and a white horse appears, whose rider is called Faithful and True, who in righteousness judges and makes war. His eyes are like a flame of fire; on his head are many diadems; he has a name written that no one knows but himself; he is clothed in a robe dipped in blood; his name is called the Word of God. The armies of heaven follow him on white horses, clothed in fine linen. From his mouth comes a sharp sword with which to strike down the nations. On his robe and on his thigh is written: King of kings and Lord of lords. The beast and its armies are gathered. The beast and the false prophet are thrown alive into the lake of fire. The rest are slain by the sword of the one riding the horse.

Revelation 20

The Millennium and the Final Judgment

Chapter 20 contains the millennium — the only passage in the Bible that explicitly describes a thousand-year reign of Christ — and the final judgment before the great white throne. An angel descends from heaven with the key to the bottomless pit and a great chain; he seizes the dragon, the ancient serpent who is the devil and Satan, binds him for a thousand years, throws him into the pit, and seals it. The millennium then: John sees thrones with those seated on them, to whom judgment was committed. He sees also the souls of those who had been beheaded for the testimony of Jesus and for the word of God, who had not worshiped the beast — they came to life and reigned with Christ for a thousand years. This is the first resurrection. Blessed and holy is the one who shares in the first resurrection, for the second death has no power over them. After the thousand years Satan is released, goes out to deceive the nations at the four corners of the earth — Gog and Magog — and gathers them for battle. Fire comes down from heaven and consumes them; the devil is thrown into the lake of fire, where the beast and false prophet already are, and they will be tormented day and night forever and ever. Then the great white throne: earth and sky flee from the one seated on it. The dead are judged from what was written in the books, each one according to his works. Death and Hades are thrown into the lake of fire — this is the second death. Anyone whose name is not found written in the book of life is thrown into the lake of fire.

Revelation 21

The New Heaven, New Earth, and New Jerusalem

Chapter 21 is the great resolution. John sees a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away and the sea was no more. He sees the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. A loud voice from the throne announces: behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God. He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning nor crying nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away. The one seated on the throne says: behold, I am making all things new. Then the New Jerusalem itself in extended description: the city is twelve thousand stadia on a side, a perfect cube; its walls are jasper, its streets of gold transparent as glass; twelve gates of pearl, three on each side, named for the twelve tribes; twelve foundations bearing the names of the twelve apostles. There is no temple in the city — the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple. No need of sun or moon — the glory of God is its light, and its lamp is the Lamb. The nations will walk by its light; the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it; its gates will never be shut by day — and there will be no night there.

Revelation 22

The River of Life and the Closing Benediction

Chapter 22 completes the vision of the New Jerusalem and closes the entire book. The angel shows John the river of the water of life, bright as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb down the middle of the great street of the city. On either side of the river is the tree of life with its twelve kinds of fruit, yielding its fruit each month, and the leaves of the tree are for the healing of the nations. No longer will there be anything accursed; the throne of God and of the Lamb will be in the city, and his servants will worship him; they will see his face, and his name will be on their foreheads. There will be no night; they will need no light of lamp or sun, for the Lord God will be their light, and they will reign forever and ever. Then the angel confirms the trustworthiness of these words, and Jesus himself speaks: I am coming soon. Blessed is the one who keeps the words of the prophecy of this book. John falls at the angel's feet, is told not to worship the angel but God. He is told not to seal up the words of this prophecy, for the time is near. Another word from Jesus: I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end. Blessed are those who wash their robes, so that they may have the right to the tree of life. The Spirit and the bride say, come. The one who hears says, come. The one who is thirsty, come. The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all. Amen.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

Apocalyptic as a Genre

Revelation cannot be read well without the literary genre to which it belongs. Apocalyptic emerged in Jewish literature in the second century BCE as a way to address communities under pressure — encoding political critique in symbolic vision that the oppressor could not use against the writer.

The Lamb at the Centre of the Throne

In chapter 5, John is told the lion of Judah has conquered and can open the sealed scroll. He turns expecting a lion. He sees a Lamb, standing as if it had been slain. The substitution is the theological centre of the book.

The Whore of Babylon and the New Jerusalem

The book is structured around two contrasting female figures. The whore of Babylon — Rome, the imperial city of commerce and bloodshed. The bride of the New Jerusalem — the city of God descending from heaven. The reader is asked which city she is a citizen of.

The Numbers and the Sevens

Revelation is built around numerical structures. Seven is the number of completion. Four is the created world. Twelve is the people of God. Three-and-a-half is the broken time of suffering. And 666 is a number that explicitly invites decoding.

How to Read the Book

Revelation has been read in more incompatible ways than any other book in the Bible. The four major schools of interpretation — preterist, historicist, futurist, idealist — each captures something the others miss. The responsible reader needs all four.

John of Patmos
Seer and writer

The author of the book, exiled to the small Aegean island of Patmos for his Christian witness during the persecution under Domitian (mid-90s CE). He identifies himself as John (1:1, 1:4, 1:9, 22:8) and as a brother and partner in the tribulation of the seven churches. Whether he is the apostle John, son of Zebedee, is contested — the differences in style, vocabulary, and theology between the gospel and the apocalypse make a single author unlikely to many scholars. What is undisputed is that the writer is a Jewish-Christian deeply formed in the Hebrew Bible, capable of constructing one of the most complex pieces of apocalyptic literature ever produced.

The Lamb
The central figure of the vision

In chapter 5, when no one is found worthy to open the sealed scroll, John is told the lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered. He turns expecting a lion and sees a Lamb, standing as if it had been slain. The substitution is the theological centre. The Lamb opens the seven seals, stands on Mount Zion with the 144,000, is the lamp of the New Jerusalem, and sits on the throne with the one who sits on the throne. The image asserts that heaven rules not by conquering force but by the slain-and-living Lamb, and the pattern of cross and resurrection is built into the cosmology of the book.

The Dragon
Satan, the deceiver

The great red dragon of chapter 12, with seven heads and ten horns, whose tail sweeps down a third of the stars of heaven. He stands before the woman who is about to give birth, intending to devour the child. The text gives the dragon a fourfold identification (12:9): that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world. War breaks out in heaven and the dragon is thrown down. He gives his authority to the beast from the sea in chapter 13. He is bound for a thousand years in chapter 20, loosed briefly, and at last thrown into the lake of fire.

The Beast
The antichrist figure

There are actually two beasts in chapter 13. The beast from the sea has seven heads and ten horns, draws its authority from the dragon, and is given to make war on the saints. One of its heads has a mortal wound that has healed — most likely a reference to the Nero redivivus legend current in the late first century. The beast from the land has two horns like a lamb but speaks like a dragon; it makes the earth worship the first beast and forces all to receive the mark of the beast — 666 — without which no one can buy or sell. The figure has had an enormous afterlife in later Christian apocalypticism, where it became the antichrist of medieval and modern speculation.

The Whore of Babylon
Imperial city

The female figure of chapters 17 and 18, seated on a scarlet beast, dressed in purple and scarlet, holding a golden cup of abominations, drunk with the blood of the saints. On her forehead: Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes. The angel decodes the symbolism explicitly: the seven heads of the beast are seven mountains — the reference, in the first-century Mediterranean, is unmistakable. Rome is the city on seven hills. The long lament for her fall in chapter 18 is one of the most precise indictments of imperial economy in any ancient text, ending its cargo list with slaves and human lives.

The Bride / New Jerusalem
The city of God

The structural counterpart to the whore of Babylon and the figure of the book's closing vision. The New Jerusalem comes down out of heaven from God in chapter 21, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband — a perfect cube, twelve thousand stadia on a side, walls of jasper, streets of gold, twelve gates of pearl. No temple — the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple. The river of the water of life flows from the throne, and the tree of life stands on either side, with leaves for the healing of the nations. The figure is at once city, bride, and people of God.
