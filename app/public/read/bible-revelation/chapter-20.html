Summary

Chapter 20 contains the most disputed passage in the entire book and one of the most disputed in the entire New Testament: the millennium. The three major Christian positions on the thousand years — premillennialism (Christ will return before the millennium and reign on earth during it), postmillennialism (the church will progressively Christianize the world and Christ will return at the end of a golden age), and amillennialism (the thousand years is a symbolic description of the present age of the church between the resurrection and the final return) — have generated enormous theological controversy and continued to do so. The key exegetical question is whether the first resurrection (20:4-5) is a literal bodily resurrection of martyrs before the millennium or a spiritual resurrection (conversion and participation in Christ's life) experienced by the church throughout the present age. The amillennial and postmillennial readings take the latter view; premillennialism takes the former. All three positions have been held by serious biblical scholars, and the passage alone does not resolve the debate.

The Gog and Magog of the brief post-millennial battle (20:7-10) are drawn from Ezekiel 38-39, where Gog of the land of Magog is the leader of a northern coalition that attacks Israel in the last days and is destroyed by divine intervention. In Revelation the names have become symbolic for all the nations gathered against God at the end — they are from the four corners of the earth, a universal rather than specifically northern force. The fire from heaven that destroys them recalls both Ezekiel 39:6 and the fire from heaven that the false prophet performed as a sign in chapter 13, but this fire is divine and direct. The great white throne judgment (20:11-15) is the most compressed final-judgment scene in the New Testament. Earth and sky flee from the one seated on the throne — the dissolution of the present created order. The books are opened, and also the book of life. The double criterion — each one according to his works, but also the book of life — has generated as much theological discussion as any passage in the epistle tradition. The lake of fire receiving Death and Hades as personified powers is the book's climactic symbolic statement: death is not the final reality. The second death is.
