Revelation 10

Between the sixth and seventh trumpets, a mighty angel descends with a little scroll — John eats it and is recommissioned as a prophet.

Summary

The interlude of chapter 10 slows the trumpet sequence at its most intense point and returns the reader's attention to John himself and the act of prophesying. The mighty angel's description is deliberately parallel to the inaugural vision of Christ in chapter 1 — the face like the sun, the voice like a lion, the feet like pillars of fire — raising the question of whether this angel is a Christophany or a high-ranking angelic being carrying divine commission. The consensus in modern scholarship is that he is an angel rather than Christ, partly because John is elsewhere careful not to confuse angelic messengers with Christ (see 19:10, 22:8-9 where John's attempt to worship an angel is firmly refused), but the deliberate parallelism with chapter 1 is certainly intentional and emphasizes the divine authority behind the commission. The detail that seven thunders spoke but their content was sealed (10:4) is one of the rare moments in Revelation where the visionary explicitly withholds information. The book thus acknowledges its own incompleteness: there are things John saw and was not permitted to record. The mystery of God that will be fulfilled at the seventh trumpet (10:7) links this interlude to the cosmic scope of the book's closing vision.

The scroll-eating scene (10:8-11) draws directly on Ezekiel 3:1-3 and signals that John understands himself to be standing in the tradition of the Hebrew prophets. In Ezekiel the scroll is the lament and mourning and woe written on it that the prophet is to speak to the rebellious house of Israel; the sweetness of eating it is the delight of the divine word before its difficult content must be proclaimed. In Revelation the same duality applies: the scroll is sweet in the mouth (the revelation of the mystery of God, the coming of the new Jerusalem, the victory of the Lamb) and bitter in the stomach (the suffering, martyrdom, and tribulation that the prophecy announces and that John himself is experiencing in exile). The recommissioning — you must again prophesy about many peoples and nations and languages and kings — echoes Jeremiah 1:10 and places John in the tradition of the prophet sent to the nations. The interlude is the book's internal commissioning narrative, its own account of why John is writing what he is writing.
