Revelation 22

The river of life flows from the throne; the tree of life returns; and the book closes with the oldest liturgical exchange in Christianity — come, Lord Jesus.

Summary

Chapter 22 closes the New Jerusalem vision with two images drawn from the beginning of the Hebrew Bible. The river of the water of life (22:1-2) recalls the river flowing out of Eden in Genesis 2:10-14, as well as the river flowing from the temple in Ezekiel 47:1-12 and the living waters flowing from Jerusalem in Zechariah 14:8. The tree of life (22:2) recalls the tree in the garden of Eden from which Adam and Eve were excluded after the fall (Genesis 3:22-24). Revelation's closing vision thus frames the entire biblical narrative as a movement from the garden-with-tree-of-life to the city-with-tree-of-life — from the original creation to the new creation, with the long journey of human history in between. The detail that the tree's leaves are for the healing of the nations (22:2) is drawn from Ezekiel 47:12 and suggests that the eschatological city is not simply a restoration of the pre-fall Eden but an expansion and universalization of it: the tree that was in one garden is now in one city that encompasses all nations.

The closing section of the book (22:6-21) is a rapid dialogue between multiple voices — the angel, Jesus, John, the Spirit, the bride, the reader — in a way that breaks down the narrative frame and addresses the reader of the book directly and urgently. The three-fold I am coming soon from Jesus (22:7, 12, 20), matched by the three-fold come from the Spirit, the bride, and the reader (22:17, 20), is the oldest attested Christian liturgical exchange: maranatha, come Lord, appears in 1 Corinthians 16:22 and the Didache and is here given its fullest literary form. The beatitude for those who keep the words of the prophecy of this book (22:7) echoes the opening beatitude of 1:3 and frames the entire book as a gift to its readers rather than a puzzle to be decoded. The warning against adding to or taking from the words of the book (22:18-19) is a formula common in ancient literature to mark a text's completeness and authority, found also in Deuteronomy 4:2 and 12:32. The final benediction — the grace of the Lord Jesus be with all — is an epistolary closing that returns the book to its opening frame: Revelation is, from first to last, a letter from a community in exile to communities under pressure, written in the form of a vision, ending with the grace common to all the letters of the New Testament.
