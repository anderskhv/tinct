The Communist Manifesto

1848 CE · by Karl Marx & Friedrich Engels

The book in brief

The Communist Manifesto argues that all recorded history is the history of class struggle, that capitalism is a transient stage which creates the conditions for its own overthrow, and that the working class must seize political power and abolish private ownership of the means of production. The text is short and propulsive — a pamphlet rather than a treatise — and it makes its case in four sections: the historical role of the bourgeoisie, the position and tasks of the proletariat, an attack on rival socialist schools, and the relation of communists to other parties of opposition. It ends with a ten-point program and the line that became its slogan: workers of all countries, unite.

Marx and Engels wrote it on commission from the Communist League, a small organization of émigré German workers based in London. The text was published in February 1848 in German, weeks before revolutions broke out in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan, and a dozen other European capitals. Marx was twenty-nine, Engels twenty-seven. The first printing ran to a few hundred copies; for the next twenty years the Manifesto was an obscure document of a defeated movement. Its rise to global influence began in the 1870s and accelerated through the Second International, the Russian Revolution, and the Cold War.

It is the most consequential political pamphlet ever written. As literature it is also unusual — Marx's polemical voice braided with Engels's economic compression, the diagnosis of capitalism as relentlessly modern delivered in prose that is itself relentlessly modern. The Manifesto's admiration for what the bourgeoisie has built is as vivid as its conviction that the bourgeoisie must fall.

Why it still matters

History as Class Struggle

The Manifesto argues that history is the story of class conflict — between masters and slaves in antiquity, lords and serfs under feudalism, factory owners and workers under capitalism. The framework treats every political event as the visible surface of an economic antagonism beneath it: revolutions, wars, religious movements, cultural shifts all reread as the play of class interest. The claim has shaped historical writing far beyond the communist movement; even historians who reject the conclusion borrow the question. Asking who profits from a given arrangement, who controls the means of production, whose labor is being exploited and at what wage — the question is now standard equipment in social and economic history. The Manifesto did not invent class analysis, but it gave the method its sharpest formulation, and almost every historian working today has inherited the move whether or not they would acknowledge the source.

Capitalism as a Dynamic Process

Marx and Engels were among the first to see capitalism not as a fixed economic system but as a process — concentrating capital, dissolving traditional structures, creating global markets, reshaping every relationship into a transaction. The Manifesto's description of bourgeois society — that all that is solid melts into air, that the bourgeoisie cannot exist without constantly revolutionizing the instruments of production — anticipates the language of disruption, globalization, and creative destruction by a hundred and fifty years. Joseph Schumpeter, no Marxist, took the phrase creative destruction straight from Marx. The diagnosis of capitalism as relentlessly modern, restless, dissolving every fixed relationship into a calculation of profit and loss, is one of the most accurate descriptions of the system any of its critics or defenders have produced. Twenty-first-century writers describing platform capitalism, financialization, or the gig economy are still working in vocabulary the Manifesto established.

A Document That Made History

The Manifesto did not remain theory. The Paris Commune of 1871 invoked it. The Russian Revolution of 1917 was made in its name. The Chinese, Cuban, and Vietnamese revolutions all claimed it as foundational, and dozens of post-colonial movements across Africa and Asia took it as a reference text in the second half of the twentieth century. Roughly a third of the world's population lived under governments calling themselves communist at some point in that century. Whatever one thinks of those regimes — and the record, in lives lost and freedoms suspended, is mostly catastrophic — they are unintelligible without the text that justified them. The Manifesto is the rare political document whose consequences are commensurate with its ambition. To read it is to read the source of an immense amount of what happened next, and to understand why a thirty-page pamphlet from 1848 is still cited in political argument today.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The bourgeois revolution is incomplete.

Marx does not simply attack the bourgeoisie — he spends several pages admiring it. The bourgeoisie has been the most revolutionary class in history, dissolving feudalism, creating the world market, dragging every nation into modernity. Read Part I asking: what does Marx think the bourgeoisie actually achieved, and why does that achievement contain the seed of its own undoing?

Class struggle as the engine of history.

The opening sentence is the thesis: the history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles. Every political conflict, every war, every cultural transformation is, for Marx, a surface expression of deeper antagonisms between economic classes. Read asking whether the historical examples he gives actually support the framework — and where the framework strains.

The 10-point program.

Part II ends with a concrete list: ten demands the Communists would immediately enact upon seizing power. A graduated income tax. Free education. Abolition of child labour. Centralisation of credit in a national bank. Read the list and note how many of its demands are now enacted in liberal democracies — and ask what that means for the revolutionary argument.

The cash nexus.

The most vivid passage in the Manifesto describes how the bourgeoisie has drowned every human bond — religious devotion, chivalry, family sentiment — in the icy water of egotistical calculation. "All that is solid melts into air." Read asking whether this description of modernity feels more like a critique or an accurate diagnosis, and whether Marx thought it could be reversed.

Key figures

Karl Marx
THE THEORIST

A Prussian-born philosopher and journalist, twenty-nine years old in 1848, recently expelled from France and writing in Brussels. Trained in Hegelian philosophy, radicalized by the suppression of his newspaper, Marx supplied the Manifesto its historical theory and its polemical edge. He had not yet written Capital. He would spend the next thirty-five years in London building the economic argument the Manifesto only sketches.

Friedrich Engels
THE PARTNER

Son of a Rhineland textile manufacturer, twenty-seven in 1848, who had spent the early 1840s managing his father's mill in Manchester and writing The Condition of the Working Class in England from what he saw there. Engels supplied the empirical knowledge of factory production and the editorial discipline that compressed Marx's drafts. He also supplied the money, for forty years, that kept the Marx household afloat.

The Bourgeoisie
THE MODERN RULING CLASS

The class that owns the means of production — factories, capital, the world market. Marx credits the bourgeoisie with more revolutionary energy than any class in history: it destroyed feudalism, created the global economy, and pulled every nation into modernity. That same energy, he argues, has produced the proletariat — and so produced the conditions for its own overthrow at the hands of the class it created.

The Proletariat
THE GRAVEDIGGERS

The class that sells its labor for wages — the industrial working class produced by the same machinery that made the bourgeoisie powerful. The Manifesto's central claim is that the proletariat is the first class in history whose liberation requires the abolition of all classes, including itself. It cannot rule a society as the bourgeoisie did; it can only end the conditions that produced it.

The Utopian Socialists
THE RIVALS

Saint-Simon, Fourier, and Owen — the earlier socialist thinkers Marx attacks in Part III. Marx grants their critique of capitalism but rejects their method: model communities, moral persuasion, appeals to enlightened employers. They imagined socialism as a plan to be drawn up; Marx insists it can only emerge from the contradictions of the existing order. The polemic against them defines what scientific socialism, by contrast, is supposed to be.

The Old Powers
THE HOLY ALLIANCE

Pope and Czar, Metternich and Guizot — the figures named in the opening sentence as the powers gathered to exorcise the spectre of communism. Pope Pius IX, Czar Nicholas I, Austrian chancellor Klemens von Metternich, and French foreign minister François Guizot represented the conservative European order restored after Napoleon. Within months of the Manifesto's publication, that order would be in flames across the continent.
