The Aeneid

19 BCE · by Virgil

The book in brief

The Aeneid is the story of Aeneas, a Trojan prince who survives the fall of Troy and is destined by the gods to found the line that will become Rome. He flees the burning city carrying his father on his back. He is shipwrecked at Carthage, where Queen Dido takes him in and falls in love with him; the gods order him to leave, and Dido kills herself with his sword as his fleet pulls away. He visits the underworld and is shown the future of Rome by his dead father. He arrives in Italy and must fight a war against the local prince Turnus for the land and the king's daughter. The poem ends in the middle of the duel — Aeneas standing over the wounded Turnus, who is begging for his life, and choosing to kill him.

Virgil composed it in Latin hexameter for the emperor Augustus between 29 and 19 BCE. He read sections aloud in the imperial household and was still revising when he died. He left instructions that the unfinished manuscript be burned. Augustus refused. The Aeneid is twelve books long, deliberately modeled on Homer — the first six echo the Odyssey of wandering, the last six echo the Iliad of war — and was published with two unfinished half-lines preserved as Virgil left them.

It is the great Latin epic and the founding myth of the Roman state, written by a poet who saw the Republic die and the Empire begin. Dante chose Virgil as his guide through hell because the Aeneid was the closest thing Latin literature had to a sacred text. T. S. Eliot called it the classic of Europe. The poem celebrates Roman destiny and refuses to look away from what destiny costs — half-loyal to the conquered, written in praise of the conquerors, never quite resolved on whether the founding was worth the price.

Why it still matters

The Founding of an Empire as Tragedy

The Aeneid was commissioned, in effect, to give Augustus's new Rome a national epic on the scale of Homer's Greece. Virgil delivered the epic. He did not deliver the propaganda. Aeneas leaves Dido, who kills herself; he kills Turnus, who has surrendered; he founds the Roman state. The poem does not celebrate any of these acts. It shows founding as costly and asks, with steady refusal to provide a clean answer, whether the cost was worth what was built. Rome's own poet, writing for Rome's first emperor, was not entirely sure. No other founding myth of any major civilization has been written with this much skepticism about its own subject. Augustus saved the manuscript Virgil had asked to be destroyed; whether the emperor read what Virgil had actually written, and what he made of it, is one of the better questions in the history of literature.

The Half-Heard Voice of the Defeated

The Aeneid is the story of Troy's survivors — the losing side of Homer's Iliad — written by a poet whose own Roman empire was built largely on conquest. Virgil keeps showing the reader those who lose. Dido, abandoned at Carthage, becomes the figure for every people Rome will later destroy. Turnus dies begging for life. The Latin women rage as their sons are killed. The souls in the underworld walk in long quiet lines. Virgil does not abandon his Roman loyalty. He does, again and again, give the conquered the most affecting language in the poem. The technique has been borrowed by every later writer who has tried to be loyal to a victorious side without lying about what victory cost.

Dante's Guide for a Reason

Dante put Virgil in Limbo because as a pagan he could not enter Paradise, but he made him the speaker for human reason and the one who guides the pilgrim through hell and most of purgatory. The Divine Comedy is unthinkable without the Aeneid: Dante's underworld borrows directly from Book VI, his political vision borrows from Anchises's prophecy in the same book, and his sense of poetry as a sacred vocation borrows from Virgil's example. Reading the Aeneid opens up Dante, and through Dante it opens up most of medieval and Renaissance literature. Milton's Paradise Lost is partly an English answer to Virgil. Tasso, Spenser, and Tennyson are all working in the same tradition. The Aeneid is the trunk; the rest of European epic is the branching.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The price of destiny.

Aeneas is called "pious" more than any other character — but his pietas requires him to leave Dido, abandon the past, and subordinate everything personal to a future he will not live to see. The poem keeps the cost visible.

Dido's tragedy.

She is the most fully realised character in the poem — a brilliant queen who loves the wrong man at the wrong moment. Her books (I and IV) are the emotional heart of the Aeneid. What Aeneas does to her is not presented as simple.

Homer's shadow.

Books I–VI echo the Odyssey; Books VII–XII echo the Iliad. Virgil knew his readers would hold both in their heads. Notice where Aeneas is a lesser hero than Odysseus or Achilles — and ask whether that is a flaw or the whole point.

Duty as a philosophy of life.

Pietas is what makes Aeneas Roman rather than Greek. The poem invites you to ask whether a man who suppresses all personal desire for collective purpose is admirable or hollow — and never quite gives you a clean answer.

Key figures

Aeneas
The Duty-Bound

Trojan prince, son of Venus, survivor of Troy's fall, destined founder of the line that will become Rome. He carries his father out of the burning city on his back and loses his wife in the smoke. He does what fate and gods require, including leaving Dido and killing Turnus. Whether his obedience makes him heroic or hollow is the question the poem refuses to settle. Virgil calls him pius — devoted, dutiful — more than a hundred times, with steady ambiguity.

Dido
Queen of Carthage

The widowed queen of Carthage, who has built a great city in exile after her husband's murder. She receives Aeneas's storm-battered fleet, listens to his account of Troy, and falls catastrophically in love with him during a hunt interrupted by a divine storm. When the gods order him to leave, she kills herself with the sword he leaves behind, cursing his line. Her story occupies Books I and IV. It is the emotional center of the poem and the place where Virgil's loyalty most visibly cracks.

Turnus
The Rival

Prince of the Rutulians, betrothed to the Italian princess Lavinia before Aeneas arrived to claim her. He is not a villain. He is a young warrior defending the woman he was promised and the land he was born in, and the poem makes you understand why his fight is his to fight. The last six books are largely his story. He dies in the final lines, surrendering, asking for mercy that Aeneas cannot bring himself to grant.

Juno
The Divine Obstacle

Queen of the gods, implacable enemy of Troy ever since Paris judged Venus more beautiful than her. She drives the plot through twelve books of divine stubbornness — the storm that wrecks Aeneas at Carthage, the war in Italy, the rage that takes hold of Turnus and the Italian queen Amata. Even at the end, when Jupiter has overruled her, she negotiates terms. She does not lose so much as accept a settlement.

Venus
Aeneas's Mother

Goddess of love, mother of Aeneas, counterweight to Juno on Olympus. She protects her son without ever being able to remove the suffering from his path. She appears to him disguised as a huntress in Book I, helps fashion his shield in Book VIII, lobbies Jupiter on his behalf. The gods of the Aeneid are powerful; they are not omnipotent against fate, and a mother who is also a goddess can only do so much.

Anchises
Father, Voice from Below

Aeneas's mortal father, carried out of Troy on his son's back, dies on the journey before they reach Italy. In Book VI, Aeneas descends to the underworld and finds him standing in the Elysian Fields, where Anchises shows him the parade of unborn Roman souls — Romulus, Caesar, Augustus, the heroes of the Republic. The scene is the philosophical heart of the poem. The future the living are sacrificing for is shown as actually present, just below.
