Notes from Underground

1864 CE · by Fyodor Dostoevsky

The book in brief

Notes from Underground is a short novel told in two unequal parts by an unnamed narrator known only as the Underground Man — a forty-year-old retired civil servant living alone in a wretched St. Petersburg flat. The first part, "Underground," is a long, contradictory, addressed-to-the-reader monologue: he attacks the rationalist utopian thinkers of his decade, defends the human right to behave irrationally, and sketches a self-portrait of a man too conscious to act and too proud to fit in. The second part, "Apropos of the Wet Snow," is a memoir of three episodes from his twenties, sixteen years earlier. He spends two years plotting revenge against a strange officer for an offense the officer did not register. He invites himself to a farewell dinner for a school acquaintance he despises, gets drunk, humiliates himself, and follows them to a brothel. There he meets a young prostitute named Liza, talks her into believing she could leave the life she is in — and when she comes to his flat the next day, takes everything he said back. The two parts together show how a man becomes the kind of man who can only speak from underground.

Dostoevsky published it in 1864 in his magazine Epoch, a year after the death of his first wife and his brother. He was forty-three, broke, and had not yet written any of the novels he is now famous for. The book was meant in part as a polemic against Nikolai Chernyshevsky's enormously influential utopian novel "What Is to Be Done?" — the Crystal Palace passages mock Chernyshevsky directly. It was overlooked at the time and has since been read as the opening of modern psychological fiction and the founding text of literary existentialism.

It is the book where Dostoevsky's late voice arrives. Everything he writes afterward is in conversation with the man speaking from this floor.

Why it still matters

Against the Rational Utopia

The novel demolishes the assumption — central to Enlightenment optimism and to the Russian utopian socialism Dostoevsky was attacking by name — that human beings, once shown what is genuinely in their rational self-interest, will choose it. The Underground Man insists that they will not. He argues that people will choose suffering, perversity, and self-destruction precisely to demonstrate that they are not predictable; that two times two equals four is, in his phrase, "the beginning of death"; that he would rather smash up a Crystal Palace utopia than live inside one. The argument is deliberately contradictory and partly self-mocking, but the core sticks: a creature whose freedom can be predicted is no longer free. Every regime since 1864 that has tried to engineer humanity into rational happiness has run into the wall this monologue describes — and the contemporary debate about whether human behavior can be modeled and shaped at scale is having the same argument again, by other names.

The Birth of the Resentful Man

The novel introduces a type that fiction had not quite assembled before: the bitter, hyperconscious, paralyzed underground dweller — a man too aware of his own motives to act cleanly on any of them, too proud to belong to ordinary society, too clear-eyed to be soothed by any of its consolations. He nurses two-year grudges against people who have forgotten him. He plans elaborate revenge speeches and never delivers them. When real intimacy is offered to him, by Liza, he flees into cruelty because cruelty is something he understands. Dostoevsky drew the figure once in 1864 and the type has only multiplied: through Hamsun, Sartre, Camus, Ralph Ellison's invisible man, Dostoevsky's own later Raskolnikov and Stavrogin, and into the present, where any number of voices online speak in something startlingly close to the same register. The Underground Man is the great-great-grandfather of the modern resentful narrator, and the book is still the place to study him at full strength.

Free Will At Any Cost

The novel asks what people will pay to remain free, and the answer it gives is: almost everything, and they will pay it spitefully. The Underground Man insists "two times two makes five" not because he is wrong about arithmetic but because he refuses to live in a world that reduces him to arithmetic. He prefers his own destruction to his own predictability. Dostoevsky takes the position seriously even while showing what it does to a person who lives by it. The Underground Man is alone, ill, contemptible, and right about something. The novel does not resolve the tension between his freedom and his misery; it presents both as costs of the same thing. How much freedom is worth, and what one is willing to lose to keep it, has not become an easier question in the century and a half since.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The argument against rational utopia.

The underground man's entire monologue in Part I is an attack on the idea that people can be perfected by reason — that once you know what's good for you, you'll do it. He argues, convincingly, that human beings want the freedom to act irrationally, even self-destructively. Read to see how far that argument goes, and whether it refutes or confirms everything you believe about human nature.

Self-awareness as paralysis.

The underground man knows too much about himself to act. Every impulse is immediately accompanied by its own analysis, every feeling by its counter-feeling. This is the novel's central psychological claim: that beyond a certain degree of consciousness, action becomes impossible. Read to test whether this is a diagnosis of a type, or a diagnosis of modernity.

An unreliable narrator who knows he's unreliable.

The underground man tells you he's lying, then lies again. He contradicts himself and points out the contradiction. He performs emotion and then undercuts the performance. This is Dostoevsky's formal invention: a narrator whose unreliability is itself the confession. Read to see how the novel's form carries the argument its content can only assert.

The seed of Dostoevsky's masterpieces.

Raskolnikov's theory, Ivan Karamazov's rebellion, Stavrogin's cold pride — all of them are the underground man, extended and embodied in action. Read this as the philosophical skeleton Dostoevsky spent the rest of his life putting flesh on. Every idea in Crime and Punishment and The Brothers Karamazov is first sounded here, in miniature.

Key figures

The Underground Man
The Narrator

Forty years old, retired from a minor government clerkship on a small inheritance, living alone in a damp Petersburg flat he describes as a hole. Brilliant, bilious, hyperconscious, and unable to do anything useful with any of it. He addresses the reader directly through Part I, arguing, confessing, retracting, contradicting himself, sneering at his own contradictions. Part II is the memoir of how he became this person. He is Dostoevsky's most sustained portrait of a mind permanently at war with itself.

Liza
The Encounter

A twenty-year-old prostitute the Underground Man meets in a Petersburg brothel near the end of Part II. He delivers a long sentimental speech about how she could still leave this life. She believes him. She comes to his flat the next day and finds him screaming at his servant over wages, humiliated by his own poverty. He turns on her with a cruelty that is the philosophy of Part I made action. Her steady silence is the only moment anyone in the novel manages something the Underground Man cannot.

Zverkov
The Schoolmate

An officer and former schoolmate the Underground Man loathes with spectacular intensity, partly because Zverkov is healthy, confident, and socially competent in ways the Underground Man cannot be and cannot stop wanting to be. He is having a farewell dinner before being posted to the Caucasus. The Underground Man invites himself, gets drunk, picks a fight, paces around the table while the others ignore him, and follows them to the brothel afterward. The dinner is Part II's humiliating centerpiece and one of the most uncomfortable sustained scenes Dostoevsky ever wrote.

Simonov
The Organizer

Another former schoolmate, the one who organizes the dinner for Zverkov. Not malicious, simply indifferent to the Underground Man, which is, to the Underground Man, much worse. The Underground Man owes Simonov a small sum and has to borrow more from him to attend the dinner. Simonov lends it without comment. He is the novel's representative of ordinary social life — functional, unselfconscious, faintly exasperated — and therefore alien.

Apollon
The Servant

The Underground Man's elderly manservant. Sullen, dignified, contemptuous of his employer. He will not be hurried. He performs his duties on his own schedule with the manner of a man doing his master a personal favor. Their long-running war over a few rubles in unpaid wages is one of the novel's running dark comedies and the trigger for the disastrous scene with Liza. Apollon is the one person in the book the Underground Man is definitively unable to intimidate, and the small-scale image of every authority he secretly cannot bear.

The Crystal Palace
The Target

Not a character but a vision. Chernyshevsky's 1863 utopian novel "What Is to Be Done?" had popularized an image of a future society organized entirely by rational self-interest — borrowed in part from the iron-and-glass building erected in London for the 1851 Great Exhibition. In that imagined world, enlightened understanding of one's own good would make human misery obsolete. Part I of Notes from Underground is a sustained assault on that idea. The question the novel raises is whether the Underground Man wins the argument or merely escapes from it.
