Hosea a guided tour

8th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Twelve Prophets

A prophet in the last decades of the northern kingdom is told to marry a wife of whoredom, give their children names that mean Not-Pitied and Not-My-People, and live the marriage in public as a sign. Hosea is the shortest, strangest, and most personally exposed of the prophetic books.

The book in brief

Hosea is the only one of the writing prophets whose ministry is set entirely in the northern kingdom of Israel. He prophesies through the last decades of its independence — from the long late prosperity of Jeroboam II into the chaos that ended with the Assyrian destruction of Samaria in 722 BCE. The book opens with a command: go, marry a wife of whoredom, because the land has committed great whoredom by departing from the LORD. Hosea obeys. He marries Gomer daughter of Diblaim. Three children follow, each given a name that is itself an oracle: Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah (Not-Pitied), Lo-ammi (Not-My-People). The marriage is prophetic theatre performed in public.

The first three chapters build the marriage as a parable. Chapters 4 through 14 are the prophetic oracles proper: the priests grown fat on sin, the kings rising and falling like foam on water, Ephraim mixing himself among the peoples like a half-baked cake. Then, almost intolerably in the middle of the indictment, chapter 11 — God as the parent who taught Ephraim to walk and cannot give him up. The book ends in chapter 14 with a call to return, a promise of dew and lily and restored vine, and a final line that hands the book to the reader and asks what they will do with it.

Hosea 1 of 14
Hosea 1

The Marriage as Living Parable

Chapter 1 opens the book's foundational metaphor without delay: Hosea son of Beeri is told to marry Gomer daughter of Diblaim because the land has committed great whoredom by departing from the LORD. The marriage is not private; it is prophetic theater performed in a small village where everyone will see it. Three children follow, each given a name that is itself an oracle. Jezreel names the valley where Jehu massacred the house of Ahab, and promises that the bow of Israel will be broken there. Lo-ruhamah (Not-Pitied) is the name of the second child, a daughter — God will no longer have pity on Israel, though Judah he will still save. Lo-ammi (Not-My-People) is the third, with the most exposed explanation in the chapter: you are not my people, and I am not your God. The chapter then turns without warning: the same Yahweh who has just declared the severance announces a reversal — Lo-ammi will be called my people, Lo-ruhamah will be called pitied, and in the place where it was said 'Not-My-People,' they shall be called children of the living God. The grammar of the book — indictment held inside the same breath as restoration — is established in this first chapter. Read it as a frame; everything else opens from inside it.

Hosea 2

Allure Her Into the Wilderness

Chapter 2 is the book's most sustained use of the marriage metaphor. The chapter falls into two sections: a lawsuit (verses 2–13) and a restoration (verses 14–23). In the lawsuit, the husband charges the wife publicly — she said, I will go after my lovers who give me my bread and my water, my wool and my flax; she did not know that it was I who gave her the grain, the wine, and the oil. The goods of the land — crops, festivals, the rhythm of the agricultural year — are all misdirected gifts. The LORD will hedge her in with thorns, take back the grain and wine and wool and flax, and end the feasts. The restoration, when it comes, is not a return to the previous arrangement but something stranger: I will allure her and bring her into the wilderness and speak tenderly to her. The wilderness is not punishment here — it is the place of renewed intimacy, the place where the covenant at Sinai was first spoken. She will call him 'my husband' and no longer 'my master.' The Baals will be removed from her mouth. The covenant will extend outward to the beasts of the field, the birds of the air, the bow and sword broken in the land. And the names turn again: I will say to Lo-ammi, You are my people; and he shall say, You are my God. Read this chapter as the full statement of the book's theology in miniature — indictment, stripping, wilderness, and the renewed betrothal.

Hosea 3

Buy Her Back for Fifteen Pieces of Silver

Chapter 3 is the shortest in the book — five verses — and the most precise. The command in verse 1 is exact: Go again, love a woman who is loved by a man yet is an adulteress, just as the LORD loves the children of Israel, though they turn to other gods and love cakes of raisins. Hosea buys her for fifteen pieces of silver and a homer and a half of barley — a price scholars have connected to the value of a slave in Exodus 21, though the purchase here is the re-purchase of his own wife. He then places a condition on the reunion: she is to live with him many days without prostitution and without any man, and he will do likewise. The period of discipline prefigures the period Israel will spend without king, prince, sacrifice, pillar, ephod, or household gods — the stripping of all political and religious structure — before returning in the latter days to seek the LORD their God and David their king. The chapter is structured as an acted parable with an interpretation attached. The act: buying the wife back. The interpretation: Israel will spend a period without its apparatus of state and cult, then seek, tremble, and return. The chapter is brief because it means to be. Three chapters of figure, and then the prophetic oracles begin. Read chapter 3 as the hinge: everything before it is the parable, everything after is the argument the parable makes.

Hosea 4

No Knowledge of God in the Land

Chapter 4 opens the long section of prophetic oracles that runs to chapter 14, and it opens with one of the sharpest formulations of the prophetic critique in the Hebrew Bible. The charge is threefold: no truth (emet), no mercy (hesed), no knowledge of God (daat elohim) in the land. Then the inventory of consequent crimes: swearing, lying, killing, stealing, adultery; blood touches blood. The chapter turns immediately to the priests, who bear specific responsibility for the daat elohim that is missing. They feed on the sin of the people; they set their hearts on the people's iniquity; the more they multiply, the more they sin. The key verse comes in the middle of the indictment: My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge. Because you have rejected knowledge, I reject you from being a priest to me. Since you have forgotten the law of your God, I also will forget your children. What follows is a diagnosis of mutual corruption: as the people, so the priest; like mother, like daughter. The chapter closes with a warning against judging Judah by the same standard — Israel's particular cult at Gilgal and Beth-aven (a dismissive alteration of Bethel, 'house of God,' to 'house of vanity') is the target here. Read this chapter as the book's diagnostic: it names what is wrong before prescribing anything. The disease is daat — knowing that is also relating — and it is the disease that makes all the other pathologies possible.

Hosea 5

Ephraim Shall Be Desolate

Chapter 5 extends the lawsuit from the priests to the political leadership — princes, house of Israel, house of the king — all summoned to hear the charge. The geographic specificity is notable: Mizpah, Tabor, Shittim are named as sites of the snare the prophet's opponents have set. The chapter diagnoses a spirit of whoredom that has entered Israel so thoroughly that they do not know the LORD. Their deeds do not permit them to return to their God — the apostasy has become so habitual that the capacity for return is itself impaired. The middle of the chapter addresses the arrogance of Israel, which testifies to their face: Ephraim will stumble in his guilt, Judah with him. The foreign-alliance policy comes under attack in verses 13: When Ephraim saw his sickness and Judah his wound, Ephraim went to Assyria, and sent to the great king. But he is not able to cure you, and he will not heal your wound. The chapter ends with a striking image of the LORD as a lion — I will be like a lion to Ephraim, like a young lion to the house of Judah. I, even I, will tear and go away; I will carry off, and no one shall rescue. Then I will return to my place, until they acknowledge their guilt and seek my face. The lion tears, withdraws, and waits. The withdrawal of the divine presence is itself a form of judgment in Hosea — more terrible, in some ways, than active punishment.

Hosea 6

I Desire Mercy, Not Sacrifice

Chapter 6 is organized around a contrast: Israel's words of return in verses 1–3, and the LORD's response in verses 4–11. The people's speech is beautifully worded: Come, let us return to the LORD; for he has torn us, that he may heal us; he has struck down, and he will bind us up. After two days he will revive us; on the third day he will raise us up, that we may live before him. Let us know; let us press on to know the LORD. The problem with this speech, the chapter immediately makes clear, is not its theology but its depth. The LORD's response in verse 4 is anguished rather than satisfied: What shall I do with you, O Ephraim? What shall I do with you, O Judah? Your love is like a morning cloud, like the dew that goes early away. The speech has the right words. But the love it describes evaporates by mid-morning. The famous verse follows: For I desire hesed and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings. The indictment continues through the chapter: they have transgressed the covenant at Adam, the priests are bandits at Shechem and commit villainy. The chapter closes with the harvest image — the harvest is appointed when I restore the fortunes of my people — which looks forward to the chapter 14 restoration. The key movement to notice: genuine return (the book's central demand) is distinguished here from the correct form of return. The form of the speech is right; the love behind it is not yet deep enough.

Hosea 7

Ephraim Is a Half-Baked Cake

Chapter 7 is one of the book's most imagistically dense sections. The opening two verses set the frame: when the LORD would heal Israel, the iniquity of Ephraim is revealed — the healing itself exposes the wound. The political diagnosis is specific: the kings and princes are glad at the wickedness of the court; there is conspiracy, assassination, the rulers fall from wine. The three central images then do the sustained diagnostic work. Ephraim is a cake not turned — a flatbread baked on one side and left raw on the other, half-burned and half-raw. Strangers devour its strength, yet he does not know it. Gray hairs are sprinkled upon him, yet he does not know it. The not-knowing is the diagnosis inside the image: a kingdom that has lost the self-awareness to see its own state. The dove image follows: Ephraim is like a silly dove, without sense — calling to Egypt, going to Assyria. The foreign-policy shuttling that looks like strategic flexibility is diagnosed as the flight of a bird that does not know where it is safe. The chapter closes with the treacherous bow — Israel has been like a bow that its maker cannot use, because it returns to the wrong place. The bow shoots the arrows of foreign alliance rather than the arrows of covenant faithfulness. The last verse is an indictment of the lament itself: they do not cry to me from the heart; they wail upon their beds. The form of penitence is present; the heart-cry is not.

Hosea 8

They Sow the Wind

Chapter 8 is the book's most concentrated statement of the irony of Israel's cult: they have made many altars for sinning; the altars have become to them things for sinning. The chapter opens with an alarm — Set the trumpet to your lips! One like a vulture is over the house of the LORD, because they have transgressed my covenant and rebelled against my law. The charge then catalogues specific covenant violations: Israel has rejected the good. The enemy will pursue him. They have set up kings without my asking; they have made princes without my approval. They have made silver and gold into idols for their own destruction. The image of the Samarian calf — the golden calf that Jeroboam I had set up at Bethel and Dan to prevent his subjects from going south to worship at Jerusalem — is addressed with contempt: I have spurned your calf, O Samaria. A craftsman made it; it is not God. The calf of Samaria shall be broken to pieces. The foreign-policy critique continues: Israel mixes with the nations like a wild donkey wandering alone; they hire lovers among the nations. The chapter's most famous verse comes near the end: they sow the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind. The standing grain has no heads; it shall yield no flour. If it were to yield, strangers would devour it. The image of the harvest without return — effort without result, worship without effect — is the chapter's closing image.

Hosea 9

The Prophet Called a Fool

Chapter 9 moves the indictment to the cultic calendar and the prophetic office itself. The opening charge addresses Israel at a harvest festival — perhaps the Feast of Tabernacles, the great autumn feast: Do not rejoice, O Israel! Do not exult like the peoples; for you have played the whore, forsaking your God. The joy of harvest is broken because the relationship the harvest celebrates has been broken. The chapter predicts the end of the feasts: they will not remain in the land of the LORD; Ephraim shall return to Egypt, and in Assyria they shall eat unclean food. The days of punishment have come; the days of recompense have come. The land they love will bear no harvest; the womb will be barren. The middle of the chapter addresses the prophets directly, and it is the passage where Hosea places himself most explicitly in the tradition of prophets: the prophet is the watchman of Ephraim with God, yet a fowler's snare is on all his paths, and hostility in the house of his God. They have deeply corrupted themselves as in the days of Gibeah — the reference to the gang rape of the Levite's concubine in Judges 19, one of the darkest episodes in the narrative corpus. The prophet who speaks this is called a fool, a madman, because of the greatness of the iniquity. The chapter closes with a meditation on Baal Peor and Gilgal — two sites of ancient transgression — and the verse that is among the harshest in the book: every evil of theirs is in Gilgal; there I came to hate them.

Hosea 10

Israel Is a Luxuriant Vine

Chapter 10 opens with a compressed diagnosis of Israel's cultic history: Israel is a luxuriant vine that yields its fruit. The more his fruit increased, the more altars he built; as his country improved, he improved his pillars. The logic is ironic: prosperity, which should have produced gratitude and deeper covenant faithfulness, has instead funded more elaborate apostasy. The material success of the Jeroboam II period — the long prosperity that opened the book's setting — is the engine of the corruption. The chapter then turns to the political collapse: their heart is false; now they must bear their guilt. The LORD will break down their altars and destroy their pillars. They have no king because they do not fear the LORD; as for the king, what could he do for us? The kingship itself is dismissed — the very institution they clamored for in 1 Samuel has become a nullity. The calf of Beth-aven will be carried to Assyria as tribute to the great king; the high places of Israel will be destroyed. The middle of the chapter addresses Israel in the second person in a series of imperatives that are simultaneously lament and summons: sow for yourselves righteousness; reap steadfast love; break up your fallow ground, for it is time to seek the LORD. Then the reversal: but you have plowed iniquity; you have reaped injustice; you have eaten the fruit of lies. The chapter closes with the battle at Beth-arbel and Shalman — historical references now obscure — and the warning that the mother was dashed in pieces upon her children. A day of complete destruction is coming.

Hosea 11

How Can I Give You Up, Ephraim?

Chapter 11 is the book's centre and by many readers' judgment its single most astonishing passage. The register shifts without warning from the sustained indictment of chapters 4–10 to a voice of parental memory. When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son. The more I called them, the more they went from me. They kept sacrificing to the Baals and offering incense to idols. Yet it was I who taught Ephraim to walk; I took them up by their arms, but they did not know that I healed them. I drew them with cords of human kindness, with bands of love. I was to them as those who lift infants to their cheeks; I bent down to them and fed them. This is not the husband-wife vocabulary of chapters 1–3; it is the parent-child vocabulary, and it is more intimate for the small domestic detail: the lifting to the cheek, the bending down to feed. Then the judgment continues — they will return to Egypt, Assyria will be their king, the sword will rage against their cities — and then, mid-verse, the speech breaks. How can I give you up, Ephraim? How can I hand you over, Israel? How can I make you like Admah? How can I treat you like Zeboiim? My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender. I will not execute my fierce anger; I will not again destroy Ephraim; for I am God and not a man, the Holy One in your midst. Read the chapter twice. The second reading catches what the first misses: the judgment has not been cancelled, only the final execution of it. The parent who refuses to give up is also the parent who is still angry. These two things are not resolved. They are held.

Hosea 12

Jacob Fled to the Field of Aram

Chapter 12 is the book's most typologically complex section, using the Jacob narrative from Genesis as a lens through which to read the present apostasy of the northern kingdom. The chapter opens with the foreign-alliance indictment: Ephraim feeds on the wind and pursues the east wind all day long; they multiply falsehood and violence; they make a treaty with Assyria and oil is carried to Egypt. The political is then reflected in the ancestral: the LORD has an indictment against Judah and will punish Jacob according to his ways. The Jacob references are specific and condensed: in the womb he took his brother by the heel, and in his manhood he strove with God. He strove with the angel and prevailed; he wept and sought his favor. He met God at Bethel, and there God spoke with him — and now the prophet's turn — the LORD God of hosts, the LORD is his memorial name. The implication is double: Jacob the ancestor wrestled with God and prevailed, and the place where he prevailed (Bethel) is now the site of the apostasy (Beth-aven). The chapter then addresses the prophet's role in Israel's history: by a prophet the LORD brought Israel up from Egypt, and by a prophet he was preserved. The closing verse returns to judgment: his LORD will leave his blood-guilt on him and will return to him his disgrace. Chapter 12 is the book's most bibliocentric section — the argument is made by reading Israel's ancestors against the present generation and finding the present generation insufficient to the legacy.

Hosea 14

Return, O Israel, to the LORD

Chapter 14 is the shortest chapter in the book and the most carefully composed. The structure is in three movements: a call to return with a supplied prayer (verses 1–3), the LORD's response (verses 4–8), and a closing wisdom saying addressed directly to the reader (verse 9). The call in verse 1 uses the verb shuv — return — that has run through the book as its central demand. The supplied prayer acknowledges iniquity, asks that the people be received graciously, and offers the calves of our lips — words rather than animal sacrifice — as the form of the return. The prayer also explicitly renounces the failed strategies: Assyria will not save us; we will not ride on horses; we will say no more 'Our God' to the work of our hands. The response is the book's most concentrated statement of restoration: I will heal their faithlessness; I will love them freely, for my anger has turned from them. I will be as the dew to Israel; he shall blossom like the lily; he shall take root like the trees of Lebanon. They shall return and dwell beneath my shadow; they shall flourish like the grain; they shall blossom like the vine. The vine that was condemned in chapter 10 for multiplying altars is now the image of a people restored to flourishing under the right conditions. The final verse of the book is addressed to no character inside the story but to the reader: Whoever is wise, let him understand these things; whoever is discerning, let him know them; for the ways of the LORD are right, and the upright walk in them, but transgressors stumble in them. The book ends by refusing to close. It puts the question of which kind of reader you are, and leaves it open.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The marriage as covenant

The single most original move in Hosea is the figure that organizes the entire book: the relation between Yahweh and Israel as the relation between a husband and an unfaithful wife. The figure is not a passing simile; it is the structural metaphor through which the book's whole theology runs.

The named children

Gomer bears three children, and each is given a name that is itself an oracle. Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah, Lo-ammi — named after a massacre, named Not-Pitied, named Not-My-People. They grow up under those names in the village. Then the names turn.

How can I give you up, Ephraim?

Chapter 11 is, by common consent of readers across many traditions, the most astonishing single passage in the Book of the Twelve. After ten chapters of relentless indictment, the voice that has been pronouncing judgment changes register without warning.

No knowledge of God in the land

The charge in chapter 4 is the most precise formulation of the prophetic critique anywhere in the Hebrew Bible: no truth, no mercy, no knowledge of God in the land. By "knowledge of God" Hosea does not mean theological information.

Return

The book's last chapter is one of the most carefully composed restorations in the prophetic literature, and it earns its place by refusing to soften any of what has come before. Chapter 14 closes by handing the book back to the reader.

Key figures

The 4 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Hosea
Prophet of the northern kingdom

Son of Beeri. The only writing prophet whose ministry is set entirely in the northern kingdom of Israel. Commanded by Yahweh to marry a wife of whoredom and to live the marriage in public as a sign of what Israel has done to its God — a command he obeys. Speaks of God in the language of a wounded husband and a parent who has taught a small child to walk, and introduces with this book the marriage figure that will shape the rest of the Hebrew prophetic tradition and large stretches of the New Testament.

Gomer, daughter of Diblaim
Hosea's wife

A wife of whoredom, the text says — though whether this means she was a prostitute when Hosea married her, or that she became unfaithful afterward, or that the description is purely symbolic of Israel, has been argued in every century since. Bears three children whose names are themselves oracles. Leaves the household, by whatever means, and is bought back in chapter 3 for fifteen pieces of silver and a homer and a half of barley. The book records what is done; it does not record what she thought of any of it.

Israel / Ephraim
The northern kingdom, the unfaithful wife

The northern kingdom, called Israel in the formal indictment and Ephraim in the more intimate verses, after its largest tribe. The wife in the marriage figure; the child taught to walk in chapter 11. By Hosea's day, two centuries of separate political existence and a long history of Baal worship and imperial alliance. The prophet's diagnosis is not that Israel has formally renounced Yahweh — the cult continues — but that the people have ceased to know Yahweh in the practical, faithful, relational sense. The book's tenderness toward Ephraim is the more startling for being unmistakable.

Yahweh
Wounded husband, parent who will not let go

The voice that runs through the book is unusually exposed for a prophetic text — a voice that pleads, accuses, remembers tenderly, breaks off mid-judgment, and at the centre of chapter 11 says that he will not act on the anger that would be the just response, because he is God and not a man. The Yahweh of Hosea is the source of much of the later prophetic and apostolic vocabulary about hesed — steadfast love, covenant fidelity that does not let go even when there is reason to. The book is the place where that vocabulary is first worked out in detail.
