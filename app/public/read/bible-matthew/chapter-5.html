Matthew 5 of 28

Matthew 5

Jesus goes up the mountain and begins the Sermon on the Mount: blessings for the poor in spirit, the mourning, the meek — then six antitheses reinterpreting the law from the heart.

Summary

The Beatitudes are a series of macarisms in the wisdom tradition, but the conditions they bless are consistently unexpected. The poor in spirit, the mourning, the meek, the persecuted — none of these are the conditions that wealth, power, or social standing would identify as favorable. Matthew's version (in contrast to Luke's briefer and more socioeconomically pointed version in Luke 6) consistently uses the interior or spiritual qualification: poor in spirit rather than simply poor; hunger and thirst for righteousness rather than simply hunger. This has been read as a spiritualizing of Luke's more material beatitudes, or as Matthew's address to a community that was not economically destitute. In either reading, the Beatitudes set the kingdom's values in deliberate opposition to the world's standard valuation of conditions.

The six antitheses of chapter 5 are the most theologically charged material in the sermon. The formula you have heard that it was said but I say to you is unprecedented in the rabbinic tradition: rabbis cite earlier authorities; they do not speak in their own name against Torah commands. The gospel's Jesus speaks in his own name and intensifies the commands rather than relaxing them, which rules out the antinomian reading. The result is a vision of righteousness that cannot be externally enforced — it reaches into anger before the act, into desire before the deed, into the willingness to reconcile before offering worship. The chapter closes with the call to be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect — a demand that has generated centuries of debate about whether it is an impossible ideal meant to drive the hearer to grace or a genuine description of the life the Sermon envisions.
