Matthew 5

Jesus goes up the mountain and begins the Sermon on the Mount: blessings for the poor in spirit, the mourning, the meek — then six antitheses reinterpreting the law from the heart.

Matthew 5Chapter 5 opens the Sermon on the Mount with the Beatitudes: nine macarisms — blessed are — that attach the condition of blessedness to the conditions worldly wisdom does not recognize as blessed: poverty of spirit, mourning, meekness, hunger for righteousness, mercy, purity of heart, peacemaking, persecution.
  6. Matthew 6Chapter 6 takes up the three practices of Jewish piety — almsgiving, prayer, and fasting — and gives instructions that consistently redirect their motivation from public recognition to the Father who sees in secret.
  7. Matthew 7Chapter 7 closes the Sermon on the Mount with a series of counsels and images that together constitute the sermon's final movement toward decision.
  8. Matthew 8Chapters 8-9 form the narrative counterpart to the Sermon on the Mount: where chapters 5-7 demonstrate the authority of Jesus' teaching, chapters 8-9 demonstrate the authority of his deeds.
  9. Matthew 9Chapter 9 continues the miracle cycle with a scene that adds theological weight to the healings.
  10. Matthew 10Chapter 10 is the second of the five great discourses, the missionary discourse, delivered to the twelve apostles whose names are given in full for the only time in the gospel.
  11. Matthew 11John, in prison, hears about the works of the Christ and sends disciples to ask: are you the one who is to come, or should we look for another? Jesus answers not with a direct assertion but with a list of deeds that echo Isaiah 35 and 61: the blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have good news proclaimed to them.
  12. Matthew 12Chapter 12 opens two Sabbath controversies.
  13. Matthew 13Chapter 13 is the third of the five great discourses, the parables of the kingdom, and it is the longest single sequence of parables in any gospel.
  14. Matthew 14Chapter 14 opens with the death of John the Baptist, narrated in flashback.
  15. Matthew 15The Pharisees and scribes ask why Jesus' disciples transgress the tradition of the elders by not washing their hands before eating.
  16. Matthew 16The Pharisees and Sadducees ask for a sign from heaven; Jesus refuses, offering only the sign of Jonah, and warns the disciples to beware the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees.
  17. Matthew 17Six days after Peter's confession, Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain and is transfigured before them: his face shines like the sun and his garments become white as light.
  18. Matthew 18Chapter 18 is the fourth of the five great discourses, the discourse on the church, addressed to the disciples' question about who is greatest in the kingdom.
  19. Matthew 19Jesus leaves Galilee for Judea beyond the Jordan, where the Pharisees test him on divorce: is it lawful to divorce one's wife for any cause? Jesus refers them to Genesis: God made them male and female, and the two become one flesh; what God has joined, let not man separate.
  20. Matthew 20Chapter 20 opens with the parable of the workers in the vineyard.
  21. Matthew 21Chapter 21 begins the Jerusalem section of the gospel.
  22. Matthew 22Chapter 22 opens with the parable of the wedding banquet: a king gives a feast for his son; the invited refuse; the servants are killed; the king destroys their city; others from the roads are brought in — but one guest arrives without a wedding garment and is bound and cast into outer darkness; many are called but few are chosen.
  23. Matthew 23Chapter 23 is the longest single denunciation in the gospel, and it is addressed to the crowds and the disciples, not only to the Pharisees.
  24. Matthew 24Leaving the temple, the disciples point out its buildings; Jesus says not one stone will be left on another.
  25. Matthew 25Chapter 25 continues and closes the Olivet discourse with three parables of the end.
  26. Matthew 26Chapter 26 opens the Passion narrative.
  27. Matthew 27Chapter 27 narrates the Passion from the morning trial before Pilate to the burial.
  28. Matthew 28Chapter 28 opens at dawn on the first day of the week with Mary Magdalene and the other Mary coming to the tomb.

Summary

The Beatitudes are a series of macarisms in the wisdom tradition, but the conditions they bless are consistently unexpected. The poor in spirit, the mourning, the meek, the persecuted — none of these are the conditions that wealth, power, or social standing would identify as favorable. Matthew's version (in contrast to Luke's briefer and more socioeconomically pointed version in Luke 6) consistently uses the interior or spiritual qualification: poor in spirit rather than simply poor; hunger and thirst for righteousness rather than simply hunger. This has been read as a spiritualizing of Luke's more material beatitudes, or as Matthew's address to a community that was not economically destitute. In either reading, the Beatitudes set the kingdom's values in deliberate opposition to the world's standard valuation of conditions.

The six antitheses of chapter 5 are the most theologically charged material in the sermon. The formula you have heard that it was said but I say to you is unprecedented in the rabbinic tradition: rabbis cite earlier authorities; they do not speak in their own name against Torah commands. The gospel's Jesus speaks in his own name and intensifies the commands rather than relaxing them, which rules out the antinomian reading. The result is a vision of righteousness that cannot be externally enforced — it reaches into anger before the act, into desire before the deed, into the willingness to reconcile before offering worship. The chapter closes with the call to be perfect as your heavenly Father is perfect — a demand that has generated centuries of debate about whether it is an impossible ideal meant to drive the hearer to grace or a genuine description of the life the Sermon envisions.
