Macbeth

1606 CE · by William Shakespeare

The book in brief

Macbeth is a Scottish general returning from a victorious battle when three witches stop him on a heath and tell him he will be Thane of Cawdor and then king. Within minutes a messenger confirms the first prophecy. Macbeth writes to his wife, who reads the letter and immediately starts planning the murder. When King Duncan arrives at their castle as a guest, the Macbeths kill him in his sleep and frame his guards. Macbeth takes the throne. The rest of the play is what he does to keep it: he murders his friend Banquo, slaughters the wife and children of a rival, and watches his wife unravel into sleepwalking madness. He dies in single combat at the end of Act V, his head carried onstage.

Shakespeare wrote Macbeth around 1606, shortly after King James — a Scot, and a man fascinated by witchcraft — took the English throne. The play draws on Holinshed's Chronicles for its outline and on James's own treatise Daemonologie for its supernatural texture. It is the shortest of the major tragedies, roughly half the length of Hamlet, and the most concentrated. There are no subplots and almost no comic relief.

The play is most famous for its language about guilt as a physical condition. Macbeth sees a dagger floating in the air; he sees Banquo's ghost at his own banquet; Lady Macbeth scrubs at a hand she knows is clean. The verse runs at extraordinary pressure — "tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow," "sleep no more," "out, damned spot" — and the play tracks moral collapse with a clinical precision none of Shakespeare's other tragedies attempt. It feels as if it is happening in real time, with consequences arriving faster than the people inside them can think.

Why it still matters

The Quickest Tragedy

Macbeth runs roughly 2,100 lines — half the length of Hamlet, shorter than Lear or Othello, the most compressed of the major tragedies. There are no subplots in the conventional sense. There is almost no comic relief. The play's only sustained comic scene, the porter at the gate hung over and pretending to be hell's gatekeeper, lasts a few minutes and is followed immediately by the discovery of the king's body. Every scene tightens the screw. The sense that the play is happening faster than the characters can think is part of its meaning: Macbeth never has time to absorb what he has done before he has to do the next thing, and the play's habit of cutting from a murder to the next conspirators' meeting denies him — and the audience — any pause to grieve. Modern thrillers and prestige-television tragedies, anything in the Breaking Bad lineage where moral collapse accelerates rather than pauses, are working in territory Shakespeare laid down here.

Guilt as a Bodily Fact

Macbeth is the play in which Shakespeare locates conscience in the body before the mind. Macbeth sees a dagger floating in the air before the murder and his own blood-soaked hands afterward; he hears a voice cry "Sleep no more, Macbeth doth murder sleep" and cannot rest. He sees Banquo's ghost at his banquet and shouts at an empty chair while the court watches. Lady Macbeth, who insisted in the moments after the murder that "a little water clears us of this deed," ends the play sleepwalking and rubbing at a hand she knows is clean: "all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand." The interior life is staged as hallucination, as physical symptom, as insomnia, as scrubbing at nothing. The play argues that guilt is not a thought you have but a thing your body does — a position later writers from Dostoevsky's Raskolnikov to Patricia Highsmith's Tom Ripley have spent careers extending, and contemporary trauma fiction still works the same ground.

The Witches' Question

The witches predict three things in their first encounter with Macbeth and all three come true — but the play leaves open whether they cause the events or merely describe them. Macbeth might have done it without them. He has, after all, evidently been considering it already; his wife's letter-reading in Act I suggests the couple has discussed the throne before the play opens. The witches name an ambition he carried into the scene with him. That ambiguity is the play's most lasting question, and Shakespeare refuses to settle it. The witches are real enough that Banquo sees them too, and unreal enough that they vanish into the heath leaving no trace. The play suggests that a person is responsible for what they do when someone tells them what they already wanted to hear. Conspiracy theorists, social-media algorithms, charismatic flatterers — anything that hands a person back the version of themselves they were already looking for — operates in the territory Shakespeare opened up here.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Did the witches cause this?

They predict, but they do not command. Macbeth hears the prophecy and immediately starts planning. The question of whether they planted the idea or simply reflected it back runs through every act.

Ambition as a form of madness.

By Act III, Macbeth is seeing daggers and ghosts. The play tracks how the first crime does not resolve the fear that produced it — it multiplies it. Watch what he imagines he needs to do next, and why.

Lady Macbeth's undoing.

She calls on spirits to unsex her and fill her with cruelty. It works — until it doesn't. Her disintegration runs in counterpoint to his: as he hardens, she collapses. The sleepwalking scene is the play's most devastating moment.

Good man or bad man revealed?

We are told at the start that Macbeth is brave, loyal, and honoured. Then within two acts he is a murderer. The play keeps the question open: was this always in him, or did the witches and his wife unlock something that had no prior existence?

Key figures

Macbeth
The Tragic General

A celebrated Scottish warrior introduced as "valour's minion," returning from battle a hero. He hears the witches' prophecy and is immediately convulsed by what he himself calls "horrible imaginings." He knows, before he kills Duncan, exactly what he is about to do and why it is wrong; he describes the king's virtues in precise theological terms and then kills him anyway. That lucidity is what makes him tragic rather than merely villainous — and what makes the final hardening, when he can no longer feel anything, so terrible.

Lady Macbeth
The Other Engine

The harder of the two at the start. She reads Macbeth's letter, fears he is too "full o' the milk of human kindness," and calls on the spirits to unsex her and fill her, top to toe, with direst cruelty. She plans the murder and steadies her husband's nerve. But the resolve she summoned does not last. By Act V she is sleepwalking, scrubbing at imaginary blood, and her death — offstage, almost casually reported — is the moment Macbeth realizes nothing matters anymore.

The Three Witches
The Weird Sisters

Three figures the play calls the Weird Sisters — "weird" in the older sense of fate. They appear to Macbeth and Banquo on a heath and again in a cavern, prophesy in riddles, and stir a cauldron with eye of newt and toe of frog. The play never fully explains what they are: witches, fates, hallucinations, devils. Everything they say comes true, but always in a way Macbeth has not anticipated. They predict; they do not command — and that distinction is the play's central question.

Banquo
The Mirror

Macbeth's closest companion at the start, a fellow general who hears the same prophecy on the same heath. The witches tell Banquo his sons will be kings though he himself will not. Banquo's response is to question, hesitate, and refuse to act on what he heard. Macbeth has him murdered for it. Banquo's ghost returns at the banquet — visible only to Macbeth, sitting silently in the king's chair — and his presence becomes the measure of every choice Macbeth refused to make.

Duncan
The Sacrificed King

The king of Scotland — gracious, trusting, generous, and wholly undeserving of what happens. Macbeth himself catalogs his virtues in soliloquy: "his virtues will plead like angels, trumpet-tongued, against the deep damnation of his taking-off." Duncan is killed in his sleep early in Act II, before the audience knows him well, and the play makes his murder feel cosmically wrong despite giving him little stage time. The horses eat each other on the night he dies.

Macduff
The Reckoning

The Thane of Fife, who refuses to attend Macbeth's coronation feast and flees to England to raise an army. Macbeth, on the witches' second prophecy, has Macduff's wife and children slaughtered in revenge. Macduff hears the news in England and the scene of his grief — "He has no children" — is one of the most devastating in Shakespeare. He is the man the play has been preparing as the answer to Macbeth, and he kills him with a grief the play has earned.
