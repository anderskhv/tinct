Poetics

335 BCE · by Aristotle

The book in brief

The Poetics is Aristotle's treatise on poetry — though it is mostly a treatise on tragedy, and behind that on the nature of art itself. He defines poetry as mimesis (imitation), distinguishes it from history (history tells what happened, poetry tells what could happen), identifies the six elements of tragedy in their order of importance (plot, character, thought, diction, melody, spectacle), and analyzes Sophocles's Oedipus Rex as the model of how a tragedy should be structured. He describes the recognition scene, the reversal of fortune, the unity of action, the tragic flaw, and catharsis — the purification of pity and fear that tragedy produces in its audience.

The text was composed at the Lyceum in Athens in the 330s BCE, during Aristotle's second period in the city. Like the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics, it is almost certainly lecture notes — terse, sometimes uneven, occasionally elliptical. Twenty-six short chapters survive. A second book, on comedy, was famously lost, an absence Umberto Eco built into the central plot of The Name of the Rose. The text was effectively unknown to medieval Europe until Arabic commentators reintroduced it; the first Latin translation appeared in 1498, and from the Renaissance onward it became the most influential single work of literary criticism in the Western tradition.

Its argument is the architecture of how Western culture thinks about story. The three-act structure, the importance of plot over character, the recognition scene as turning point, the idea that art shows what is universal rather than what is merely true — all of these are Aristotelian. From Horace's Ars Poetica through medieval poetics through neoclassical theater theory through screenwriting manuals, the underlying framework has not changed much. Modern story consultants in Hollywood are working downstream of the Poetics, often without knowing it.

Why it still matters

The Architecture of Story

Almost every later theory of how stories work — three-act structure, character arc, the climax-and-recognition pivot, the necessary unity of action — descends in some form from the Poetics. Horace's Ars Poetica works downstream of it. Renaissance theater theory enforced it (sometimes badly) as the three unities. Neoclassical drama lived inside it. The screenwriting handbooks that shape modern film and television — Syd Field, Robert McKee, Blake Snyder — are doing Aristotle in modern English without saying so. Reading the Poetics is reading the architecture itself, before the layers of misinterpretation and revision were applied. The framework is older than every story most modern readers have ever heard, and most of those stories are still using it.

What Art Is For

Plato had argued in the Republic that poetry should be exiled from the just city because it imitates appearances rather than truths and corrupts the soul. The Poetics is Aristotle's quiet answer to his teacher. Poetry, he argues, is more philosophical than history because it shows the universal — the kind of thing that would happen in the kind of situation, given the kind of person — rather than the merely particular fact. Tragedy is not a corrupter of the soul but a discipline of feeling. The argument is the founding defense of art as a form of knowledge in the Western tradition. Almost every later case for why literature matters is a version of it.

The Question of Catharsis

In a single clause near the start of the treatise Aristotle says that tragedy effects a catharsis of pity and fear through the events it represents. He never defines what he means. The word can be translated as purification, purgation, or clarification; each translation produces a different theory of what art does to its audience. Freud thought it was abreaction. Brecht rebelled against it. Modern aesthetic philosophy still has not settled the question. The ambiguity has been the most generative single ambiguity in the history of literary theory. Reading the original line in context — short, uncertain, opening a door rather than closing one — is to see exactly where the argument starts.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The architecture of plot.

Aristotle ranks the six elements of tragedy and puts plot first — over character, over thought, over everything. Read for the argument and ask whether the modern preference for character-driven storytelling is right or whether Aristotle was.

Mimesis and the question of art.

Aristotle defines poetry as imitation — but imitation of what, and to what end? The opening chapters quietly redefine what art is and what it is for. Notice how much philosophy is being done before he ever gets to tragedy.

The Oedipus model.

Aristotle keeps returning to Oedipus Rex as the example of how tragedy should work — recognition, reversal, unity of action, the necessary horror of self-discovery. Read the Poetics with that play in mind and watch the abstract claims become concrete.

Catharsis: the question without an answer.

Tragedy produces pity and fear, and through them effects a catharsis. Aristotle never explains exactly what catharsis means — purification, purgation, intellectual clarification. The argument has been running for two and a half millennia.

Key figures

Aristotle
The Author

Composing in his late forties or early fifties, lecturing at the Lyceum in Athens during his second residence in the city. The Poetics is one of his most concentrated works, written in compressed Greek that occasionally edges toward shorthand. He is not romanticizing his subject. He is dissecting it — what makes a tragedy work, what the elements are, how plot and character relate, why mimesis matters. The voice is the philosopher-zoologist treating poems the way he treated octopuses.

Oedipus Rex
The Model Tragedy

Aristotle returns to Sophocles's play more than to any other example. It is the demonstration of nearly every principle he sets down — the unity of action, the perfect plot, the recognition scene that coincides with the reversal, the protagonist whose error rather than wickedness produces the disaster. The play is older than the Poetics by about seventy years; Aristotle reads it as the canonical achievement of the form. Modern readers can study it knowing Aristotle's theory of tragedy was reverse-engineered from this single play.

Homer
The Master Poet

Aristotle treats Homer as the supreme epic poet and the model of how to construct a long poem. He praises Homer for not telling the whole Trojan War in the Iliad and the whole travel-story in the Odyssey — for choosing a single action and arranging events around it. The Aristotelian unity of action descends in part from his reading of Homer. The contrast between Homer and lesser epic poets is one of the running threads of the Poetics.

Mimesis
The Central Concept

The Greek word for imitation, but in Aristotle's hands a much wider idea than that translation suggests. Art is mimesis — imitation of action, of character, of the kinds of things people do under the kinds of circumstances they find themselves in. Mimesis is not copying the actual; it is showing the possible, the universal, the kind. Plato had used the same word to dismiss poetry as twice-removed from truth. Aristotle uses it to defend poetry as more philosophical than history.

Catharsis
The Famous Riddle

Tragedy, Aristotle says, produces pity and fear and through them effects a catharsis of those emotions. Catharsis appears in the Poetics in one short clause and is never explained. Whether it means medical purgation, moral purification, intellectual clarification, or aesthetic distance has been debated for 2,400 years. Almost every later theory of why an audience watches sad stories is in some sense a translation of this single ungrammared word.

Anagnorisis
The Recognition

The moment in a tragedy when ignorance becomes knowledge — when Oedipus realizes who he is, when characters discover the truth that the audience has long known. Aristotle identifies anagnorisis as the most powerful structural device available to the tragedian, especially when it coincides with the reversal of fortune. The recognition scene as a literary technique — the masked stranger turns out to be the king, the orphan turns out to be the heir — descends directly from this analysis.
