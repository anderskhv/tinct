Practical advice for the poet

Three pieces of practical advice: visualise the scene as if you were a spectator; work with gesture as much as language; sketch the general outline before filling in episodes.

Summary

Aristotle turns from the principles of tragedy to practical advice on construction. The first piece of advice is about visualisation: in constructing the plot and working it out with the proper diction, the poet should place the scene as far as possible before his eyes, seeing everything with the utmost vividness. Only by seeing the action clearly — as if he were one of the spectators — can he find what is in keeping and avoid inconsistency. The poet who cannot see the scene cannot catch the mistakes of logic and emotion that the audience will immediately notice.

The second piece of advice concerns gesture: the poet should work out the play with appropriate gestures as he composes. Those who feel emotion are most convincing through natural sympathy; the person in a passion of anger or distress conveys it most authentically because he speaks from feeling. The craft of writing, on this account, involves something like the craft of acting — a sympathy with the character being represented that generates authentic expression.

The third piece of advice is about the order of construction. The poet should first sketch the general outline of the story, then fill in the episodes and amplify in detail. The general outline can be stated very briefly — the story of the Iphigenia, for instance, reduces to a few sentences about a girl sacrificed, carried off, and made a priestess, who recognises her brother and saves him. The episodes — what actually happens in the play — are built on that skeleton. After the outline is fixed and the names given, the work is to fill in the episodes with probable and necessary action.
