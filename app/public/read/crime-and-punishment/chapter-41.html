Epilogue, Chapter 2 — the riverbank

Easter week. The prison hospital. A dream of a plague that turns thinking men against each other. Then the riverbank. Sonya's hand. The New Testament under the pillow.

Summary

It is Easter week of the second year. Raskolnikov has been ill — not from the labor or the food, the narrator says, but from his own pride. He has been refusing Sonya's presence at the visiting bench. The peasants in the camp regard him as a godless intellectual. At Easter prayers some attacked him for his refusal to cross himself. He has been transferred to the prison hospital with a fever.

In the hospital he has a final dream. A plague has come from the depths of Asia — a microscopic infection that takes men in their reason. Each infected man becomes convinced that he alone possesses the truth, that his judgments cannot err, that the proper response to disagreement is destruction. Whole cities go mad with certainty. They form sects and burn each other. By the end of the dream only a few people remain in the world. Raskolnikov wakes soaked in sweat. The dream is the novel's last word on the theory of extraordinary men: it has been a virus, and it would consume the world.

He recovers and returns to the work-detail. One bright morning at the riverbank, where the prisoners work at the kilns, Raskolnikov sits on a log and looks across the wide Siberian river. The far shore is treeless and empty. Time, on the far bank, is older than history. Sonya appears beside him for the first time since her illness. She does not speak. He takes her hand. Without thinking, he slides off the log, kneels in front of her, and embraces her knees. He weeps. She weeps. He has been, in this moment, resurrected. Under his pillow is the New Testament Sonya gave him. He has decided this morning that he will open it. The story of his renewal will be another tale. The story of his crime is finished.
