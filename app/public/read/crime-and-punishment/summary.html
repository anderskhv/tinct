Crime and Punishment

1866 CE · by Fyodor Dostoevsky

The book in brief

Crime and Punishment is the story of Rodion Romanovich Raskolnikov, a poor and brilliant former student in St. Petersburg, who murders an old pawnbroker with an axe — and her sister, who walks in by accident — and then spends the rest of the novel coming apart. He has worked out a theory that some men, the extraordinary men, are entitled to break the moral law in pursuit of greater goods. He believes he is one of them. The murder is supposed to confirm it. Instead, almost from the moment the axe falls, his theory dissolves. His body knows what his mind has refused to admit. He becomes ill, mistrustful, frantic, and irrational; he confesses by accident, almost; he is hunted, gently and patiently, by an investigating magistrate named Porfiry who already suspects him.

The novel is also the story of his sister Dunya, who is about to marry a man she does not love to save the family from ruin; of his mother, whose love is itself a kind of pressure; of his friend Razumikhin, whose decency anchors him; of Sonya, the gentle prostitute whose life is one long act of self-sacrifice; and of Svidrigailov, the rich predator who is the novel's image of what Raskolnikov could become if his theory were true.

Written in 1866 in monthly installments under crushing financial pressure, Crime and Punishment is one of the most morally serious novels ever written and one of the most readable. Dostoevsky uses the structure of a thriller — a murder, a manhunt, a confession — to ask a question philosophy was just then beginning to take seriously: if there is no God, is everything permitted? The novel does not give a tidy answer. It gives Raskolnikov's body, refusing the theory his mind has produced.

Why it still matters

The First Modern Psychological Novel

Crime and Punishment goes inside a single damaged consciousness and stays there. The reader knows what Raskolnikov is thinking before, during, and after the murder. Dostoevsky tracks every shift — the rationalization, the panic, the strange exaltation, the physical revulsion, the days of fever and dream. No earlier novel had attempted that kind of sustained interior portraiture. The technique would shape everything that came after: Joyce, Mann, Faulkner, Bellow, Roth, Murakami all work in territory Dostoevsky opened. To read Crime and Punishment is to watch fiction discover that the most dramatic event in a novel is not what happens — the murder, the chase, the confession — but the unbroken voice of the mind that experiences them. It is the book that taught the novel to be psychological; everything modern fiction does with the inner life is in some way downstream of what happens in this single damaged head.

If There Is No God, Is Everything Permitted?

The question that haunts the second half of the novel — Ivan Karamazov will pose it explicitly fifteen years later — is already at the heart of Crime and Punishment. Raskolnikov has worked out, on a piece of paper, that some men have the right to break the moral law for the sake of something greater. Napoleon. Lycurgus. Newton. The murder is supposed to prove he is one of them. But the body refuses. He vomits. He hallucinates. He cannot eat. The novel argues that the moral law is not a rule we choose to follow or not — it is built into us, and breaking it does not free us, it sickens us. Whether or not God exists, Dostoevsky says, this much is true. The novel does not preach. It simply shows what happens to a man who has tried to think his way out of his own conscience and found his body will not let him.

Sonya, and the Ladder Down

The novel's moral center is not a philosopher but a meek nineteen-year-old prostitute who sells herself to keep her drunkard father's children fed. Sonya cannot argue Raskolnikov out of his theory; she does not even try. She reads him the story of Lazarus from her Bible. She offers to follow him to Siberia. She loves him, and the novel insists that this love — humble, almost stupid by intellectual standards — is wiser than every theory in his head. Dostoevsky's argument is that the descent into self-destruction is not reversed by argument but by a particular kind of love that meets the sufferer where he has fallen. Sonya is not a symbol. She is a person, and the novel is exact about her poverty, her humiliations, her quiet refusals. But what she does for Raskolnikov is what Dostoevsky believed only Christian love could do: she sits with him in the dark and waits for him to come back.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The theory that destroys itself.

Raskolnikov has an idea: extraordinary people are above ordinary moral law. The murder is a test of the idea. I want to watch Dostoevsky dismantle it from the inside — not by argument but by showing what living inside that idea actually does to a person.

Guilt as a physical thing.

Raskolnikov doesn't brood quietly — he sweats, hallucinates, collapses, rages. Dostoevsky invented a new kind of psychological realism. I want to pay attention to how guilt is rendered as something the body experiences, not just the mind.

Sonya as the answer.

She lives in conditions worse than Raskolnikov's and chooses compassion over theory. She is Dostoevsky's thesis made human. I want to understand what she represents and whether her faith actually refutes Raskolnikov or simply outlasts him.

Redemption or surrender?

The epilogue offers what looks like a conversion. But Raskolnikov confesses only after every exit is closed. I want to read the novel asking whether his transformation is real, or whether Dostoevsky imposes a resolution the story doesn't quite earn.

Key figures

Raskolnikov
Rodya

A poor and brilliant former student of law, twenty-three, living in a coffin-sized room in St. Petersburg. He has worked out a theory of extraordinary men, kills an old pawnbroker to test it, and then spends the rest of the novel disintegrating. He is proud, generous, cruel, tender, sick, lucid, and contradictory. The whole novel is the inside of his head, and the novel's argument is that the head he has built cannot live with what his hands have done.

Sonya Marmeladov
Faith as the Answer

A nineteen-year-old who has gone on the streets to feed her drunkard father's starving children. She is meek, devout, completely without irony. She listens to Raskolnikov's confession without flinching. She offers to follow him to Siberia. She is the novel's moral pole, and Dostoevsky writes her as an actual person — poor, humiliated, occasionally exasperated — not a saint. Her love, almost stupid by argument, is what eventually pulls Raskolnikov back into the human world.

Porfiry Petrovich
The Investigator

Brilliant, jovial, patient, dangerous. He suspects Raskolnikov from very early on but plays an unsettling, talkative game with him for most of the novel — letting Raskolnikov talk himself toward confession rather than confronting him. He is the novel's image of intelligence in the service of the moral law, and his long conversations with Raskolnikov are some of the finest dialogue Dostoevsky ever wrote.

Dunya
Avdotya Romanovna

Raskolnikov's sister, beautiful and proud. She has agreed to marry the cold and calculating Luzhin to save the family from ruin — a sacrifice her brother cannot bear. Her plotline runs in parallel to his: a different test of moral strength under pressure, a different decision about what one is willing to barter for security. She refuses Luzhin. She survives Svidrigailov. She marries the decent Razumikhin. She is the novel's quiet counterargument to its hero.

Svidrigailov
The Mirror

A rich, sensual, possibly murderous landowner who has set his sights on Dunya. He is what Raskolnikov's theory looks like in a man who never bothered to talk himself into it — pure appetite, pure will, pure indifference to other people. He says aloud the things Raskolnikov has only thought, and his presence is the novel's image of the future Raskolnikov is fighting against. His final scene is one of the most haunting in the book.

Razumikhin
The Friend

A bear of a man, perpetually broke, perpetually cheerful, devoted to Raskolnikov despite everything. He nurses Raskolnikov through fever, manages his affairs, falls in love with Dunya. He is Dostoevsky's portrait of ordinary decency — not brilliant, not theoretical, simply present. The novel needs him: against the spiraling consciousness of its hero, Razumikhin is what an undamaged human being looks like. He is the floor everyone else is standing on.
