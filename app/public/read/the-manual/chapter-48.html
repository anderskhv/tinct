Summary

The opening contrast is the section's spine. The uninstructed person's condition: all benefit and harm expected from externals — from how things go, from how others treat them, from whether the world cooperates. The philosopher's condition: all benefit and harm expected from themselves — from their own judgments, intentions, and responses. Everything in the Manual has been building toward this contrast, and Section 48 names it plainly. Between these poles is the zone of progress, with specific observable signs.

The signs Epictetus lists are behavioral and mostly negative: they blame no one, praise no one excessively, complain and accuse no one, say nothing important about themselves. The positive descriptions are just as striking: when hindered or frustrated, they blame themselves — not because they caused every external difficulty but because the frustration is their own response, and their response is in their power. When praised, they laugh at the praise to themselves — not out of false modesty but because they understand that the praise is an external and not the measure of their worth. When censured, they make no defence — because the censure is also an external and their conduct stands or falls on its own terms.

The final image — watching oneself as an enemy lying in wait — is one of the most memorable in the Manual. It captures the quality of attention Stoic self-monitoring requires. An enemy lying in wait is watching for exactly the moment when you let your guard down, when you assent without examination, when you let an impression carry you before it has been tested. The person making progress applies that same quality of attention to their own inner life, because they know that the primary threat to their freedom is not external but internal — their own unexamined reactions.
