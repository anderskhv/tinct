Section 52

Keep three lines ready for when the day goes wrong: one from Cleanthes, one from Euripides, one from Socrates at his trial. They are sufficient.

Summary

The first quotation is from Cleanthes's Hymn to Zeus, preserved in part because Epictetus quotes it here and in the Discourses. 'Lead me, Zeus, and you too, Destiny — wherever you have appointed me to go. I will follow without hesitation. And if I refuse and prove base, I shall follow nonetheless.' The prayer captures the Stoic theology of necessity with precision. You will go where fate leads regardless; the choice is whether to go willingly or to be dragged. Willingness is the only freedom available in this domain, and it is the freedom the Manual has been teaching throughout. The line 'I will follow nonetheless' is not defeat but clarity: the wise person does not pretend there is an escape route that does not exist.

The second quotation is from Euripides, from a play now lost: 'Whoever yields to necessity with grace, we call wise, and one who understands the divine.' The word 'grace' is doing significant work. Not reluctant compliance, not bitter acceptance, but something more like the posture of a person who understands what necessity is and can move with it rather than against it. Understanding the divine, in this context, means understanding that the structure of the world is not arranged around your preferences and that this is not a failure of the divine but a feature of it. The wise person recognizes this and adjusts.

The third quotation is Socrates at his trial, responding to the men who will vote to execute him: 'Crito, if it pleases the gods, so let it be. Anytus and Melitus can kill me, but they cannot harm me.' This is the Manual's closing image and its most complete embodiment of the dichotomy of control. Anytus and Melitus have power over Socrates's body — they can and will arrange his death. They have no power over his will, his judgment, his inner state. The harm that matters — the corruption of the ruling mind — is not in their hands. The Manual closes where it began: with the distinction between what is up to us and what is not, now illustrated by a man standing before his execution with the distinction intact.
