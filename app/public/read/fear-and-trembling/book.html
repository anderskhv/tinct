Preface

Tinct Modern English — translated from Danish

Our age is putting on ein wirklicher Ausverkauf — a genuine clearance sale — and not only in trade: in the realm of ideas too. Everything can be had at such a rock-bottom price that one begins to wonder whether in the end anyone will still want to bid. Every speculative billiard-marker who conscientiously keeps score of the momentous progress of modern philosophy, every private lecturer, tutor and student, every smallholder and lodger in philosophy, does not stop at doubting everything but goes further. It would perhaps be untimely and inopportune to ask them where they actually get to. But it is surely polite and modest to take it as settled that they have doubted everything; otherwise it would be a strange thing to say, that they went further. This preliminary movement, then, they have all made — and presumably so easily that they do not find it necessary to let fall a single word about how. For not even the person who anxiously and with concern looked for a little enlightenment found any: not a guiding hint, not a small dietary prescription for how one is to conduct oneself under this enormous task. “But surely Descartes did it?” Descartes, a venerable, humble and upright thinker whose writings no one, surely, can read without the deepest emotion, did as he said and said as he did. Alas! Alas! Alas! In our time that is a great rarity! Descartes, as he himself repeats often enough, did not doubt with regard to faith. (“Memores tamen, ut jam dictum est, huic lumini naturali tamdiu tantum esse credendum, quamdiu nihil contrarium a Deo ipso revelatur ..... Præter cætera autem, memoriæ nostræ pro summa regula est infigendum, ea quæ nobis a Deo revelata sunt, ut omnium certissima esse credenda; et quamvis forte lumen rationis, quam maxime clarum et evidens, aliud quid nobis suggerere videretur, soli tamen auctoritati divinæ potius quam proprio nostro judicio fidem esse adhibendam.” [Remembering, however, as has already been said, that this natural light is to be trusted only so long as nothing to the contrary is revealed by God himself ..... Above all, though, we must fix in our memory as the supreme rule that what God has revealed to us is to be believed as the most certain of all things; and even if perhaps the light of reason, however clear and evident, should seem to suggest something else to us, we must still put our faith in divine authority alone rather than in our own judgment.] Cf. Principia philosophiæ, part one, § 28 and § 76.) He did not cry “Fire!” and make it everyone’s duty to doubt; for Descartes was a quiet, solitary thinker, not a bellowing night watchman in the street. He modestly admitted that his method had significance only for himself, and that it arose in part from the muddle his earlier learning had been in. (“Ne quis igitur putet, me hic traditurum aliquam methodum, quam unusquisque sequi debeat ad recte regendam rationem; illam enim tantum, quam ipsemet secutus sum, exponere decrevi... Sed simul ac illud studiorum curriculum absolvi (sc. juventutis, quo decurso mos est in eruditorum numerum cooptari, plane aliud coepi cogitare. Tot enim me dubiis totque erroribus implicatum esse animadverti, ut omnes discendi conatus nihil aliud mihi profuisse judicarem, quam quod ignorantiam meam magis magisque detexissem.” [So let no one suppose that I mean to hand down here some method that everyone ought to follow in order to guide his reason rightly; for I have resolved to set out only the one I myself have followed... But as soon as I had finished that course of study (namely, of my youth), at the end of which it is the custom to be admitted to the ranks of the learned, I began to think something quite different. For I found myself entangled in so many doubts and so many errors that I judged that all my efforts to learn had done me no other good than to uncover my ignorance more and more.] Cf. Dissertatio de methodo, pp. 2 and 3.) — The ancient Greeks, who did after all know something about philosophy too, held doubting to be the task of a whole life, because the skill of doubting is not acquired in days or weeks. The old veteran among them, who had served his full time, attained it: through every snare he had held doubt in equilibrium, had fearlessly denied the certainty of the senses and the certainty of thought, and had incorruptibly withstood the anxiety of self-love and the insinuations of fellow-feeling. What the Greeks took for a life’s task and their veteran attained — that is what everyone in our time begins with.

Original (1843)

Ikke blot i Handelens, men ogsaa i Ideernes Verden foranstalter vor Tid ein wirklicher Ausverkauf. Alt faaes for en saadan Spot-Priis, at det bliver et Spørgsmaal, om der tilsidst er Nogen, der vil byde. Enhver spekulativ — Marqueur, der samvittighedsfuldt pointerer den nyere Philosophies betydningsfulde Gang, enhver Privatdocent, Repetent, Student, enhver Udflytter og Indsidder i Philosophien bliver ikke staaende ved at tvivle om Alt, men gaaer videre. Maaskee vilde det være utidigt og ubetimeligt at spørge dem, hvor de dog egentlig komme hen, men høfligt og beskedent er det vel at ansee det for afgjort, at de have tvivlet om Alt, da det jo ellers var en besynderlig Tale, at de gik videre. Denne foreløbige Bevægelse have de da Alle gjort og formodentlig saa let, at de ikke finde det fornødent at lade et Ord falde om, hvorledes; thi end ikke den, der ængstelig og bekymret søgte en lille Oplysning, fandt en saadan, et veiledende Vink, en lille diætetisk Forskrift om, hvorledes man forholder sig under denne uhyre Opgave. „Men Cartesius har jo gjort det?“ Cartesius, en ærværdig, ydmyg, redelig Tænker, hvis Skrifter vist Ingen kan læse uden den dybeste Rørelse, han har gjort, hvad han har sagt, og sagt, hvad han har gjort. Ak! Ak! Ak! det er en stor Sjeldenhed i vor Tid! Cartesius har, som han selv ofte nok gjentager, ikke tvivlet i Forhold til Troen. („Memores tamen, ut jam dictum est, huic lumini naturali tamdiu tantum esse credendum, quamdiu nihil contrarium a Deo ipso revelatur ..... Præter cætera autem, memoriæ nostræ pro summa regula est infigendum, ea quæ nobis a Deo revelata sunt, ut omnium certissima esse credenda; et quamvis forte lumen rationis, quam maxime clarum et evidens, aliud quid nobis suggerere videretur, soli tamen auctoritati divinæ potius quam proprio nostro judicio fidem esse adhibendam. Cfr. Principia philosophiæ, pars prima § 28 og § 76). Han har ikke raabt Brand og gjort det til Pligt for Alle at tvivle, thi Cartesius var en stille eensom Tænker, ikke en brølende Gadevægter; han har beskedent bekjendt, at hans Methode kun havde Betydning for ham selv og havde tildeels sin Grund i hans tidligere forqvaklede Viden. (Ne quis igitur putet, me hic traditurum aliquam methodum, quam unusquisque sequi debeat ad recte regendam rationem; illam enim tantum, quam ipsemet secutus sum, exponere decrevi... Sed simul ac illud studiorum curriculum absolvi (sc. juventutis, quo decurso mos est in eruditorum numerum cooptari, plane aliud coepi cogitare. Tot enim me dubiis totque erroribus implicatum esse animadverti, ut omnes discendi conatus nihil aliud mihi profuisse judicarem, quam quod ignorantiam meam magis magisque detexissem. Cfr. Dissertatio de methodo p. 2 og 3). — Hvad hine gamle Grækere, der dog ogsaa forstode sig lidt paa Philosophien, antog at være en Opgave for hele Livet, fordi den tvivlende Færdighed ikke erhverves i Dage og Uger, hvad den gamle udtjente Strider opnaaede, der havde bevaret Tvivlens Ligevægt gjennem alle Besnærelser, uforfærdet negtet Sandsens Vished og Tankens Vished, ubestikkelig trodset Selvkjærlighedens Angst og Medfølelsens Insinuationer — dermed begynder i vor Tid Enhver.
