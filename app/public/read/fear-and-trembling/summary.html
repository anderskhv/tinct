Fear and Trembling

1843 CE · by Søren Kierkegaard

The book in brief

Fear and Trembling circles a single story from Genesis 22: God commands Abraham to take his son Isaac up Mount Moriah and kill him as a burnt offering, and Abraham obeys. He saddles his ass at dawn, walks three days in silence, builds the altar, binds his son, and raises the knife. The angel stays his hand. The book asks how it is possible to call this man the father of faith rather than a murderer, and what kind of faith would be required to be him. Kierkegaard rewrites the Moriah story four different ways in the opening pages. He compares Abraham with Agamemnon at Aulis, with the tragic hero of Greek drama, with Mary at the annunciation, with the merman of Danish folktale. Around the story he builds a systematic argument about three Problemata: whether there is a teleological suspension of the ethical, whether there is an absolute duty to God, whether Abraham's silence to Sarah and Isaac was ethically defensible.

Kierkegaard published it in Copenhagen in October 1843, at thirty, under the pseudonym Johannes de Silentio, the same week he published Repetition. He had broken his engagement to Regine Olsen two years earlier and could not stop writing his way around what that act had cost him. The book is short — under 200 pages — and reads in turns as theology, philosophical drama, polemic against Hegel, and the elaborately disguised confession of a man who had given up the woman he loved without believing she would be returned to him. It is the founding text of Christian existentialism.

Its effect on later thought has been wildly disproportionate to its length. Heidegger's reading of anxiety, Sartre's account of the leap, Buber's I and Thou, Levinas's ethics of the absolute Other, Derrida's Gift of Death — all return to Fear and Trembling. So does almost every twentieth-century theology willing to take the offence of faith seriously. The book refuses to make Abraham comprehensible. It insists that any version of religion that has made him comfortable has stopped being religion. The argument has unsettled both the believing and the unbelieving for nearly two centuries — and continues to surface whenever a public figure claims private divine instruction.

Why it still matters

What Faith Actually Requires

The book asks whether faith is a comfort or a scandal. Kierkegaard's answer is the second. Real faith, on his account, asks the believer to stand alone before God in the moment when every ethical principle, every social bond, every public reason says otherwise. Abraham cannot explain himself to Sarah, to Isaac, to the universal. He acts on an absolute duty that admits no translation. The argument has unsettled Christian thought for nearly two centuries. It has made comfortable forms of religion harder to defend, because Kierkegaard's Abraham keeps showing up to ask whether they would have made the climb. Modern readers hear it whenever a public figure claims private divine instruction and a society has to decide whether the claim is reverence, delusion, or worse.

The Knight of Faith

Kierkegaard's image of the knight of faith is one of the strangest figures in the history of Christian thought. He looks ordinary. He is fully at home in the finite — eats his dinner, walks down the street, indistinguishable from the burghers around him. Inside him an infinite movement is happening that no one sees. He has given up everything and believes he will get it back. The image is the founding figure of Christian existentialism. It also reverses the Romantic ideal of the visibly suffering hero. Kierkegaard insists that real faith does not need to perform itself. The knight of faith is harder to recognize than the knight of resignation precisely because nothing about him announces his condition. The whole inwardness is the point.

The Limits of the Universal

Kierkegaard argues that there are moments when the individual's relationship to God is higher than the universal moral law. He calls this the teleological suspension of the ethical. He also knows the argument is dangerous. The Preliminary Expectoration spends pages distinguishing Abraham from a fanatic, and the Problemata insist that the suspension cannot be claimed as a license, because the knight of faith can give no public account of why he is exempt from the universal — that is exactly his condition. The book leaves the danger intact. The questions it raises about authority, conscience, and the limits of ethics remain open. Whenever someone claims to have heard from God against the moral consensus, Kierkegaard's diagnostic categories are still the sharpest available.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The teleological suspension of the ethical.

This is the book's central claim: Abraham's act cannot be justified by any universal moral law. He is not Agamemnon sacrificing Iphigenia for a public cause. He has no external reason to give Sarah, no duty to invoke, no tragedy to perform. Faith, Kierkegaard argues, places the individual in a direct relationship with God that stands above the ethical — not by violating it, but by suspending it from a higher vantage point. Read asking whether any such vantage point is possible, or whether Kierkegaard has simply named a danger.

Knight of faith vs. knight of infinite resignation.

Kierkegaard draws a stark contrast between two responses to an impossible loss. The knight of infinite resignation makes the movement of renunciation perfectly — he gives up Isaac, accepts the loss, and finds a kind of tragic dignity in his sacrifice. But he never gets Isaac back. The knight of faith makes one further movement: he gives up Isaac and simultaneously, in the same breath, believes he will receive him back. Only one of these figures returns home. Read asking what the second movement actually requires — and whether you have ever made it.

Against Hegel: faith cannot be mediated.

The book is a polemic. Its target is a culture — personified in assistant professors, speculative philosophers, and smug clergymen — that has swallowed Hegel and now believes faith is merely an early stage of reason, a preliminary movement that the System has long since surpassed. Kierkegaard's entire effort is to show that Abraham does not belong in the System: he cannot be understood, mediated, or made comfortable. The comic parade of intellectuals in the Preface is already the argument. Read it as an attack on the idea that philosophy has faith covered.

Johannes de Silentio as a failed witness.

The narrator is not Kierkegaard. Johannes de Silentio openly admits, from the first page to the last, that he is not a knight of faith — that he cannot make the movement, cannot understand it, can only circle and admire and confess his own incapacity. This is not modesty. It is a structural choice: the book is written from outside faith, by someone who can describe every contour of what he cannot possess. Read asking why Kierkegaard chose this voice, and what it does to the argument that a philosopher cannot even approach this topic in his own name.

Key figures

Abraham
The Father of Faith

Seventy-five when first called out of Haran, a hundred when Isaac is born, and old enough to be his great-grandfather when commanded to climb Moriah. Walks three days from Beersheba in silence, builds the altar, binds the boy, raises the knife. Kierkegaard refuses to make him a tragic hero or an ethical exemplar. Abraham acts on a private absolute duty he cannot translate into the universal language of ethics. He is, for Johannes de Silentio, the greatest human being who ever lived — and the most incomprehensible.

Isaac
The Son

The child of promise, born to Sarah at ninety and to Abraham at a hundred. Old enough on the road to Moriah to carry the wood for the burnt offering himself, and to ask his father where the lamb is. Kierkegaard imagines four different versions of what Isaac sees on the mountain — including one in which Abraham deliberately makes himself look like a murderer so that Isaac can keep his faith in God. The story turns on whether Isaac understands and on what is preserved between father and son when they walk back down.

Johannes de Silentio
The Pseudonymous Author

Kierkegaard's mask. Writes the book from a position of admitted incapacity. He is not a knight of faith, cannot make the movement, and says so on almost every page. He circles Abraham with admiration, rewrites the story four different ways in the Exordium, and confesses again and again that he cannot understand what Abraham did. The inadequacy is not rhetorical humility. It is Kierkegaard's argument that faith cannot be approached from outside, and that a philosopher who pretends otherwise is deceiving himself.

The Knight of Faith
The Figure Who Returns

Kierkegaard's highest human type. Makes the full movement of resignation — giving up the beloved, accepting the loss absolutely — and then, by virtue of the absurd, believes he will receive it back in this life. Abraham is the supreme instance. Johannes describes one walking down Østergade in Copenhagen looking exactly like a tax-collector, indistinguishable from the burghers around him, the infinite movement happening invisibly inside him. Fully at home in the finite and fully oriented toward the infinite at the same instant.

The Knight of Infinite Resignation
The Figure Who Lets Go

Noble but insufficient. Has made the movement of renunciation perfectly, surrendered the finite in favor of the infinite, and accepted loss without complaint. Lives with a kind of tragic dignity that Greek tragedy understands and rewards. But never makes the further movement — never believes the beloved will be returned. He keeps the loss; the knight of faith refuses to. Kierkegaard insists resignation is the easier of the two, even though it looks heroic, because it stays within categories reason can recognize.

Sarah
The Wife on the Other Side

Abraham's wife, Isaac's mother, the woman who waited ninety years for a child and who is not told what is happening when her husband and her son leave for Moriah at dawn. The third Problema turns on her: Abraham cannot speak to Sarah because what he is doing admits no translation into the universal language of marriage and family. Kierkegaard reads Abraham's silence to her as part of what makes him a knight of faith rather than a tragic hero. Sarah is the cost the book quietly insists on.
