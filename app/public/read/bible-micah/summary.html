Micah a guided tour

8th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Twelve Prophets

A village prophet watches Assyria roll past his own towns, turns their place-names into bitter puns of doom, and delivers the line the entire prophetic tradition will live on: do justice, love mercy, walk humbly with your God.

The book in brief

Micah is seven chapters from the rougher contemporary of Isaiah — a prophet from Moresheth-Gath, a small village in the Judean lowlands, twenty-five miles southwest of Jerusalem. He prophesies in the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, across the years of the Assyrian advance: the destruction of Samaria in 722, the campaigns through the lowland, Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem in 701. He watches the Assyrian armies roll past his own village toward the capital, and the early chapters lament the towns of his region by name in a series of bitter puns on their place-names.

The book is structured in three alternating movements of judgment and hope. Chapters 1–3 are the indictment: the rulers eat the people like meat in a kettle; the prophets proportion their message to the size of the meal; Zion will be plowed as a field. Chapters 4–5 turn abruptly to the famous peace oracle and the prophecy of Bethlehem. Chapters 6–7 close with YHWH's courtroom — the great do-justice-love-mercy answer — and a final lament that ends on the question embedded in the prophet's own name: Who is a God like you?

Micah 1 of 7
Micah 1

YHWH descends; the lowland laments

Chapter 1 opens the book with a theophanic vision: the LORD comes down from his holy temple, treads on the high places of the earth, and the mountains melt under him like wax before fire. The target is named immediately — Samaria and Jerusalem, the two capitals, whose transgressions have brought the descent. Samaria's sentence is stark: she will be made a heap in the open country, her stones poured into the valley, her foundations laid bare. The second half of the chapter shifts south into Micah's own Shephelah, the lowland between the coastal plain and the Judean hills, and becomes a lament for the towns the Assyrian advance is crushing. The lament uses puns on place-names — Gath, tell it not; Beth-le-aphrah, roll in the dust; Lachish, harness the steeds, for she is the beginning of sin to the daughter of Zion. The effect is grief shaped into wordplay, sorrow given the sharpness of wit. Micah is not watching from a safe distance; these are his neighbours. The chapter closes with the prophet himself in mourning, going barefoot and naked, howling like a jackal, because the stroke of the LORD has reached the gate of Jerusalem and the wound is incurable.

Micah 2

Land-grabbers and the exile decree

Chapter 2 opens with one of the sharpest economic indictments in the prophetic literature. Woe to those who devise wickedness and work evil on their beds — when morning dawns they perform it, because it is in the power of their hand. They covet fields and seize them, houses and take them. The sin is not a spontaneous outburst; it is premeditated at night and executed at dawn. The LORD's response is proportional: I am devising disaster against this family. The fields they seized will be distributed to others; at the assembly of the LORD, no one will cast the line for them by lot. The rest of the chapter gathers the social and prophetic fallout. False prophets are condemned for telling the people not to prophesy — the accusation is that the establishment has tried to silence the very voice that would warn them. Micah's counter-oracle is bitter: they want a prophet who will say, 'I will preach to you of wine and strong drink' — that is the prophet they will get. A remnant oracle closes the chapter: God will gather those who have been stripped of their inheritance and lead them out, with their king going before them and the LORD at their head.

Micah 3

Cannibals, hired prophets, and Zion plowed

Chapter 3 is the climax of the book's first movement of judgment and one of the most precise indictments of a governing class in the prophetic literature. Three oracles, each opening with a vocative: to the rulers, to the prophets, and then to all three classes together. The first oracle against the rulers moves through an image of cannibalism — tearing skin, eating flesh, breaking bones, chopping people up like meat in a kettle — that is designed to prevent the audience from reaching for a softer metaphor. When the rulers cry to the LORD in their distress, he will hide his face from them, because they have done evil. The second oracle against the prophets names the mechanism: they cry peace when they have something to eat and declare war against him who puts nothing into their mouths. The professional prophet calibrates his message to his meal. The third oracle joins rulers, priests, and prophets in a single sentence of culpability and closes with the verse that will be quoted a century later in Jeremiah 26 when Jeremiah's own life hangs on it: Therefore because of you Zion shall be plowed as a field, Jerusalem shall become a heap of ruins, and the mountain of the house a wooded height. The chapter is the pivot of the book's structure.

Micah 4

Swords into plowshares; Zion's exile and return

Chapter 4 opens with an abrupt reversal after the judgment of chapter 3. In the latter days, the mountain of the house of the LORD shall be established as the highest of the mountains; nations shall stream to it, and the LORD shall judge between many peoples; they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more. The oracle is almost word for word the same as Isaiah 2:2-4, which has generated a long debate about priority. Micah's version has one verse that Isaiah does not: but they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree, and none shall make them afraid. The vine and the fig tree are the smallholder's idiom of peace, and they are the prophet of the lowland's signature. The chapter does not remain in that future. It turns quickly to the present: the people are to go to Babylon. The exile will happen. But they will be redeemed there; the LORD will gather the lame, the cast-off, the afflicted, and the remnant will become a strong nation. The chapter closes with the nations gathered against Zion like a farmer threshing grain — and Zion threshing them instead, consecrating their gain to the LORD of the whole earth.

Micah 5

Bethlehem named; the shepherd-ruler

Chapter 5 opens at the siege — daughter of troops, the siege is laid against us — and then shifts to the long future. The pivot verse is the prophecy of Bethlehem: But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah, who are little to be among the clans of Judah, from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel, whose coming forth is from of old, from ancient days. The deliberate smallness is the point. The ruler comes not from the great city but from the village where David had been born to Jesse, returning to the older geography of the line before it became imperial. He will stand and shepherd his flock in the strength of the LORD; they shall dwell secure; he shall be their peace. The pastoral idiom is consistent with the whole book's lowland register. The chapter then describes the defeat of Assyria — seven shepherds and eight princes of men raised against them — and the purging of the people from their idolatries: horses cut off, strongholds broken down, sorceries cut off, carved images destroyed. The chapter reads as the full reversal of the Assyrian present: the siege at the opening becomes the shepherds who will defeat Assyria, the weakness of Bethlehem becomes the strength of the coming ruler, the arrogance of the cities becomes their purging.

Micah 6

YHWH's courtroom; what the LORD requires

Chapter 6 is the great courtroom scene of the book. The LORD calls the people to contend before him; the mountains and foundations of the earth are summoned as witnesses. The LORD's prosecution opens with a gentle, almost wounded question: O my people, what have I done to you? How have I wearied you? Answer me. He recites the history of his deliverances — the exodus from Egypt, the redemption from the house of slavery, Moses, Aaron, and Miriam sent before them, the thwarted curse of Balak and Balaam, the safe crossing from Shittim to Gilgal. The people's response in the next verses is a liturgical anxiety climbing a scale: calves a year old, thousands of rams, ten thousands of rivers of oil, the firstborn child for the sin of the soul. The escalation is the prophet's target. And then Micah cuts the scale off with the most quoted line in the book: He has shown you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God? Three verbs. The chapter does not end there; it turns to a second indictment of commercial fraud — dishonest scales, deceitful weights — and announces the consequences: eating but not being satisfied, sowing but not reaping, treading olives but not anointing with oil.

Micah 7

Household collapse and the closing doxology

Chapter 7 closes the book on a note that is simultaneously its darkest and its most intimate. The prophet looks at the society around him — the godly has perished from the earth, no upright man remains, they all lie in wait for blood, prince and judge alike ask for a bribe. The collapse has reached the household: trust no neighbour, guard the doors of your mouth from her who lies in your arms, the son treats the father with contempt, a man's enemies are the men of his own house. The description is of a society so thoroughly corrupted that even the smallest social unit — the family — has come apart. And then Micah does what the book has done throughout: he turns. But as for me, I will look to the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation. The first-person voice is unusual in the prophetic literature; we hear the prophet alone, in the dark, wrapping his confidence in the LORD. The book closes with the great doxology: Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity and passing over transgression for the remnant of his inheritance? He will again have compassion; he will tread our iniquities underfoot; you will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea. The closing question — who is a God like you — is the punning answer to the prophet's own name, Mikhayahu, which means who is like the LORD. Micah closes his book with his own name as the question, answered by the steadfast love that does not let go.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

Do justice, love mercy, walk humbly

The most-quoted single line in the prophetic tradition. The setting is a courtroom; the people have just escalated their offering to the firstborn child. The prophet cuts the entire scale off in three verbs.

The ruler from Bethlehem

The prophecy of chapter 5 names a village six miles south of Jerusalem. The future king will not come from the great city but from the small one, of the line that came from there before the city was the city.

The leaders named

Chapter 3 names roles and mechanisms. Rulers eat the people like meat in a kettle. Prophets adjust their message to the size of the meal. Priests teach for hire. The precision is the indictment.

Swords into plowshares

The most famous oracle of peace in the Hebrew Bible — shared, almost word for word, with Isaiah. Micah adds one verse Isaiah does not have: the vine, the fig tree, the smallholder sitting safe.

Who is a God like you

The book closes with the prophet's own name as a question. Mi-el kamokha — who is a God like you — is the punning answer embedded in mikhayahu, the name Micah. The answer is the steadfast love that does not let go.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Micah of Moresheth
Village prophet, contemporary of Isaiah

From Moresheth-Gath, in the Shephelah, the lowland between the coastal plain and the Judean hills, about twenty-five miles southwest of Jerusalem. Prophesies in the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah of Judah, across the years of the Assyrian advance. He is the rougher voice of his generation — closer to the village, harder on the urban elite, less polished in his Hebrew, and more willing to say what the courts of Jerusalem do not want to hear. His name means who is like the LORD, and the book closes by turning the name into a doxology.

The Rulers of Jerusalem
Urban elite, indicted in chapter 3

Named with precision rare in the prophetic literature: heads who hate the good and love the evil, who tear the skin from the people, who eat the flesh of the people and chop them like meat in a kettle. They are also the heads who give judgment for a bribe. Micah holds them as a class, never as named individuals, and lays the destruction of Zion at their feet in the final verse of chapter 3.

The Prophets-for-Hire
Professional class, indicted in chapter 3

The prophets who cry peace when they have something to eat but declare war against him who puts nothing into their mouths. Not false prophets in the sense of worshipping other gods — they are the professional prophets of Jerusalem whose message is proportioned to the size of the meal. Micah, who was no prophet by trade, is exactly the figure who can name the mechanism.

The Coming Ruler
Bethlehem promise, chapter 5

From Bethlehem Ephrathah, little among the clans of Judah — the village where David was born to Jesse the Ephrathite. He will stand and shepherd his flock in the strength of the LORD; the people will dwell secure; he shall be their peace. The Christian tradition reads the prophecy through Matthew 2; the prophetic context adds that the future king comes not from the great city but from the village, of the older line that preceded the city.

The Voice in the Courtroom
The three-verb answer, chapter 6

The figure who cuts off the people's escalating offer — calves, thousands of rams, rivers of oil, the firstborn — with three verbs: do justice, love mercy, walk humbly with your God. The Hebrew text leaves the voice ambiguous: prophet, LORD, or unnamed third. The ambiguity has been part of the line's strange power across twenty-seven hundred years of Jewish, Christian, and secular ethical tradition.

The Remnant
The she'erit ya'akov — who remains

The recurring figure in the book's later chapters: those who survive the Assyrian destruction and walk in the name of the LORD their God. Not the elite — the surviving small holding, the village left after the army has passed. The book's closing verses name the remnant as the inheritance God still claims after the cities have fallen. Micah gives the prophetic literature its strongest doctrine of the remnant, shaped by the geography of the towns he watched the Assyrians destroy.
