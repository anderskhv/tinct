Micah 3

The rulers eat the people like meat in a kettle; the prophets adjust their message to the size of the meal.

Summary

The third chapter compresses the book's indictment into three parallel oracles addressed to Jerusalem's leadership classes with surgical precision. The rulers are indicted in the figure of the butcher: they tear the skin from the people, eat their flesh, flay them, break their bones in pieces, chop them up like meat in a kettle. The image is extreme by design. It forecloses the retreat to administrative language — the rulers have not merely made bad policy or been indifferent; they have consumed the people they were appointed to govern. Their punishment fits the image: when they cry to the LORD in their distress, he will hide his face, because they have practiced evil.

The second oracle against the prophets names a specific mechanism of corruption. The professional prophet — whose stipend depends on the patronage of the ruling class — has aligned the direction of his prophecy to the direction of the payment. Full table, the message is peace; empty table, the message is war against whoever failed to provide. The prophets are not named as idolaters or foreigners; they are the established prophetic guild of Jerusalem, whose office has been converted into a service industry. All three classes — rulers who judge for a bribe, priests who teach for hire, prophets who divine for money — are gathered into the chapter's closing sentence, which will become one of the most cited verses in the entire prophetic corpus. Micah names Zion itself as forfeit. The mountain of the house will become a wooded height. The verse is quoted in Jeremiah 26 during Jeremiah's trial, cited by the elders as precedent that a prophet who says such things should not be put to death. Micah's three-oracle chapter functions, a century later, as case law in a capital proceeding.
