Scene 7 of 11

The first messenger and the hinge

A herdsman reports what the women are doing on the mountain, and one quiet question from the stranger finds what the king has been hiding.

Summary

Dionysus raises the chorus from the ground and tells them how he got free. Pentheus never touched him. In the stables he tied up a bull instead, sweating and biting his lips, while the god sat by and watched. Then the house shook, fire flared on Semele's tomb, and Pentheus, thinking the palace was burning, set his servants hauling water. Next he ran inside with a sword and stabbed at an apparition the god had made. Finally the god brought the building down, and Pentheus dropped his sword and collapsed. Pentheus comes out raging and orders the towers sealed; the stranger asks whether gods cannot pass over walls. A herdsman arrives from Cithaeron and asks whether he may speak freely. Pentheus promises he will not be harmed.

The herdsman's report is the first of the play's two great messenger speeches. At sunrise he saw three bands of women asleep under the trees, led by Autonoe, Agave and Ino, lying modestly, not drunk as Pentheus claims. Agave woke them at the sound of cattle. They let down their hair, belted their fawn-skins with snakes, and young mothers suckled fawns and wolf-cubs. One struck a rock and water sprang out; another planted her wand and the god sent up wine; others scratched the earth and milk came up, and honey dripped from their staffs. The herdsmen hid to capture Agave as a favour to the king. When they sprang out, she called the women on them. The herdsmen escaped; the cattle did not. The women tore heifers and bulls apart with their hands, raided the villages below the mountain, carried fire in their hair without being burned, and drove off armed villagers whose spears drew no blood. The herdsman urges the king to accept the god, who gave men wine; without wine, he says, there is no love and no other pleasure left.

Pentheus orders his soldiers to muster at the Electran gate. Dionysus warns him not to take up arms against a god, and offers to bring the women back without weapons; Pentheus suspects a trick and calls for his armour. Then the stranger asks, in Way's line, "Wouldst thou behold them camped upon the hills?" Pentheus answers at once: "Ay—though with sumless gold I bought the sight." From that moment the stranger leads the conversation. To go unseen, Pentheus must wear a linen robe, long hair, a headband, a fawn-skin and a thyrsus. He protests, hesitates, and goes into the palace still undecided. Dionysus turns to the chorus: "Women, the man sets foot within the toils." He will drive Pentheus mad, have him led through Thebes dressed as a woman, and send him to his death at his mother's hands.
