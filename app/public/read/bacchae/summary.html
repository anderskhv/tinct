The Bacchae a guided tour

405 BCE · Greek tragedy

A god comes home to the city of his birth and is refused. He does not argue. He arranges the conditions in which the city's young king dresses himself in women's clothes, climbs a mountain to spy on the rites he has banned, and is torn apart by his own mother. The Bacchae is the strangest play in the Greek tragic corpus and the one no one has fully agreed about for two and a half thousand years.

The book in brief

The Bacchae is the play in which the god Dionysus comes home to the city of his birth — Thebes — disguised as a mortal priest of his own cult, and demands recognition. His mother Semele was a Theban princess; his cousin Pentheus is now the young king. Pentheus refuses to acknowledge the new god — calls his rites a fraud, his followers hysterical women, his priest a foreign charlatan. The whole play is the patient unfolding of what Dionysus does about the refusal.

Across eleven scenes the god allows himself to be arrested, escapes the prison without effort, and drives the women of Thebes — including Pentheus's mother Agave — into Dionysian frenzy on the slopes of Mount Cithaeron. He then persuades Pentheus, the young king who has banned the rites, to dress as a woman and climb the mountain to spy on them. The women find him. They tear him apart with their bare hands. His mother carries his head back to Thebes believing it is a lion's, and her father Cadmus walks her, sentence by sentence, to the recognition of what she is holding.

Euripides wrote the play in self-imposed exile at the Macedonian court, in the last year or two of his life. It was performed posthumously in Athens in 405 BCE and won first prize, a distinction Euripides had won only a handful of times in his life. It is one of the strangest plays in the Greek tragic corpus, and one of the most violent. Aristotle is silent on it. Nietzsche built his theory of tragedy around it. Wole Soyinka and Donna Tartt and Anne Carson have rewritten it. No one has ever fully agreed on what Euripides meant by it.

Scene 1 of 11
Scene 1

The god alone

Dionysus enters alone before the palace of Thebes, having exchanged his divine form for a mortal one. He names himself: son of Zeus and of Semele, Cadmus's daughter, who died in the lightning that brought on his birth. Her tomb beside the palace still smoulders. Her sisters claimed she had been seduced by a mortal and blamed Zeus for it. In return he has driven them, and every woman in the city, out of their homes and onto Mount Cithaeron. Pentheus, to whom Cadmus has handed the throne, shuts the god out of offerings and prayers. Dionysus says he will prove his godhead to Pentheus and to all Thebes, calls his Lydian women to beat their timbrels around the palace, and leaves for the mountain.

Scene 2

The chorus arrives

The chorus of Asian women, alone on stage, sing the entrance song of Dionysus. They order anyone in their path to step indoors and keep silent. They bless the initiate whose life is pure and who joins the revel on the hills. They retell the god's birth: Semele struck by Zeus's thunder, the unborn child hidden in his father's thigh, fastened with golden clasps, until he was born a horned god crowned with snakes. They call on Thebes to wreathe itself in ivy and briony, and they trace the timbrel back to the dancers of the infant Zeus. The song ends on the mountain itself, with ground running with milk, wine and honey, and the old prophet Tiresias arriving at the palace door.

Scene 3

The two old men and the young king

Tiresias calls Cadmus out of the palace. Both are dressed for the rites, and both mean to dance on the mountain despite their age. They refuse to ride, since that would lessen the god's honour. Pentheus returns from abroad, furious at reports that the city's women have left home for drink and lovers in the hills under a Lydian stranger. Seeing his grandfather and the prophet in fawn-skins, he mocks them. Tiresias gives a long defence of the god: Demeter gives men bread, Dionysus gives them wine, and with it rest from grief and sleep. Cadmus urges the king to honour the god, if only for the family's sake. Pentheus orders Tiresias's seat of prophecy wrecked and the stranger hunted down.

Scene 4

The chorus on holiness

The chorus sing the first stasimon. They call on Sanctity, the power that rules even the gods, and ask whether she heard the king's insults. They describe what Dionysus gives: dancing, laughter with the flutes, an end to care, sleep after wine. Unbridled talk and lawless folly end in disaster, they sing, while a quiet, sensible life holds firm; cleverness is not wisdom, and those who reach too high do not live long. They long to escape to Cyprus, or to Pieria and Olympus where the Muses live. Their god loves peace and gives wine's joy to rich and poor alike. As the song ends, Pentheus returns and his servants bring in the stranger, bound.

Scene 5

The god in chains

A servant brings in the stranger and reports, uneasy, that he gave himself up smiling and held out his hands to be tied. Worse, the women Pentheus had jailed are back on the mountain: their fetters fell off and the doors opened by themselves. Pentheus studies the prisoner's long hair and pale skin and questions him. The stranger, who is Dionysus, answers calmly. He is from Lydia, the god initiated him face to face, the rites are secret and mostly held at night. Pentheus threatens to cut his hair, takes his thyrsus, and orders him locked in the stables. Dionysus warns that the god will release him when he chooses, and will make Pentheus pay for the insult.

Scene 6

The prison undone

The chorus sing the second stasimon. They reproach Dirce, the Theban spring that once washed the newborn god, for rejecting them now. They call Pentheus a monster bred from the earth-born Echion, like the giants who fought the gods, and beg Dionysus to come down from Olympus and stop him. Then the god's voice calls from inside the palace. The chorus answer in fragments. An earthquake shakes the building, the stone lintels sway, and at the god's command a blaze of fire surrounds Semele's tomb. The women throw themselves to the ground. Dionysus walks out of the palace.

Scene 7

The hinge

Dionysus tells the chorus how he escaped: Pentheus tied up a bull thinking it was his prisoner, fought an imagined fire, and stabbed at a phantom while the palace fell. Pentheus comes out furious. A herdsman arrives from Cithaeron with the first messenger speech: the women asleep in modest calm, nursing fawns and wolf-cubs, bringing water, wine and milk from the ground; then, when the herdsmen tried to seize Agave, tearing cattle apart and raiding villages, unharmed by spears. Pentheus calls out his army. Dionysus warns him, then asks whether he would like to see the women on the hills. Pentheus says yes, at any price. He agrees to be dressed as a woman and goes inside.

Scene 8

The chorus on wisdom

The chorus sing the third stasimon. They long to dance all night again, and compare themselves to a fawn that has escaped the hunters' nets and dogs and found shelter in the woods. Their refrain asks what greater prize the gods can give than a victorious hand held over an enemy's head, and answers that honour is always precious. They sing that the power of the gods moves slowly but never misses, hunting down those who exalt their own unbelief. They end by calling happy the person whose good fortune grows day by day. Inside the palace, Pentheus is being dressed in women's clothes.

Scene 9

The dressing

Dionysus calls Pentheus out of the palace. The king appears in a woman's robe, long hair and a headband, already deranged: he sees two suns and two cities of Thebes, and the stranger looks to him like a bull with horns. He asks whether he looks like Ino or his mother. Dionysus tucks a stray lock back under the headband, straightens the belt and hem, and shows him how to carry the thyrsus. Pentheus imagines lifting Cithaeron on his shoulders, then decides to hide among the pines instead of using force. He is told he will be carried home in his mother's arms, and takes it as an honour. Alone, Dionysus calls on Agave and her sisters to receive him.

Scene 10

The chorus on the spy

The chorus sing the fourth stasimon, the most violent of their songs. They call on the hounds of Madness to race to the mountain and drive the women to tear apart the man who has come dressed as a woman to spy on them. They imagine his mother seeing him first and crying out that no woman bore him, that he must be a lioness's cub or a Gorgon's spawn. They call on Justice to come with her sword and cut the throat of Echion's son. Finally they call on Dionysus to appear as a bull, a many-headed dragon or a flaming lion and snare the hunter among the Maenads. A messenger enters from the mountain.

Scene 11

Agave with the head

The second messenger reports Pentheus's death. On the mountain the stranger bent a pine to the ground, set Pentheus at its top and let it rise; then he vanished, and a voice from the sky told the women to punish the man mocking their rites. They pulled the tree down. Pentheus tore off his headband and begged his mother to know him. She did not, and the women tore him apart. Agave arrives with his head on her thyrsus, calling it a lion's. Cadmus brings in the gathered body and, through a series of questions, brings her back to her senses. Dionysus pronounces the family's fate, and Cadmus and Agave part for exile.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The god you cannot refuse

Dionysus does not argue with Pentheus. He does not need to. He arranges the conditions in which Pentheus destroys himself — and the play stages the arrangement, step by patient step.

Reason and ecstasy

Pentheus is a young, capable, rational king. He is destroyed because he refuses to make room — in his city or in himself — for what reason cannot account for.

The seduction of Pentheus

The scene in which Dionysus dresses Pentheus in women's clothes is the most uncomfortable in Greek tragedy. Pentheus is not tricked. He is willing. Something in him wants what he has been forbidding.

The mother who does not know

Agave comes down from the mountain carrying her son's head, believing it is a lion's. Cadmus walks her, sentence by sentence, to the truth. Euripides does not cut away.

The play at the end of a life

Euripides was the Greek tragedian most sceptical of the gods. He wrote The Bacchae in his last year, in exile in Macedonia. It was performed after his death and won first prize. No one has ever fully agreed what he meant by it.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Dionysus
God of wine and ecstasy

Son of Zeus and the Theban princess Semele, returning to the city of his birth disguised as a mortal priest of his own cult. Calm, beautiful, and without mercy — not because he is cruel but because he is a god and Pentheus has refused him. He delivers the prologue himself, names the plan, and lets it unfold. He does not raise his voice. He does not need to.

Pentheus
King of Thebes

Grandson of Cadmus, cousin of Dionysus, the young king who has inherited the throne and is determined to govern. Rigid, politically serious, genuinely alarmed by what the women of his city are doing on the mountain — and, as the disguised god discerns within minutes of meeting him, fascinated by it. The play turns on the moment he stops resisting and agrees to put on the women's clothes.

Agave
Mother of Pentheus

Daughter of Cadmus and sister of Semele. She is among the women of Thebes driven to the mountain in divine frenzy. She does not appear until the final scenes, when she descends carrying what she believes is the head of a mountain lion she has killed with her bare hands. The recognition Cadmus walks her through is the emotional climax of the play.

Cadmus
Founder of Thebes

Pentheus's grandfather and Semele's father. He has accepted Dionysus — partly from genuine piety, partly from dynastic calculation — and spends the play trying to warn his grandson away from the collision he can see coming. He survives to gather the pieces of Pentheus's body from the mountain and to walk Agave back to herself. His final scene with her is the tenderest in the play.

Tiresias
The blind prophet

The ancient seer of Thebes, in fawn-skin and ivy alongside Cadmus, ready to dance for the new god despite his age. His speech to Pentheus in the first episode is the play's most sustained rational defence of Dionysus — the god as the necessary wet element in nature, the counterpart to dry rationality, a force that cannot be excluded without consequence. Pentheus ignores every word.

The Chorus
Eastern women

Fifteen women from Lydia and Phrygia who have followed Dionysus from the east to Thebes. They sing the great central odes of the play — on the joy of the rites, on the slow justice of the gods, on the wisdom of the simple life. They are the only constant on stage and the only ones who never lose composure. By the end they are praying for Pentheus's destruction.
