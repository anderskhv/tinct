Scene 3 of 11

Tiresias, Cadmus, Pentheus

Two old men dressed for the god's dances, and the young king who comes home, finds them in fawn-skins, and answers with the anger that will destroy him.

Summary

Tiresias comes to the palace door and calls for Cadmus. Cadmus comes out already dressed for the rites, fawn-skin on, thyrsus in hand, glad to forget his age. They agree to go to the mountain on foot; a chariot, Tiresias says, would diminish the god's honour. They are the only men in Thebes who will dance, and they take that as proof of their good sense. Tiresias adds that the god makes no distinction between young and old; he wants honour from everyone. Then Cadmus sees Pentheus hurrying toward the palace.

Pentheus has been away and has come back to bad news. The women of the city, his mother Agave and his aunts Ino and Autonoe among them, have left their homes. He is sure the rites are a cover for wine and for meeting men in the woods. Some of the women are already in prison. He has heard of a stranger from Lydia with perfumed golden hair who spends day and night with the women, and he swears to cut off his head. Then he sees the prophet and his own grandfather in fawn-skins. He calls it a sight for laughter, tells Cadmus to throw off the ivy, and accuses Tiresias of promoting the new god for the fees that come with prophecy.

Tiresias answers with the play's longest defence of Dionysus. There are two chief powers among men, he says. Demeter, the earth, feeds people with dry food. Semele's son matched her gift with the juice of the grape, which brings rest from grief and sleep, "the oblivion of our daily ills." He explains the story of the thigh as a confusion of words, says the god brings prophecy and panic in battle, and insists the god will not corrupt chaste women. Cadmus is more practical: even if this is no god, call him one, since it brings honour to Semele and the family. He reminds Pentheus of Actaeon, torn apart by his own hounds for boasting against Artemis. Pentheus orders Tiresias's seat of augury overturned and sends men to capture the "girl-faced stranger" so he can be stoned. Tiresias and Cadmus leave for the mountain, holding each other up.
