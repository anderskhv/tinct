Scene 11 of 11

The messenger, Agave, the judgement

The second messenger's report from the mountain. Agave at the palace carrying her son's head and calling it a lion's. Cadmus bringing her back to the truth, one question at a time. The god pronouncing what comes next.

Summary

A servant of Pentheus returns from Cithaeron and tells the chorus that the king is dead. They rejoice, and he rebukes them. Then he gives the second great messenger speech. He, Pentheus and the stranger hid in a grassy hollow above a glen where the women sat at peaceful tasks. Pentheus could not see them and asked to climb a tree. The stranger pulled the top of a tall pine down to the ground, bending it like a bow, set Pentheus on it, and let it rise slowly. Pentheus was now more visible to the women than they were to him. The stranger vanished, and a voice from the sky, apparently the god's, called on the women to take vengeance on the man who mocked him and his rites. A column of fire flashed and the glen went silent. At the second call the women rushed up, stoned him, and finally tore the pine out of the ground. Pentheus fell. He pulled off his headband, touched his mother's cheek, and begged her to recognise him. She did not. Agave, Ino, Autonoe and the others tore him apart, and Agave is now bringing his head to the city on her thyrsus.

The chorus sing briefly, and Agave enters. She is exultant and still possessed. She tells the chorus she has trapped a young lion without nets, and invites them to share the feast. She calls for her father, and for Pentheus to fix the head above the palace entrance. Cadmus arrives with attendants carrying a bier: he has gathered Pentheus's scattered body from the woods. Agave offers him her trophy. Cadmus does not tell her what it is. He asks her to look at the sky, and whether it looks the same; she says it is brighter, and that something in her mind is changing. He asks whom she married and what son she bore. Then he asks whose head she is holding. She looks, and sees it is her son's. Cadmus tells her plainly that she and her sisters killed him, on the same mountain where Actaeon died, because the city denied the god. He mourns the grandson who once protected him.

The text has a gap here, and in Way's translation the god's speech begins mid-sentence. Dionysus foretells that Cadmus and his wife Harmonia will be turned into snakes, will lead a foreign army against Greek cities, and will finally be brought by Ares to the land of the blessed. Agave pleads that the family has sinned; the god answers, "I am a God: ye did despite to me." Cadmus and Agave embrace and part for exile, and Agave asks to be taken to her sisters, far from Cithaeron. The chorus end the play with lines that also close several other plays by Euripides: the gods bring about the unexpected, leave unfulfilled what people expected, and open paths no one foresaw.
