First Stasimon

The chorus, alone with the audience, respond to what Pentheus has just said, and set out the life their god offers.

Summary

Alone on stage after the king and the old men have gone, the chorus sing the first of the play's four stasima. They address Sanctity, a goddess who rules over the gods yet stoops to earth on golden wings, and ask whether she has heard Pentheus railing against the son of Semele. They describe what that god claims as his own: leading people into the dance, laughter mixed with the flutes, the moment when the worried forget their troubles, and the sleep that comes over drinkers at the feast.

The second stanza turns to warning. Unbridled tongues and lawless folly come to one end, which is disaster. A calm and sensible life stays steady and keeps a household together. The gods live far off but still watch. Cleverness is not the same as wisdom, the chorus sing, and anyone who reaches for things beyond a mortal's grasp will lose even what he has. This is said to Pentheus without naming him.

The last two stanzas move into longing. The chorus wish they could escape to Cyprus, Aphrodite's island, or to Pieria, where the Muses sing, and to the slopes of Olympus where the Graces and Desire are, and where the rites are allowed. Their god, the son of Zeus, loves feasts and loves Peace; he gives the pleasure of wine to the high and the low alike, and he hates the man who refuses a happy life. The chorus say the faith of ordinary people is enough for them. Pentheus comes back onstage, and a servant leads in the stranger in bonds.
