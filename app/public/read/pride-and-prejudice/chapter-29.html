Summary

Collins leads the Hunsford party up to Rosings with the pride of a man displaying his greatest treasure, instructing them at every step in what they are about to receive. The house does not disappoint — the hall, the staircase, the drawing room all meet expectation. Lady Catherine de Bourgh receives them politely, placed at a distance from them, large and not yet old. Miss de Bourgh, her daughter, is small, pale, and sickly-looking; Mrs. Jenkinson, her companion, exists entirely to attend to Miss de Bourgh. Collins gazes at Lady Catherine as the model of aristocratic condescension; Sir William is awed into near silence, relieved only by muttered comparisons to court.

Lady Catherine talks a great deal and likes the sound of her own opinions. She questions the entire party — their eating, their traveling, their family arrangements — and turns her attention to Elizabeth with particular directness: how many sisters, are they all out at once, what is their governess, their accomplishments, their ages? Elizabeth answers composedly and, when Lady Catherine ventures a disapproving observation about the Bennet family's approach to education, gently corrects her. Lady Catherine is not accustomed to being corrected; she receives it with a surprise that amounts to something close to respect.

Dinner is splendid — the whole meal as fine as Collins's predictions — and the evening ends at cards. Collins plays whist in silent gratitude, periodically reminding himself what an honor it is to be there. Elizabeth and Mrs. Collins sit with Lady Catherine, who continues to talk, offer opinions, find fault, and pronounce generally on matters of behavior, education, and society. Elizabeth catches enough of Darcy's character in Lady Catherine's account of him to feel her curiosity about his expected visit sharpen.
