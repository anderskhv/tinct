Pride and Prejudice

1813 CE · by Jane Austen

The book in brief

Pride and Prejudice is the story of Elizabeth Bennet, the second of five daughters of a country gentleman whose estate is entailed away from his daughters, and Mr. Darcy, the wealthy and proud master of Pemberley. They meet at a country ball. He insults her. She decides she dislikes him. He decides — slowly, against his own resistance — that he is in love with her. He proposes badly. She refuses, with fury. Each then has to face what the other was right about: he that he has been arrogant, she that she has misjudged him. By the time he proposes again, both have changed. They marry.

Around this central plot, Austen weaves four others: the marriages (or near-marriages) of Elizabeth's four sisters, the social fortunes of the absurd Mr. Collins, and the long shadow of Wickham — the charming officer whose lies Elizabeth believes early and discovers late. The Bennet household, presided over by an indifferent father and a frantic mother, supplies the comedy. The world beyond the household — the entail, the small income, the necessity of marriage as a financial fact for women — supplies the stakes.

Published in 1813, after fifteen years of revision (Austen began it in 1796 as "First Impressions"), Pride and Prejudice is the most beloved novel in English. The opening sentence is the most famous opening in fiction. The dialogue invented free indirect discourse a century before modernism formally claimed it. The plot is romantic comedy in the strict technical sense — courtship, obstacles, recognition, marriage — but the moral architecture beneath is severe: about how easily first impressions are mistaken for judgment, about the gap between the people one appears to be and the person one is, about the work it takes to see another person clearly enough to love them well.

Why it still matters

How the Novel Learned to Think

Austen perfected free indirect discourse — the technique that lets a narrator slip into a character's mind without leaving the third person. When the narrator says "It is a truth universally acknowledged," the voice is not the narrator's belief but the gossip of a small society, ironized. When Elizabeth thinks Darcy proud, the thought reads as both her judgment and the narrator's gentle correction of it. This double voice — character and narrator overlaid, neither cancelling the other — is the engine of every great novel since. James, Joyce, Woolf, Mansfield, Munro all work in the technical vocabulary Austen made standard. To read Pride and Prejudice is to watch the English novel acquire its central technical resource. It is also to discover that the resource was invented not for high seriousness but for comedy — for letting a foolish character speak in her own voice while the narrator quietly corrects her.

Reading Other People

The novel's title names two failures of perception. Darcy's pride keeps him from seeing the people around him generously. Elizabeth's prejudice keeps her from seeing the people around her accurately. The plot is the slow and painful correction of both — and the correction is moral, not just factual. The point is not that Elizabeth was wrong about Darcy; it is that she was wrong in a way that mattered, that revealed something about her own willingness to be flattered. The point is not that Darcy was proud; it is that his pride blinded him to the harm he did. The novel insists that the work of seeing other people clearly is not optional, and not easy, and never finished. Modern readers in a culture of instant judgment recognize the lesson immediately. Austen's diagnosis of how we get other people wrong is still current and still painful.

The Honesty of Romance

It is fashionable to call Pride and Prejudice escapist. It is not. Underneath the courtship plot is an unsentimental analysis of the marriage market in Regency England, in which women without fortunes had no other route to security. Charlotte Lucas marries Mr. Collins, a man she does not love and barely respects, because the alternative is dependency on her brother for life. Lydia's elopement with Wickham is not a romantic mishap — it is a real catastrophe that would have destroyed her sisters' chances too. Austen never lets you forget what is at stake. The romance between Elizabeth and Darcy is a happy ending, but it is a happy ending shadowed by the knowledge of how few of those her contemporaries would get. The novel is a love story; it is also a quietly pointed examination of the economic conditions that decide whose love stories are allowed to end well.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Intelligence as the only real virtue.

The novel consistently rewards characters who see clearly and punishes those who perform. I want to read it as Austen's sustained argument that good judgement — not wealth, not rank, not even kindness — is the one quality that makes a person worth marrying.

The stakes of the marriage market.

Elizabeth Bennet is funny and smart and entirely at the mercy of men she can't control. A wrong marriage means a wasted life; no marriage means poverty. I want to read the comedy without losing sight of what is actually at stake for every woman in every scene.

Darcy's humiliation as education.

He is rich, handsome, well-connected, and entirely wrong about himself. His first proposal to Elizabeth is one of the great literary disasters. I want to track exactly what it takes to break his pride — and whether Austen presents that transformation as realistic.

Elizabeth as unreliable narrator of her own life.

She is witty and self-aware — and badly wrong about Wickham, wrong about Darcy, wrong about Jane's feelings. I want to read the novel asking where Elizabeth's self-knowledge fails her, and whether Austen intends her as a cautionary tale as much as a heroine.

Key figures

Elizabeth Bennet
Lizzy

The second of five sisters, the sharpest mind in the family, her father's favorite. She is twenty, witty, proud of her own discernment, and — she discovers — wrong about almost everything that matters. The novel is the slow correction of her judgment, and the slow recognition that the man she most despised is the man she has most undervalued. She becomes, in the course of the book, the most clear-eyed character in English fiction.

Mr. Darcy
Master of Pemberley

Wealthy, proud, awkward, and — beneath all that — deeply principled. He insults Elizabeth at their first meeting and spends the rest of the novel correcting the version of himself that produced the insult. His first proposal is one of the worst proposals in literature. His second is one of the most earned. The progress between them is the moral architecture of the book.

Jane Bennet
The Eldest Sister

Beautiful, gentle, slow to judge anyone harshly. She falls in love with Mr. Bingley, Darcy's friend, at the same ball where Elizabeth meets Darcy. Their romance is the novel's quiet B-plot — straightforward where Elizabeth and Darcy's is complicated. Jane's near-loss of Bingley shows what is at stake for women in this world: the wrong rumor, the wrong friend, the wrong moment, and the marriage that would have made you safe is gone.

Mr. Bennet
The Father

Master of Longbourn, an estate that will pass to a male cousin on his death. He is intelligent, witty, and emotionally absent — preferring his library to his family. The novel is gently severe about him: his irony is funny, but his unwillingness to engage with his daughters' future is a moral failure. He loves Elizabeth without doing anything to protect her.

Mrs. Bennet
The Mother

Frantic, undignified, single-minded about getting her daughters married — and, in the world of the novel, completely correct that this is the only thing that matters. Austen makes her ridiculous, and then keeps reminding you that her ridiculousness is the rational response to a system in which her daughters' survival depends on five marriages. The book's most uncomfortable balance: she is laughed at, and she is right.

Mr. Wickham
The Charming Officer

A handsome militia officer, full of anecdotes about his hard usage at the hands of Mr. Darcy — most of them lies. Elizabeth believes him, and the novel spends much of its middle section unpicking why she did. He elopes with the youngest Bennet sister Lydia, threatening the family's whole respectability, and is bought off by Darcy. He is the novel's most polished portrait of a charming man you should not trust.
