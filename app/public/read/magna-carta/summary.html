Magna Carta

1215 CE · by King John of England

The book in brief

Magna Carta is a settlement imposed on King John of England by his rebellious barons in June 1215. Its sixty-three clauses are mostly the small change of medieval governance — feudal dues, forest rights, the fate of foreign mercenaries — alongside a handful of principles that have echoed across eight centuries: that the king is subject to law, that no free man may be imprisoned without lawful judgment, that justice cannot be sold, refused, or delayed. The document is not a constitution. It is a peace treaty between a king and the men he had pushed too far.

The treaty was sealed at Runnymede on 15 June 1215. John had lost Normandy to France in 1204 and spent a decade levying ever-heavier taxes to fund a campaign to win it back. He alienated the barons through extortion and hostage-taking, quarreled bitterly with Pope Innocent III, and lost a disastrous battle at Bouvines in 1214. By the spring of 1215 the barons had renounced their allegiance and seized London. Magna Carta was the price John paid for his throne. He repudiated it within weeks; the Pope annulled it within ten; civil war resumed and John died of dysentery in October 1216. The charter was reissued under his young son and again under later kings, until it became the foundation document its signatories had never imagined.

It is a document that outgrew itself. Most of its provisions are dead letter, feudal vocabulary describing a vanished social order. But the few clauses that mattered, especially clause 39 on lawful judgment, were durable enough to make the whole charter the symbol of constitutional government. Seventeenth-century parliamentarians invoked it against the Stuarts. American colonists invoked it against George III. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights stands in its line. The text is medieval; the myth is modern; the relationship between the two is part of the story.

Why it still matters

The King Is Subject to Law

The principle that even a king must obey the law is the most consequential idea Magna Carta committed to writing. The principle was not new in 1215 — medieval canon law had been arguing some version of it for two centuries — but the charter put it in a form that could be cited, copied, and enforced. Eight hundred years of constitutional government rest on this single claim: that political authority is bounded by something other than itself. Every later doctrine of the rule of law, of constitutional limits on executive power, of the separation between sovereign and law, traces back through Magna Carta to the moment a group of armed barons made an English king sign a document admitting he was not absolute. The principle is durable enough that contemporary courts in dozens of countries still invoke it in cases the medieval drafters could never have imagined.

Due Process Begins

Clause 39 — that no free man may be seized, imprisoned, dispossessed, or destroyed except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land — is the foundation of due process in every common-law jurisdiction. The clause traveled. It was reaffirmed in the reissues of 1216, 1217, 1225, and 1297, embedded in English statute, invoked by Sir Edward Coke against the Stuart kings in the seventeenth century, transplanted to colonial American charters in the eighteenth century, and written into the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments of the United States Constitution. The American writ of habeas corpus, the European Convention on Human Rights' right to a fair trial, and the procedural guarantees of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights all stand in clause 39's lineage. Forty words written for thirteenth-century barons became the procedural backbone of modern liberty across two continents.

A Document That Outgrew Itself

Most of Magna Carta's sixty-three clauses concern medieval matters with no contemporary application — scutage rates, fish-weirs in the Thames, the precise dimensions of a London quart, the wardship of minor heirs. The charter was a peace treaty between a particular king and his particular barons, and it failed within weeks of its signing. But the few principles that mattered — that the king is bound by law, that justice cannot be sold, that free men cannot be imprisoned without judgment — were powerful enough to make the whole document the symbol of constitutional government. The myth grew larger than the text, and the gap between text and myth is itself part of what political documents do. The 1215 charter was a failure. What happened to it afterward — the reissues, the misreadings, the strategic invocations across eight centuries — is the actual story of how constitutional government was built.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The political crisis behind the charter.

Who were the rebel barons, and what had John actually done? The charter emerged from a specific breakdown — lost wars, extortionate taxation, the king's habit of taking hostages, selling offices, and overriding feudal custom whenever it suited him. Reading with this angle means tracking which clauses address which abuse, and understanding Runnymede as a transaction, not a revolution.

The Clause 39 lineage.

One clause — no free man seized or imprisoned except by lawful judgement of his peers — became the cornerstone of eight centuries of constitutional law. Reading with this angle means tracing a single idea: how forty words written for medieval barons became habeas corpus, the Fifth Amendment, and the legal bedrock of liberal democracy.

The medieval feudal world.

The Magna Carta is dense with terms that have no modern equivalent: scutage, relief, wardship, seisin, mort d'ancestor. Reading with this angle means decoding that vocabulary and seeing the document as a picture of a working feudal society — how land was held, inherited, and taxed, and what obligations flowed between lords, vassals, and the king.

Constitutional origins.

The Magna Carta is often called the first constitutional document. But what does that mean? The 1215 version was annulled, reissued, revised, and reinterpreted across centuries before it became a symbol. Reading with this angle means asking how a failed peace treaty between a king and his barons came to underpin American democracy, international human rights law, and the modern concept of the rule of law.

Key figures

King John
SIGNATORY UNDER DURESS

King of England from 1199 to 1216. Intelligent, suspicious, and erratic, John had lost Normandy to France in 1204 and spent a decade levying ever-heavier taxes to fund campaigns to reclaim it. His abuse of feudal custom — extortionate reliefs, arbitrary seizures, hostage-taking, the sale of royal offices — united the barons against him. He sealed the charter intending to repudiate it at the first opportunity, and he did. He died of dysentery in October 1216, sixteen months after Runnymede.

Stephen Langton
ARCHBISHOP AND MEDIATOR

Archbishop of Canterbury and the most important broker at Runnymede. Langton had been at the center of the bitter dispute between John and Pope Innocent III for years — John had blocked his appointment, the Pope had laid England under interdict, and Langton had spent five years in exile in France. He used his position to help draft the charter's language and push both sides toward agreement. Some historians credit him with supplying the legal framework that gave the document its durability.

Pope Innocent III
THE ANNULMENT

The most powerful pope of the medieval period. Innocent had backed John's restoration to papal favor after the five-year interdict that closed England's churches. When John appealed to him after Runnymede, Innocent annulled Magna Carta within ten weeks, calling it shameful, demeaning, illegal, and unjust, and excommunicating the barons who had imposed it. His annulment made no practical difference — John died the following year, and the charter was reissued under different kings without papal objection.

The Twenty-Five Barons
THE ENFORCEMENT COUNCIL

Clause 61 created a council of twenty-five barons with the legal right to seize royal lands and castles if the king violated the charter. It was Magna Carta's enforcement mechanism — and the clause that made it a direct threat to royal authority. Pope Innocent cited clause 61 specifically when annulling the document; no later reissue of the charter included it. The clause is the most explicitly revolutionary thing the 1215 settlement contained, and the first thing that was struck out when the settlement was rewritten.

Clause 39
THE DURABLE PRINCIPLE

Forty words: "No free man shall be seized or imprisoned, or stripped of his rights or possessions, or outlawed or exiled, or deprived of his standing in any other way, nor will we proceed with force against him, or send others to do so, except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land." Clause 39 is the cornerstone of habeas corpus, of due process, of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. Almost everything later generations meant by Magna Carta is contained in this single sentence.

Free Men
THE PROTECTED CLASS

The liberties of Magna Carta were granted to all free men of the kingdom. In 1215, free men were a legal category — not serfs, not villeins, not the majority of the population. The barons negotiating at Runnymede meant themselves and their tenants, not the unfree laborers who worked their fields. Over subsequent centuries, as serfdom declined and the legal definition of freedom expanded, the charter's protections expanded with it. The phrase became universal only by being repeatedly reinterpreted beyond its original scope.
