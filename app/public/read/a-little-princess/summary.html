A Little Princess

1905 CE · by Frances Hodgson Burnett

The book in brief

A Little Princess is the story of Sara Crewe, a wealthy seven-year-old whose father places her at Miss Minchin's Select Seminary in Edwardian London while he returns to manage his fortune in India. Sara is bright, imaginative, fluent in French, and loved by everyone — staff, classmates, and the youngest students whom she mothers. On her eleventh birthday her father dies and his fortune collapses. Sara is stripped of everything overnight: her rooms, her dolls, her dresses, her place at the school. Miss Minchin, who had toadied to the heiress, installs the orphan in a freezing attic and works her as an unpaid drudge. The middle of the book is Sara surviving this in cold and hunger — feeding the rat in the wall, telling stories to the scullery maid Becky, refusing to let cruelty define what she is. Then the Magic begins. Warm food appears in the attic, then blankets, then a fire. The man who has been searching London for Captain Crewe's lost daughter has been living next door all along.

Frances Hodgson Burnett wrote it twice. She first published Sara's story as a magazine novella in 1888 — eighty pages, a smaller arc. After it was adapted as a successful stage play, she rewrote and expanded it into the novel that appeared in 1905, almost twice the length, with the rich middle of the attic, Becky, and Melchisedec the rat. By then Burnett was already famous for Little Lord Fauntleroy and was writing The Secret Garden in parallel.

The book's reputation has shifted over a century. To readers who first met it as children, it is the consolation of a girl who refuses to be diminished. To later readers, it is also a sharp short novel about how cruelty wraps itself in respectability — Miss Minchin is not a witch from a fairy tale, she is a school principal — and about what survives in a person when status, money, and protection are stripped away in a single afternoon. The class politics are Edwardian and unsparing: Becky, born into service, is treated as something less than human by almost everyone except Sara, and the novel does not pretend otherwise.

Why it still matters

Imagination as Armor

Sara survives the attic by an act of imagination she practices openly: she pretends she is a princess, and treats every degradation as something a princess would face down without flinching. The book is precise about how this works — not as escape from reality but as a way of refusing to let the world's contempt write itself onto her. When Miss Minchin screams at her, when Lavinia mocks her, when she is sent into the rain on errands without dinner, Sara silently remembers that princesses had to bear worse. Burnett does not present this as a child's coping mechanism the reader is meant to outgrow. She presents it as the discovery of what dignity actually is. The book has been read by children for a century as a manual for surviving cruelty without becoming small. It still works.

Cruelty in Plain Sight

Miss Minchin is not a witch or an ogre. She is a respectable Victorian schoolmistress with a successful business, a sister, a public reputation, and church connections. Her cruelty toward Sara after the bankruptcy is not a tantrum — it is a calibrated set of decisions about how unprofitable children should be housed, fed, and worked, defended in language any reader of her time would recognise as ordinary good sense. Burnett is unusually exact about the shape this kind of cruelty takes: how it prefers other people's children, how it borrows the language of charity, how it punishes any sign that the punished child has not internalised her diminished status. For a children's book, A Little Princess is one of the clearest portraits in English of how respectable cruelty actually operates.

What 'Princess' Means

The book's argument is that the word 'princess' is not about rank or wealth — it is about a particular way of holding oneself in the world, especially when the world is unkind. Sara works this out by force of necessity. When she had every advantage, the title was a game she played; once everything is taken, it becomes the only thing she has, and she holds to it deliberately. The novel ends with her fortune restored, but it has spent its full middle showing that the restored fortune is not the point. What the book leaves a reader with is not a fairy-tale wish for diamonds back but a definition of dignity: it is the thing you keep behaving as if you have, when there is no external evidence for it left at all.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Imagination as a way of surviving.

Sara pretends she is a princess to bear what Miss Minchin does to her. I want to follow how the book uses imagination — not as escape, but as a way of refusing to let the world define her.

Miss Minchin's respectable cruelty.

Miss Minchin is not a fairy-tale villain. She is a respectable schoolmistress whose cruelty is public and ordinary. I want to track how the book shows that kind of cruelty wrapping itself in respectability.

Sara and Becky.

Sara is rich, then poor; Becky was poor from the start. They become friends across a class line that Edwardian London usually held tight. I want to follow what the book is saying about who they are to each other.

What 'princess' actually means.

Sara keeps calling herself a princess, even in the attic. I want to read the book paying attention to what she means by that word, and how the meaning shifts as everything else is stripped away.

Key figures

Sara Crewe
The Princess Inside

The seven-year-old daughter of an English captain stationed in India. Her mother is dead; her father has just brought her to London to be educated at Miss Minchin's Select Seminary. She is unusually bright, fluent in French, and described by everyone as old for her age — thoughtful, courteous, comfortable with adults. The book follows her from her arrival as the school's wealthiest pupil, through her father's death and the loss of his fortune, into the attic where she works as an unpaid drudge — and out the other side. She holds to her own definition of who she is throughout, which is what the book is about.

Captain Crewe
The Father

Sara's young, doting widowed father, an English officer in India. He is the warm center of the book's first chapter and dies offstage in the seventh, leaving Sara legally and financially alone. His death, and the bankruptcy that follows, are the hinge of the novel. He is loved, foolish, generous, and trusting — the trust kills him. The book never lets the reader forget him.

Miss Minchin
The Schoolmistress

The owner of Miss Minchin's Select Seminary for Young Ladies. Tall, sharp-faced, a respectable Edwardian businesswoman. While Sara is rich she fawns; the moment the bankruptcy is announced she shifts in a single afternoon to treating the orphan as a free servant. Her cruelty is not theatrical — it is the cruelty of someone protecting a business and her own dignity, and it is the more frightening for how ordinary it is. Her sister Miss Amelia is a softer and weaker copy of her, and the book is patient about the difference.

Becky
The Scullery Maid

Fourteen years old, born into service, working for Miss Minchin in the kitchens. Sara meets her sleeping exhausted in front of the schoolroom fire and treats her as a person from that moment on, which transforms Becky's life. After Sara's fall, Becky is moved to the attic next to her, and the two become daily companions in cold and hunger. Becky is the closest reader Sara has — she takes Sara's stories as gospel, and her loyalty is the book's quietest love.

Mr. Carrisford
The Indian Gentleman

An English gentleman who returns from India ill, broken, and racked with guilt. He had been Captain Crewe's business partner and friend; in the panic that followed the failure of the diamond mines he believed Crewe was ruined and lost his nerve. Crewe's fortune was in fact intact, but Crewe died before learning so, and Carrisford has spent the years since searching London for the lost daughter he is now too sick to find. He moves into the house next to Miss Minchin's seminary without realising who lives in the attic across the wall.

Ram Dass
The Magician

Mr. Carrisford's Indian servant, a Lascar who has come to London with him. He sees Sara in the attic from the next-door roof one evening and recognises in her the daughter his master has been searching for, before anyone else does. Through a series of soundless night-time crossings of the wall — carrying coal, hot food, blankets, eventually whole transformations of the room — he becomes the literal hand of the Magic that begins, in the second half of the book, to find Sara in her attic. Sara never sees him do it. The book is careful about who is allowed to know what.
