Antigone

441 BCE · by Sophocles

The book in brief

Antigone opens the morning after a civil war. Antigone's two brothers, Eteocles and Polynices, have killed each other fighting for the throne of Thebes. The new king Creon — their uncle, Oedipus's brother-in-law — decrees that Eteocles will be buried with full honors and Polynices, the rebel, will be left to rot outside the walls. Anyone who buries him will die. Antigone defies the decree. She buries her brother with a thin scattering of dust, is caught, and is brought before Creon. The play is the collision that follows — between a king who has staked his authority on the edict and a woman who refuses to let her brother lie unburied.

Sophocles wrote Antigone around 441 BCE, when he was about fifty-five and at the height of his powers in the Athenian theater. It is the third in the order of the Theban plays' action but the first written. The play is short — eleven scenes in roughly 1,350 lines — and built around a single confrontation that ramifies through every other relationship in the house: Creon and his son Haemon, Creon and the prophet Tiresias, Antigone and her sister Ismene, Antigone and herself.

It is the most argued-over play in the Greek tragic canon. Hegel called it the perfect tragedy because no character is simply wrong — Creon represents the rational claims of the political community, Antigone the older claims of family and the gods, and each is a partial truth that destroys the other. The play refuses to settle the question. Two and a half millennia of readers have not settled it either.

Why it still matters

Two Laws That Cannot Both Be Obeyed

The play stages a collision between divine law — the duty to bury kin, older than any city — and human law, in the form of Creon's decree against burying a traitor. Both have moral weight. Both demand obedience. Antigone chooses one and dies for it; Creon chooses the other and loses his son, his wife, and his standing. Sophocles refuses to declare a winner. He shows what happens when two genuine goods collide and neither side is willing to give ground. The shape of that collision — civil disobedience grounded in higher law — is the form taken by every later argument about conscience against the state, from Aquinas through Thoreau to Martin Luther King's Letter from Birmingham Jail.

The Stubborn Sister

Antigone is one of the first fully realized female protagonists in Western literature. She speaks for herself, argues her own case, and dies without retracting a word. She is articulate, defiant, and absolutely sure she is right. Whether her certainty is virtue or pride has been argued about for two and a half millennia. She does not weep when sentenced. She tells Creon plainly that she expected nothing better from him. Sophocles makes you take her completely seriously while also showing what her absolutism costs the people around her — Ismene, Haemon, even the Chorus. She is admirable and she is terrifying. The play insists on both.

What Tyranny Looks Like Up Close

Creon is not a monster. He is a new ruler making a defensible call about civil order in the wake of a civil war. The play shows how a reasonable position becomes tyranny — through inflexibility, through pride, through refusing to listen to anyone who disagrees. Haemon tells him no man is wise enough to stand alone. Tiresias tells him the city is polluted. The Chorus hints. Creon hears all of it as personal attack. Sophocles is precise about the mechanism: tyranny is not a different kind of person from a reasonable ruler; it is what a reasonable ruler becomes when he cannot bear to be told he is wrong. Modern political readers from Hannah Arendt to Jean Anouilh have used the play as a diagnosis.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Divine law vs. civic law.

Creon's edict is perfectly rational — the state cannot function if every citizen decides which laws to honour. Antigone's refusal is equally coherent — the gods' unwritten laws precede any king's decree. The play refuses to let either argument win. Notice how each character accuses the other of exactly what they are themselves doing: mistaking private will for universal principle.

Hegel's reading: both right, both doomed.

Hegel called Antigone the perfect tragedy because neither character is simply wrong. Creon represents the rational claims of political community; Antigone represents the claims of family and the divine. Each is a partial embodiment of ethical life, and each destroys the other. Read the play asking not who is right but why being right is not enough.

The woman who says no.

Antigone is not a rebel without a cause. She knows exactly what she is doing, states her reason plainly, and accepts the consequence without flinching. She is the first figure in Western literature to stand against state authority on grounds of conscience alone. Notice how the men around her respond — not with argument but with incredulity that she dares.

Creon as the tragic hero.

Antigone dies early and unbowed. Creon survives long enough to watch everything he loves destroyed by his own hand. The Chorus addresses him as protagonist; Tiresias speaks to him as the man whose error has set events in motion. The counter-reading — that this is Creon's tragedy more than Antigone's — has real textual weight. Test it.

Key figures

Antigone
The One Who Buries Him

Daughter of Oedipus, sister to both brothers, and engaged to Creon's son Haemon. She enters the play having already made her decision and asks her sister Ismene to help her bury Polynices. When Ismene refuses, she goes alone. Caught and brought before Creon, she does not deny what she did or beg for her life — she argues that the gods' law preceded his. She is the first figure in Western literature to refuse a state law on grounds of conscience.

Ismene
The Sister Who Refuses

Antigone's younger sister and the first person Antigone tells. Ismene loves her sister and is terrified for her — she refuses to help not from indifference but from a clear-eyed reading of the odds. When the burial is discovered, she tries to share the blame so she can share the death. Antigone refuses to let her. Sophocles is not unkind to Ismene. She is what reasonable fear looks like next to absolute conviction.

Creon
The New King

Brother of Iocasta, uncle of Antigone, now ruler of Thebes after both his nephews killed each other. The edict against burying Polynices is his first act of governance — a line drawn to establish that the state is sovereign over family loyalty. He is not cruel by nature. He is a new ruler who has confused firmness with wisdom, and he keeps confusing them long after his son and the prophet have warned him otherwise.

Haemon
Creon's Son

Engaged to Antigone and Creon's only surviving heir. He comes to his father not to plead for his betrothed but to offer counsel: the city is murmuring, the edict feels wrong, no man is wise enough to stand alone. The scene between father and son is one of the most painful in the play — Haemon is the one character who tries to give Creon a way out before it is too late, and Creon will not take it.

Tiresias
The Blind Prophet

The old seer who has never yet brought Thebes a false word. He arrives after Antigone has been sealed in the cave, when the consequences of Creon's decision are already moving in ways no one can see. He reports what the gods have shown him: the altars are polluted, the birds will not feed, the city is sick. Creon accuses him of being bribed. Tiresias leaves. By the time Creon believes him, the damage is finished.

The Chorus
Elders of Thebes

Fifteen Theban elders summoned by Creon at the start to witness his accession and endorse his edict. They sing the celebrated 'Ode to Man' early on — a hymn to human cleverness that the rest of the play quietly tests against the limits of cleverness. They never openly defy Creon. They watch, hedge, and eventually realize the man they are advising is walking toward ruin they could have helped him avoid.
