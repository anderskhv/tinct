Niels Lyhne

1880 CE · by Jens Peter Jacobsen

The book in brief

Niels Lyhne is the story of a Danish dreamer who tries to live without God and never quite knows what to put in God's place. He is born on a rural estate in 19th-century Jutland to a poetic, romantically miserable mother and a steady, prosaic father. From childhood he hovers between their two temperaments, drawn to poetry and inwardness. As a boy he watches his beautiful aunt Edele die slowly of illness; her atheism, calmly held in the face of the village priest's appeals, becomes the first anchor of his unbelief. As a young man in Copenhagen he loves the older Mrs. Boye, a worldly widow who treats him as a man for the first time. He visits his friend Erik, a sculptor married to Fennimore, and conducts an affair with Fennimore that ends catastrophically. Years later he marries the gentle Gerda, who dies; their child dies; Niels enlists in the Second Schleswig War and is shot. The novel ends at his deathbed, where a priest is offered to him and he refuses.

Jens Peter Jacobsen wrote it slowly, over six years, while dying of tuberculosis. He had trained as a botanist and translated Darwin into Danish before turning to fiction. The novel was published in Copenhagen in 1880, when he was thirty-three. He lived four more years. The book is short — about 250 pages — but covers a whole life, in twelve loosely linked sections that move by emotional logic rather than incident. Its prose is lyric, microscopic, attentive to weather and to the smallest movements of feeling.

It quietly reshaped European literature. Rilke read it as a young man and never stopped reading it; Stefan Zweig and Thomas Mann admired it. It is the great Scandinavian novel about losing one's faith and trying to keep going.

Why it still matters

Living Without God

The novel is one of the first in European literature to take the lived consequences of unbelief with full seriousness. Niels casts off God in adolescence after watching his aunt Edele die calmly without faith, and the novel follows him through every test the next thirty years can find — first love, marriage, fatherhood, the death of a child, his own war wound. Each loss is offered to him as an occasion to recant. He refuses each time, including at the end, when a priest is brought to his bedside and he sends the priest away. Jacobsen does not present this refusal as triumph. The novel insists that the hardest thing about losing God is not the metaphysics but what it does to ordinary moments — the bedside of a sick child, the letter to a grieving mother, the temptation to comfort yourself with a prayer you no longer believe. Few books since have measured the cost more honestly.

The Romantic Bet

Niels stakes his life on the proposition that beauty, feeling, dreams, and poetry can stand in for what religion used to provide — that an inner life, vivid enough, can ground a meaningful existence even without a heaven beyond it. The novel respects the bet. Jacobsen gives Niels real talent, real love, real moments of nearly mystical perception. And then, with the patience of a man who has been watching his own death approach for years, he tests the bet against everything that actually breaks a person: an unfaithful affair he never recovers from, a son who dies in his arms, a wife who loses her faith and never replaces it before she dies. The novel does not declare the bet a failure. It shows what living inside it looks like in long stretches. Whether romantic feeling can substitute for religion is a question every secular adult since 1880 has had to answer for themselves.

Living In Your Head

Niels is a man who lives mostly inside his own head. He plans a poet's life, drafts speeches he will never deliver, falls in love with women he then watches in his imagination instead of meeting in the room. The novel is exact about the texture of this kind of inwardness — its luminosity and its paralysis, the way it produces beautiful private worlds and makes ordinary action feel slightly false. Around him everyone else simply does things: Erik sculpts, Mrs. Boye flirts, Fennimore takes a lover, the war breaks out, Niels enlists almost as a way of finally acting. Jacobsen writes from the inside of this temperament without pretending it is a virtue. The novel is the ancestor of every later fiction about the over-examined inner life — a fault line that runs through any number of contemporary biographies and feeds, even now, much of what people read about themselves online.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Living with ideals reality defeats.

Niels believes in things — in beauty, in honesty, in the people he loves. Reality defeats each belief in turn. I want to follow what the novel proposes about how to live when that keeps happening.

Jacobsen writing while dying.

Jacobsen had tuberculosis during composition. Death enters the novel again and again — not as an event but as a presence. I want to read it with that biography in mind and see what changes.

The pattern in the relationships.

Niels loves several women and loses each of them differently. I want to track the pattern — what he keeps doing, what he keeps failing to do — and understand what the novel is saying about love and self-knowledge.

Atheism under pressure.

Niels is a committed atheist in a world that still reaches for God in moments of extremity. I want to follow how his unbelief holds or fails under the pressures the novel places on it, and what Jacobsen is arguing.

Key figures

Niels Lyhne
The Dreamer

The son of a poetic mother, Bartholine, and a steady, prosaic father. He grows up reciting his mother's verses and watching his aunt Edele die without faith. He moves to Copenhagen, studies vaguely, falls in love repeatedly, becomes an atheist on principle, marries late, loses his wife and child, and enlists in the war. He spends most of his life inside his own head. The novel measures, patiently, what that costs him and the people who love him.

Bartholine
The Mother

Niels's mother. She married a steady country gentleman believing he would carry her into the world of poetry she dreamed about, and discovered he had no such intention. She lives most of her life inside the books she reads and the verses she recites to her son. She is the source of Niels's inwardness and the first warning the novel gives about it. Her late trip with him to Lake Garda, where she finally sees the Italy she had imagined for forty years, is one of the book's quietest scenes.

Edele
The Aunt Who Dies Without God

Niels's young aunt. She comes to the family estate to recover from illness and is courted ineffectually by the local priest in the language of consolation. She dies without conceding any of it. Her calm refusal to accept the pastor's offers is the moment Niels's atheism is born. She is on the page only briefly, but the novel returns to her again and again, as the standard against which Niels measures every later test of his unbelief.

Mrs. Boye
The Widow

An older Copenhagen widow, witty, worldly, and unattached. She takes Niels seriously as a man for the first time and conducts a relationship with him that he is too young to understand. She talks about books and Paris and married women's freedoms. She eventually sees that he is not the partner she needs and lets him go. The relationship leaves him more complicated than it found him, which is exactly what such relationships are for in a novel of this kind.

Fennimore
The Catastrophe

Niels's friend Erik's wife. The novel's most devastating relationship: Niels visits Erik, who has stopped working and started drinking, and Fennimore, who is lonely and bitter, falls into an affair with Niels almost without deciding to. When Erik dies suddenly in an accident, the affair collapses under what they both have done. Fennimore turns on Niels with fury that the novel takes seriously. It is the chapter the book never quite recovers from, and it is the one Niels does not.

Gerda
The Late Marriage

The young woman Niels marries late, after years of drifting. She is gentle, religious, and unworldly. She and Niels have a son. She loses her faith on her deathbed and dies frightened. Their son dies soon after. Niels sits with each of them through their last hours — the test the novel has been preparing for him since Edele's death. What he does and does not do at those bedsides is the moral center of the second half of the book.
