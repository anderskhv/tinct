Summary

Victor arrives at Ingolstadt in a state of mixed grief and embarrassment — grieving his mother, embarrassed about the alchemical studies he can no longer entirely defend. He meets two professors. The first is Krempe, an irritable, ugly man who, on hearing what Victor has been reading, mocks him: "Have you really spent your time studying such nonsense?" Victor leaves the meeting humiliated and almost ready to abandon the field.

The second is Professor Waldman. Waldman lectures on chemistry with a reverence that strikes Victor immediately. He acknowledges, briefly, that the modern science he is going to teach grew out of the questions the alchemists were asking — questions about the principles of life, the structure of matter, the limits of human knowledge. The alchemists, Waldman says, asked great questions but had bad methods. The methods have improved; the questions are still alive. He invites Victor, after the lecture, to use his laboratory. Victor leaves the meeting transformed.

Within weeks Victor has thrown himself into the new studies with the same obsessiveness he had brought to the old. Waldman becomes his teacher. Krempe is ignored. Victor begins to focus, narrowly, on a single question — the principle of animation. He has not said yet what he intends to do with the answer. The chapter ends with him deep in the work, two years away from any of his family or friends, in a state his university acquaintances are beginning to find alarming.
