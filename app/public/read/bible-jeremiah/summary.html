Jeremiah a guided tour

late 7th–early 6th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Latter Prophets

A boy from a priestly family in Anathoth is told he was set apart before birth and appointed a prophet to the nations. He protests that he cannot speak. For forty years he does nothing but speak — and watches the city he loves fall.

The book in brief

Jeremiah is the longest book in the Bible by word count and the most psychologically intimate of the prophetic books. A young man from Anathoth, a small priestly town three miles north-east of Jerusalem, is called by God around 627 BCE and preaches for more than forty years — through the slow collapse of the kingdom of Judah, through the Babylonian conquest of 587, through the destruction of the temple — until he is dragged into Egyptian exile against his will. The book he leaves behind preserves his oracles, his symbolic actions, and his confessions: six passages scattered through chapters 11–20 in which the prophet quarrels with his own calling in a register that has no parallel in any other prophetic book.

What makes Jeremiah unlike any other prophetic book is the quality of its interiority. The reluctant prophet who answered God's call with ah Lord God, I cannot speak, for I am a child spent forty years trying to hold the word back and discovering he could not. Chapter 31 contains the new covenant oracle, the source of the phrase that names the Christian New Testament. Chapter 32 records the prophet buying a field at Anathoth during the Babylonian siege — an act of hope in the middle of the worst hour. Chapter 36 describes the burning of the scroll by King Jehoiakim and the dictation of it all again, longer. The book is a document of what it costs to tell the truth for forty years to people who do not want to hear it.

Jeremiah 1 of 52
Jeremiah 1

The Call of the Boy Prophet

Chapter 1 is among the most psychologically charged call narratives in the Hebrew Bible. God tells the young Jeremiah that before he was formed in the womb he was known, before he was born he was set apart, appointed a prophet to the nations. Jeremiah answers: ah Lord God, I cannot speak — I am only a child. The divine response brooks no objection: do not say I am only a child, for wherever I send you you shall go. Two visions follow immediately. The first is an almond branch — God tells Jeremiah he is watching over his word to perform it. The second is a boiling pot tilted from the north — evil will pour from the north onto all the land. Jeremiah is told he will be set as a fortified city, an iron pillar, a bronze wall against the kings of Judah, its officials, priests, and people. They will fight against him but not prevail, because God is with him to deliver him. The chapter establishes the central paradox of the whole book: the most reluctant prophet in the canon is given the most terrible and sustained commission.

Jeremiah 2

Broken Cisterns and Forgotten Springs

Chapter 2 opens the long indictment of Israel that occupies most of the book's first section. God calls to Jerusalem's memory the faithfulness of her early days — the devotion she had in the desert, following the Lord into a barren land. That memory is now a reproach. Israel has abandoned her God and chased after worthless idols. The nations do not change their gods, even gods that are not gods; but Israel has exchanged her glory for what cannot profit. The image that carries the chapter is one of the most rhetorically powerful in the whole book: my people have committed two evils — they have forsaken me, the spring of living water, and have hewed out for themselves cisterns, broken cisterns that can hold no water. The chapter traces the trajectory of Israel's failure from the wilderness entry to the present: priests, lawyers, rulers, and prophets have all participated in the apostasy. Even in the most recent political alliances — with Egypt and Assyria — the people have run back and forth seeking help from everywhere except their God. The indictment closes on a note of near despair: how lightly Israel treats guilt, claiming innocence and saying, I have not sinned.

Jeremiah 3

Return, O Faithless Israel

Chapter 3 opens with the legal problem: under Mosaic law, a man whose wife has left him for another cannot take her back even if the second husband dies (Deuteronomy 24:1–4). Israel has done worse than the law contemplates — she has played the harlot with many lovers and still expects to return. Yet the charge pivots into an invitation. God calls to faithless Israel — not to the sister kingdom of Judah, which has seen Israel's fate and not repented but only pretended — but to Israel directly: return to me, for I am merciful, I will not be angry forever. The phrase faithless children, return is repeated three times in the chapter, its echo giving the passage the cadence of a father calling across a great distance to children who have wandered. The chapter also introduces the oracle of the shepherds after God's own heart who will feed the people with knowledge and understanding — a pastoral image that anticipates the new covenant language of chapter 31. The chapter ends with the imagined return of the faithless children, confessing their shame, acknowledging that the hills and mountains have not saved them, that in the Lord alone is the salvation of Israel. It is a vision of return the narrative of the rest of the book will make very difficult to see as having occurred.

Jeremiah 4

The Foe from the North Approaches

Chapter 4 begins with a continued call to repentance — if Israel will return, let her return to the Lord, remove her detestable things, circumcise herself to the Lord and remove the foreskin of her heart, lest God's wrath go out like fire because of the evil of their deeds. Then the alarm begins. A trumpet is blown in Zion. A destroyer of nations has set out. An evil is coming from the north. The military disaster approaching is described in language of gathering terror: standards are raised, forces withdraw into fortified cities, the lion from his thicket, the destroyer of nations on his way. The chapter includes one of the most harrowing passages in the book — Jeremiah's vision of the coming catastrophe rendered in cosmic terms: I looked at the earth and it was formless and empty, and to the heavens and they had no light. The phrase formless and empty (tohu wa-bohu) is the same phrase used to describe the earth before creation in Genesis 1:2. Jeremiah sees the coming destruction as an undoing of creation itself. The cities have fled. The fruitful land has become a desert. At the very end of the chapter the prophet speaks in his own voice: my bowels, my bowels — I am pained at my very heart. The agony is not rhetorical. The prophet who delivers the warning is destroyed by the same vision.

Jeremiah 5

No One Seeks Truth in Jerusalem

Chapter 5 opens with an extraordinary challenge. Go through Jerusalem's streets, God tells Jeremiah, look and see, search her squares. Can you find one person who does justice and seeks truth? If you can, God will pardon the city. The challenge echoes Abraham's negotiation over Sodom, but with a still lower threshold — one righteous person rather than ten. Jeremiah searches, and the search fails. Even when the people swear by the living Lord, they are swearing falsely. The poor have been foolish, the great have broken the yoke and burst the bonds; the prophet compares them to fed stallions, each neighing after his neighbor's wife. A foreign nation will be brought against them — a nation whose language Judah does not know, whose quiver is an open tomb. The chapter ends with the remarkable question: shall I not punish them for these things? And it is posed not in anger but with an almost plaintive rhetorical weight, as if God is asking the audience to evaluate the situation with him. The chapter closes on a note that will recur: prophets prophesy falsely, priests rule by their own authority, and the people love to have it so — and what will they do when the end comes?

Jeremiah 6

The Ancient Paths, Rejected

Chapter 6 is the last chapter of the opening section of oracles before the temple sermon. It continues the alarm — the army from the north is gathering around Jerusalem, the city must be evacuated, for Jerusalem is to be punished. The military language is close-up and specific: commanders tell their troops to attack at noon, to attack in the evening, to not hold back. Shepherds with their flocks encamp all around her. But the theological center of the chapter is the moment God calls the people to stand at the crossroads and look, ask for the ancient paths, the good way, and walk in it. They will find rest for their souls. But the people answered: we will not walk in it. Watchmen are set over the people, but the people say: we will not listen. The refusal is not ignorance but decision — they have weighed the options and turned away. The chapter closes with God telling Jeremiah that the whole house of Israel has rejected the law of the Lord; therefore the nations will be set as witnesses against them. The chapter also contains one of the rare passages in which Jeremiah speaks of himself with a measure of exhaustion: I am full of the wrath of the Lord, I am weary of holding it in. The prophet's exhaustion is a motif the confessions will amplify.

Jeremiah 7

The Temple Sermon

Chapter 7 contains one of the most controversial sermons in the Hebrew Bible. Jeremiah is commanded to stand in the gate of the Lord's house and deliver his message to everyone who enters. The message is a direct assault on the idea that the temple's existence guarantees God's protection. Do not trust in these deceptive words, the prophet says: the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord — the repetition itself mocks the magical thinking it describes. The temple is no talisman. If the worshippers are genuinely just — caring for the alien, the orphan, and the widow, not shedding innocent blood, not following other gods — then God will let them dwell in this place. But if they steal, murder, commit adultery, swear falsely, burn incense to Baal, and then come and stand before him in this house and say we are safe — then they have made it a den of robbers. God has seen it. He can do to this house what he did to Shiloh, where the ark had once rested and which had been destroyed. The chapter ends with God telling Jeremiah not even to pray for this people — do not offer prayer or intercession for them, for I will not hear you. The silencing of intercession is one of the most theologically acute moments in any prophetic book.

Jeremiah 8

No Balm in Gilead

Chapter 8 continues and deepens the indictment of Judah, introducing one of the most poignant questions in the Hebrew prophets. The chapter begins with an oracle of shocking desecration: the bones of Jerusalem's leaders will be dug up and exposed to the sun, moon, and stars they had worshipped — a final humiliation, refused even the dignity of burial. Even the stork knows its season, the swallow and crane their time of migration — but the people do not know the ordinance of the Lord. They say we are wise, we have the law of the Lord, but the scribes have handled it falsely with their lying pens. The chapter moves between oracles of accusation and passages of grief that are difficult to assign clearly to God or to the prophet — the ambiguity is probably intentional. The harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved. For the hurt of my people I am hurt; I mourn, and dismay has taken hold of me. Then the question that gives the chapter its lasting resonance: is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there? Why then has the wound of my people not been healed? The question is medical, geographical, and theological at once — Gilead was famous for its healing resin; the question is whether any medicine exists for what ails Judah. The chapter ends without an answer.

Jeremiah 9

Weeping in the Wilderness

Chapter 9 is one of the most intensely personal chapters in the first half of the book. Jeremiah opens with the wish that he had a lodging place of wayfarers in the desert — somewhere he could leave his people and be alone — because they are all adulterers, an assembly of treacherous people. The litany of their failures is social and intimate: they are trained to lie, they slander neighbors, they deceive friends, they teach their tongues falsehood, they tire themselves out committing iniquity. They will not know the Lord. The chapter then shifts to a mourning song — God himself mourning the destruction to come — and God calls for the professional mourning women to be summoned, those who know the dirges, so they can teach their daughters lamentation. The image of professional mourners teaching their craft as the most necessary skill in the approaching catastrophe is darkly practical. The chapter closes with one of the great leveling passages in the prophetic literature: do not let the wise boast in wisdom, or the strong in strength, or the rich in riches. Let whoever boasts boast in this alone — that they understand and know me, that I am the Lord who practices steadfast love, justice, and righteousness on the earth. For in these things I delight.

Jeremiah 10

Idols Have Mouths but Cannot Speak

Chapter 10 is a meditation on idolatry that reads like a sustained philosophical polemic. The nations cut down a tree from the forest, fasten it with hammer and nails so it does not move, decorate it with silver and gold, and bow down to it. It is like a scarecrow in a cucumber field — it cannot speak, it cannot walk, it must be carried. Then the contrast: the Lord is the true God, the living God, the everlasting King. At his wrath the earth quakes, the nations cannot endure his indignation. He made the earth by his power, established the world by his wisdom, stretched out the heavens by his understanding. Every goldsmith is put to shame by his idols, for his images are false, and there is no breath in them. They are worthless, a work of delusion. The chapter closes with a passage in Jeremiah's own voice — a prayer for correction that stops short of destruction: correct me, Lord, but in just measure, not in your anger, lest you bring me to nothing. Then a call to pour out God's wrath on the nations that do not know him, and on the families that do not call on his name — for they have devoured Jacob, and devoured him and consumed him and laid waste his habitation.

Jeremiah 11

The Broken Covenant and a Plot to Kill

Chapter 11 opens the section of the book known as the confessions. The chapter has two distinct parts. The first is a covenantal sermon: God tells Jeremiah to proclaim the terms of the covenant to the people of Judah and Jerusalem. The covenant is the one God made when he brought the ancestors out of Egypt: obey my voice, do all I command you, and I will be your God and you will be my people. The ancestors did not listen; neither have their children. Jeremiah is told not to pray for this people, for God will not listen. They may cry out, but God will not hear. The second part of the chapter reveals a personal crisis that the reader has not been prepared for: Jeremiah learns that the men of his own hometown of Anathoth are plotting to kill him. Do not prophesy in the name of the Lord or you will die by our hands. He did not know about the plot; God revealed it. His response is the first of the confessions: O Lord of hosts, who judges righteously, who tests the heart and the mind, let me see your vengeance upon them, for to you I have committed my cause. The intimacy of the betrayal — his own community, his own neighbors — gives the first confession its specific sharpness.

Jeremiah 12

Why Do the Wicked Prosper?

Chapter 12 contains the second of Jeremiah's confessions — the most direct complaint in the book about the problem of divine justice. Jeremiah brings his case to God: you are in the right, O Lord, when I complain to you — but let me put my case to you. Why does the way of the guilty prosper? Why do all who are treacherous thrive? You planted them and they have taken root; they grow and produce fruit; you are near in their mouths but far from their hearts. God's answer is not consolation but challenge: if you have raced with men on foot and they have wearied you, how will you compete with horses? If you fall down in a safe land, how will you manage in the thicket of the Jordan? The answer pushes the question back rather than answering it — you have not yet seen the worst. The chapter then shifts to God's own lament over the destruction of his heritage, his beloved, his vineyard. The desolation is described in terms of agricultural waste — shepherds destroy the vineyard, the whole land is laid waste, the pleasant fields turned to desert because no one takes it to heart. The chapter closes with an oracle of limited hope for the nations — if a nation learns the ways of God's people, it will be built up.

Jeremiah 13

The Ruined Linen Belt

Chapter 13 contains the first of Jeremiah's major symbolic actions. God tells him to buy a linen belt, put it around his waist, and not let it touch water. Then he is told to go to the Euphrates and hide it in a cleft of rock. A long time later — the text does not say how long — God tells him to return to the Euphrates and retrieve it. The belt has been ruined; it is good for nothing. The interpretation is given immediately: thus will God ruin the pride of Judah and the great pride of Jerusalem. As the belt clings to the waist of a man, so God had caused the whole house of Israel and Judah to cling to him — to be his people, his praise, his glory. But they would not listen. The chapter then continues with an oracle against pride in general terms — pride, God says, should break down; there is a time coming when the cities of Judah will be given to exile. The chapter closes with a devastating apostrophe to Jerusalem: if you ask why these things have come upon you, it is because of the greatness of your iniquity. Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots? Then also you can do good who are accustomed to do evil. The rhetorical question implies that the situation is beyond remediation.

Jeremiah 14

Drought, Sword, and Famine

Chapter 14 is organized around a drought. Judah mourns, her gates languish, her people cry out to the ground, farmers are dismayed. Even the doe abandons her newborn because there is no grass. The wild donkeys stand on bare heights, panting for air like jackals. Jeremiah intercedes — he acknowledges Judah's iniquity, confesses her apostasy, and appeals to God's name: do not spurn us, for your name's sake. But God's response is a direct prohibition of intercession: do not pray for the welfare of this people. When they fast, I do not listen to their cry. When they offer burnt offering and grain offering, I do not accept them. But I will consume them by sword, by famine, and by pestilence. Jeremiah then raises the problem of the false prophets — they are telling the people peace and no sword or famine. God is emphatic: I did not send them, I did not command them, I did not speak to them. They are prophesying a false vision. The chapter ends with another of Jeremiah's intercessions that is allowed — this time a corporate lament: remember your covenant with our ancestors, do not break it, do not spurn us for your name's sake.

Jeremiah 15

The Third Confession: I Am Weary

Chapter 15 contains the third of the confessions, the one that comes closest to accusing God of bad faith. The chapter opens with God's word that even if Moses and Samuel stood before him interceding for Judah, he would not relent — the judgment is fixed. Four kinds of destruction are appointed: those for death, to death; those for the sword, to the sword; those for famine, to famine; those for captivity, to captivity. Jeremiah's personal lament follows: woe is me, my mother, that you gave birth to me — a man of contention and strife with the whole land. But the sharpest moment comes when Jeremiah accuses God directly: O Lord, you know — remember me and take note of me. You who take me and I become a reproach. Then: will you be to me like a deceptive brook, like waters that fail? The charge — that God is like a stream that dries up when you need it most — is the most direct accusation of divine unreliability in the entire book. God's response is not exoneration but recommissioning: if you return, I will take you back; you shall stand before me. The prophet must be more valuable than what he says, returning God's word to the people rather than their worthless words to God. The calling does not change; the cost of holding it does not change; but the relationship is not cancelled.

Jeremiah 16

Forbidden to Marry

Chapter 16 contains one of the most unusual commands in the prophetic literature. God tells Jeremiah not to take a wife, not to have sons or daughters in this place — because the sons and daughters born here, and the mothers who bore them, will die of deadly diseases in this land. They will not be lamented or buried; they will be like dung on the surface of the ground. Jeremiah is also forbidden to enter a house of mourning and forbidden to enter a house of feasting and rejoicing. These prohibitions are explained as signs: the absence of marriage is a sign of the absence of future; the absence of mourning is a sign that there will be so many dead that mourning will be impossible; the absence of feasting is a sign that gladness is over. The chapter then promises restoration — after the judgment, God will bring the people back from the north country and all the lands where they have been driven. The restoration promise in the middle of the chapter has the effect of placing the loss in a longer frame: the celibacy and prohibition are signs for this generation, not declarations about the ultimate end. The chapter closes with an oracle of universal recognition — all nations will come to God when the judgment is complete and acknowledge that their fathers had inherited lies.

Jeremiah 17

The Heart Is Deceitful Above All Things

Chapter 17 is a collection of wisdom sayings, laments, and sabbath legislation that share a common theme: the unreliability of human self-knowledge and the necessity of trusting God rather than human arrangements. The chapter opens with an indictment — the sin of Judah is written with an iron pen, engraved on the tablet of their hearts and on the horns of their altars. The contrast that follows between trusting in humans and trusting in God is one of the most quoted passages in the prophetic literature: cursed is the man who trusts in humans and makes flesh his strength, whose heart turns away from the Lord — he will be like a shrub in the desert. Blessed is the man who trusts in the Lord — he will be like a tree planted by water, sending out roots toward the stream. Then the famous observation: the heart is deceitful above all things and desperately sick — who can understand it? Only the Lord, who searches the heart and tests the mind. The fourth confession follows immediately: heal me, O Lord, and I shall be healed; where is the word of the Lord? Let it come! Jeremiah is being mocked by people who ask when his prophecies will be fulfilled. He disclaims any wish for disaster — he has not desired the fatal day. The chapter closes with an extended sabbath oracle: if the people keep the sabbath, the city will survive; if they do not, God will kindle a fire in its gates.

Jeremiah 18

The Potter's House

Chapter 18 contains the first of Jeremiah's major symbolic actions of clay and pottery. God tells Jeremiah to go down to the potter's house, and there God will let him hear his words. Jeremiah goes. He watches the potter working at his wheel; the vessel being made is spoiled in his hand, so the potter remakes it into another vessel, as seemed good to him. God's word then comes: can I not do with you, O house of Israel, as this potter has done? The image is used not for punishment but for possibility — the potter's reshaping of the marred clay is a figure for divine freedom to deal with nations as their conduct warrants. If a nation repents of evil, God will relent of the intended disaster; if a nation turns to evil after being promised good, God will relent of the intended good. The oracle is addressed as much to foreign nations as to Israel — it is a general theological principle about divine responsiveness to human conduct. The chapter then records the fifth confession, provoked by the specific threat the theological principle creates: the people say come let us devise plots against Jeremiah. He responds with another call for vengeance: remember how I stood before you to speak good for them. Let their children be given over to famine.

Jeremiah 19

The Smashed Jar

Chapter 19 pairs with chapter 18 — where the potter's house showed the possibility of reshaping, the smashed jar shows a point past reshaping. God tells Jeremiah to buy an earthen flask, take some of the elders of the people and the senior priests, go out to the valley of Hinnom by the Potsherd Gate, and proclaim the words God gives him. The valley of Hinnom — just south of Jerusalem — had been the site of child sacrifice to Baal, offerings burned in fire for the god Molek. God's indictment focuses on this specific crime: they have filled this place with the blood of innocents. Therefore the place's name will change — it will be called the valley of Slaughter. Then Jeremiah breaks the flask in front of the men with him and delivers the interpretation: thus says the Lord of hosts, even so will I break this people and this city, as one breaks a potter's vessel so that it cannot be mended. The breaking is specifically irreversible — a clay jar that has been smashed cannot be remade; unlike the potter's reforming of soft clay, the shards of a fired vessel cannot be rejoined. Jeremiah then goes to the court of the Lord's house and repeats his oracle there. The connection between the smashing and the temple ground brings the symbolic action directly into the institutional center the temple sermon had attacked.

Jeremiah 20

Cursed Be the Day I Was Born

Chapter 20 is the climax of the confessions and the most psychologically extreme passage in any prophetic book in the Hebrew Bible. The chapter opens with a narrative: Pashhur the priest, the chief officer of the temple, hears Jeremiah prophesying and has him beaten and put in stocks at the Benjamin Gate. When Jeremiah is released the next day, he renames Pashhur: Terror on Every Side — because God will give him and his friends and all Judah into the hands of the king of Babylon. Then the sixth confession: O Lord, you have deceived me, and I was deceived. The word translated deceived (or enticed, or persuaded) implies manipulation — the prophet feels he was drawn into the calling by something stronger than his consent. You are stronger than I am, and you have prevailed. I have become a laughingstock all day long. Then the impossible situation: if he speaks, everyone mocks him; if he tries to stop, the word is like fire in his bones and he cannot hold it in. After a moment of trust — the Lord is with me as a dread warrior — the chapter closes with the most despairing speech in the entire book: cursed be the day on which I was born. Cursed be the man who brought the news to my father. Let that man be like the cities the Lord overthrew. Why did I come out of the womb to see trouble and sorrow?

Jeremiah 21

Zedekiah Asks and Receives the Truth

Chapter 21 marks a shift in the book. We move from the largely topical oracles of chapters 1-20 into the biographical narrative that dominates the second half. The chapter opens with King Zedekiah sending two officials — Pashhur son of Malchiah and Zephaniah the priest — to Jeremiah with a request: inquire of the Lord on our behalf, because Nebuchadnezzar is attacking us; perhaps God will perform one of his wonderful deeds as in former times and make the Babylonian forces withdraw. The chapter is set during the final siege of Jerusalem (588-587 BCE). Jeremiah's answer is uncompromising. God will turn the weapons of Judah's own hands against them. God himself will fight against Jerusalem in anger and fury and great wrath. He will strike down those in the city with pestilence. Then a choice is given to the people: whoever stays in the city will die by sword, famine, or pestilence; whoever goes out and surrenders to the Babylonians will live, keeping his life as a prize of war. The oracle closes with a direct address to the house of David: execute justice every morning, deliver the oppressed from the hand of the oppressor, or God's wrath will go out like fire. The chapter opens the long section of confrontation between Jeremiah and the city's rulers that will run through to the fall of Jerusalem.

Jeremiah 22

The Kings Indicted

Chapter 22 is the most sustained royal indictment in the book. Jeremiah is sent to the house of the king of Judah and told to deliver a conditional promise: if the king does justice — delivering the robbed from the hand of oppressor, not oppressing the resident alien, orphan, or widow, not shedding innocent blood — then the palace will stand forever. But if the king does not listen, the palace will become a ruin. The chapter then moves through three specific kings. Shallum (Jehoahaz) is addressed: do not weep for the dead king (Josiah) but weep for him who goes into exile, for he shall return no more. Jehoiakim is addressed in the most famous royal oracle of the chapter: he has built his house by unrighteousness, made his neighbor serve without pay, says he will build a great house with spacious upper rooms. The cedar-paneled palace is described with biting irony; then the question: do you think you are a king because you compete in cedar? Your father (Josiah) did justice and it was well with him. But you have eyes only for your dishonest gain. He will be buried with the burial of a donkey, dragged outside Jerusalem's gates. Then Jehoiachin is addressed: write this man down as childless — none of his offspring will succeed on the throne of David.

Jeremiah 23

False Prophets and the Righteous Branch

Chapter 23 opens with an oracle against the shepherds — the ruling class, the kings — who have scattered and driven away the sheep of God's pasture. After them God himself will gather the remnant, bring them home, and raise up shepherds over them who will not make them afraid. Then the messianic oracle: behold, the days come when God will raise up a righteous Branch for David who shall reign as king, deal wisely, execute justice in the land. His name will be: the Lord is our righteousness (in Hebrew, YHWH Tzidkenu). The oracle is one of the key Davidic messianic texts in the prophetic literature; Christian tradition reads it as a forward reference to Christ; Jewish tradition reads it as a forward reference to the restored Davidic kingdom. Then the chapter turns to a long and biting polemic against false prophets — the longest sustained treatment of the problem of false prophecy in any prophetic book. They prophesy by Baal, they commit adultery, they walk in lies, they strengthen the hands of evil. They say to those who despise the word of the Lord: it shall be well with you. They have not stood in the council of the Lord. The chapter ends with an oracle about the burden of the Lord — a wordplay on the Hebrew word massa, which means both a prophetic utterance and a burden: if anyone says this is the burden of the Lord, God will cast him off.

Jeremiah 24

Two Baskets of Figs

Chapter 24 is brief but theologically striking. God shows Jeremiah two baskets of figs set before the temple after Nebuchadnezzar had carried away Jehoiachin and the craftsmen and smiths from Jerusalem in 597 BCE. One basket has very good figs, like first-ripe figs; the other has very bad figs, so bad they cannot be eaten. God explains: the good figs represent the exiles carried to Babylon — God regards them as good and will set his eyes on them for good. He will bring them back and plant them and not pluck them up. He will give them a heart to know him; they will be his people and he will be their God, when they return with their whole heart. The bad figs represent Zedekiah, his officials, and the remnant of Jerusalem who remained — those who went to Egypt. God will make them a horror, a disgrace, a byword, a taunt among all the peoples. He will send sword, famine, and pestilence until they are utterly destroyed. The vision inverts the conventional expectation: the people who went into exile are the ones with hope; the people who stayed in Jerusalem or fled to Egypt are the ones condemned. The chapter plants a question the reader will have to carry through the rest of the book: what does it mean to be the good remnant?

Jeremiah 25

Seventy Years and the Cup of Wrath

Chapter 25 is one of the key structural chapters of the book. It is dated to the fourth year of Jehoiakim (605 BCE), which was also the first year of Nebuchadnezzar. Jeremiah recaps twenty-three years of preaching: since the thirteenth year of Josiah, he has been speaking and the people have not listened. He has sent the servants the prophets again and again; they have not listened and not inclined their ears. Therefore God will bring Nebuchadnezzar — called his servant — against this land and its inhabitants and against all the surrounding nations. The land will become a waste; the people will serve the king of Babylon for seventy years. After seventy years, God will punish the king of Babylon and make the Chaldean land a perpetual waste. Then a remarkable symbolic action: Jeremiah is given a cup of the wine of wrath to make all the nations drink. He goes from nation to nation — Jerusalem, the kings of Judah, Pharaoh king of Egypt, all the kings of the west, Arabia, Zimri, Elam, Media — and they must all drink it. The list covers virtually the entire known world. The chapter ends with the image of God himself roaring from his holy habitation like a lion, announcing judgment on all the ends of the earth. The slain will cover the ground and will not be lamented.

Jeremiah 26

The Temple Sermon's Aftermath

Chapter 26 is the narrative complement to the temple sermon of chapter 7 — the same event, told not as oracle but as biography. At the beginning of Jehoiakim's reign, God sends Jeremiah to stand in the court of the Lord's house and deliver his message to everyone coming to worship. The message is the same as chapter 7: if you will not listen and walk in the law I set before you, I will make this house like Shiloh. As soon as Jeremiah finishes, the priests, the prophets, and all the people seize him: you shall die. Then the princes of Judah hear about it and come from the king's house to sit in judgment. The case is made: this man should be put to death because he has prophesied against this city. Jeremiah defends himself in a speech that is among the most direct in the book: the Lord sent me to prophesy against this house. Now therefore amend your ways. As for me, I am in your hands — do with me what seems good to you. But know that if you put me to death, you will bring innocent blood on yourselves and on this city and its inhabitants. The elders of the land then intervene, citing the precedent of the prophet Micah who had prophesied against Jerusalem under Hezekiah — and had not been put to death. Jeremiah is acquitted. But the chapter closes with the death of the prophet Uriah, who had said the same things as Jeremiah and had been hunted down and killed by Jehoiakim — a counterpoint that makes Jeremiah's survival look less like vindication than like contingency.

Jeremiah 27

The yoke of Babylon

Chapter 27 opens early in Zedekiah's reign, when envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon arrive in Jerusalem, apparently exploring the possibility of a coordinated revolt against Babylon. Jeremiah performs one of his most dramatic symbolic actions: he makes a yoke of straps and bars, puts it on his own neck, and sends word to the envoys — and to Zedekiah himself — that God has given all these lands into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar; any nation that submits will survive, any that resists will be destroyed. He tells the priests and the people not to listen to the prophets who are promising a swift end to Babylonian dominance, and he warns Zedekiah not to let the temple vessels that had not yet been taken to Babylon become leverage for false hope. The yoke on his neck is the message: submission is not apostasy but survival. Chapter 28 will stage the direct confrontation this symbolic act provokes.

Jeremiah 28

Hananiah breaks the yoke

Chapter 28 is the most sharply drawn scene of prophetic conflict in the Hebrew Bible. In the temple, before the priests and the people, Hananiah ben Azzur of Gibeon breaks the wooden yoke off Jeremiah's neck and prophesies that within two years God will break Nebuchadnezzar's yoke and return the temple vessels and King Jehoiachin from exile. Jeremiah's response is careful and restrained: amen, may the Lord do so — but the tradition is that the prophet who speaks peace is authenticated by the peace coming to pass. The narrative pauses. Jeremiah goes his way. Later he returns with a counter-oracle: you have broken a yoke of wood; in its place shall be a yoke of iron. And Hananiah will die that year for speaking rebellion against the Lord. The chapter reports that Hananiah died in the seventh month — within two months of the confrontation. The story is the canonical case study of true versus false prophecy, and the book's answer is that they cannot be distinguished in advance, only by the event.

Jeremiah 29

Seek the welfare of the city

Chapter 29 is a letter — one of the very few documents actually quoted at length in any prophetic book. Jeremiah sends it from Jerusalem to the elders, priests, prophets, and people who had been carried into exile to Babylon with Jehoiachin in 597. The content is startling. Instead of urging resistance or hoping for a swift return, Jeremiah tells the exiles to build houses, plant gardens, take wives, have children, and seek the shalom — the welfare, the flourishing, the peace — of the city where they have been taken. In its peace you will find your peace. The exile, he says, will last seventy years. False prophets among them are claiming it will be short; they should not listen. Chapter 29 then records oracles against two specific false prophets in Babylon — Ahab ben Kolaiah and Zedekiah ben Maaseiah — and a confrontational letter from another exile prophet, Shemaiah, who has written to Jerusalem criticizing Jeremiah's letter. Jeremiah responds with an oracle that Shemaiah's line will have no future. The chapter's theological center is the instruction to seek the welfare of the city of exile — a teaching that has shaped Jewish diaspora theology for two and a half millennia.

Jeremiah 30

Terror on every side, then restoration

Chapter 30 opens the section scholars call the Book of Consolation — chapters 30 and 31 together form the most sustained passage of hope in the entire book of Jeremiah. After forty years of mostly unrelenting warnings of disaster, the register shifts. God tells Jeremiah to write down in a book all the words he has spoken, because days are coming when he will restore the fortunes of Israel and Judah and bring them back to the land. The chapter acknowledges the reality of what is coming — Jacob's trouble, a time of anguish unlike any other — but insists that Israel will be saved out of it. The wounds of the people are described as incurable by ordinary physicians, but God will heal them himself. Those who have devoured Jacob will themselves be devoured; those who have scattered Israel will be scattered. The oracle is not soft; it does not minimize the disaster. But it is clear that the disaster is not the end. Chapter 30 sets the framework for chapter 31's more detailed promises.

Jeremiah 31

A new covenant on the heart

Chapter 31 is the theological summit of the book of Jeremiah and one of the most consequential chapters in the Hebrew Bible. It opens with oracles of restoration for both northern and southern tribes — Ephraim weeping in the wilderness, Rachel mourning for her children, but told to dry her tears because the children will return from the enemy's land. The famous verse — a voice is heard in Ramah, lamentation and bitter weeping, Rachel weeping for her children — is quoted in Matthew's gospel as fulfilled in the slaughter of the innocents at Bethlehem. Then comes the new covenant oracle in verses 31-34: God will make a covenant not like the Sinai covenant, which the people broke. This covenant will be written on their hearts, not on tablets of stone; they will all know God from the least to the greatest; God will forgive their iniquity and remember their sin no more. The phrase new covenant — in Greek, kaine diatheke — is the source of the name New Testament. The chapter closes with oracles affirming that Jerusalem will be rebuilt.

Jeremiah 32

Buy the field at Anathoth

Chapter 32 is one of the most precisely dated chapters in the book: the tenth year of Zedekiah, 588/587 BCE, when the Babylonian army has been besieging Jerusalem for months and Jeremiah has been confined in the court of the guard by the king. Into this scene of maximum hopelessness, God tells Jeremiah that his cousin Hanamel will come and offer to sell him the ancestral field at Anathoth, his hometown three miles north of the besieged city. Hanamel arrives, exactly as predicted, and Jeremiah buys the field — weighs out seventeen shekels of silver, signs and seals the deed, has it witnessed, and hands it to Baruch for safekeeping in an earthenware jar, so that it will last for a long time. The symbolic meaning is explicit: thus says the Lord of hosts, the God of Israel: houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land. Jeremiah then prays one of the longest prayers in the book, expressing bewilderment at this act of hope in the face of disaster. God's long answer fills the rest of the chapter.

Jeremiah 33

The righteous Branch and David's line

Chapter 33 continues the series of restoration oracles begun in chapters 30-31, again dated to Jeremiah's confinement in the court of the guard during the siege. God tells the prophet to call upon him and he will answer with great and hidden things not yet known. The oracles that follow address the physical restoration of Jerusalem — the houses and palaces torn down to provide building materials for defenses against the Babylonian siege ramps will be rebuilt; the city will be filled with joy and gladness, the sound of the bridegroom and the bride. Then the chapter turns to two promises that will anchor much later messianic theology: the promise of a righteous Branch to be raised up for David, who will execute justice and righteousness in the land, and the parallel promise that the Levitical priests will never lack a man to stand before the Lord. These two covenants — with David and with Levi — are as unbreakable as God's covenant with day and night, as permanent as the laws of creation. The chapter is among the richest in the Book of Consolation.

Jeremiah 34

Broken covenant with enslaved Hebrews

Chapter 34 opens with an oracle to Zedekiah during the Babylonian siege: the city will fall and be burned, and Zedekiah will be captured and will look Nebuchadnezzar in the eye before being taken to Babylon — but he will die peacefully and be mourned with the customary burning of spices. Interestingly, this prediction is partially correct: Zedekiah does meet Nebuchadnezzar face to face, his sons are killed before his eyes, his eyes are put out, and he is taken to Babylon, where he apparently dies without the violent death one might have expected for a rebel vassal. The chapter then records a remarkable incident: at some point during the siege — apparently when the Babylonian army temporarily withdrew, presumably in response to an Egyptian advance (compare chapter 37) — the nobles of Jerusalem made a covenant in the temple to free all their Hebrew slaves, as the law of release required. They freed them. Then, when the Babylonian army returned, they reversed the release and re-enslaved their former slaves. God's response is withering: since you have not listened, I will proclaim a release for you — to sword, pestilence, and famine.

Jeremiah 35

The faithful Recabites

Chapter 35 is set in Jehoiakim's reign, not Zedekiah's, and represents a flashback within the largely chronological narrative of chapters 34-45. The Recabites — a clan of semi-nomadic Israelites who descended from Jehonadab ben Recab, a supporter of Jehu's purge of the house of Ahab in 2 Kings 10 — have come into Jerusalem because of the Babylonian and Aramean armies overrunning the countryside. Their ancestor had commanded them never to drink wine, never to build houses, never to plant vineyards, but to live in tents. Jeremiah brings them into a chamber of the temple and sets bowls of wine before them. They refuse, citing their ancestor's command across multiple generations. Jeremiah uses their refusal as a public indictment of Judah: a human ancestor's command has been kept through generations by this small clan; God has been speaking to Judah generation after generation and has not been heard. The chapter closes with a promise that the Recabites will not lack a man to stand before the Lord forever.

Jeremiah 36

Jehoiakim burns the scroll

Chapter 36 is the most extraordinary episode in the book concerning the book's own composition, and one of the most vivid scenes of biblical narrative. In the fourth year of Jehoiakim — 605 BCE, the year of the battle of Carchemish, when Babylon's regional dominance was established — God commands Jeremiah to take a scroll and dictate onto it all the words he has spoken against Israel and Judah. Jeremiah cannot go to the temple himself, so he dictates to Baruch, who reads the scroll three times: in the temple before all the people, in a chamber before the royal officials, and then before King Jehoiakim himself in his winter house. As each column of the scroll is read, Jehoiakim cuts it off with a penknife and throws it into the brazier until the whole scroll is burned. Three courtiers urge him not to burn it; he does not listen. Jeremiah and Baruch hide. God tells Jeremiah to take another scroll and dictate the whole thing again — and this time add more. The scroll he burned still exists, in the shape of the book the reader holds.

Jeremiah 37

Imprisoned for treason

Chapter 37 returns to the Zedekiah period and the Babylonian siege. The Babylonian army has temporarily withdrawn from Jerusalem after learning that the Egyptian army of Pharaoh Hophra is advancing. King Zedekiah sends Jehucal and the priest Zephaniah to Jeremiah with a private request: pray for us. Jeremiah's response is that the Babylonian army will return and burn the city — the Egyptians will not save them. As Jeremiah attempts to leave Jerusalem through the Benjamin Gate, heading toward his hometown of Anathoth to claim a property during the siege's temporary lull, the guard captain Irijah arrests him on a charge of defecting to the Chaldeans. Jeremiah denies it, but the officials have him beaten and imprisoned in the house of Jonathan the secretary, where a vaulted cistern has been made into a dungeon. After many days Zedekiah secretly summons him and asks again for a word from the Lord. Jeremiah tells him the same thing — the city will fall — and asks to be transferred out of the cistern, which appears to be a death sentence in slow motion. Zedekiah moves him to the court of the guard.

Jeremiah 38

The cistern of Malchiah

Chapter 38 is the most harrowing of the chapters recording Jeremiah's imprisonment. The officials — Shephatiah, Gedaliah, Jucal, and Pashhur — hear that Jeremiah is still in the court of the guard telling the people to surrender to Babylon, and they go to Zedekiah demanding his death: this man is weakening the hands of the soldiers and of all the people, seeking not their welfare but their harm. Zedekiah's reply is telling: he is in your hands, for the king can do nothing against you. They lower Jeremiah with ropes into the cistern of Malchiah in the court of the guard, where there is no water but only mud, and he sinks into the mud. An Ethiopian eunuch in the royal household, Ebed-Melech, hears about the imprisonment and appeals directly to the king at the Benjamin Gate; the king grants permission for the rescue. Ebed-Melech and thirty men lower old rags and worn-out clothes and ropes to Jeremiah, pad the ropes under his armpits, and draw him up from the mud. Then Zedekiah summons Jeremiah privately and asks him one more time for the truth. Jeremiah delivers it: surrender and live; resist and the city burns. Zedekiah cannot do it — he fears his own people more than Babylon.

Jeremiah 39

The fall of Jerusalem

Chapter 39 records the fall of Jerusalem in 587 BCE with measured, almost clinical brevity. In the ninth year of Zedekiah, Nebuchadnezzar and all his army came against Jerusalem and besieged it; in the eleventh year of Zedekiah, the fourth month, on the ninth day of the month, a breach was made in the city. The Babylonian commanders sat in the middle gate. Zedekiah and all the men of war flee through the king's garden, through the gate between the two walls, out toward the Arabah. The Babylonian army pursues and overtakes him on the plains of Jericho. He is taken to Riblah, where Nebuchadnezzar speaks with him in judgment. His sons are slaughtered before his eyes, along with all the nobles of Judah. Then his eyes are put out and he is bound in chains and carried to Babylon. The chapter then records, separately, the instructions given to the Babylonian commander Nebuzaradan regarding Jeremiah: release him, look after him, and do whatever he says. The commander frees Jeremiah from the court of the guard and entrusts him to Gedaliah.

Jeremiah 40

Gedaliah governs the remnant

Chapter 40 opens at Ramah, where Jeremiah has been taken in chains among the Judahite captives being marched north toward Babylon. The Babylonian captain Nebuzaradan finds him there, frees him, and gives him the choice: come to Babylon, where he will be well cared for, or remain in the land. Jeremiah chooses to remain and goes to Gedaliah ben Ahikam at Mizpah. Gedaliah has been installed by the Babylonians as governor over the remnant population left in Judah. He tells the military commanders who gather to him that it will go well if they serve the king of Babylon, gather the summer harvest, and settle in their towns. The chapter records that Jews who had fled to Moab, Ammon, Edom, and other neighboring countries begin returning when they hear that Gedaliah has been given authority over the land. A harvest of wine and summer fruits is gathered. But the chapter ends ominously: Johanan ben Kareah brings Gedaliah a warning that Ishmael ben Netaniah, sent by the Ammonite king, is planning to assassinate him. Gedaliah does not believe it and refuses Johanan's offer to kill Ishmael preemptively.

Jeremiah 41

Gedaliah assassinated

Chapter 41 records the assassination of Gedaliah and its bloody aftermath. In the seventh month — at the feast of Gedaliah, commemorated in Jewish tradition to this day — Ishmael ben Netaniah arrives at Mizpah with ten men and kills Gedaliah at a meal, along with the Judahite men and the Babylonian soldiers who were with him. The next day, before anyone knows, eighty men from Shechem, Shiloh, and Samaria arrive with offerings for the house of the Lord — pilgrims moving south to the ruins of the temple, their beards shaved and their clothes torn, bearing grain offerings and incense. Ishmael comes out to meet them weeping, persuades them to come inside the town — then kills seventy of them and throws their bodies into a cistern. He keeps ten alive because they promise him food stores. He then takes captive the rest of the people of Mizpah — the daughters of the king and all those Nebuzaradan had left in Gedaliah's care — and sets out toward Ammon. Johanan ben Kareah hears what has happened, pursues with his men, and overtakes Ishmael at the great pool at Gibeon. The captives defect to Johanan; Ishmael escapes to Ammon with eight men.

Jeremiah 42

Do not go to Egypt

Chapter 42 is the formal consultation of Jeremiah by all the people who have gathered near Bethlehem after the assassination of Gedaliah — Johanan ben Kareah, the military commanders, and everyone from the least to the greatest. They come to Jeremiah with a solemn request: inquire of the Lord your God where we should go and what we should do. They make an explicit oath: whatever God says, they will do. Jeremiah promises to withhold nothing and goes away to pray. After ten days, the word of the Lord comes. The answer is clear and without qualification: stay in the land of Judah and do not be afraid of the king of Babylon; God will have compassion, cause Nebuchadnezzar to have mercy, and the people will continue in the land. But if they say they will go to Egypt to escape war and famine, the sword and famine they fear will overtake them there. God is clear that he knows they will not obey. The people had already decided to go to Egypt when they asked; they asked wanting validation, not direction. The next chapters confirm this.

Jeremiah 43

Dragged to Egypt

Chapter 43 records the decision and departure the previous chapter had foreseen. When Jeremiah finishes delivering the oracle from chapter 42, Johanan ben Kareah and the commanders declare that he is lying — that Baruch ben Neriah has stirred him up against them in order to hand them over to Babylon. They take everyone: all the men, women, and children who had remained in Judah; all who had returned from neighboring countries; and Jeremiah and Baruch themselves — and they go to Egypt, in direct defiance of the command. They arrive at Tahpanhes, a border city in the Nile Delta. There Jeremiah performs one more symbolic act: God tells him to take large stones and hide them in the mortar of the pavement at the entrance to Pharaoh's palace at Tahpanhes, in the sight of the Judahite men. He announces that Nebuchadnezzar will spread his canopy over these stones and come and smite the land of Egypt, burning the temples of the Egyptian gods. The exile has not brought safety; it has imported the prophecy into the place of supposed refuge.

Jeremiah 44

The queen of heaven in Egypt

Chapter 44 is the last datable event in Jeremiah's life and the most despairing of his confrontations with his own people. He addresses all the Judahites living in Lower and Upper Egypt — Migdol, Tahpanhes, Memphis, and the land of Pathros — reminding them of the disasters God brought on Jerusalem because of the idolatry of its people and kings. He tells them that the same fate awaits them in Egypt if they continue burning incense to other gods. The response of the men and their wives is remarkable for its directness and its inverted theology: we will not listen to you; we will burn incense to the queen of heaven as we and our ancestors and our kings and officials did in the towns of Judah and the streets of Jerusalem, for then we had plenty of food and prospered and saw no disaster. It was when we stopped that disaster came. Jeremiah counters that the disaster came because of the idolatry, not the abandonment of it, and delivers his final oracle: the entire community in Egypt will be destroyed by sword and famine; only a small remnant will escape. The chapter ends with a sign oracle tied to Pharaoh Hophra's fate. The book never records Jeremiah's death.

Jeremiah 45

A word to Baruch alone

Chapter 45 is the shortest in the second half of the book and the most intimate. Dated to the fourth year of Jehoiakim — the same year as the composition and burning of the scroll in chapter 36 — it records a personal word from God to Baruch ben Neriah, delivered through Jeremiah. Baruch has apparently been lamenting: woe is me, for the Lord has added sorrow to my pain; I am weary with my groaning and find no rest. God's oracle to him is brief and unsentimental: you asked for great things for yourself — do not ask for them. For I am bringing disaster on all flesh, but I will give your life to you as a prize of war wherever you go. The chapter is the only oracle in the prophetic books addressed solely to a prophet's scribe. It situates Baruch's individual grief in the largest possible frame — God is dismantling what he has built and pulling down what he has planted — and offers only this: survival. The life given as a prize of war is enough, given what is happening to everyone else.

Jeremiah 46

Oracle against Egypt

Chapters 46-51 gather the oracles against foreign nations, a feature common to the major prophetic books. Chapter 46 opens with Egypt. The first oracle is dated to the fourth year of Jehoiakim, 605 BCE, and addresses the Egyptian army before the battle of Carchemish — the dramatic defeat at which Nebuchadnezzar shattered Egyptian power in the Levant. The imagery is vivid: muster the horsemen, mount the horses, polish the spears, put on the coats of mail — and then the rout, the mighty stumbling, not rising, crushed in the north by the river Euphrates. The second oracle is longer and addresses a future invasion of Egypt by Nebuchadnezzar himself — the swift heifer, the fair heifer of Egypt, is destroyed; the hired soldiers in her army are like fattened calves, they also turn and flee together, they do not stand; the day of the Lord of hosts is the day of reckoning. The oracle closes with a promise to the Israelites: do not fear, my servant Jacob; for I am with you to save you.

Jeremiah 47

Oracle against the Philistines

Chapter 47 is the oracle against the Philistines, the shortest of the foreign-nation oracles in Jeremiah. It is dated before Pharaoh struck Gaza — a historical event that may be associated with Pharaoh Necho's campaign in 609 BCE or an earlier Egyptian raid. The oracle is almost entirely sustained metaphor: waters rise from the north, swelling into an overflowing torrent, flooding the land and city, making men cry out and fathers abandon children from sheer helplessness. The Philistine cities of Tyre and Sidon — allied with Philistia — are mentioned. The Philistines cry out; the remaining strength of their valley is destroyed; baldness comes on Gaza, Ashkelon is cut off. The oracle ends with a poetic address to the sword of the Lord: how long will you not be quiet? Put yourself into your sheath, rest and be still! But the answer is implicit — it will not rest; it has been given a charge against Ashkelon and the seacoast, and it will not return until it has done what God has appointed.

Jeremiah 48

Oracle against Moab

Chapter 48 is the longest of the foreign-nation oracles in Jeremiah and one of the most complex. Moab — the territory east of the Dead Sea, traditional neighbor and sometime enemy of Israel — falls under an extended indictment. The oracle moves city by city through Moab's towns: Nebo, Kiriathaim, Heshbon, Dibon, Aroer, Jahaz, Mephaath, Kerioth. The imagery shifts between military destruction, agricultural metaphor, and personal lament. Moab is compared to a wine vessel that has rested on its lees, undisturbed and unchanged: because Moab has been settled from its youth and has rested on its dregs, has not been poured from vessel to vessel, it has not gone into exile and its taste remains in it. The oracle also indicts Moab's pride in Chemosh, its national god, who will go into captivity with its priests and officials. One of the most striking moments is when God himself appears to lament over Moab's destruction — the same grief shown for Shiloh in chapter 7. The chapter closes with a promise that God will restore Moab's fortunes in the latter days.

Jeremiah 49

Oracles against Ammon, Edom, and beyond

Chapter 49 gathers oracles against five nations in sequence: the Ammonites, Edom, Damascus, Kedar and Hazor, and Elam. The Ammonite oracle is addressed through their god Milcom, asking why the Ammonites have taken possession of Gad — an Israelite territory — when Israel had no sons to inherit it. A Rabbah (present-day Amman) will fall, but God will restore Ammon's fortunes afterward. The Edom oracle is the most intense: the pride of your heart has deceived you, you who live in the clefts of the rock. Though you rise as high as the eagle, I will bring you down from there. No one will be left. The oracle against Damascus is brief — a city of joy, a town of merriment, its young warriors will fall in its streets. The Kedar and Hazor oracle warns the Arabian tribes that Nebuchadnezzar has plans for them. The Elam oracle closes the chapter with a vision of Elam's king and officials scattered and God setting his throne in Elam — followed, again, by a restoration promise for the latter days.

Jeremiah 50

Babylon shall fall

Chapter 50 opens the two-chapter oracle against Babylon that is the climax and conclusion of the nations-oracle collection. Bel is put to shame, Marduk is dismayed, the land of the Chaldeans is made desolate. Israel and Judah together will seek the Lord their God, asking the way to Zion with their faces turned toward it, saying let us join ourselves to the Lord in an everlasting covenant that will never be forgotten. The oracle then turns to the agent of Babylon's destruction: a nation from the north, a great coalition of nations from the ends of the earth. Babylon is a hammer for the whole earth — but the hammer will be shattered. The oracle develops at length the legal argument that Babylon's punishment is just: it has sinned against the Lord, has been arrogant, has rejoiced over the destruction of Israel and was not sorry. Therefore God is avenging his people. The chapter closes with an extended image of Babylon as a lion driven from the Jordan's thicket by the sudden flooding of the river — the lion that terrorized the land suddenly routed.

Jeremiah 51

The scroll sunk in the Euphrates

Chapter 51 continues the great oracle against Babylon begun in chapter 50, developing the themes of reversal and divine justice at greater length and greater poetic intensity. God is winnowing Babylon, the destroyer of the whole earth. The oracle addresses the mountains of Ararat, Minni, and Ashkenaz — peoples to the north and northwest — to come against Babylon. Babylon is made drunk on the cup of God's judgment and vomits it up. Her walls fall, her gates are burned, her peoples are weary. The oracle pauses for a remarkable insertion: the maker of all things is not like the idols that men fashion, for he has formed all things and Israel is his tribe, his inheritance and the Lord of hosts is his name. Then the war imagery resumes: a destroying wind is sent against Babylon; her walls collapse; fire burns in her palaces; the waves of the sea rise over her. At the chapter's end, Jeremiah writes all these words on a scroll, gives it to Seraiah ben Neriah when he travels to Babylon with Zedekiah, and instructs him to read it aloud in Babylon and then tie a stone to it and throw it into the Euphrates, saying thus shall Babylon sink and never rise.

Jeremiah 52

Historical appendix: the fall retold

Chapter 52 is an explicit historical appendix, nearly identical to 2 Kings 24:18-25:30, appended to the book as a verification of the prophecies that have preceded it. It opens with Zedekiah's reign and his rebellion against Babylon — the trigger for the final siege. It records the breach of the walls, the flight, the capture on the plains of Jericho, the confrontation at Riblah, the execution of Zedekiah's sons and officials before his eyes, the blinding and deportation. Then it details the destruction itself: Nebuzaradan arrives, burns the house of the Lord and the royal palace and the houses of Jerusalem, breaks down the walls, and carries the remaining population to Babylon. The temple vessels are catalogued in detail — the bronze pillars, the sea, the stands, all brought to Babylon. The chapter records three deportations totaling 4,600 people. It closes with a scene that does not appear anywhere in the book of Jeremiah itself: in the thirty-seventh year of Jehoiachin's exile, Evil-merodach king of Babylon releases him from prison, sets his throne above the thrones of the other captive kings, and gives him a daily allowance for the rest of his life. The book ends not with Jeremiah but with a captive king eating at a foreign table.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Reluctant Prophet

Jeremiah is the prophet who did not want to be one. The refusal in chapter 1 runs through the whole book. Forty years later, in chapter 20, he curses the day he was born. In between, the confessions chart what genuine prophetic vocation costs a man who has no choice but to carry it.

The Temple Sermon

Jeremiah stands at the gate of the Lord's house and tells the worshippers that the temple will not save them if they do not amend their ways. The sermon is nearly kills him. Twenty-two years later, the temple is burned to the ground.

The New Covenant

In chapter 31, in the middle of the exile oracles, Jeremiah delivers the promise that gives the New Testament its name. The old covenant, written on stone tablets, has been broken. The new one will be written on hearts.

Symbolic Actions

Jeremiah performs his message more than any other prophet — a linen belt, a broken flask, a yoke worn around Jerusalem, a field bought during a siege. The symbolic actions are not illustrations of the word; they are the word, in a register the spoken word alone could not reach.

The Confessions

Six passages scattered through chapters 11–20 in which Jeremiah speaks back to God about what the calling has cost him. He complains, accuses, calls down curses, tries to quit. The confessions are the most personal passages in any prophetic book in the Hebrew Bible.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Jeremiah
Prophet of Anathoth

Born around 645 BCE in Anathoth, a priestly town three miles north-east of Jerusalem, into a family of priests descended from Abiathar. Called to prophecy around 627 BCE as a young man — perhaps still a teenager. Preached for forty years through five kings and three Babylonian invasions. The most psychologically vivid prophet in the Hebrew Bible — beaten, stocked, imprisoned, lowered into a cistern, forbidden to marry, dragged into Egyptian exile against his will. His confessions (chapters 11–20) are the most personal passages in any prophetic book. Tradition holds he was eventually killed by his own people in Egypt.

Baruch ben Neriah
Scribe and probable editor

Jeremiah's secretary and the probable author of much of the biographical prose in the book. A scribe from a prominent Jerusalem family — his brother Seraiah served as quartermaster to King Zedekiah. Witnesses the purchase of the field at Anathoth (chapter 32). Central to chapter 36, where he reads Jeremiah's scroll in the temple before it is burned by King Jehoiakim, then helps dictate the whole thing again. Chapter 45 contains a personal oracle to Baruch — the only such oracle to a prophet's scribe in the Hebrew Bible. A clay seal bearing his name was discovered in 1975 and is one of the most striking archaeological confirmations of a named biblical figure outside the royal line.

Zedekiah
Last king of Judah

The last king of Judah, installed as a Babylonian vassal in 597 BCE after the deportation of his nephew Jehoiachin. Reigned eleven years. Drawn repeatedly into private conferences with Jeremiah, asking for a word from the Lord and receiving the same word each time: surrender to Babylon and live; resist and die. He cannot act on it. Rebels against Babylon, brings the final siege on the city, flees by night when the walls are breached, is captured on the plains of Jericho. His sons are killed before his eyes; his eyes are then put out. He dies in Babylon. The book treats him as the embodiment of what happens to a man who knows the truth and cannot act on it.

Hananiah ben Azzur
False prophet

The prophet from Gibeon who confronts Jeremiah in chapter 28. Jeremiah is walking around Jerusalem with a wooden yoke, counselling submission to Babylon. Hananiah prophesies the opposite: within two years God will break Babylon's power, return the temple vessels, and bring King Jehoiachin home. He takes the wooden yoke off Jeremiah's neck and breaks it. Jeremiah returns later with a word from the Lord: you have broken the yoke of wood; in its place you shall have a yoke of iron. Hananiah dies within two months. The chapter is the central canonical case study of how true and false prophets are distinguished — and the answer the book gives is that they cannot be, in advance, by hearers. Only the event tells.

Nebuchadnezzar
King of Babylon

Nebuchadnezzar II, king of Babylon from 605 to 562 BCE. Defeats Egypt at Carchemish in 605, reduces Judah to vassal status, invades three times — 605, 597, and 587 — the last time destroying Jerusalem and the temple. Jeremiah's oracles consistently identify him as the instrument of God's judgment against Judah and counsel submission; this is the position for which Jeremiah is repeatedly accused of treason. Nebuchadnezzar treats Jeremiah well after the conquest — orders his commanders to look after him, gives him his choice of going to Babylon or remaining in the land.

The Recabites
Faithful clan

A small clan descended from Jehonadab the son of Recab. Their ancestor had commanded them never to drink wine, never to build houses, never to plant vineyards, but to live in tents — a semi-nomadic existence preserving an older Israelite way of life against the assimilations of settled Canaan. In chapter 35 Jeremiah brings them into a chamber of the temple and offers them wine. They refuse. They have kept the command of their ancestor for generations and will not break it. Jeremiah uses them as a living indictment: a small clan has kept a human ancestor's command for centuries; the people of Judah have not kept God's command for a single generation.
