Summary

God commands Jeremiah not to take a wife and not to have sons or daughters in this place. The explanation is explicit: the sons and daughters born here will die of deadly diseases, unlamented and unburied, left on the surface of the ground like dung. The command to celibacy is not a religious discipline for Jeremiah's benefit; it is a performed sign directed at the community. Jeremiah's unmarried state is a statement about the future of this generation in this land — there will not be one. He is also forbidden to enter a house where there has been a death, forbidden to mourn or lament for the dead, forbidden to participate in condolences. And forbidden to enter a house of feasting, to sit with them in eating and drinking.

Each prohibition is accompanied by an interpretation: mourning has been withdrawn from the people; feasting and gladness have been taken away. The dead will lie unburied because the disaster will be so total that ordinary customs will be suspended. The chapter then pivots to restoration — a promise that God will bring the people back from the north country and all the lands where they have been driven, as surely as he brought them from Egypt. The restoration oracle places the judgment in a longer arc: this generation's loss is not the end of the story. The chapter closes with a universalist oracle: the nations will one day come to God and acknowledge that their fathers inherited only lies and worthlessness. The prophet who is forbidden intimacy in the present is the vehicle of a promise addressed to a future beyond his own life.
