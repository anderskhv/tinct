Summary

The servants have been sent away and the conversation shifts in register. Fyodor Pavlovitch pours more brandy and addresses Ivan directly, dropping the performance that has sustained him through the day. He wants to know: is there a God? Is there a hell? What does Ivan actually think, given that Ivan has been publishing opinions about God in the Petersburg journals and clearly knows more about it than Fyodor Pavlovitch does?

Ivan's answer is carefully measured: there is no immortality, there is almost certainly no God, and the moral systems built on those foundations are therefore built on sand. He says this not cruelly but as a statement of what he believes to be true. Fyodor Pavlovitch listens. His response is unexpectedly specific: he confesses that when he gets drunk, he is frightened of hell. Not of death but of hell—of the image of hooks and fire that he knows, rationally, to be mythology and cannot stop believing in when the brandy runs.

This small exchange is significant because it humanizes Fyodor Pavlovitch in a direction the novel does not often take. He is not entirely without inner life; he has fears he cannot rationalize away, and he is willing, in private and in drink, to admit them. The chapter also develops the quality of Ivan's relationship with his father: Ivan is capable of genuine patience with Fyodor Pavlovitch when the performance drops, and Fyodor Pavlovitch, stripped of his audience, is capable of a kind of vulnerability. They are more alike than either would prefer.
