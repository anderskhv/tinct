The Brothers Karamazov

1880 CE · by Fyodor Dostoevsky

The book in brief

The Brothers Karamazov is the story of a father and his three legitimate sons in a small Russian town, and of the murder that pulls them apart. Fyodor Pavlovich is a buffoon and a sensualist who has neglected all of his children. Dmitri, the eldest, is a former soldier consumed by debt and desire; he is competing with his own father for the same woman, Grushenka, and screaming his rage about it through the town. Ivan, the middle son, is a brilliant atheist intellectual who has built a philosophy from which he cannot live. Alyosha, the youngest, is a novice at the local monastery, devoted to the elder Zosima. There is also Smerdyakov, the epileptic servant whom everyone half-knows is Fyodor's bastard. When the father is found murdered, Dmitri is arrested. The novel asks who actually killed him.

Dostoevsky began the novel in 1878 and finished it in 1880; he died three months after publication. He was fifty-eight, in poor health, and had recently lost a young son to epilepsy — a grief he poured into Alyosha. The book is divided into four parts and twelve sub-books, with an epilogue, and runs to nearly 800 pages. It contains his most famous set pieces: Ivan's chapter "Rebellion," the prose poem of "The Grand Inquisitor," and Zosima's deathbed exhortations. It was meant as the first of two novels about Alyosha; the second was never written.

It is the most argumentative novel ever written, and Dostoevsky meant the argument to go all the way down. Belief and unbelief, love and cruelty, fathers and sons, freedom and submission — none of these are settled by speech in this book, only by what people do.

Why it still matters

The Strongest Case Against God

Ivan's two chapters — "Rebellion" and "The Grand Inquisitor" — make the most serious case against God ever set inside a novel. In "Rebellion" he hands Alyosha a folder of newspaper clippings about tortured children and says he refuses any heaven that is purchased at this price; he returns his ticket. In "The Grand Inquisitor" he imagines Christ returning to sixteenth-century Seville and being arrested by a cardinal who explains, patiently and without anger, that humanity cannot bear the freedom Christ offered and would rather have bread and authority. Dostoevsky was a believer, and he gave the argument away — its full strength, its full eloquence — to a character he could not refute by argument. Alyosha's reply is to kiss his brother. The novel's wager is that this is enough. Whether the wager pays is the question every reader has to settle for themselves.

What Is Owed to a Father Who Did Nothing

Fyodor Pavlovich has been a terrible father by any measure. He drank, abandoned his sons, mocked them when they came back, refused Dmitri what was legally his. He is the kind of father almost nobody would defend. And yet the entire Karamazov plot turns on what is owed to him simply because he is the father — owed by sons who hate him, owed by a court that must judge his murder, owed by a religious tradition that taught honor as a duty before love. The novel does not soften the question. Dmitri shouts that he wishes his father dead; Ivan thinks it; Smerdyakov acts on it. Each of them is implicated by the wish even when his hands are clean. It is the deepest treatment in literature of what is owed to authority and family that has not deserved it — a question that recurs every time a generation sits across a table from one that has failed it.

The Whole World in One Book

Dostoevsky tried to put everything he had into this novel: the murder mystery, the courtroom drama, the philosophical disputation, the saint's life of Zosima, the children's plot around the dying boy Ilyusha, the long chapter on the elder's teaching, the hallucinated devil who visits Ivan in fever. The book is uneven by design — Tolstoy was tighter, Turgenev cleaner — but it includes things no tidy novel could. Dostoevsky wanted the form to hold theology, criminology, child psychology, and the texture of small-town gossip on the same page, and to refuse to choose between them. The unevenness is part of the argument: a real life is not a tidy form. Most contemporary literary novels still try to do one thing well. Karamazov is the standing reminder of how much a novel can attempt when its author refuses to leave anything out.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Faith vs. doubt.

Ivan cannot accept a God who permits children to suffer. Alyosha cannot accept a world without God. Their conversation in the tavern is the sharpest collision between these two positions in all of literature. Neither wins.

The Grand Inquisitor.

Ivan’s prose poem imagines Christ returning to 15th-century Seville, only to be arrested by the Church. The Inquisitor explains, calmly, why humanity cannot bear freedom and must be ruled. It is one of the most disturbing arguments ever written—and Dostoevsky believed the only answer to it was not an argument.

Three brothers as three paths.

Dmitri is passion—desire, guilt, and redemption. Ivan is reason—brilliant, proud, and ultimately unable to live inside his own conclusions. Alyosha is faith—not naïve, but tested and chosen. Dostoevsky built them to stand for three ways of being human. Watch which one the novel endorses.

Zosima’s alternative.

The elder Zosima teaches something radically different from Ivan’s rebellion: that everyone is responsible to everyone, that love is not a feeling but a practice, and that the world’s suffering is met not by argument but by presence. His chapters are Dostoevsky’s answer to the Grand Inquisitor.

Key figures

Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov
The Father

A drunk and shameless landowner who has somehow held onto his money. He fathered Dmitri with one wife and Ivan and Alyosha with another, and abandoned all three to be raised by relatives or servants. He is also the suspected father of Smerdyakov, who works in his kitchen. As the novel opens, he is competing with Dmitri for Grushenka and refusing to give Dmitri his rightful inheritance. He is comic, repulsive, occasionally pitiable — and the wound that runs through the entire family.

Dmitri Karamazov
The Eldest Son

A former army officer, twenty-eight, chronically in debt and unable to govern his appetites. He is convinced his father has cheated him of his mother's inheritance, and he says so loudly enough that the whole town hears it. He is engaged to the proud Katerina Ivanovna while throwing himself at Grushenka. The night of the murder he tears through town with a brass pestle in his pocket, threatening to kill his father — which is exactly why the case against him will look airtight.

Ivan Karamazov
The Intellectual

The middle brother, twenty-three, brilliant and cold. He has built a philosophy that questions whether anything is forbidden if there is no God. He delivers "Rebellion" and "The Grand Inquisitor" to Alyosha in a tavern over fish soup. He has been talking with Smerdyakov for months without quite admitting what those conversations are about. The novel quietly tests whether Ivan can live inside the argument he has so beautifully made — and the answer arrives in the form of a fever and a hallucinated devil.

Alyosha Karamazov
The Novice

The youngest brother, twenty, a novice at the local monastery. Dostoevsky calls him the hero of the book in its first sentence. He is devoted to the elder Zosima and constitutionally unable to judge anyone, including his father. Where Ivan argues, Alyosha listens. After Zosima dies and his body shockingly begins to decay, Alyosha's faith is tested — and the chapter "Cana of Galilee," when he kisses the earth at midnight, is the novel's quiet answer to the Grand Inquisitor.

Father Zosima
The Elder

The revered monk who has taken Alyosha as his disciple. His teachings — given in long talks recorded by Alyosha — argue that each person is responsible to everyone for everything, that active love is harder and more real than the love that lives in dreams, and that paradise is already here, hidden, if anyone would notice it. He dies early in the novel. His memory, and the manuscript Alyosha keeps of his words, is the spiritual counterweight to Ivan.

Smerdyakov
The Fourth Son

The illegitimate son of Fyodor Pavlovich by a mute beggar woman, raised as a servant in his own father's kitchen. Pale, epileptic, contemptuous of everyone. He has read Ivan's writings carefully, and his long late-night conversations with Ivan have a meaning Ivan refuses to face. When the question of the murder is finally answered, the answer is in his hands, and the way he delivers it to Ivan is one of the most chilling exchanges in the book.
