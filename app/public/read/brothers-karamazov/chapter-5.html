V. Elders

The narrator explains the institution of the elder—the monk to whom you surrender your will entirely—and why the practice is controversial in the Russian church.

Summary

The chapter opens by clarifying that Alyosha is not physically weak or otherworldly—he is ruddy and healthy—and that his decision to enter the monastery was not flight from life but a choice made from genuine conviction. He had simply concluded that giving everything was better than giving two roubles instead of everything and attending mass instead of following.

The narrator then explains the elder system in detail. An elder—a starets—is a monk who accepts disciples and takes their very will into his keeping. The disciple submits completely, not because he is forced to but because he believes the elder's judgment to be closer to God's than his own. This self-abnegation is, paradoxically, a path toward spiritual freedom: by surrendering a smaller will, the disciple eventually recovers something larger. The practice has its critics inside the Church, who accuse it of fostering delusion in the elder and stunted dependency in the disciple.

Father Zosima is the presiding elder at the local monastery, revered by some and resented by others. The narrator notes that Zosima had already indicated to Alyosha that his path led out of the cloister and into the world—that the monastery was a preparation, not a destination. This instruction to leave will come back with urgency when Zosima is dying. The chapter functions as a theological foundation for everything the elder will say and do in the chapters ahead.
