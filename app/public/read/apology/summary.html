The Apology

399 BCE · by Plato

The book in brief

The Apology is Plato's account of Socrates's defense at his trial in Athens in 399 BCE. Socrates is seventy years old. Two charges have been brought against him: impiety — not believing in the gods of the city — and corrupting the youth. The Apology is the speech he gives in his own defense before a jury of five hundred and one Athenian citizens. He does not apologize in the modern sense. He explains his philosophical method, refuses to flatter the jury, cross-examines his accuser in the middle of his own speech, and tells the assembled citizens that if they kill him they will damage themselves more than they damage him. The jury votes guilty by a small majority. The penalty phase follows — Socrates proposes, half-seriously, that he be honored with free meals at public expense. The jury votes for death by an even larger majority. He drinks hemlock a few weeks later.

The text is short — a few hours' reading — and it is the founding document of Western philosophy as a way of life. Plato structured it in three parts: the main defense, the counter-proposal during the penalty phase, and a final address to the jurors after sentencing. Socrates is sixty-nine going on seventy, has lived through plague and civil war, and has watched the Thirty Tyrants come and go.

It is also the founding example of someone choosing principle over survival when the choice was real and the cost was immediate. Socrates could have been acquitted. A few tears, a show of regret, his children paraded before the jury — these were standard Athenian courtroom technique. He refuses every one of them. He argues that a good man cannot be harmed in any way that matters, and that the unexamined life is not worth living. Whether he is right is the question philosophy has been arguing about ever since.

Why it still matters

The Examined Life

Socrates says: the unexamined life is not worth living. The line is one of the most quoted in philosophy, and the Apology is the argument for it — given by a man who knows he will be killed for living that way. The claim is more demanding than it first sounds. Examination, for Socrates, means cross-questioning yourself and others until your inconsistencies become visible — until you stop confusing what you have heard with what you actually believe. He is not recommending self-help reflection. He is recommending a method that destroys most of what most people think they know. Two and a half thousand years of philosophy have been arguing about whether he was right to recommend it.

Standing By the Argument

Socrates does not flatter the jury. He does not minimize his views to make them easier to swallow. He does not promise to stop philosophizing in exchange for acquittal. When asked what penalty he deserves, he proposes — half in earnest — that the city honor him with free meals. The Apology is a portrait of someone refusing to live by misrepresenting what he thinks, even when the cost is his life. The position is not a pose. He had been offered escape after the verdict, and he refused that too. The text became the founding case study for what philosophical commitment looks like when it is tested. Bertrand Russell's anti-war stance and Václav Havel's letters from prison both invoke it.

The Founding Death

Socrates's death is the founding martyrdom of philosophy. Christianity has Christ; philosophy has Socrates. The parallels are not coincidence — early Christian writers explicitly compared the two trials, and Renaissance thinkers from Erasmus onward used Socrates as a pre-Christian figure of righteous suffering. Reading the Apology is reading the trial that has shaped the way philosophers see themselves for two and a half millennia: as people whose loyalty is to the argument rather than to the city, to truth rather than to survival. Whether that self-understanding is admirable or self-flattering is itself a Socratic question. The text is what made it one.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Why he refuses to beg.

Socrates is 70, and he knows the jury. A few tears and a show of regret would probably have saved him. He refuses. I want to read the Apology asking what in his philosophy makes begging impossible — and whether that principle holds up under pressure.

The examined life.

"The unexamined life is not worth living." It is the most famous line in Western philosophy and it is said by a man about to die for living it. I want to read the Apology asking what examination actually requires — and what Socrates thought most people were missing.

Socrates as gadfly.

He describes himself as a gadfly attached to a great but sluggish horse — Athens — whom the gods sent to sting it into wakefulness. I want to read the Apology as a warning about what societies do to their irritants, and whether the gadfly metaphor still holds.

Athens on trial.

Socrates is technically the defendant. But his speech keeps turning the question back on the jury — what does it say about you that you want this? I want to read the Apology as a double trial, and ask which party comes out worse.

Key figures

Socrates
The Defendant

Seventy years old, barefoot, famously ugly, a stonecutter's son who served bravely as an infantryman in three campaigns. He has spent his life questioning Athenians — politicians, poets, craftsmen — about whether they really know what they claim to know, and finding that they do not. He has been found inconvenient. He could leave; exile is offered. He will not. His defense is that the unexamined life is not worth living, and that a good man cannot be harmed.

Meletus
The Accuser

The young poet who formally brings the charges — impiety and corrupting the youth. Socrates calls him to the stand in the middle of his own defense and cross-examines him on the spot. Within a few exchanges Meletus has contradicted himself: he says Socrates corrupts everyone he meets, then admits he believes Socrates is the only person in Athens who corrupts the young. Plato lets the contradiction stand. Meletus is the visible accuser, but the trial is not really about him.

Anytus
The Political Force

The real power behind the prosecution — a wealthy democratic politician, prominent in the restoration of democracy after the Thirty Tyrants. He considers Socrates a threat to the recovered city: Socrates was associated with Critias and Alcibiades, the most notorious products of his teaching. Anytus is barely mentioned in the speech, but Socrates implies he is the one whose influence will determine the verdict. Plato writes him as the force, not the figurehead.

The Jury of 501
Athenian Citizens

Five hundred and one ordinary citizens of Athens, selected by lot, who vote on guilt and on sentence. Socrates addresses them throughout — sometimes respectfully, often with pointed irony, occasionally with what reads as contempt. They convict him by a margin of about thirty votes. After he proposes free meals at public expense as his counter-penalty, they vote death by a much larger margin. Plato was in the room. He never wrote a word about the experience that was not informed by it.

The Oracle at Delphi
The Puzzle That Started Everything

Socrates's friend Chaerephon once asked the oracle whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The oracle said no one was. Socrates was confused — he knew he knew nothing — and spent decades interviewing people who claimed wisdom in order to refute the god. He found that none of them had it either. He concluded the oracle meant his wisdom was knowing he knew nothing. This is the activity Athens has put him on trial for.

Athens
The Society on Trial

The world's first democracy, fresh from defeat in the Peloponnesian War, fresh from the brief tyranny of the Thirty, anxious about its identity and its losses. It executes its most famous philosopher for asking uncomfortable questions about whether anyone in the city actually knows what they claim to know. Socrates predicts in his closing remarks that the verdict will be remembered as a stain on the city. He was right. The Apology is the document that made it so.
