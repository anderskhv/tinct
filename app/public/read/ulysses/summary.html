Ulysses

1922 CE · by James Joyce

The book in brief

Ulysses follows Leopold Bloom, a thirty-eight-year-old Jewish advertising canvasser, through one day in Dublin: Thursday, June 16, 1904. He fries a kidney for breakfast, buys soap, attends a funeral, places a newspaper ad, eats a Gorgonzola sandwich, watches a young woman on a beach, drinks in a maternity hospital, hallucinates in a brothel, walks home with a younger man, makes cocoa in his kitchen, and gets into bed beside his wife at two in the morning. Around him is Stephen Dedalus, a twenty-two-year-old failed poet still in mourning for his mother, who drifts across Bloom's path five times before they finally meet near midnight. The book closes with Bloom's wife Molly, alone in bed and awake, in a forty-five-page unpunctuated monologue that ends with the most-quoted "yes" in literature.

Joyce wrote Ulysses between 1914 and 1921, in Trieste, Zurich, and Paris. He published it in 1922 in Paris because no Anglophone publisher would touch it; it was banned for obscenity in the United States until 1934 and in the United Kingdom until 1936. The novel maps onto Homer's Odyssey episode by episode — Bloom is Odysseus, Stephen is Telemachus, Molly is Penelope — and Joyce gave each of its eighteen episodes a distinct hour, location, organ of the body, and prose technique.

Ulysses is the founding work of literary modernism and the most ambitious novel in English. It contains interior monologue, newspaper headlines, parodies of every prose style from Anglo-Saxon to Victorian magazine, a chapter in catechism, a chapter staged as a play. The technical detail of Dublin is so exact that Joyce claimed the city could be reconstructed from his book if it were destroyed. The novel's argument is that consciousness — ordinary, drifting, sexual, hungry, grieving consciousness — is the proper subject of fiction, and that an ad salesman's Thursday is as worth attending to as any king's homecoming.

Why it still matters

One Day, Eighteen Styles

Joyce divided Ulysses into eighteen episodes and wrote each in a different prose register. The first three are relatively conventional interior monologue, easing the reader in. After that, the techniques start coming apart on purpose. The Aeolus chapter is broken up by newspaper headlines and editorial parodies. The Oxen of the Sun episode runs the entire history of English prose styles in chronological pastiche, from Anglo-Saxon alliterative verse through Bunyan and Sterne to a contemporary American religious tract. Circe is staged as a hallucinatory play with stage directions and impossible scene changes. Ithaca is written entirely in catechism — question and answer — describing Bloom's kitchen down to the volume of water in his teakettle and the trajectory of his urination. The form is the argument. A single ordinary day in a single city contains every register a writer could use, and no single register is enough. Every later novel that mixes techniques across its chapters is in this novel's debt.

Ordinary Heroism

Homer's Odysseus is a king, a warrior, a survivor of the Trojan War who washes up on Phaeacian shores naked and is recognized for who he is. Joyce's Odysseus is an ad salesman whose wife is sleeping with another man, who buys a bar of lemon soap and forgets to pay for it, who eats a Gorgonzola sandwich alone in Davy Byrne's pub, who masturbates on Sandymount Strand watching a stranger during a fireworks display, who walks home in the small hours and lets a young drunk poet he has just met sleep in his kitchen. Joyce's argument is that the patterns Homer found in heroic life — wandering, return, recognition, the offer of food to a stranger, the long delay before a husband and wife actually see each other again — are present in any sufficiently attentive Thursday in any sufficiently attended-to city. Most modern fiction has worked from this premise since. The premise is the modern novel's permission to take seriously the lives that epic could not.

The Final Monologue

Molly Bloom's closing chapter — episode eighteen, called Penelope — runs forty-five pages in eight long sentences with almost no punctuation, almost no paragraph breaks, and almost no concession to the conventional reader. She is alone in bed in the small hours, drifting between memory and immediate physical sensation, working through her marriage, her lover that afternoon, her dead infant son, her childhood in Gibraltar, her sense of her own body and what it has been, her tenderness for her husband she rarely shows him, and finally the single afternoon on Howth Head years earlier when Bloom proposed and she said yes. The technique is Joyce's purest version of stream of consciousness, and the chapter ends — "yes I said yes I will Yes" — with the most-quoted affirmation in English-language fiction. Reading it is encountering the book's argument that consciousness is not what novels had assumed it was: not orderly, not single-tracked, not in sentences, not divisible into thought and sensation, but a continuous drift the prose has to invent a new technique to follow.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The formal experiment, episode by episode.

Joyce reinvents his narrative technique for each of the eighteen episodes — interior monologue, catechism, newspaper parody, operatic libretto. I want to read Ulysses as a laboratory, noticing how each stylistic choice shapes what we know and feel about the same characters.

Bloom as the modern Odysseus.

Homer's hero was cunning, brave, and exceptional. Joyce's hero buys a pork kidney. I want to read the Homeric parallels carefully — understanding what Joyce gains by mapping epic onto the mundane, and whether Bloom's decency is a form of heroism the ancient world couldn't have named.

The father and the son.

Stephen Dedalus is young, brilliant, and homeless in spirit. Leopold Bloom lost his son Rudy as an infant and has never recovered. Their paths converge on June 16 in a way that feels inevitable and doesn't resolve into anything neat. I want to follow what passes between them.

Bloomsday as real autobiography.

June 16, 1904 was the day James Joyce first walked out with Nora Barnacle — the woman he loved for the rest of his life. He chose it deliberately. I want to read the novel with that biographical key in hand, understanding what Joyce was doing by immortalising a particular Thursday in Dublin.

Key figures

Leopold Bloom
The Wanderer

A thirty-eight-year-old Dublin advertising canvasser of Hungarian-Jewish descent. His son Rudy died at eleven days old, eleven years before the novel opens, and he and his wife Molly have not slept together fully since. He is curious, kind, scientifically minded, mildly cuckolded, and quietly grieving. He likes inner organs of beasts and fowls. He carries a potato in his pocket. The novel follows his consciousness for most of fourteen hours and finds, in the texture of one ordinary man's attention, a hero Homer could not have imagined.

Stephen Dedalus
The Young Artist

A twenty-two-year-old schoolteacher, occasional poet, and former seminarian, returned from a year in Paris when his mother fell ill and refused to pray at her deathbed. The novel inherits him from Joyce's earlier book A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man. He is brilliant, proud, articulate, broke, and spiritually homeless. He is Telemachus looking for a father he cannot quite name, and his own actual father — the drunken, sentimental Simon Dedalus — is everywhere in the city he is trying to leave.

Molly Bloom
Leopold's Wife

Born Marion Tweedy in Gibraltar to a Spanish-Jewish mother and an Irish father, now a thirty-three-year-old professional singer in Dublin. She has spent the afternoon in bed with her concert promoter Blazes Boylan. She is sensual, sharp, funny, and intelligent in a way most of the men in the book do not register. Her closing monologue — eight unpunctuated sentences across forty-five pages, drifting through memory, lust, marriage, and the line between them — is the most famous passage in modernist fiction and the novel's last word on what consciousness is.

Buck Mulligan
Stephen's Flatmate

A medical student who shares the Martello tower at Sandycove with Stephen and an English visitor. Mulligan opens the novel intoning a Latin parody of the Mass while shaving on the parapet. He is brilliant, mocking, bawdy, and corrosive — a wit who uses comedy as a way of refusing to take anything seriously, including grief. He has called Stephen's mother "beastly dead." Stephen will not forgive him. Joyce based him on his real-life friend and rival Oliver St. John Gogarty.

Blazes Boylan
The Rival

A Dublin man-about-town, concert promoter, and Molly's lover. Bloom knows the affair is happening; he knows Boylan has an appointment at his house at four o'clock; he watches a clock cross four o'clock and keeps walking. Boylan appears physically only briefly — picking up a basket of fruit on Grafton Street, riding a hansom — but his presence shadows every scene Bloom navigates between morning and night. He is what Bloom is not: confident, unreflective, sexually direct, and uninteresting on the inside.

Gerty MacDowell
The Girl on the Beach

A young Dublin woman, slightly lame, sitting on Sandymount Strand at dusk while Bloom watches her from a distance during a fireworks display. The episode that contains her — Nausicaa — is written in the prose style of late-Victorian women's magazines, and her interior monologue runs in that register before the technique gives way to Bloom's. Joyce uses her to perform one of the novel's most exact stylistic experiments: a consciousness shaped almost entirely by the romantic fiction it has consumed, observed by another consciousness that is not.
