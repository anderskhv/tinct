Symposium

385 BCE · by Plato

The book in brief

The Symposium is a dialogue about love. Six men at a drinking party in the house of the tragic poet Agathon, the morning after his first prizewinning play, decide to take turns making speeches in praise of Eros. Phaedrus speaks first, then Pausanias, then the doctor Eryximachus. Aristophanes the comic playwright speaks fourth and tells the most famous version of the story — that humans were originally double creatures, four-armed and four-legged, split by the gods as punishment, ever since each half searching for its other. Agathon delivers a beautiful empty rhetorical performance. Socrates speaks last, refusing to praise Eros and instead reporting what a priestess named Diotima once told him: that love is not a god but a longing, and that the longing leads, if followed, all the way up to the eternal Form of Beauty itself. Then Alcibiades crashes in drunk, garlanded, and gives a seventh speech — not in praise of Eros but in praise of Socrates, the man he could not seduce.

Plato wrote it in mid-career, around 385 BCE, set at a party that took place in 416 — the year before Alcibiades's catastrophic Sicilian Expedition and his exile from Athens. The dialogue is told at three removes: Apollodorus tells a friend what Aristodemus told him about a party Aristodemus attended. The frame is deliberate. Plato is preserving the evening across distance.

The Symposium is one of the most read of his dialogues partly because it is a great piece of writing. The comedy is real, the philosophy escalates, and the final entrance of Alcibiades reframes everything that came before — turning a series of theoretical accounts of love into a confession about one specific man who was loved and who failed to become what loving him required. It has shaped how the West has thought about love for two thousand years.

Why it still matters

Six Theories of Love

The dialogue is structured as competing accounts. Phaedrus argues love is the oldest god and the source of courage. Pausanias splits Eros into a heavenly and a common form, defending the love between men and youths as the higher kind. Eryximachus, the doctor, expands love into a cosmic principle of harmony between opposites. Aristophanes tells the myth of split halves. Agathon delivers a virtuoso rhetorical performance. Socrates reports the priestess Diotima. Each speech is a serious attempt to say what love is — for the beloved, for the city, for beauty, for the divine. The Symposium is the founding catalog of what love has meant in the West, and most later treatments are variations on one of these six positions. The dialogue's structure is itself part of its argument: love is approached from many sides because no single approach catches it.

The Aristophanes Speech

The story of humans as halves of original double beings, looking for our other half — that is where it comes from. Zeus splits the original spherical creatures because they have grown too proud, then has Apollo turn their faces toward the wound so they will remember. Each of us, ever since, walks through the world looking for the missing half. The image has become a permanent part of how romantic love gets imagined; you can hear it in pop songs, wedding speeches, and the casual assumption that there is one specific person you are meant to find. Plato gives Aristophanes the most beautiful account in the dialogue precisely because he wants to refute the most attractive version of the wrong answer.

Socrates and the Ladder of Beauty

Socrates, attributing the speech to Diotima, describes love as a ladder. You begin by loving one beautiful body. You realize the beauty in that body is the same beauty in other bodies and learn to love physical beauty in general. You ascend to loving beauty of soul, then beauty of laws and institutions, then beauty of knowledge, and finally beauty itself — the eternal Form, neither growing nor decaying, the source from which everything beautiful borrows. The ladder is the founding text of Platonic love and one of the most ambitious arguments in philosophy: that the desire that makes you stop on the street to look at someone is the same desire that, properly trained, leads to the contemplation of the eternal.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

What love actually is.

Diotima's ladder — from physical attraction to the Form of Beauty — is one of the strangest and most ambitious arguments in all of philosophy. I want to follow it carefully and see whether it holds.

Eros as a philosophical problem.

Why does Plato take erotic longing seriously as a route to wisdom? I want to understand what distinguishes Eros from friendship, duty, or affection — and what Plato thinks it is actually pointing toward.

Alcibiades and the limits of philosophy.

His speech is the most personal thing in all of Plato — a confession from a man who knew exactly what he should do and chose otherwise. I want to understand what that tells us about the gap between knowledge and action.

Why the myth is wrong.

Aristophanes' speech is the most memorable in the dialogue — the story of humans split in two. But Plato includes it precisely because he thinks it's the most seductive error. I want to understand why Plato rejects it.

Key figures

Socrates
The Last to Speak

Arrives unusually clean, having stopped on a neighbor's porch to think through a problem before coming in. Refuses to give his own speech and instead reports what Diotima of Mantinea told him years ago. His method dismantles every previous account without seeming to attack any of them. He is the only sober man in the room at the end of the night and walks away at dawn while everyone else sleeps.

Alcibiades
Who Arrives Drunk

The most gifted and most disastrous Athenian of his generation. Bursts in late, garlanded, demanding wine, only realizing Socrates is already there. Gives the seventh speech — not in praise of Eros but in praise of Socrates, telling the story of the night he tried to seduce him and was gently and devastatingly refused. The speech is the most personal thing Plato ever wrote, and it foreshadows the catastrophe Alcibiades is about to bring on Athens.

Aristophanes
The Comic Playwright

The author of The Clouds, which had mocked Socrates a decade earlier and contributed to the public hostility that would later kill him. He arrives with hiccups and has to wait his turn. He tells the myth of split souls — once spherical creatures with four arms and four legs, cut in two by Zeus, ever since searching for our other half. The most memorable speech in the dialogue, and the one Plato thinks is most seductively wrong.

Diotima
The Absent Teacher

A priestess of Mantinea whom Socrates says taught him about Eros years before. She is not in the room. She is, in Plato's body of work, the only figure who teaches Socrates rather than being taught by him. In a dialogue full of Athenian men praising love, the deepest account belongs to a woman who is not there. The ladder of beauty is hers, not his.

Agathon
The Host

A young tragic poet celebrating the prize his first tragedy has just won at the festival. Beautiful, talented, polished. Gives the speech immediately before Socrates — full of balanced clauses, mythological flourishes, and assertions about Eros being the youngest and most delicate of the gods. Socrates dismantles it with three questions. The contrast between rhetorical surface and philosophical substance is the dialogue's central technique.

Phaedrus
Who Proposed It

The young man who suggested an evening of speeches in praise of Eros. He gives the first one, arguing that love is the oldest of the gods and the source of courage — that lovers will fight more bravely because they cannot bear to look weak in front of the beloved. Earnest and limited. He frames a topic the others will spend the rest of the night escalating beyond anything he imagined.
