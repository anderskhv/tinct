Luke 18

A widow wears down a corrupt judge; a tax collector goes home justified; a ruler cannot give up his wealth and goes away sad.

Summary

The parable of the persistent widow illustrates the necessity of prayer without ceasing by analogy with a corrupt judge. A widow, who has no resources to bribe or connections to influence, repeatedly appears before a judge who neither fears God nor cares about people, demanding vindication against her adversary. The judge resists until her persistence makes him capitulate — lest by her continual coming she wears him out (the Greek verb literally means to give a black eye). If an unjust judge eventually grants justice because of persistence, how much more will God give justice to those who call to him, and quickly. The question ends the parable: when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth? The parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector follows. Two men pray at the temple. The Pharisee's prayer catalogues his distinction from others and his religious practice. The tax collector stands at a distance, will not raise his eyes, beats his breast, and asks for mercy. Jesus's verdict: the tax collector, not the Pharisee, goes home justified. The parable is framed explicitly against those who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and treated others with contempt.

When people bring infants to Jesus for him to touch them and the disciples prevent it, Jesus calls the children to him and says whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child shall not enter it. A ruler asks about inheriting eternal life; Jesus traces back through the commandments, and the ruler says he has kept them since youth. Jesus says one thing remains: sell all that you have, distribute to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come and follow. The ruler is very rich and goes away sad. The disciples' perplexity prompts the camel and the needle's eye teaching — it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom — and Jesus's response to their question of who then can be saved: what is impossible with man is possible with God. Peter notes that the disciples have left their homes and followed him. Jesus answers with a promise of return in this life and eternal life. A third passion prediction follows, the disciples still not understanding. At Jericho a blind man calling out from the roadside — Son of David, have mercy on me — is silenced by the crowd but shouts louder, is brought to Jesus, and receives his sight. He follows, glorifying God.
