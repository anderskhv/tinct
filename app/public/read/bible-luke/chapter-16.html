Luke 16

A dismissed steward secures his future through debt reduction; a beggar named Lazarus dies and finds himself in Abraham's arms.

Summary

The parable of the dishonest manager presents an interpretive difficulty that has occupied commentators for centuries. A manager accused of mismanagement is called to account and dismissed. Before surrendering his position, he reduces the debts owed to his master by the master's debtors — one owed a hundred measures of oil gets the bill reduced to fifty; one owed a hundred measures of wheat gets the bill reduced to eighty. The manager's calculation is explicit: since he cannot dig and is ashamed to beg, he will create obligations that will result in his being received into homes after he loses his management. The commendation that follows is the master's commendation of the manager's shrewdness, not his honesty. The application Jesus draws is oblique but pointed: the children of this age are more prudent in dealing with their own generation than the children of light; use worldly wealth to make friends for yourselves so that when it fails, they may welcome you into eternal dwellings. Faithfulness in small things and large; impossibility of serving two masters; the Pharisees who love money hearing all this and ridiculing it. Jesus answers: what is exalted among human beings is an abomination in the sight of God.

The story of Lazarus and the rich man is unique to Luke and is the only parable in any of the gospels in which a character has a proper name — Lazarus. The rich man dresses in purple and fine linen and feasts every day. At his gate lies Lazarus, covered in sores, longing for what falls from the table, while the dogs come and lick his sores. Both die. Lazarus is carried by angels to Abraham's bosom; the rich man is buried and in Hades, in torment, and lifts his eyes and sees Abraham far away with Lazarus beside him. He appeals to Abraham — Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus to dip the end of his finger in water and cool my tongue, for I am in agony in this flame. Abraham answers that between them is fixed a great chasm that cannot be crossed in either direction. The rich man asks that Lazarus be sent to his father's house to warn his five brothers; Abraham says they have Moses and the prophets; let them listen to them. If they do not listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be convinced if someone rises from the dead. The parable does not explain why the rich man is in torment and Lazarus is not, but the contrast in their earthly conditions is the story's ground.
