A Midsummer Night's Dream

1595 CE · by William Shakespeare

The book in brief

A Midsummer Night's Dream tangles three plots over a single night. In Athens, Hermia loves Lysander but her father insists she marry Demetrius; faced with a death sentence or a convent, she and Lysander run into the woods. Helena, who loves Demetrius, tells him the plan and follows. In a parallel Athens, six tradesmen meet to rehearse a tragedy of Pyramus and Thisbe to perform at the duke's wedding. In the wood that surrounds both, Oberon and Titania, king and queen of the fairies, are quarreling over an Indian boy. Oberon sends his servant Puck to fetch a love-flower whose juice makes the sleeper love the first creature seen on waking. Puck applies it to the wrong men, transforms the weaver Bottom into a half-donkey, and the queen of the fairies wakes to fall in love with him.

Shakespeare wrote the play in the mid-1590s, around the same time as Romeo and Juliet — the two are companions, the comedy and tragedy of young love. The verse is some of his most lyrical: Titania's monologue on the disordered seasons, Oberon's "I know a bank where the wild thyme blows," Puck's epilogue. The mechanicals' performance of Pyramus and Thisbe parodies Romeo and Juliet's plot inside its own comic frame.

It is among the most-performed of Shakespeare's plays, and one of the few whose argument is made through structure rather than speech. Four lovers redirect their affections under the influence of a flower; a fairy queen falls for a man with a donkey's head; a troupe of amateurs takes their hopeless play with absolute seriousness. By morning everyone returns to themselves slightly altered. The Dream treats love, theater, and authority as varieties of the same enchantment, and refuses to say which version is more real.

Why it still matters

A Comedy About How Love Lies

Each of the four young Athenians spends a night in the wood being made to love the wrong person by the juice of a flower. Shakespeare uses the device to argue something serious: that romantic love feels like total clarity from the inside but looks like enchantment from the outside. The same person can be "the right one" and "a complete mistake" in a single night, and the difference is determined by chemistry, suggestion, and which face you happen to see when you first wake up. Demetrius leaves the wood still under the spell — Oberon never lifts it — and the Athenian court treats his renewed love for Helena as restored to its proper object, even though the audience knows it is the same magic Oberon imposed on Titania for laughs. The play does not flag this as a problem. It just lets the audience notice. Modern readers raised on dating apps and the language of "compatibility" recognize the territory immediately.

The Mechanicals

The six Athenian tradesmen who rehearse and stage Pyramus and Thisbe — Bottom the weaver, Quince the carpenter, Flute the bellows-mender, Snug the joiner, Snout the tinker, Starveling the tailor — are some of Shakespeare's funniest creations and the source of one of his deepest interests. They take their hopeless play with absolute seriousness. They worry whether the lion will frighten the ladies and propose a prologue assuring them it is only a man in a costume. They worry whether moonlight can be staged and consider bringing in a lantern. They worry about the wall and finally let one of their number play it. The aristocrats in the audience laugh at them throughout the performance. Shakespeare gives the mechanicals the dignity the aristocrats withhold, and the play's funniest scenes are also its most generous. Ordinary people taking art seriously, against the condescension of those who think they know better — Shakespeare returns to this pattern across his career, and Midsummer is its purest version.

The Forest Outside the City

Athens in the play is rule, marriage law, and the threat of execution or convent confinement for daughters who refuse the husband their father has chosen. The forest outside Athens is dream, transformation, and disorder. Almost everything that matters in the play happens in the forest — the lovers' rearrangement, Bottom's translation, Titania's enchantment, the resolution of the fairy quarrel — and almost nothing in the city is the same on the morning after, even if the characters cannot quite say what changed. Shakespeare establishes a permanent geography here that English literature has used ever since: the wood is where the city's order is suspended and the changes happen that the city cannot. Hawthorne's Scarlet Letter and its forest meeting between Hester and Dimmesdale, the moors of Wuthering Heights, the heath of King Lear, the campsite in any number of contemporary coming-of-age novels — they all owe something to the topography Shakespeare laid down in this play.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Love as temporary insanity.

The love potion is a metaphor for something Shakespeare thinks is already true: romantic love makes people irrational, arbitrary, and easily redirected. Notice whether the play thinks this is beautiful or absurd — or both.

Shakespeare on theatre.

Bottom and his friends are terrible actors who take their play completely seriously. Their performance of Pyramus and Thisbe — and the aristocrats' condescending commentary — is Shakespeare examining the relationship between performer, play, and audience.

Bottom's translation.

He is turned into an ass and loved by the Fairy Queen — and takes both in stride with a kind of natural dignity. His waking speech about the dream he cannot describe ("I have had a most rare vision") is one of the most quietly profound moments in all Shakespeare.

Which world is more irrational?

Athens has laws forcing daughters to marry men chosen by fathers or face death. The fairy world has enchantments forcing people to fall in love with donkeys. Shakespeare keeps the question open: civilisation and magic are competing versions of coercion.

Key figures

Puck
Oberon's Servant

Also called Robin Goodfellow, a sprite who works for the fairy king and treats human confusion as entertainment. He fetches the love-flower, applies its juice to the wrong Athenian, watches the resulting chaos with delight, and sets it right only after Oberon insists. He gives the play its closing lines — the famous epilogue offering the whole performance back to the audience as a dream — and asks, gently, whether any of what they just saw was real.

Oberon
King of the Fairies

He starts the entire plot by quarreling with his wife over a changeling boy and deciding to humiliate her in revenge. He is not presented as wicked, only capricious — capricious in the way the powerful tend to be when their dignity is bruised. He directs Puck, designs the punishment, and ultimately reverses it. The play's most beautiful verse — the bank where the wild thyme blows — belongs to him.

Titania
Queen of the Fairies

Bewitched by her husband's spite into loving Bottom the weaver while he wears the head of a donkey. Her scenes with Bottom are at once farcical and unexpectedly tender; she garlands his ears with roses and asks her fairies to feed him apricots. She is not diminished by the enchantment. Her opening speech about how the lovers' quarrel has thrown the seasons themselves into disorder is one of the play's strangest and most adult passages.

Bottom
The Weaver

An Athenian weaver and enthusiastic amateur actor who wants to play every part in his troupe's tragedy. Puck transforms his head into a donkey's. Titania falls in love with him. He accepts both with imperturbable courtesy, asking the fairy attendants for hay and a barber. His waking speech — "I have had a most rare vision" — is one of the quietest, strangest passages in Shakespeare. He is the play's most grounded character, paradoxically most himself when he is least human.

Hermia and Helena
The Mortal Friends

Childhood friends from Athens whose friendship nearly snaps under the strain of the night. Hermia loves Lysander; her father has chosen Demetrius for her. Helena loves Demetrius; he has cast her aside for Hermia. Once the love-juice rearranges the men, both Athenians court Helena, and Hermia accuses her oldest friend of conspiring against her. Their quarrel — "we, Hermia, like two artificial gods" — is the most emotionally serious passage in the play, and the part the comedy nearly fails to recover from.

Lysander and Demetrius
The Athenian Suitors

The two young men whose affections the love-juice keeps redirecting. They are barely distinguishable, which seems to be the point. Each one loves Hermia at the start, both love Helena in the middle, and the play's ending pairs them off again on the basis of which enchantment Oberon has chosen not to remove. The women love them with total conviction; the men redirect their love like weather vanes. Shakespeare is making an argument about who is doing the actual emotional work.
