Summary

Robert proposes the swim. Everyone follows; the lovers and the woman in black trail behind. Music drifts faintly from Klein's hotel. Strange scents in the air — sea, weeds, a field of white blossoms nearby. The moon has fallen on the world "like the mystery and softness of sleep." Edna has spent the entire summer trying to learn to swim, taking instruction from the men, the women, even the children. Robert has conducted nearly daily lessons and is on the verge of giving up. A "certain uncontrollable fear" overtakes her in the water unless there is a hand close by.

That night she is "like the little tottering, stumbling, clutching child who suddenly realizes its power and walks alone for the first time." She could have shouted for joy. She did. She lifts her body to the surface with a sweeping stroke. "A feeling of exultation overtook her, as if some power of great import had been given her — to control the working of her body and her soul." She wants to swim where no woman has swum. She does not join the games; she swims out alone, "intoxicated with her newly conquered power," reaching out for "something unlimited in which to lose herself."

She turns. The shore looks impossibly far. "A quick vision of death struck her soul, and for a moment appalled and enfeebled her senses." She rallies. She mentions the encounter only once, to Léonce: "I thought I should have perished out there alone." She walks home alone before the others have even dressed. Robert overtakes her. They speak briefly. "I wonder if anything will ever move me again the way Mademoiselle Reisz's playing moved me tonight." He teases her about a Gulf spirit; she is wounded by what sounds like flippancy. He helps her into the hammock that swings before her cottage and sits on the step rolling a cigarette. They do not speak. "No multitude of words could have been more significant than those moments of silence, or more charged with the first felt stirrings of desire." When the bathers' voices approach, he says good night. She does not answer.
