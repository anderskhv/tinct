The Oresteia

458 BCE · by Aeschylus

The book in brief

The Oresteia is a trilogy of three plays — Agamemnon, The Libation Bearers, and The Eumenides — telling the story of the cursed house of Atreus across two generations. In the first, Agamemnon returns from ten years at Troy and his wife Clytemnestra murders him in his bath, in revenge for his sacrifice of their daughter Iphigenia at the start of the war. In the second, their son Orestes returns from exile and, on the command of Apollo, kills Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus to avenge his father. In the third, the Furies — ancient goddesses of blood vengeance — pursue Orestes for the killing of his mother. The case cannot be resolved by either side, and Athena steps in. She founds the first court of human justice in Athens and lets a jury decide. They split evenly. Athena casts the deciding vote for acquittal, and the Furies are persuaded to accept a new place in the city as the Eumenides, the Kindly Ones.

Aeschylus produced the trilogy at the City Dionysia in 458 BCE, two years before his death. It is the only complete tragic trilogy that has survived from the ancient world. Across three plays, twenty-six scenes, and roughly four thousand lines, it stages a transformation: from blood-vengeance enforced privately to public justice administered by an institution. The setting moves from the palace of Argos to the temple of Apollo at Delphi to the Areopagus court in Athens — the actual hill on which Athenian homicide trials were heard, a hundred yards from the theater where the play was being performed.

It is the founding work of Western drama as a sustained political and philosophical form. The argument it stages — that civilization required taking vengeance out of private hands and giving it to an impartial public institution — is the foundational claim of legal order in the Western tradition. Aeschylus is not subtle about what he is doing. He has the goddess of wisdom found a court on stage. Twenty-five hundred years of legal theory have been arguing with the implications.

Why it still matters

The Founding of Justice

The trilogy ends with the establishment of the Areopagus — the first court of law — to settle Orestes's case. The myth dramatizes the argument that civilization required taking vengeance out of private hands and giving it to an impartial institution, with rules of evidence, opposing advocates, and a verdict by citizens rather than by gods or kin. Aeschylus is making the argument explicit on stage: the goddess of wisdom founds a court, and the cycle of blood that has destroyed two generations is interrupted not by a better killer but by a new procedure. The argument was new in 458 BCE and is still alive in any debate about due process, vigilante violence, and the legitimacy of state justice.

The Cycle of Blood

The first two plays show how vengeance breeds vengeance. Iphigenia is sacrificed to start a war. Agamemnon is murdered for it. Clytemnestra is murdered in return. Each killing has its own complete moral logic and each demands the next. The trilogy is a study in why families and societies cannot end this kind of pattern from inside it — every act of justice in the old order is an act of revenge, and every act of revenge calls forth the next one. Aeschylus's diagnosis is that the cycle requires outside arbitration to break: a third party who is not a participant. The argument has been borrowed by political theorists from Hobbes to René Girard, and it is the foundation of every modern argument for the legitimacy of the state's monopoly on violence.

The Furies Are Not Wrong

The Furies, who hound Orestes through the third play, are not portrayed as evil. They are the ancient law of blood — older than Apollo, older than Athena, older than the Olympian order itself — and they have a coherent argument: a son who kills his mother cannot simply walk free, no matter what god ordered the killing. The trilogy is honest that the new justice does not replace the old one cleanly. The Furies are renamed the Eumenides — the Kindly Ones — and given a permanent place under the new order, with their own shrine and their own role. Aeschylus's point is that justice does not eliminate them; it incorporates them. The image of the older retributive impulse housed inside the new institution rather than abolished by it has been one of the most influential ideas in legal theory.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

From revenge to justice.

The trilogy is structured as a transformation: blood revenge (Agamemnon) demands blood revenge (The Libation Bearers), until the cycle becomes impossible to close (The Eumenides). Athena's court does not simply acquit Orestes — it replaces the logic of retaliation with the logic of deliberation. Watch how Aeschylus builds the pressure until no individual act of killing can resolve it.

Clytemnestra: Aeschylus's most original creation.

Clytemnestra dominates the first play in ways no female character in earlier Greek literature does. She is strategist, orator, deceiver, and killer — and Aeschylus gives her arguments that are not easily dismissed. She killed a man who killed her daughter. Pay attention to how the Chorus responds to her: they keep trying to disbelieve her, and she keeps being right.

The Furies and the birth of law.

The Furies are not villains. They are the oldest moral logic there is: blood demands blood, the debt must be paid, the killer must suffer. Athena does not destroy them — she persuades them to accept a new role. The Eumenides is a courtroom drama that is also a negotiation between two entirely coherent systems of justice. Ask which one you find more compelling.

Cassandra's scene: foresight as suffering.

The most celebrated scene in Agamemnon belongs to a woman who is already a prisoner and has already accepted she will die. Cassandra sees what is about to happen in the palace and tells the Chorus — who do not believe her, because the gods cursed her to be disbelieved. She names what is coming with complete accuracy and walks through the door anyway. This is the most famous scene of foresight in Greek tragedy.

Key figures

Agamemnon
King of Argos

Commander of the Greek forces at Troy and king of Argos. He sacrificed his daughter Iphigenia at Aulis to get a wind for his fleet, and his wife has not forgotten it. He does not appear until late in the first play, returning home in triumph after ten years at war. Clytemnestra meets him at the gate, persuades him to walk into the palace on a path of crimson cloth, and kills him in his bath. His body is rolled out for the audience to see.

Clytemnestra
Queen of Argos

Agamemnon's wife. She has governed Argos alone for the ten years her husband was at Troy, taken his cousin Aegisthus as her lover, and planned the murder with patience and precision. Aeschylus gives her arguments — about Iphigenia, about her husband's conduct, about the conditions of her own life — that the Chorus cannot answer. She walks out of the palace with her husband's blood still on her and addresses the city without apology.

Cassandra
Trojan Prophetess

Daughter of Priam, princess of Troy, brought home as Agamemnon's slave. Apollo gave her the gift of prophecy and, when she refused him, cursed her so that no one would believe what she said. She stands outside the palace and sees in real time what is about to happen inside it — the bath, the axe, her own death — and tells the Chorus. They cannot follow her. She walks through the door anyway. The scene is the longest sustained moment of foresight in Greek tragedy.

Orestes
The Avenger

Son of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, sent away from Argos as a child. He returns in the second play, on the command of Apollo, to kill his mother and her lover. He hesitates at the moment — looks at Clytemnestra's bared breast and asks his friend Pylades whether he can really do it — and Pylades reminds him of what Apollo has ordered. He kills her. The Furies come for him immediately. He spends the third play on trial for his life.

The Furies
Spirits of Blood Vengeance

Ancient goddesses, older than the Olympian gods, who pursue those who shed kindred blood. They appear in the third play as Orestes's prosecutors — howling, sleepless, relentless, correct by their own ancient logic. Aeschylus does not write them as villains; they are the older moral order. Athena does not destroy them; she persuades them. By the end they have been renamed the Eumenides, the Kindly Ones, and given a permanent home in Athens.

Athena
Goddess of Wisdom

She arrives in the final play to settle a case neither Apollo nor the Furies can resolve. Her solution is not a verdict; it is an institution. She founds the Areopagus and seats a jury of Athenian citizens, who split evenly — and she casts the deciding vote for acquittal. Then she persuades the Furies to accept a new role rather than be exiled, and the trilogy ends with a procession leading the new goddesses into their place in Athenian civic life.
