Agamemnon — Second Stasimon

The chorus sings of Helen — first as a lioness's cub brought home as a pet, beautiful and destructive; then as the cause of Troy's ruin.

Summary

The second stasimon opens with an extended image: someone brought home a lion's cub, raised it by hand, let it play among the children, fed it from the table. Then it grew up. It tore the household apart. The chorus applies the image to Helen — brought to Troy in beauty, revealed in carnage.

The ode turns to the spirit of Ruin (Ate), which enters a house first as the spirit of Delight, then reveals itself as Destruction. Everything that came with Helen was welcome at first: the bride, the celebration, the peace. Then the grief began. The ode does not resolve the question of whether Helen is the cause or the occasion of Troy's destruction; that is precisely its point. The city cannot tell a beautiful disaster from a beautiful gift until it is too late.

The stasimon ends with the observation that Justice shines in smoke-darkened dwellings and honours the righteous life, while she averts her eyes from the gold-decked halls of the powerful. The chorus is not quite able to name what it is circling. But the audience knows that somewhere behind the walls of the palace, Clytemnestra is preparing the bath.
