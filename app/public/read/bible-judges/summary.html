Judges a guided tour

c. 6th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Former Prophets

Between Joshua and Samuel, Israel keeps repeating the same mistake. Twelve deliverers rise, save the people, and die. The people immediately forget. The book ends with a question it refuses to answer: who will rule when no one does?

The book in brief

Judges is the dark book between Joshua and Samuel — the period of charismatic deliverers who rise when Israel is in trouble and disappear when the trouble is past, leaving the people to slip back into idolatry and chaos. Deborah sings on a hilltop. Gideon throws down his father's altar to Baal. Jephthah makes a vow that costs him his daughter. Samson pulls down the temple of Dagon on his own head. The book closes in two stories of such horror that its final verse — in those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes — reads as both an explanation and a warning.

Judges reaches its final form during or just after the Babylonian exile in the 6th century BCE, edited as part of the Deuteronomistic History that runs from Joshua through 2 Kings. The Song of Deborah in chapter 5 is widely considered one of the oldest pieces of Hebrew poetry surviving anywhere, possibly going back to the twelfth century BCE; other materials are clearly later. The book covers the period between the death of Joshua and the rise of the prophet Samuel — roughly the late thirteenth to the eleventh century BCE — when Israel is a loose confederation of tribes in the central highlands of Canaan, surrounded by Philistines on the coastal plain, Moabites and Ammonites east of the Jordan, and various Canaanite city-states still occupying parts of the land Joshua was supposed to have taken.

Judges 1 of 21
Judges 1

The Tribes Inherit Their Portions

Chapter 1 functions as a historiographical preface to the whole book, cataloguing the success or failure of each tribe to dispossess the Canaanites after Joshua's death. Judah and Simeon, working together, push into the south and take the hill country; Caleb's daughter Achsah negotiates additional water rights from her father in a scene of striking assertiveness. But the pattern quickly shifts. Several tribes — Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, Naphtali, and Dan — fail to drive out the inhabitants of their allotted cities, settling instead for a policy of forced labor. This uneven record of conquest and accommodation is not framed by the editors as a geographical note but as the first symptom of a deeper failure. The people who were supposed to be separated from Canaanite religious practice find themselves living alongside it. The incomplete conquest is the soil in which the book's central drama will grow.

Judges 2

The Editors' Theological Statement

Chapter 2 is the hermeneutical key to Judges. It opens with the angel of the Lord at Bochim delivering a formal accusation: you have not obeyed my voice, you made covenants with the inhabitants of the land, therefore I will not drive them out before you. The people weep — 'Bochim' means 'weeping' — and sacrifice. Then Joshua dies, and the generation that knew the works of the Lord passes away. What follows is the editors' explicit statement of the book's governing cycle: sin, oppression, cry, deliverance, peace, death of the judge, sin again. The pattern is laid out methodically. This is not a narrative chapter so much as a theological preface that makes sense of everything to follow. Read it as the editorial voice of the Deuteronomistic compilers, who are also explaining why Israel ended up in Babylon.

Judges 3

Othniel, Ehud, and Shamgar

Chapter 3 introduces the judge cycle in action for the first time. Othniel's story (verses 7-11) is the cleanest, most schematic — it runs through the full cycle in five verses, with no personality, no complication. He is the paradigm. Ehud's story (verses 12-30) is something else entirely: a tale of considerable narrative craft and dark comedy. Ehud is left-handed — or possibly disabled in his right hand — which allows him to conceal a short sword on his right thigh where a Moabite guard would not think to search him. He secures a private audience with the enormously fat king Eglon on the pretext of a secret message from God. The killing and its immediate aftermath are rendered with what can only be described as narrative enjoyment. The chapter ends with Shamgar, who gets a single verse.

Judges 4

Deborah, Barak, and Jael

Chapter 4 is the prose account of the same events that chapter 5 will sing. Read this first and then the poem — the contrast between registers illuminates both. Deborah is presented without apology as judge, prophet, and military adviser. When Barak refuses to go to battle without her, she agrees but tells him the glory of the victory will go to a woman. The battle unfolds at the Kishon River, where Sisera's nine hundred iron chariots are routed. Sisera flees on foot to the tent of Jael the Kenite, whose husband is at peace with Jabin the Canaanite king. Jael offers hospitality and shelter, gives him milk, covers him with a rug — and then kills him while he sleeps. The chapter closes with the death of Jabin and the deliverance of Israel.

Judges 5

The Song of Deborah

The Song of Deborah in chapter 5 is widely considered one of the oldest pieces of Hebrew poetry in the entire Bible, possibly dating to the twelfth century BCE, nearly contemporaneous with the events it describes. Read it in parallel with chapter 4's prose, noting how the poem amplifies certain moments — the trembling earth and mountain torrent, the praise for tribes that came and the curse on Meroz that did not — while adding the devastating closing image of Sisera's mother at her window. The poem curses Meroz for failing to join the battle, blesses Jael among women with triple blessing, and then turns its camera from the victorious tent to the anxious window of a mother in Hazor. This closing movement transforms a war poem into something larger.

Judges 6

Gideon Called from the Winepress

Chapter 6 is the opening of the Gideon narrative, which runs through chapter 8 and is the longest major-judge story in the book. The scene of Gideon's call is carefully set: he is threshing wheat in a winepress — a confined, hidden space — because the Midianites sweep through the land at harvest, destroying everything, stripping the land bare. He is hiding when the angel addresses him as a mighty warrior. His reply is not pious gratitude; it is sharp theological complaint. His calling then proceeds through a series of signs: the fire consuming the meal, the nocturnal demolition of his father's altar to Baal, and then the famous fleece — wet when the ground is dry, dry when the ground is wet. The signs accumulate under Gideon's persistent uncertainty. Pay attention to the portrait of a man the Lord is choosing who would very much prefer not to be chosen.

Judges 7

The Three Hundred and the Torches

Chapter 7 is the central action of the Gideon narrative and one of the most memorable military scenes in the book. The logic of the reduction — from thirty-two thousand to ten thousand to three hundred — is the theological argument made tangible: the fewer the soldiers, the clearer that the victory belongs to the Lord rather than to Israel's strength. The lapping test at the stream has puzzled commentators for centuries; the point is not which method of drinking is more alert but that the reduction is to happen, and three hundred is the chosen number. The night attack is theatrical in conception: each man holds a torch hidden in a clay jar and a trumpet, and at the signal smashes the jar, blows the trumpet, and shouts. The Midianites, hearing sounds on all sides in the dark, panic and turn their swords on each other.

Judges 8

Gideon's Ambivalent Aftermath

Chapter 8 is the darkest portion of the Gideon narrative. After the great victory, Gideon pursues the two remaining Midianite kings across the Jordan and executes them. On the way he asks the towns of Succoth and Penuel for provisions for his exhausted men; both refuse on the grounds that he hasn't yet caught the kings. When he returns victorious, he punishes both towns with grim thoroughness — the elders of Succoth flogged with thorns and briers, the tower of Penuel torn down and the men killed. Gideon then refuses the kingship in one of the book's most famous speeches. But he asks for the gold earrings, makes an ephod, and the text says bluntly: all Israel whored after it there, and it became a snare to Gideon and his family. He has seventy sons and a son by a concubine in Shechem named Abimelech. Gideon dies; Israel returns to idolatry within a generation.

Judges 9

Abimelech's Brief and Brutal Kingship

Chapter 9 is the longest chapter in Judges and the only episode in the book devoted to an anti-judge — a man who makes himself king by violence and holds it for exactly three years. Abimelech's story is the fulfilment of the dark seed Gideon planted: the son by the concubine of Shechem uses his mother's family connections to get silver from Baal-berith's temple, hires worthless men, returns to his father's house at Ophrah, and kills his seventy half-brothers on a single stone. Only Jotham, the youngest, escapes by hiding. The chapter contains the Parable of the Trees — the oldest extended parable in the Hebrew Bible — which Jotham delivers from the top of Mount Gerizim as a curse. Abimelech's kingship collapses in internal violence; he is mortally wounded when a woman drops a millstone on his head from a tower wall. He commands his armor-bearer to thrust him through with a sword so no one can say a woman killed him.

Judges 10

The Minor Judges and the Ammonite Threat

Chapter 10 performs two functions. First it lists two minor judges — Tola and Jair — in a telegraphic register that is characteristic of the minor-judge passages: a name, a tribe, a number of years, and a burial notice. Then the chapter records the most theologically pointed exchange between Israel and the Lord in the book. Israel does evil again, serving a comprehensive list of foreign gods — seven different ones are named. The Ammonites oppress them for eighteen years. The people cry out. The Lord's response is not the usual raising of a deliverer but a pointed speech: go and cry out to the gods you have chosen; let them save you in the time of your distress. Israel repents, puts away the foreign gods, and serves the Lord. The chapter ends with Israel gathered at Mizpah in Gilead asking: who will begin the fight against the Ammonites? The answer will be Jephthah.

Judges 11

Jephthah's Vow and His Daughter

Chapter 11 is one of the most morally complex chapters in the Hebrew Bible. Jephthah's career is presented in its full arc: the illegitimate birth, the exile, the recall by the very men who expelled him, the careful negotiation of terms, the diplomatic correspondence with the Ammonite king that covers the entire history of the land dispute, and then the victory. And then the vow. The text narrates the daughter's emergence and the fulfillment of the vow without editorial comment, which is itself a kind of comment. The daughter — never named — becomes the subject of a mourning ritual described as an ancient Israelite custom. Jephthah's rash vow, the daughter's dignity and composure, and the text's silence about whether God required or approved of the sacrifice — these are left to the reader. This is one of the passages where the book's refusal to editorialize is most demanding.

Judges 12

Shibboleth and the Minor Judges

Chapter 12 begins with a conflict that is structurally parallel to the Ephraimite quarrel after Gideon's battle in chapter 8, but this time Jephthah's response is not diplomacy — it is war. The men of Ephraim cross to Zaphon and threaten Jephthah. He says he called them and they did not come; the Lord gave him the Ammonites. They fight, and the Gileadites seize the fords of the Jordan. The shibboleth test is one of the most famous passages in the book — Gileadite soldiers ask every fleeing man to say 'shibboleth'; if he says 'sibboleth,' indicating an Ephraimite dialect that could not produce the initial sh-sound, he is killed on the spot. Forty-two thousand Ephraimites die. The chapter then lists three minor judges — Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon — in the telegraphic register of the minor-judge sections. Jephthah dies after six years.

Judges 13

The Annunciation of Samson

Chapter 13 is the birth narrative of Samson, and it is the most elaborate annunciation scene in Judges. The Philistines have oppressed Israel for forty years — the longest oppression in the book — and the Lord's response is not a military commander but a child consecrated before birth. The angel appears to a barren woman whose name is never given — she is identified only as the wife of Manoah of the tribe of Dan. The encounter is formal: no wine, no strong drink, no unclean food, no razor on his head, for the child is to be a Nazirite of God from the womb. Manoah's subsequent conversation with the angel, and his anxiety about their having seen God, provides some of the chapter's dramatic texture. The birth and growth of the child who will begin to save Israel from the Philistines follow. The irony the book establishes here — a deliverer consecrated from birth, dedicated to complete separation, who will spend his adult life entangled with Philistine women — is set up with careful attention.

Judges 14

Samson's Wedding and the Riddle

Chapter 14 introduces the adult Samson and establishes the pattern that will govern the rest of his narrative: a desire for a Philistine woman, a display of supernatural strength, a riddle or trap, betrayal by intimacy. His parents object to the foreign marriage — there are women among our own people. Samson's reply is blunt: get her for me, for she is right in my eyes. The text then offers its own editorial note, remarkable for its directness: his father and mother did not know that this was from the Lord, for he was seeking an opportunity against the Philistines. The riddle — out of the eater came something to eat, out of the strong came something sweet — is based on the lion's carcass with honey that Samson encounters on the way to Timnah but tells no one about. The Philistine guests extract the answer from his bride by threatening her family. Samson's furious response — you plowed with my heifer — is the beginning of a cycle of escalating violence that will run through the next two chapters.

Judges 15

Foxes, Fire, and the Jawbone

Chapter 15 escalates the private vendetta between Samson and the Philistines into a series of increasingly spectacular acts of violence. The three hundred foxes with torches between their tails, released into the standing grain, olive orchards, and vineyards of the Philistines, is one of the book's most vivid and peculiar images. The Philistines retaliate by burning Samson's wife and father-in-law; Samson retaliates by striking them with great slaughter. The men of Judah then, in a scene charged with irony, come to bind Samson and hand him over to the Philistines to spare themselves collective punishment — his own people delivering him to his enemies. The Spirit of the Lord rushes on him at Lehi, the ropes fall off his hands, he picks up a fresh jawbone of a donkey and kills a thousand men. The chapter ends with his prayer — I have killed a thousand men — and the Lord opening the spring at Lehi to quench his thirst. He judges Israel for twenty years.

Judges 16

Delilah, the Shaving, and the Temple of Dagon

Chapter 16 is the climax and conclusion of the Samson narrative. The chapter moves through three encounters: the prostitute of Gaza and the midnight removal of the city gates, the prolonged campaign by Delilah to discover the source of his strength, and the final scene in the temple of Dagon. The Delilah sequence is structured as three failed attempts followed by a fourth successful extraction — the pattern of three-plus-one that recurs throughout the Hebrew Bible. The text notes plainly that the fourth time Delilah presses him until his soul is vexed to death and he tells her everything. There is no exculpation. His final prayer — the only sustained prayer Samson utters in the whole narrative — is for revenge, not restoration. He pushes against the pillars and the temple collapses. The book's statement that the dead he killed at his death were more than those he killed in his life closes the narrative. He is buried between Zorah and Eshtaol, where the Spirit of the Lord first began to stir him.

Judges 17

Micah's Shrine and the Wandering Levite

Chapter 17 begins the two-part appendix with which Judges closes, and the shift in tone is immediate. The judge cycle is over; there is no military crisis, no national figure. Instead there is Micah, a man of Ephraim who steals eleven hundred silver pieces from his mother (the same sum, scholars have noted, that each Philistine lord offered Delilah), then returns them when she utters a curse, and uses part of them to make carved and metal images for a household shrine. A Levite from Bethlehem wanders through looking for a place to live; Micah hires him as his personal priest and pays him ten pieces of silver, a garment, and his living. Now I know that the Lord will prosper me, Micah says, because I have a Levite as priest. The verse stands as a commentary on what the book has been showing all along: improvised religion filling the space where authorized worship should be.

Judges 18

The Migration of Dan

Chapter 18 is the first of the book's two closing appendices. The tribe of Dan has not yet secured an inheritance and sends five spies to reconnoitre. Passing through Micah's territory, they recognize the voice of the Levite and consult him about their journey; he gives a favorable oracle. They proceed north, find the city of Laish — peaceful, isolated, with no one to deliver it — and return to report. Six hundred Danites march north and stop at Micah's house. The five spies tell their companions about the household shrine. They steal the images, the ephod, and the household gods. When the Levite objects, they invite him to come with them as priest to an entire tribe rather than to a single household. He is delighted and goes. They take Laish, burn it, rebuild it as Dan, and set up Micah's carved image. The chapter closes with the genealogical note that the Levite's name is Jonathan son of Gershom, son of Moses, and that his descendants serve as priests for the Danite shrine until the exile.

Judges 19

The Levite's Concubine at Gibeah

Chapter 19 is one of the most disturbing chapters in the Hebrew Bible, and the text makes no attempt to soften it. The events at Gibeah deliberately echo the story of Lot in Sodom from Genesis 19, but here the concubine — unlike the Lot narrative, which ends without the women actually being surrendered — is pushed out and dies. The chapter proceeds with documentary precision: the reconciliation in Bethlehem, the long delay by the hospitable father-in-law, the late departure, the choice of Gibeah over Jebus (Jerusalem, then a foreign city), the old man who takes them in. Then the men of the city surrounding the house and demanding the guest. Then the substitution. Then the dawn. The Levite's act of cutting the body into twelve pieces and sending one to each tribe of Israel is the act that triggers the war in the next two chapters. Read the chapter in full — the slowness of the narrative in its first half makes the speed of its second all the more devastating.

Judges 20

The War Against Benjamin

Chapter 20 is the longest military account in Judges and is remarkable for what it shows about what has gone wrong with Israel as a political and moral community. The assembly at Mizpah is the largest gathering in the book — four hundred thousand men with swords. The Levite tells his version of events. All Israel demands that Gibeah be surrendered. The tribe of Benjamin refuses and musters twenty-six thousand soldiers plus seven hundred left-handed slingers from Gibeah itself. What follows is a three-day battle in which Israel loses badly the first two days — forty thousand dead — despite inquiring of the Lord and receiving encouragement. On the third day they use an ambush strategy and destroy Gibeah. Benjamin is cut down in the flight — twenty-five thousand swordsmen in one day. All the cities of Benjamin are burned. Six hundred Benjaminite men escape to the rock of Rimmon.

Judges 21

Benjamin Preserved — 'No King in Israel'

Chapter 21 closes the book and extends the moral horror of chapter 19-20 in a different register: the horror of improvised solutions to self-inflicted catastrophe. Israel has sworn at Mizpah that no one will give his daughter to a Benjaminite in marriage, and they have killed everyone at Jabesh-gilead for not attending the assembly — except four hundred virgins. These are given to the six hundred Benjaminite survivors. There are two hundred short. The solution: go to the annual festival at Shiloh and seize two hundred of the girls dancing in the vineyards. Tell their fathers it wasn't really their fault — they were taken, not given. The fathers are not to think of themselves as oath-breakers. The improvisation is carried out and Benjamin is preserved. The book's final verse — in those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes — has appeared before in the book (17:6, 18:1, 19:1, and now in full) and functions as both the explanation and the verdict.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

The Cycle and the Decline

The opening chapters of Judges set out the pattern explicitly. What the book then shows is that the pattern does not stay flat — each iteration is darker than the last, until the cycle collapses entirely in the closing appendices.

Deborah and the Song

Chapters 4 and 5 tell the same battle twice: once in administrative prose and once in the Song of Deborah, possibly the oldest surviving Hebrew poem. The two versions sit side by side and refuse to harmonize.

Gideon and the Fleece

Gideon is the most fully developed judge in the book: a man who argues with God at the threshing floor, defeats Midian with torches and trumpets, refuses kingship in a famous speech, and then does everything a king would do anyway.

Jephthah and the Vow

Jephthah is an outcast who bargains his way to command and defeats the Ammonites. On the way he makes a vow. When he returns home victorious, his only child — his daughter — comes out the door to greet him.

Samson and the Long Decline

Samson is the most gifted of the judges and the most morally compromised. He is dedicated from birth, breaks his vow on every page, and delivers Israel only through his own death. The book's arc of decline reaches its lowest point in his personal story.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Deborah
Judge and prophet

The only female judge in the book, and the only one described as both prophet and judge in the strict sense — she sits under the palm tree between Ramah and Bethel and the Israelites come up to her for judgment. She summons Barak, accompanies him to battle when he refuses to go without her, and afterward composes the Song of chapter 5, one of the oldest surviving Hebrew poems. The land has rest for forty years after her victory. She is never undercut by the editors.

Gideon
Judge of Manasseh

Of the tribe of Manasseh, the youngest in his father's house, found threshing wheat in a winepress when the angel finds him. He breaks down his father's altar to Baal, tests God twice with the fleece, and leads three hundred men with torches and trumpets against the camp of Midian. He refuses kingship in a famous speech but makes an ephod that becomes a snare, and his son Abimelech seizes a brief and bloody kingship within a generation of his death. The most ambivalent of the major judges.

Jephthah
Outcast judge of Gilead

A Gileadite, son of a prostitute, driven out by his half-brothers, leader of a band of outlaws in the land of Tob. The elders of Gilead beg him to lead against the Ammonites. He bargains, accepts, defeats them. He is also the man who made the vow that costs him his only child. He gives the longest diplomatic speech of any judge — a sustained historical and theological argument about the land — and then destroys his own household with a single rash sentence. He judges Israel six years and dies.

Samson
Nazirite judge of Dan

Of the tribe of Dan, dedicated as a Nazirite from the womb. Endowed with extraordinary physical strength by the spirit of the Lord. Spends his adult life entangled with Philistine women and producing private vendettas against the Philistines that the text frames as deliverance of Israel. Betrayed by Delilah, blinded, imprisoned. His hair grows back. He pulls down the temple of Dagon on his own head and on three thousand Philistines. The most gifted of the judges and the most morally compromised.

Jael
Tent-peg killer

Wife of Heber the Kenite, the woman in whose tent Sisera takes refuge after the rout at the Kishon. She offers him hospitality, gives him milk in a lordly bowl, covers him with a rug, and when he is asleep drives a tent peg through his temple into the ground. The Song of Deborah blesses her among women — the most blessed of all. She fulfils Deborah's prophecy that the Lord will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman. The text is not ambivalent about her; only later tradition has been.

The Levite's Concubine
Unnamed victim

Unnamed, in the closing horror of the book. A Levite's concubine who is pushed out of a house in Gibeah when the men of the town demand the Levite. They rape her through the night. She crawls back to the threshold at dawn and dies there. The Levite cuts her body into twelve pieces and sends one to each tribe. The civil war that follows nearly destroys the tribe of Benjamin. She is one of the most damning witnesses in the Hebrew Bible against the kind of society Judges shows Israel becoming when everyone does what is right in his own eyes.
