Summary

The eighth chapter of Daniel is in some respects the most explicitly decoded of the book's apocalyptic visions: the angel Gabriel names the kingdoms represented by the ram and the goat directly (8:20-21), leaving the identification of the vision's historical referents less contested than in chapter 7. The ram with two unequal horns is the kingdom of Media and Persia; the higher horn corresponds to the greater dominance of Persia within the dual empire. The goat is the kingdom of Greece, and the conspicuous horn is its first king — a description that fits Alexander the Great precisely: his campaigns moved from west to east with extraordinary speed (the goat came without touching the ground), and he conquered the Persian Empire at its height before dying at thirty-two in 323 BCE. The four horns that arose when Alexander's great horn was broken correspond to the four main divisions of his empire among his generals after his death: roughly, the Ptolemaic kingdom (Egypt), the Seleucid kingdom (Syria and the east), Macedon, and the smaller kingdoms of Asia Minor. The little horn from one of the four is, on the scholarly consensus, Antiochus IV Epiphanes, king of the Seleucid empire from 175 to 164 BCE.

The description of the little horn's activity maps the Antiochan persecution with striking precision. The magnification against the host of heaven and the taking away of the regular burnt offering refer to Antiochus's interference with the Jerusalem temple cult; the casting down of the sanctuary and the army refers to his desecration of the temple in 167 BCE (the abomination of desolation of chapter 11:31). The question of 8:13 — how long will the vision of the regular burnt offering and the trampling of the sanctuary last? — and its answer — two thousand three hundred evenings and mornings — is one of the most-calculated passages in the book's reception history. Daniel himself responds to the vision with physical collapse, lying sick for some days before rising and attending to the king's business. The detail is characteristic of the apocalyptic convention of the overwhelmed seer — the visions are too large and too terrible for normal human endurance. Chapter 8 is also the chapter where the language shifts from Aramaic (the language of chapters 2-7) back to Hebrew, a transition that marks the move from the court tales' audience (the broader diaspora) to the specifically Jewish audience of the visions' second half.
