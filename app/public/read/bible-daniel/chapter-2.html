Summary

The second chapter of Daniel is the book's first extended dream-interpretation scene and one of its most carefully structured narratives. Nebuchadnezzar is troubled by a dream he cannot recall and summons his entire court of magicians, enchanters, sorcerers, and Chaldeans to tell him both the dream and its interpretation, threatening death if they fail and great rewards if they succeed. The Chaldeans' response is the key statement of the scene: no one on earth can reveal what the king asks; only the gods could do it, and they do not dwell with flesh. This sets up the demonstration that will follow — Daniel's God, unlike the Babylonian gods, does dwell with humanity and does reveal mysteries. Daniel asks for time, prays with Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, and in the night the mystery is revealed to him. The dream is of a great image — head of gold, chest and arms of silver, belly and thighs of bronze, legs of iron, feet partly iron and partly clay — struck at the feet by a stone cut without human hands and reduced to chaff, while the stone grows into a mountain that fills the earth. The interpretation identifies four successive kingdoms, of which Babylon is the first (the gold head), followed by lesser kingdoms until a final kingdom partly strong and partly brittle. In those days God will establish an everlasting kingdom.

The chapter's theological argument is the most explicit in the book's court-tale section. History is structured by God's plan; the kingdoms of the world are successive and temporary; the kingdom of God is permanent and will supersede them all. The image of the four metals became one of the most influential hermeneutical frames in the history of biblical interpretation — the four-empire scheme was used by Jewish and Christian interpreters to map the sequence of world powers from Babylon to their own day, and has been reapplied constantly across two and a half millennia. The scholarly consensus identifies the four kingdoms as Babylon, Media, Persia, and Greece (with the fourth empire being the Seleucid kingdom and the stone representing the Maccabean restoration or the kingdom of God), though Christian interpreters have frequently read the fourth empire as Rome. The chapter also establishes Daniel's court position, which becomes the background for the rest of the court tales: his three companions receive administrative positions that set up the conflict of chapter 3. The dream of the image and the vision of the four beasts in chapter 7 are the two poles around which the entire book is structured.
