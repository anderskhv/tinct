Summary

The ninth chapter of Daniel is structurally different from the other visionary chapters and is one of the most theologically dense passages in the Hebrew Bible. It opens with Daniel reading Jeremiah's prophecy of seventy years of desolation for Jerusalem and responding not with a vision but with a formal communal confession — a prayer that is among the most extended in the book's tradition, acknowledging Israel's persistent disobedience and appealing to God's mercy and righteousness rather than to any merit of the people. The prayer's form and language are recognisably post-exilic: its closest analogues are Nehemiah 9 and Ezra 9, suggesting that it draws on established liturgical traditions. Gabriel's oracle arrives while Daniel is still in prayer — the speed of the angelic response (swift flight, in the time of the evening sacrifice) is one of the chapter's most striking details. The oracle reinterprets Jeremiah's seventy years: not seventy years of exile but seventy weeks of years — four hundred and ninety years — determined upon the people for a purpose that encompasses the entire problem of sin, not just the Babylonian captivity.

The oracle's three-part division of the seventy weeks (seven, sixty-two, one) and its account of what happens at each transition are the basis of the enormous exegetical literature the chapter has generated. The dominant scholarly reading, calibrated against the second-century compositional date, identifies the seven weeks as running from the beginning of the exile to the decree of Cyrus (or thereabouts); the sixty-two weeks as the Persian and early Greek periods; the final week as the years of the Antiochan persecution, with the abomination of desolation in 167 BCE in the middle of the week. The anointed one cut off after the sixty-two weeks is identified by most scholars as the high priest Onias III, who was deposed and murdered in 171 BCE. Christian messianic interpretation — going back to the church fathers Tertullian and Clement — has consistently read the anointed one as Jesus, calculated the sixty-two weeks in various ways to arrive at dates in the first century CE, and identified the final week as a gap at the end of history or as the period of Christ's ministry. The diversity of interpretations is itself a datum: the oracle is constructed with enough precision to be clearly timed but enough ambiguity to support radically different readings across two millennia. What is theologically clear on any reading is the claim that the present moment of crisis is part of God's determinate plan, and that the plan has an end.
