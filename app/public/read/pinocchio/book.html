How Master Cherry the carpenter found a piece of wood that cried and laughed like a child.

“Once upon a time…”

“There was a king!” my young readers will say at once.

“No, children, you’ve got it wrong. Once upon a time there was a piece of wood.”

It was not fine, expensive wood, but an ordinary piece from a woodpile, the kind people put in stoves and fireplaces in winter to light a fire and warm their rooms.

I don’t know how it happened, but the fact is that one day this piece of wood ended up in the workshop of an old carpenter whose name was Master Antonio, though everyone called him Master Cherry, because the tip of his nose was always shiny and dark red, like a ripe cherry.

As soon as Master Cherry saw that piece of wood, he was delighted; and rubbing his hands together with joy, he muttered quietly:

“This wood has come just in time; I’ll use it to make a leg for a little table.”

No sooner said than done, he picked up his sharp axe to start stripping off the bark and cutting the wood into shape; but just as he was about to bring the axe down for the first time, his arm stopped in mid-air, because he heard a tiny, tiny voice begging:

“Don’t hit me so hard!”

Just imagine how that dear old Master Cherry felt!

He looked around the room in confusion to see where that tiny voice could have come from, and he saw no one! He looked under the workbench, and no one was there: he looked inside a cupboard that was always kept shut, and no one was there; he looked in the basket of wood shavings and sawdust, and no one was there; he opened the workshop door to look out into the street as well, and no one was there. Well, then…?

“I see,” he said, laughing and scratching his wig, “I must have imagined that tiny voice. Let’s get back to work.”

And picking up the axe again, he brought it down on the piece of wood with a huge blow.

“Ow! You hurt me!” cried the same tiny voice in pain.

This time Master Cherry froze, his eyes bulging with fear, his mouth wide open and his tongue hanging down to his chin, like a carved face on a fountain.

As soon as he could speak again, he began to say, shaking and stammering with fear:

“But where can that tiny voice that said ‘ow’ have come from…? Yet there isn’t a soul here. Could this piece of wood have learned to cry and complain like a child? I can’t believe it. Here is the wood; it’s a piece of firewood like any other, and if you put it on the fire, it would boil a pot of beans… Well, then? Could someone be hiding inside it? If someone is hiding there, so much the worse for him. I’ll soon sort him out!”

And as he said this, he grabbed the poor piece of wood with both hands and began to bang it against the walls of the room without mercy.

Then he listened to hear whether any tiny voice was complaining. He waited two minutes, and nothing; five minutes, and nothing; ten minutes, and nothing!

“I see,” he said, forcing a laugh and messing up his wig, “I must have imagined that tiny voice that said ‘ow’! Let’s get back to work.”

And because he was so frightened, he tried humming a little tune to give himself some courage.

Meanwhile, putting the axe aside, he picked up his wood plane to shave the piece of wood smooth; but as he moved the tool back and forth over it, he heard the same tiny voice laughing and saying:

“Stop! You’re tickling me all over!”
