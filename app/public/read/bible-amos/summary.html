Amos a guided tour

8th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Twelve Prophets

A herdsman from a village south of Bethlehem walks north into the boom years of Jeroboam II and tells the rich women of Samaria that they are fattened cattle, the priests that they have grown fat on injustice, and the whole prosperous kingdom that the day of the Lord they keep wishing for is darkness, not light. He is told to leave. He answers that he was no prophet — the LORD took him from following the flock. Then he goes on speaking.

The book in brief

Amos is the first of the writing prophets in canonical order and the most uncompromising voice in the Hebrew Bible on social justice. A herdsman and dresser of sycamore figs from Tekoa — six miles south of Bethlehem, on the edge of the wilderness above the Dead Sea — Amos walks north into the long prosperity of Jeroboam II, probably in the 760s or 750s BCE, and speaks in the royal sanctuary at Bethel. He has not been trained as a prophet. He carries no institutional credential. When the priest Amaziah tells him to go home to Judah and prophesy where his bread is paid, he answers: I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son. The LORD took me from following the flock. Then he goes on prophesying.

The book is short — nine chapters, two hundred and fifty verses — and structurally elegant. Chapters 1 and 2 are the oracles against the nations: a sequence in which Amos indicts Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and Judah before the trap closes on Israel with an oracle twice as long as any of the others. Chapters 3 through 6 are the social critique at full strength: the cows of Bashan, the day of the LORD as darkness, the command to let justice roll down like waters. Chapters 7 through 9 are five visions — locusts, fire, plumb line, summer fruit, the LORD at the altar — interrupted by the only narrative scene in the book: Amaziah confronting Amos and Amos answering him. The book's last eight verses, most scholars believe, were added later; until those verses, Amos offers no consolation.

Amos 1 of 9
Amos 1

The lion roars; the circle begins

The chapter opens with the superscription placing Amos in the reign of Jeroboam II, two years before the earthquake, then immediately with the roar: the LORD roars from Zion and utters his voice from Jerusalem. What follows is the first five oracles of the nations sequence — Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon — each built on the same formula: for three transgressions and for four, I will not revoke the punishment. The formula is deliberate. Three plus four equals not seven but excess: more than enough, beyond what can be overlooked. Each oracle names a specific atrocity: Damascus has threshed Gilead with iron threshing sledges; Gaza has carried into exile an entire people; Tyre has delivered a whole people to Edom and has not remembered the covenant of brotherhood; Edom pursued his brother with the sword and cast off all pity; Ammon has ripped open pregnant women in Gilead to enlarge their border. The audience Amos is addressing in the north would have nodded at each of these. The trap has not yet closed.

Amos 2

Moab, Judah, Israel — the trap closes

The chapter opens with the sixth and seventh oracles — Moab, for burning the bones of the king of Edom to lime, and Judah, for rejecting the law of the LORD — and then, having walked the full circle of nations, lands on Israel. The Israel oracle is the longest and the most personal. Where the previous seven oracles named specific atrocities of war, the Israel oracle names specific social crimes: selling the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of shoes; trampling the head of the poor into the dust; father and son going in to the same girl; lying down beside every altar on garments taken in pledge; drinking in the house of their God the wine of those they have fined. The LORD then recites what he has done for Israel — the exodus, the forty years in the wilderness, the destruction of the Amorites before them — and announces that he himself will press them down like a loaded cart presses down when it is full of sheaves. The reader who has been waiting for Israel to reach the end of the list arrives here.

Amos 3

Hear this word — you alone I have known

The chapter opens with the address Hear this word, the first of three such addresses that structure chapters 3 through 5. What follows is a sequence of rhetorical questions on the logic of consequence: do two walk together unless they have agreed to meet? Does a lion roar in the forest when it has no prey? Does a bird fall into a snare on the earth when there is no trap for it? The questions are building a case: when a trumpet sounds in a city, the people tremble; when the LORD God does nothing, he first reveals it to his servants the prophets. The LORD has roared; who can but prophesy? Then the oracle turns to the capital. Go to the mountains of Samaria and see the great tumults within her, and the oppressions in her midst. The storehouses of Samaria are storehouses of violence and robbery. The chapter closes with the LORD announcing that he will tear down the houses of the rich — the great house and the little house both — and only the corner of a bed and the part of a couch will be left, like the leg or the piece of an ear that the shepherd rescues from the mouth of the lion.

Amos 4

Cows of Bashan; prepare to meet your God

The chapter opens with the cows of Bashan address — the wealthy women of the capital whose only recorded speech is telling their husbands to bring more wine — and announces that hooks and fishhooks will take them out through breaches in the wall. Then follows one of the most sustained and bitter uses of liturgical irony in the Hebrew Bible: come to Bethel and transgress; to Gilgal and multiply transgression; bring your sacrifices every morning, your tithes every three days; offer a sacrifice of thanksgiving of that which is leavened, and proclaim freewill offerings — for so you love to do, O people of Israel. Having described the busy cult with this savage liturgical form, the LORD then recites a series of disasters he has sent — famine, drought, blight, pestilence, the destruction of some of your cities as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah — each followed by the refrain yet you did not return to me. The chapter ends, after five such refusals, with the announcement: therefore thus I will do to you, O Israel; because I will do this to you, prepare to meet your God, O Israel.

Amos 5

Let justice roll down like waters

The chapter opens with a lament — a dirge for Israel in the present tense, as if the fall has already happened: Fallen, no more to rise, is the virgin Israel; forsaken on her land, with none to raise her up. The city that marched out a thousand strong will be left with a hundred; the city with a hundred will be left with ten. Then the address shifts to Seek me and live — but seek the LORD, not Bethel or Gilgal or Beersheba, all of which will come to nothing. The passage on the day of the LORD follows: woe to you who desire it; it is darkness and not light, as if a man fled from a lion and a bear met him, or went into the house and a serpent bit him. Then comes the passage on worship and justice: I hate, I despise your feasts; I take no delight in your solemn assemblies; take away the noise of your songs; let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream. The chapter closes with woes against those who are at ease, who turn justice to wormwood and cast righteousness to the ground.

Amos 6

Woe to those at ease in Zion

Chapter 6 opens with a double woe — against those at ease in Zion and those who feel secure in Samaria — and then invites the audience to look around the region: are these nations better than Israel, are their territories larger? Then it describes, with a precision that reads like a reporter's note, the world of the upper class: they lie on beds of ivory and stretch themselves out on their couches; they eat lambs from the flock and calves from the midst of the stall; they sing idle songs to the sound of the harp; they drink wine in bowls and anoint themselves with the finest oils, but they are not grieved over the ruin of Joseph. The phrase not grieved over the ruin of Joseph is the moral centre of the chapter. The prosperous do not grieve over what their prosperity has cost the kingdom; they eat their calves and sing their songs while the social structure collapses beneath them. The LORD swears by himself that he abhors the pride of Jacob and hates his strongholds, and will deliver up the city and all that is in it. Those who now carry an entire population's crushing will themselves be crushed.

Amos 7

Three visions; Amaziah confronts the herdsman

The chapter opens with the first two visions. The LORD forms locusts at the beginning of the late planting season; Amos intercedes — How can Jacob stand? he is so small — and the LORD relents. The LORD calls for fire to devour the great deep; Amos intercedes again; the LORD relents again. Then the third vision: the LORD is standing beside a wall built with a plumb line, with a plumb line in his hand. The LORD asks what Amos sees; Amos says a plumb line; the LORD says he is setting a plumb line in the midst of his people — the wall is crooked, and the high places and sanctuaries will be laid waste, and the house of Jeroboam will fall by the sword. Amos does not intercede after the third vision. Then the only narrative scene in the book: Amaziah sends a message to Jeroboam that Amos has conspired against him in the midst of the house of Israel; the land cannot bear all his words. He tells Amos to flee to Judah and prophesy where his bread is paid, because Bethel is the king's sanctuary. Amos answers: I was no prophet, nor a prophet's son; I was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs, and the LORD took me from following the flock. Then he pronounces a personal curse on Amaziah.

Amos 8

A basket of summer fruit; the end has come

The fourth vision opens with the LORD showing Amos a basket of summer fruit. When the LORD asks what Amos sees and Amos answers a basket of summer fruit, qayitz, the LORD says: The end, qetz, has come upon my people Israel; I will never again pass by them. The pun in Hebrew — qayitz/qetz — is the vision's whole point: the fruit of summer is the fruit at the end of the season, overripe, at the limit of its keeping; and Israel is at the end of the season of its existence. The chapter then moves back to the social indictment: those who trample on the needy and bring the poor of the land to an end, saying, When will the new moon be over so we can sell grain? When will the Sabbath end so we can open the wheat market, making the ephah small and the shekel great and dealing deceitfully with false balances? The LORD swears by the pride of Jacob that he will never forget any of their deeds. The chapter closes with an announcement of the coming darkness at noon, mourning like that of an only son, and a famine — not of bread or water, but of hearing the words of the LORD.

Amos 9

The Lord at the altar; the fallen booth of David

The fifth and final vision gives no object and no question. The Lord is standing beside the altar — not as the altar's guardian but as the one who commands its destruction. Strike the capitals until the thresholds shake, and shatter them on the heads of all the people; and those who are left I will kill with the sword; not one of them shall flee away; not one of them shall escape. The cosmic scope of the pursuit follows: if they dig into Sheol, if they climb to heaven, if they hide on the top of Carmel, if they hide from his sight on the bottom of the sea, the eye of the LORD will find them. The LORD of hosts touches the land and it melts, and all who dwell in it mourn. The indictment includes the assertion that the Exodus is not Israel's exclusive claim: Did I not bring up Israel from the land of Egypt, and the Philistines from Caphtor and the Syrians from Kir? The book then announces the destruction of the sinful kingdom. The final eight verses are the only restoration passage in Amos: the fallen booth of David will be raised; the mountains shall drip sweet wine; the people will be planted in their land and never again uprooted. Most historical-critical readers take these verses as a later addition; the rest of the book has refused consolation.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

Justice rolling down like waters

The most quoted verse in Amos sits at the centre of a passage that is harder than its most famous line: I hate, I despise your feasts. The command to let justice roll down like waters is not a complement to worship — it is what Amos says instead of worship, after listing everything about the cult that God finds hateful without it.

Cows of Bashan and the rich women of Samaria

Chapter 4 opens with a figure that has shocked readers in every century: Hear this word, you cows of Bashan, on the mountain of Samaria, who oppress the poor and crush the needy. The address is calculated. Bashan was famous for its well-fed cattle. The prophet applies the image to the wealthy women of the capital with a hook through the lip.

The day of the Lord as darkness

In chapter 5 Amos performs one of the most surprising rhetorical inversions in the prophetic literature. Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD, he says. Why would you have the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light. The day every faithful Israelite has been wanting is, the prophet says, the day they should fear most.

The five visions and the plumb line

Chapters 7 through 9 give the structural spine of the book in five visions, each turning on a simple object. The first two the prophet talks God out of. The third — the plumb line — Amos does not intercede after. The wall has been measured. It is crooked.

I am no prophet

The only narrative scene in the Book of Amos sits between the third and fourth visions, in chapter 7. Amaziah, priest of the royal sanctuary at Bethel, tells Amos to go home to Judah. Amos answers that he was no prophet. The LORD took him from following the flock.

Key figures

The 3 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Amos
Herdsman and prophet from Tekoa

From Tekoa, a small village six miles south of Bethlehem on the edge of the wilderness above the Dead Sea. A herdsman — the Hebrew suggests cattle as well as sheep — and a dresser of sycamore figs. He insists, when challenged at Bethel, that he is no professional prophet: the LORD took him from following the flock and sent him north into the prosperity of Jeroboam II's Israel. The book that bears his name is the most concentrated single statement of social-justice prophecy in the Hebrew Bible. He prophesies almost entirely in the northern kingdom though he is from the south; his ministry is placed in the 760s or 750s BCE, two years before an earthquake mentioned also in Zechariah.

Amaziah
Priest of the royal sanctuary at Bethel

The state official whose confrontation with Amos in chapter 7 is the only narrative scene in the book. Sends word to King Jeroboam II that Amos has been conspiring against the throne and that the land cannot bear all his words. Then turns to Amos directly and tells him to go home to Judah and prophesy where his bread is paid, because Bethel is the king's sanctuary. The line is the cleanest articulation of the conflict between the official cult and the prophetic word in the Hebrew Bible. Amos refuses the category, refuses the instruction, and pronounces a personal curse on him — wife, children, land, life in exile.

Jeroboam II
King of Israel

Son of Joash, king of the northern kingdom from roughly 786 to 746 BCE, the longest-reigning king the north ever had and the one under whom the kingdom reached its greatest territorial extent and economic prosperity. The book of 2 Kings records his expansion of Israel's borders and notes that he did evil in the sight of the LORD; Hosea and Amos are the witnesses to what that evil looked like in practice. He never appears on stage in Amos. Amaziah sends him a message; Amos pronounces that the LORD will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword. The kingdom's prosperity outlived him by less than thirty years. Samaria fell to the Assyrians in 722.
