Medea

431 BCE · by Euripides

The book in brief

Medea is the play in which Jason — the hero of the Argonauts, the man who got the Golden Fleece — abandons his wife Medea for a younger princess from Corinth. Medea is a foreigner. She left her own country, betrayed her father, and killed her own brother to help Jason on his quest, and Jason has now decided that his future lies with a Greek bride and a royal connection. The play is the day Medea takes her revenge. Over the course of seven scenes she persuades the king of Corinth to grant her one extra day in the city, secures sanctuary in Athens from a passing king named Aegeus, sends a poisoned robe and crown to Jason's new bride that kills both her and her father, and then murders her own two sons to make Jason childless. She escapes at the end in a chariot drawn by dragons, sent by her grandfather the Sun.

Euripides wrote Medea for the City Dionysia of 431 BCE, the year the Peloponnesian War began. The play came third out of three — the lowest place — and the audience seems to have found it shocking. It is in seven scenes, structured around four confrontations: with Creon, with Jason, with Aegeus, with Jason again. Most of the action takes place outside the house. The murders happen offstage, but the children's voices are heard through the door.

It is one of the most disturbing plays in the surviving Greek tragic corpus and the most clear-eyed about the position of women. Medea is given some of the most powerful speeches in Greek about the conditions of marriage, the asymmetry of betrayal, and what an injured intelligence can become when given nothing else to do. Aristotle disliked the play. So did Horace. They did not stop it from being read. It has been continuously translated, staged, and rewritten — by Seneca, by Corneille, by Cherubini, by Christa Wolf, by Toni Morrison — for two and a half thousand years.

Why it still matters

A Woman Who Refuses to Be Pitied

Medea's first long speech to the women of Corinth is about what life is like for women in marriage — the impossibility of choosing, the dependence on the husband's good behavior, the loneliness of a foreign city, the requirement to adapt to a stranger's habits without guidance. She lists the conditions systematically. She is not appealing for sympathy; she is making an argument. The speech is one of the earliest sustained accounts of women's structural situation in Western literature, and it remains startling for how directly it speaks. Christa Wolf and Toni Morrison both wrote novels engaging with this speech. It is the foundation of every later literary argument about marriage as an institutional arrangement rather than a romantic one.

Intelligence as Threat

What makes Medea terrifying is not her violence but her clarity. She thinks through every step. She knows what she is doing. She wins every argument she enters and reads every man she meets — Creon's pride, Aegeus's longing for a son, Jason's vanity — and uses what she finds. The play is honest about something the surrounding culture preferred not to admit: intelligence in a position with no outlet for action becomes intelligence directed at destruction. Jason did not marry an injured woman. He married a brilliant strategist whose strategy he had been the only outlet for, and he has now closed that outlet. The play traces what happens next with the precision of a clinical study. Modern readers from George Eliot to Rachel Cusk have noted the diagnosis.

A Tragedy That Does Not Punish

Greek tragedies typically end with the violator destroyed. The pattern is so consistent that Aristotle treats it as a structural requirement. Medea breaks it. Medea kills her sons, kills her rival, kills her rival's father, and escapes in a chariot drawn by dragons sent by her grandfather the Sun. She suffers no punishment that the play stages. Jason is left on the ground below her, broken, cursing, denied even the bodies of his children to bury. The ending is one of the strangest in Greek drama and one of the principal reasons the play has continued to be read and rewritten for two and a half thousand years. It refuses the consolation that the structure of tragedy was supposed to provide. Wagner pointed to this ending; so did Pasolini.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Medea as argumentative genius.

Before Medea acts, she persuades. She talks Creon into one extra day — the day she needs. She wins Aegeus's promise of sanctuary with a precision that leaves him no exit. She disarms Jason long enough to send the children in with their poisoned gifts. Every scene where she speaks to a man, she gets exactly what she needs. Read the play attending to how her arguments work — what she gives each man, what she withholds, how she reads the vanity or fear in each of them.

The mother's calculation.

The decision to kill her own children is not made in a moment of madness — Euripides stages it as a deliberation, with Medea reasoning through it step by step, her maternal love fighting with her will to hurt Jason past any recovery. He gives her the argument and the counter-argument in the same speech. This is the most anatomised act of horror in ancient drama. Read asking: is she in control of herself, or isn't she — and does the play give you a clear answer?

What Jason fails to understand.

Jason's defence of himself is not stupid — he believes it. He has made a good match; he will provide for his sons; he is building a future. He cannot understand why Medea will not accept this logic, because he has never understood what she sacrificed or what the exchange meant to her. The play's quiet anatomy of male self-deception runs through every scene Jason appears in. He speaks in the language of reason and gratitude; read what that language conceals.

A woman speaks for women.

Medea's first major speech to the Chorus is one of the earliest pieces of feminist rhetoric in Western literature. She lists, systematically, the conditions of a woman's life in Greece: bought with a dowry, given to a stranger, required to adapt without guidance, unable to leave. She is not appealing for sympathy — she is making an argument. Read this speech as a political act, and consider what it means that Euripides, a man, put it in this woman's mouth.

Key figures

Medea
The Sorceress of Colchis

A foreigner, a princess of Colchis at the eastern edge of the world, granddaughter of the Sun. She betrayed her father, killed her brother, and helped Jason steal the Golden Fleece because she had fallen in love with him. Now she is the cleverest person in the play and the most dangerous, precisely because Jason has given her nothing left to lose. She speaks Greek with the deliberate clarity of someone who has had to learn it. Every man who underestimates her does so to his ruin.

Jason
The Hero Who Forgot

The leader of the Argonauts, the man who won the Golden Fleece, now in middle age and making the sensible match with Glauce, the daughter of Creon king of Corinth. He believes he is being reasonable. He believes the new marriage benefits everyone, including Medea and the children. The play shows him discovering, scene by scene, that the woman he married is still in the room and is still listening. He is the last person in the play to understand what is coming.

The Nurse
Medea's Loyal Servant

An old slave who has been with Medea since Colchis. She delivers the prologue — pacing outside the house, lamenting that the Argo ever sailed, terrified of what her mistress might do. She loves Medea and is afraid of her in equal measure. The play's opening is the audience's first warning: this woman, who knows Medea best, is praying that something will stop her.

Creon
King of Corinth

Father of Jason's new bride, Glauce. He arrives early in the play to banish Medea from Corinth. He is plain about why: he knows she is dangerous, he knows what she is capable of, and he will not have her near his daughter. Medea persuades him to grant her a single additional day before she goes. He grants it knowing he should not. He dies that night, in agony, holding the burning body of his daughter.

Aegeus
King of Athens

He appears at the structural center of the play — a childless king passing through Corinth on his way home from consulting an oracle about his lack of heirs. Medea offers him knowledge that will help him conceive a child. He offers her sanctuary in Athens in return. He extracts no guarantees and gives every guarantee, makes his promise in good faith, and leaves the stage with no idea what he has just enabled. After he goes, Medea's plan can begin.

The Chorus
Women of Corinth

A group of Corinthian women who sympathize with Medea on hearing her opening speech and promise to keep her secrets. They are the conscience of the play. They know what she is planning. They argue against the killing of the children when she begins to talk about it. They cannot stop her. By the end they have stopped trying to. Their final words are a prayer to Zeus that the audience knows will not be answered.
