Phaedo

385 BCE · by Plato

The book in brief

The Phaedo is the dialogue of Socrates's last hours. He has been condemned to death by an Athenian jury for impiety and for corrupting the young. The execution has been delayed for a month while a sacred ship returned from Delos; now the ship is back, the cup of hemlock waits at sunset, and his friends have come to the prison to spend the day with him. They expect grief. Socrates instead proposes that they talk about death and the soul. He argues — at length and from four separate directions — that the soul is immortal, that the philosopher should welcome death rather than fear it, and that what people call life has been a kind of preparation for what comes next. Then he bathes, says goodbye to his wife and children, drinks the cup, and dies.

Plato wrote it in Greek prose around 360 BCE, twenty-some years after the actual execution in 399. It is told as a reported dialogue: Phaedo, who was present, narrates the day to Echecrates, a Pythagorean philosopher who was not. The form lets Plato preserve the eyewitness immediacy while leaving room for argumentation. The Phaedo is a companion to the Apology and the Crito; together they form Plato's account of the trial, the imprisonment, and the death.

It is the most carefully written of Plato's middle dialogues. The arguments for immortality have been challenged by every serious philosopher since — Cebes himself, inside the dialogue, raises the strongest objection — and the closing scene of Socrates calmly bathing, paying his debts, and drinking the poison has shaped how the West has imagined a good death for two and a half thousand years. The poise of the dying man is deliberate. Plato is showing what philosophy is for.

Why it still matters

The Founding Argument for the Soul

The Phaedo is the first sustained Western argument that the soul outlives the body. Socrates offers four arguments in turn: from opposites (life and death generate each other, like sleep and waking), from recollection (the soul knows things it could not have learned in this life, so it existed before birth), from affinity (the soul resembles the eternal Forms, not the changing body), and from the soul's nature as the principle of life. Each has been disputed for two thousand years. Together they founded the Western philosophical tradition that took the question seriously rather than treating it as religion. Augustine read the Phaedo through Plotinus and built much of his theology on its assumptions. Aquinas inherited the question through Aristotle. Descartes recast the argument in the seventeenth century. Kant believed it could not be settled but conceded it had to be raised. Most subsequent thinking about whether there is anything in a person that survives the body is working downstream of arguments first laid out here.

A Death to Pattern Yourself On

The dialogue ends with one of the most carefully constructed scenes in ancient literature. Socrates bathes. He says goodbye to his wife and three sons. He receives the cup from the executioner without anger. He asks if it is permitted to pour a libation, is told no, drinks. He walks until his legs grow heavy, then lies down. The chill rises slowly through his body. His last words ask Crito to pay a small debt — a cock owed to Asclepius, the god of healing. Christian martyrs, Stoic suicides, and the Western image of a philosopher's death all echo this scene. Plato is showing what philosophy is for in the moment when nothing else can help.

The Limits of Argument

At the end of the discussion Cebes is convinced; Simmias still has reservations he is honest enough to name, even on the day his friend will die. Socrates does not pretend the case is closed. He says only that the arguments are the best he has, and that when human reason cannot reach further, one must take the best account available and ride it like a raft across the sea. The dialogue is honest about the gap between what philosophy can prove and what it can console. The arguments themselves remain disputed — every serious philosopher since has pointed to the place where one or another of them fails — but the dialogue's authority does not depend on their being airtight. It depends on the example of a man arguing carefully on the day of his death, knowing the arguments are not enough, and arguing anyway. The Phaedo has been honest about this for two and a half thousand years.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The arguments for immortality.

Socrates offers four separate arguments that the soul survives death. I want to follow each one carefully — understand where it works, where it fails, and whether any of them actually establish what he claims.

Performance or conviction?

Socrates argues brilliantly for immortality while his friends weep. I want to read carefully for signs of whether he genuinely believes his arguments — or whether he is, in his last hours, performing courage for others.

Death as a philosophical problem.

Socrates argues that philosophy is practice for dying — that the philosopher has been separating soul from body all along. I want to understand what this implies about how one should actually live.

The last scene.

The death of Socrates is one of the most carefully written scenes in all of ancient literature. I want to read it asking what Plato is doing — what he is preserving, what he is constructing, and why he wrote it this way.

Key figures

Socrates
About to Die

Seventy years old, in prison, hours from execution. He spends the day arguing for the soul's immortality with the calm of a man who is not pretending. He sends his weeping wife Xanthippe home early because her grief is interrupting the discussion. He bathes himself so the women will not have to wash his body. He pays a debt to Asclepius. He drinks the cup steadily and lies down. He dies more interested in the argument than in his own death.

Phaedo
The Narrator

A young follower of Socrates, freed from slavery through Socratic intervention, who was present at the execution. He tells the entire day to Echecrates from memory some time later. His emotional responses — laughing at Socrates's wit, weeping when the cup is brought, refusing to look as the chill rises — become the reader's guide to what was actually happening in the room beneath the philosophical exchange.

Cebes
The Hardest Questioner

A young Theban philosopher who had paid for Socrates's escape if he wished to take it. He presses Socrates most rigorously. He raises the deepest objection in the dialogue: even if the soul is immortal, perhaps it wears out across many incarnations like a coat across many wearers, and the soul that survives this body might still die at last. Socrates takes the objection seriously and spends the rest of the dialogue answering it.

Simmias
The Sympathetic Objector

Cebes's friend, also from Thebes, also a Pythagorean. He raises the harmony argument: perhaps the soul is to the body as the tuning is to a lyre — real, beautiful, but destroyed when the instrument breaks. Socrates spends a long passage answering it. Simmias accepts the answer but admits at the end that on a question this large, no human argument can give complete certainty. The honesty is Plato's, not just his.

Crito
The Old Friend

Socrates's oldest friend, the same Crito who in the previous dialogue tried to persuade him to escape. He is in the prison at the end. He asks the practical questions: how Socrates wishes to be buried, what messages he wants delivered. Socrates teases him for still confusing the body that will be buried with the man who is speaking. Crito closes Socrates's eyes and mouth at the end.

Echecrates
The Listener

A Pythagorean philosopher of Phlius who was not in the prison and wants to know what happened. The whole dialogue is told to him. His questions and reactions form the outer frame: the reader hears the story as he hears it, from someone who was there. His presence is the reminder that the day in the prison is being preserved across distance and time, by people who needed to keep it alive.
