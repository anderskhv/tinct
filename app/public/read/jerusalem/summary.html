Jerusalem

1901 CE · by Selma Lagerlöf

The book in brief

Jerusalem is a novel about a Swedish farming village whose inhabitants sell everything they own and emigrate to Palestine to await the second coming. The book opens in the parish of Nås in Dalarna with the Ingmarssons — a family that has farmed the same land for centuries and that the village treats with a kind of quiet awe. A young preacher named Hellgum arrives from Chicago, charismatic and certain, and convinces a substantial part of the village that holiness requires leaving Sweden behind. Karin Ingmarsdotter, the eldest sister of the central Ingmar, becomes the movement's strongest convert. Halvor her husband sells his store. Land that has not been sold in living memory is auctioned off. The novel follows what the decision costs the people who make it, the people who refuse it, and the village they all leave behind. Young Ingmar inherits the farm and the family name and is left to carry both forward without his sister.

Lagerlöf wrote it in two parts in 1901 and 1902, basing it closely on a real event: the 1896 emigration of thirty-seven peasants from the parish of Nås to Jerusalem, where they joined the American Colony founded by Anna and Horatio Spafford. She visited the colony herself in 1900 to research the second volume. She had already published Gösta Berlings saga in 1891. Jerusalem confirmed her as the leading Swedish novelist of her generation. In 1909 she became the first woman, and the first Swede, to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, and the citation singled out this novel.

The book's strangeness is that it refuses to take sides. Hellgum is not a charlatan; the conviction he kindles is real and partly admirable. The villagers who stay behind are not enlightened; they lose something when their neighbors leave. The Ingmar farm is not a sacred relic; the burden of carrying it is genuinely a burden. Lagerlöf treats land, faith, family, and emigration as forces of equal weight pulling in different directions, and she lets the cost fall where it falls. She writes in the rhythms of the Swedish folk-tale tradition, with the long quiet sentences of the saga and the patience of the church year, and the result is one of the strangest religious novels in any literature — sympathetic to belief and unsparing about what belief takes from the people who hold it.

Why it still matters

The Novel That Won the Nobel

When Selma Lagerlöf became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1909, the citation singled out Jerusalem alongside Gösta Berlings saga as the work that earned it. Sweden had been waiting for a national novelist of unambiguous stature, and Lagerlöf gave them one with this book. It is the work that made her financially able to buy back her childhood estate at Mårbacka, which her family had lost. The novel is also the moment Swedish prose stopped imitating French realism and started drawing on the saga, the folk-tale, and the church year as serious literary forms. Almost every later Scandinavian writer who works in mythic registers — Karen Blixen, Halldór Laxness, Per Petterson — is downstream of what Lagerlöf does in this book.

Religious Conviction Without Caricature

Most novels about religious revival pick a side. Either the preacher is a manipulator and the congregation are fools, or the preacher is a saint and his opponents are spiritually deaf. Jerusalem refuses both. Hellgum is neither charlatan nor prophet. The villagers who follow him are neither dupes nor martyrs. The villagers who refuse him are neither enlightened nor lost. Lagerlöf, who had grown up in a devout household and lost her own conventional faith without ever becoming hostile to belief, writes the awakening as a real force that costs real things and produces both ruin and genuine holiness. The book is one of the few European novels of its century that treats charismatic religion with neither sentimentality nor contempt.

Land, Family, and Departure

Beneath the religious surface, Jerusalem is a novel about emigration, which was the great Swedish experience of the late nineteenth century: between 1850 and 1910 nearly a fifth of the Swedish population left for somewhere else, mostly the American Midwest. Lagerlöf wrote at the height of that wave. Her Ingmarssons depart not for Minnesota but for Palestine, which is what makes the book strange, but the structure of the loss is the same one that emptied the Swedish countryside. Land that had been farmed for centuries is sold. Families are split between those who go and those who stay. Both groups feel the absence. The novel is one of the great European treatments of what mass departure does to the place left behind.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

Land versus conviction.

The Ingmarssons have farmed the same land for generations. Hellgum's gospel asks them to sell it. I want to read the novel as an argument about what a person owes the place they were born to, and what is lost when rootedness gives way to belief.

A real religious awakening.

Lagerlöf based the pilgrimage on real events — the Nås colony of 1896. I want to treat the novel as a study of how a charismatic preacher actually splits a community, and whether Lagerlöf is sympathetic to the awakening or critical of it.

The Ingmar line and its burden.

Young Ingmar inherits both the farm and the family's long, rigid sense of duty — the weight of what every Ingmar before him has done. I want to read the novel as a portrait of inheritance: what it means to carry a name, and what it costs to honour it.

Women holding the centre.

Karin, Gertrud, Brita — the novel's women carry the weight of the decisions, often more than the men do. I want to track how Lagerlöf writes them: what they give up, what they choose, and what she is saying about where conviction and endurance actually live.

Key figures

Ingmar Ingmarsson the Younger
The Heir

Inherits the farm, the name, and the family's long expectation of quiet duty when his father dies. In love with Gertrud and unwilling to leave the land. Stays behind in Nås when half the parish departs for Jerusalem and watches his sister Karin take Halvor and most of his world with her. The novel follows what it costs him to be an Ingmar at a moment when the world is asking the Ingmarssons to become something else, and whether anything is left of the family by the time the second book ends.

Karin Ingmarsdotter
The Elder Sister

Ingmar's older sister, married to Halvor and mistress of the Ingmar farm before Hellgum arrives. One of the first to believe and one of those who gives up the most: the farm, the village, the family graves, the idea of being buried beside her parents. Her conviction is the novel's strongest single force, and Lagerlöf treats it neither as fanaticism nor as sanctity. Karin keeps the colony together through hardships in Jerusalem that more brittle figures cannot survive.

Halvor Halvorsson
Karin's Husband

A merchant in the village before he marries into the Ingmar family. Steady, practical, untheatrical, the kind of man who weighs grain and keeps accounts. Follows Karin into the new faith without histrionics and liquidates a life's work when he is persuaded. His conversion is one of the novel's quietest miracles: the slow turning of a man not given to large gestures into someone who can sell everything for a conviction his ledger cannot account for. He sells the store at a loss and never complains.

Hellgum
The Preacher

Arrives from Chicago, where he has been working with American revivalists, preaching a new gospel of holiness, communal property, and departure from a fallen world. Splits the parish of Nås in two within months. Lagerlöf refuses to make him a villain or a saint. He is a man whose certainty is powerful enough to reshape other people's entire lives, and the novel keeps asking whether that power is gift or affliction. He leaves the colony before the worst hardships of the Jerusalem years.

Gertrud
The Beloved

The girl Ingmar loves and intends to marry. Drawn into Hellgum's orbit and eventually convinced she has had a vision of Christ on a Dalarna hillside. Her pull between ordinary love and spiritual calling is one of the novel's central aches. She leaves with the colony for Jerusalem; what happens to her there, and to Ingmar's relationship with her, is the place where the novel's question about faith and human attachment becomes most personal. Lagerlöf does not let either side win cleanly.

Strong Ingmar
The Elder

The old patriarch of the previous generation, sometimes called Stor-Ingmar, the embodiment of what the Ingmarsson name has meant in the parish for as long as anyone remembers. The kind of farmer to whom neighbors bring disputes for arbitration because his judgment has the weight of tradition behind it. The novel's first book turns partly on his death and on what passes to young Ingmar with the farm. What Strong Ingmar represents does not survive Hellgum's preaching intact, and the novel asks what, if anything, replaces it.
