The Epic of Gilgamesh

2100 BCE · by Anonymous

The book in brief

The Epic of Gilgamesh is the story of Gilgamesh, the historical king of Uruk in Mesopotamia, and his friendship with the wild man Enkidu. Gilgamesh begins as a tyrant — too strong, too restless, working his subjects into the ground. The gods create Enkidu as his counterweight, a man raised among animals on the steppe, and the two of them fight to a draw and become inseparable. They go together to kill the demon Humbaba in the Cedar Forest, refuse the goddess Ishtar's marriage proposal, and slay the Bull of Heaven she sends in revenge. The gods kill Enkidu for it. Gilgamesh, undone by grief, sets out across the world looking for the secret of immortality. He finds the one human who has it, the flood survivor Utnapishtim, and learns that he cannot have it himself. He comes home to Uruk and shows off the city walls.

The poem was composed and refined across more than a thousand years in multiple Mesopotamian languages and cultures — Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian. The most complete version, in Akkadian, was inscribed around 1200 BCE on twelve clay tablets and lost for nearly two millennia until rediscovered in the 1850s in the ruined library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. Some tablets are still fragmentary. New fragments are still being found.

It is the oldest surviving major work of literature, predating Homer by at least a thousand years and the Hebrew Bible by considerably more. It is the first sustained literary work humans produced, and the first to ask the questions that have not stopped being asked since: about friendship, mortality, the limits of human ambition, what to do with the knowledge of one's own death. The flood narrative on the eleventh tablet was identified, when first translated in 1872, as the source of the flood story in Genesis. It changed the way the West read its own scriptures.

Why it still matters

The Oldest Story That Still Works

Gilgamesh is at least four thousand years old. Its concerns — losing a close friend, fearing one's own death, hunting for some way to live forever, returning home empty-handed — are immediately recognizable across that distance. The poem was lost for nearly two thousand years until British excavators dug it out of Nineveh in the 1850s, and the first translation in 1872 caused a public sensation when George Smith identified the flood narrative on the eleventh tablet as the source of the Genesis flood. Reading it is encountering proof that the central human questions are older than civilization itself, and that the literary forms for asking them were already mature before Homer was born. Stephen Mitchell and Yusef Komunyakaa have translated it for contemporary audiences.

The First Friendship

The relationship between Gilgamesh and Enkidu is the first sustained portrait of male friendship in world literature. Enkidu is created as Gilgamesh's equal — explicitly, by the gods, to be a counterweight strong enough to match him — and the poem treats their bond with seriousness no later epic surpassed. They sleep beside each other, they fight beside each other, Gilgamesh dreams of Enkidu before they meet, Enkidu pulls Gilgamesh back from his recklessness. When Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh's response — his rage, his refusal to bury the body, his collapse, his flight from the city — is the first full literary treatment of grief. Modern readers from C. S. Lewis to Olga Tokarczuk have noted that no later text quite catches what this one catches about losing the person who knew you.

The Failure of the Quest

Gilgamesh travels to the end of the world to find immortality. He crosses the waters of death. He meets the one man who has it. He learns there is no immortality available to him. As consolation, Utnapishtim's wife persuades her husband to tell Gilgamesh about a plant on the sea floor that restores youth. Gilgamesh dives for it, finds it, and on the way home sets it down to bathe. A serpent eats it and sheds its skin. Gilgamesh sits on the bank and weeps. He returns to Uruk empty-handed and the poem ends with him pointing out the walls of his city to the boatman who brought him home. The argument is unsentimental and almost modern: there is no immortality, but there is what you have built, and the city outlasts the man.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The first answer to mortality.

Gilgamesh is four thousand years old and still asking the question we ask. Watch how the poem refuses easy consolation — and what it quietly offers instead.

Friendship as the hinge of everything.

Enkidu and Gilgamesh become each other's equals in a way neither expected. The second half of the epic exists entirely because one of them dies. Notice how grief becomes obsession becomes quest.

Wild vs. civilised.

Enkidu begins as an animal-man, running with gazelles. His taming is presented as both gift and loss. The poem keeps asking whether civilisation is worth what it costs.

The template of all quests.

This is where the hero journey begins in writing. The departure, the trials, the descent, the impossible boon sought and lost — every story that came after owes something to these twelve tablets.

Key figures

Gilgamesh
The King

King of Uruk, two-thirds divine and one-third mortal — which means, despite his power, he will die. He begins as a tyrant who exhausts his subjects and takes brides on their wedding nights. After Enkidu's death he refuses to wash, wears animal skins, and walks alone to the ends of the earth looking for immortality. He returns with nothing, and shows the boatman the walls of Uruk. That act of pointing is the closest thing to an answer the poem offers.

Enkidu
The Wild One

Created by the gods on the steppe to be Gilgamesh's equal and counterweight. He arrives knowing nothing of bread or clothing, drinks at watering holes with the gazelles, and is brought into civilization by the temple prostitute Shamhat. He fights Gilgamesh in the streets of Uruk, they wrestle to a draw, and they become inseparable. The gods kill him for the slaying of the Bull of Heaven, and his death is what sends Gilgamesh on the quest.

Ishtar
Goddess of Love and War

Patron goddess of Uruk. After Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill Humbaba and return in glory, she proposes marriage to Gilgamesh. He refuses her in a long speech listing the men and animals she has destroyed for sport — one of the most extraordinary insults a mortal ever delivers to a god in any literature. She demands her father send the Bull of Heaven against Uruk in revenge. Enkidu and Gilgamesh kill the Bull. The gods then sentence Enkidu to die for it.

Utnapishtim
The Immortal

The one human ever granted eternal life by the gods. He survived the flood by building an ark on instructions whispered through a reed wall, and was placed beyond the waters of death. Gilgamesh crosses those waters to find him. What Utnapishtim tells him is not comforting: immortality was a one-time grant, no council of the gods will be assembled again, and the hero must go home. His flood narrative is the source of the Genesis flood story.

Siduri
The Tavern Keeper

A barmaid living at the edge of the world whom Gilgamesh meets near the end of his journey. She delivers what may be the poem's most lasting counsel: eat your fill, dress in fresh clothes, let your wife rejoice in your embrace, look at the small child who holds your hand. The poem stages her advice as an alternative to the quest — the homely answer Gilgamesh is not yet ready to hear. By the time he returns to Uruk he is closer to it.

Shamash
Sun God

The sun god, patron of Gilgamesh and his protector through the early adventures. He provides the winds that pin Humbaba in the Cedar Forest. He intercedes with the gods on Enkidu's behalf when they sentence him to die. He cannot, in the end, give Gilgamesh the one thing he wants — eternal life — and he does not pretend otherwise. The poem treats him with respect: a powerful god who is honest about his limits.
