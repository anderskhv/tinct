John a guided tour

c. 90–110 CE · New Testament · Fourth Gospel

The gospel that begins in eternity, not Bethlehem. Seven signs. Seven I am sayings. A Farewell Discourse four chapters long. And a resurrection scene in a garden where the risen Jesus speaks one word — a name — and is recognized.

The book in brief

John is the latest of the four canonical gospels and the most theologically distinct. Where Mark, Matthew, and Luke share a basic narrative shape, John works on its own terms — long discourses instead of parables, seven carefully framed signs instead of crowds of healings, and a Jesus who speaks of himself as the Word made flesh, the bread of life, the light of the world, the resurrection and the life. The gospel opens in eternity (in the beginning was the Word) and ends with a breakfast on the beach in Galilee. Between those two scenes it produces some of the most quoted sentences in any literature, and a portrait of Jesus that has shaped Christian theology more than any other single book of the Bible.

The structure splits roughly in half. Chapters 1–12 are sometimes called the Book of Signs — the public ministry, the seven signs and the discourses they generate, the rising opposition from the religious authorities. Chapters 13–21 are the Book of Glory: the foot-washing at the last supper, the long Farewell Discourse (chapters 14–17, including the high-priestly prayer of chapter 17), the arrest, trial, crucifixion, resurrection, and post-resurrection appearances. John says plainly at the end why he wrote: these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name.

John 1 of 21
John 1

The Word and the First Disciples

Chapter 1 falls into two distinct movements. The prologue (verses 1–18) is the theological frame for the entire gospel: the Word is with God, is God, and becomes flesh. Verse 14 is the centre — the Word pitched his tent among us and we have seen his glory. The second movement (verses 19–51) is narrative: John the Baptist's testimony, the calling of Andrew and Peter, Philip and Nathanael. Two disciples follow Jesus after the Baptist points him out as the Lamb of God; they ask where he is staying and he says come and see. Nathanael, skeptical about anything from Nazareth, is told by Philip the same thing: come and see. Jesus greets Nathanael and says before Philip called you I saw you under the fig tree, and Nathanael says Rabbi, you are the Son of God. The prologue and the calling together establish John's method: cosmic claim, then concrete encounter.

John 2

Cana and the Temple Cleansing

Chapter 2 contains the first two incidents of the public ministry, set in very different registers. The wedding at Cana (verses 1–12) is domestic and quiet: Mary notices the wine has run out, tells Jesus, and tells the servants to do whatever he tells them. Six stone water jars — used for Jewish purification rites — are filled with water that becomes wine; the steward praises the bridegroom for saving the best wine until last. John notes that this was the first of his signs and that his disciples believed in him. The temple cleansing (verses 13–22) is public and confrontational: Jesus drives out the money-changers and dove-sellers with a whip of cords and quotes Psalm 69 — zeal for your house will consume me. When challenged for a sign authorizing what he has done, he says destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up. The authorities hear an architectural boast; John explains in an editorial note that he was speaking of the temple of his body, which the disciples remembered and believed after the resurrection.

John 3

Nicodemus by Night and John 3:16

Chapter 3 divides into a dialogue and a testimony. The dialogue with Nicodemus (verses 1–21) is one of the most analysed conversations in John. Nicodemus, a Pharisee and ruler of the Jews, comes by night — the darkness is probably symbolic in a gospel where light and darkness are constant themes — and acknowledges that Jesus must be from God given the signs. Jesus says you must be born again (or born from above — the Greek anōthen carries both meanings), and Nicodemus hears a physical impossibility. The dialogue opens into a discourse on the Spirit, on eternal life, on the Son of Man lifted up as Moses lifted the bronze serpent in the wilderness, and on the famous verse 16: for God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life. The second half of the chapter (verses 22–36) is the Baptist's final testimony: he is the friend of the bridegroom, not the bridegroom; he must decrease while Jesus increases; the one who comes from above is above all.

John 4

The Woman at the Well

Chapter 4 is built around the longest conversation Jesus has with anyone in any of the four gospels. Jesus, tired from the journey, sits by Jacob's well near Sychar in Samaria while the disciples go to buy food. A Samaritan woman comes to draw water. He asks for a drink. She points out that Jews and Samaritans do not associate; he offers her living water, water that becomes in the one who drinks it a spring welling up to eternal life. She asks for this water so she will not have to keep coming to the well. He tells her to call her husband; she says she has no husband; he says correctly that she has had five husbands and that the man she now lives with is not her husband. She recognises him as a prophet and raises the question of where to worship — on the Samaritan mountain or in Jerusalem. He tells her that the hour is coming and now is when true worshippers will worship in spirit and truth. She says she knows the Messiah is coming; he says I am he, the one speaking with you. The second half of the chapter returns to Galilee for the healing of the royal official's son — the second sign — by a word spoken at a distance.

John 5

The Healing at Bethesda

Chapter 5 turns on a Sabbath healing that provokes the first sustained conflict with the religious authorities and generates a major theological discourse. The healing at the pool of Bethesda (verses 1–15) is brief and strange: a man has been ill for thirty-eight years; he has no one to put him in the water when it is stirred up; Jesus says take up your bed and walk, and he does. The day is a sabbath. The authorities challenge the man for carrying his bed on the sabbath; he points to the one who healed him; they seek Jesus out and challenge him. His answer — my Father is working until now, and I am working — escalates the conflict immediately. The long discourse that follows (verses 17–47) is the first of John's major Father-Son discourses: the Son can do nothing of his own accord, only what he sees the Father doing. The Father judges no one but has given all judgment to the Son, so that all may honour the Son even as they honour the Father. The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God. The chapter closes with a forensic argument: John the Baptist bore witness, the works bear witness, the Father bears witness, and the scriptures themselves bear witness — yet the authorities refuse to come to Jesus that they may have life.

John 6

Bread of Life: Feeding and Discourse

Chapter 6 is the longest in the gospel and the most demanding. The feeding of the five thousand (verses 1–15) is the only miracle in all four gospels; John frames it as the fourth sign. Jesus tests Philip — where are we to buy bread? — while knowing what he is about to do. Five barley loaves and two fish feed the crowd with twelve baskets left over. The crowd wants to make him king by force; he withdraws. That night the disciples are crossing to Capernaum in the dark when Jesus walks on the sea toward them; they are afraid; he says I am he, do not be afraid. The next day the crowd finds him and he begins the bread-of-life discourse (verses 22–59), one of the most theologically dense passages in John. I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger. I am the living bread that came down from heaven; if anyone eats of this bread he will live forever. The bread I will give is my flesh, for the life of the world. The discourse becomes harder and many disciples draw back; Jesus asks the Twelve if they too want to leave. Peter answers for the group: Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life.

John 7

The Feast of Tabernacles

Chapter 7 is one of the most dramatic scenes of the public ministry, set during the week-long Feast of Tabernacles in Jerusalem. Jesus's brothers urge him to go to Jerusalem to show his works to the world — John notes that even his brothers did not believe in him — and he declines, then goes secretly. The chapter is structured around a series of crowd arguments about who Jesus is: a good man, a deceiver, the prophet, the Messiah. The authorities send officers to arrest him; he speaks about going where they cannot come; some hear the river-of-living-water saying and ask if this is the prophet; the officers return empty-handed and say no one ever spoke like this man. Nicodemus, appearing again, tries to raise a procedural objection on Jesus's behalf and is dismissed. The chapter ends with the crowd divided. The river-of-living-water saying (verses 37–39) is one of the most-cited passages in debates about the Spirit: on the last and greatest day of the feast Jesus stands and cries out — whoever is thirsty, let him come to me and drink. John adds an editorial note: this he said about the Spirit, which those who believed in him were to receive.

John 8

Light of the World and Before Abraham

Chapter 8 opens with the pericope of the woman taken in adultery (verses 1–11), absent from the earliest manuscripts and widely understood as a later addition, but narratively powerful: whoever is without sin may cast the first stone. When everyone has left, Jesus tells the woman he does not condemn her either and to go and sin no more. The chapter then enters its main conflict. Jesus identifies himself as the light of the world; the Pharisees challenge the self-testimony; he responds that his testimony is valid because he knows where he came from. The discourse on the Father moves deeper: you are from below, I am from above; you are of this world, I am not of this world. The I am sayings begin to intensify — when you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he. The chapter's climax is the controversy about Abraham. Jesus says Abraham rejoiced to see his day. The crowd says he is not yet fifty years old and has seen Abraham? He says before Abraham was, I am. The crowd picks up stones to throw at him, and he hides and leaves the temple.

John 9

The Man Born Blind

Chapter 9 is the most novelistic chapter in John and a sustained piece of dramatic irony. The healing itself is brief and strange: Jesus makes mud from spittle, anoints the man's eyes, and tells him to wash in the pool of Siloam. The man comes back seeing. What follows is a series of interrogations, each one expanding the irony. Neighbours debate whether this is even the same man; he says I am the man. They bring him to the Pharisees; the Pharisees interrogate him; he says the man is a prophet. The Pharisees summon his parents, who confirm he was born blind but refer all further questions back to their son — they are afraid of expulsion from the synagogue. The Pharisees interrogate the man again; he says if this man were not from God, he could do nothing; they drive him out. Jesus finds him and asks if he believes in the Son of Man; the man asks who that is; Jesus says you have seen him. The chapter ends with a brief exchange that crystallises the theme: Jesus says I came into this world for judgment — that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind. The Pharisees ask if they too are blind; he says if you were blind you would have no sin, but since you say we see, your sin remains.

John 10

The Good Shepherd

Chapter 10 follows directly from the expulsion of the man born blind in chapter 9 and develops two sustained metaphors. The extended allegory of the sheepfold (verses 1–18) introduces two of the I am sayings: I am the door of the sheep (verse 7) and I am the good shepherd (verse 11). The door metaphor distinguishes Jesus from thieves and robbers who climb in over the wall; whoever enters through him will be saved. The good-shepherd metaphor introduces the note of sacrifice: the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep, whereas the hired hand flees when the wolf comes. I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father. I have other sheep that are not of this fold — I must bring them also. I lay down my life that I may take it up again. No one takes it from me; I have authority to lay it down and authority to take it up again. The second half of the chapter (verses 22–42) is set at the Feast of Dedication in winter. The religious authorities press him: tell us plainly if you are the Messiah. He says the works he does in his Father's name bear witness; his sheep hear his voice; he gives them eternal life and no one snatches them from his hand. Then: I and the Father are one. They pick up stones; he asks for which good work; they say for blasphemy, because you, being a man, make yourself God.

John 11

The Raising of Lazarus

Chapter 11 is the dramatic climax of the Book of Signs and the most fully narrated miracle in John. Lazarus, the brother of Mary and Martha, is ill; Mary and Martha send word to Jesus; he delays two days and tells the disciples that Lazarus's illness is for the glory of God. By the time he arrives in Bethany, Lazarus has been dead four days. Martha comes to meet him; he tells her your brother will rise again; she says she knows he will rise in the resurrection at the last day; he says I am the resurrection and the life — whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die. Do you believe this? She says yes. Mary comes to the tomb weeping; Jesus weeps — the shortest verse in the English Bible: Jesus wept. He asks for the stone to be taken away; Martha says the body will smell after four days; he says did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God? He prays aloud to the Father — for the sake of the crowd standing by — and calls Lazarus out of the tomb. Lazarus comes out still bound in the grave clothes. The response divides the witnesses: some believe; others report to the Pharisees. The Pharisees and chief priests convene: this man is performing many signs; if we let him go on, the Romans will come and take away our nation. Caiaphas says it is better for one man to die for the people than for the whole nation to perish. John reads this as an unconscious prophecy.

John 12

Anointing, Entry, and the Hour

Chapter 12 is the bridge between the two halves of the gospel. Three distinct scenes move from Bethany to Jerusalem to a broader reflection. The anointing at Bethany (verses 1–11) is set six days before the Passover: Mary takes a pound of expensive ointment and anoints Jesus's feet, wiping them with her hair; the house is filled with the fragrance. Judas objects — why not sell this for three hundred denarii and give it to the poor? John notes that Judas was a thief; Jesus says leave her alone, she has kept it for the day of my burial; the poor you always have with you. The triumphal entry (verses 12–19) follows: Jesus enters Jerusalem on a donkey, fulfilling the Zechariah prophecy — fear not, daughter of Zion; behold, your king is coming, sitting on a donkey's colt. Greeks come to Philip and say they wish to see Jesus; this prompts the announcement (verses 23–36): the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified. A grain of wheat must die to bear much fruit. Now is the judgment of this world; now the ruler of this world will be cast out; and I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself. A voice comes from heaven; some hear thunder; some say an angel spoke. The chapter closes with an editorial reflection on the failure of belief among the authorities, who are more afraid of being put out of the synagogue than of not believing.

John 13

The Foot-Washing and Betrayal

Chapter 13 opens the Book of Glory and sets the scene for the Farewell Discourse that occupies chapters 14–17. The supper is underway; the devil has put it into Judas's heart to betray him; Jesus knows that his hour has come and that he is returning to the Father. He gets up from the table, wraps a towel around himself, and begins washing his disciples' feet. Peter objects strongly — you shall never wash my feet. Jesus says unless I wash you, you have no share with me; Peter swings to the other extreme — then wash my hands and head too. Jesus washes all of them except the one who is going to betray him. He returns to the table and offers the interpretation: do you understand what I have done? If I, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you ought to wash one another's feet. I have given you an example, so that you also should do just as I have done. The betrayal announcement follows: one of you will betray me. The disciples look at one another uncertain. Peter gestures to the Beloved Disciple, reclining next to Jesus, to ask who it is. The Beloved Disciple asks; Jesus says it is the one to whom I give this morsel — he dips it and gives it to Judas Iscariot. Satan enters into Judas; Jesus says what you are going to do, do quickly. Judas goes out into the night. The new commandment concludes the chapter: love one another as I have loved you. By this all people will know you are my disciples.

John 14

Many Rooms and the Paraclete

Chapter 14 is the opening movement of the Farewell Discourse and contains several of the most quoted passages in the gospel. Jesus tells his disciples not to be troubled — in my Father's house are many rooms; I go to prepare a place for you; I will come again and take you to myself. Thomas says they do not know where he is going, so how can they know the way? Jesus says I am the way, and the truth, and the life; no one comes to the Father except through me. Philip says show us the Father; Jesus says whoever has seen me has seen the Father; I am in the Father and the Father is in me. Whatever they ask in his name he will do, so that the Father may be glorified in the Son. He will ask the Father, and the Father will send another Helper — the Greek word is paraklētos, variously translated Comforter, Advocate, Counselor — the Spirit of truth, whom the world cannot receive because it neither sees him nor knows him. The disciples will know him because he will be with them and in them. He will teach them all things and bring to remembrance everything Jesus has said. The chapter closes with the gift of peace — not as the world gives — and the promise that the Father is greater than he is and that they should rejoice because he is going to the Father.

John 15

The True Vine and the World's Hatred

Chapter 15 moves through two large movements: the vine and branches discourse (verses 1–17) and the warning about the world's hostility (verses 18–27). The vine metaphor opens the final I am saying: I am the true vine and my Father is the vinedresser. Every branch that does not bear fruit he takes away; every branch that does bear fruit he prunes. Abide in me and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in me. I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing. This is my Father glorified, that you bear much fruit. The new commandment of chapter 13 is restated here with its fullest expression: greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends. You are my friends if you do what I command you. No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you. The second half turns to the world that will hate them, just as it hated Jesus first. The Spirit of truth who comes from the Father will bear witness about Jesus, and the disciples will also bear witness.

John 16

Sorrow Turned to Joy

Chapter 16 continues the Farewell Discourse and moves through three phases. First, the warnings: they will be put out of synagogues; those who kill them will think they are offering service to God; none of this has been told before because Jesus was with them. Now he is going to the Father and they need to know what is coming. Second, the promise of the Advocate: I tell you the truth, it is to your advantage that I go away. If I do not go, the Helper will not come; if I go, I will send him. When he comes he will convict the world concerning sin and righteousness and judgment — concerning sin because they do not believe in Jesus; concerning righteousness because he is going to the Father; concerning judgment because the ruler of this world is judged. The Spirit of truth will guide them into all the truth. Third, the sorrow-to-joy passage: in a little while you will not see me; in a little while you will see me. A woman giving birth has sorrow in the moment of labour; when the child is born she no longer remembers the anguish for joy. So it will be for them. The chapter ends with the disciples claiming understanding and Jesus questioning it: in the world you will have tribulation; but take heart, I have overcome the world.

John 17

The High-Priestly Prayer

Chapter 17 is the high-priestly prayer, unique in the New Testament: Jesus prays at length in the hearing of his disciples on the night of his arrest. The prayer falls into three movements. The first (verses 1–5) is for himself: the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you; you have given him authority over all flesh to give eternal life; this is eternal life, that they know you the only true God and Jesus Christ whom you have sent. The second (verses 6–19) is for the disciples: he has given them the words the Father gave him; they have kept them; they are in the world but not of the world; he consecrates himself for their sake so they may be consecrated in truth. The third (verses 20–26) is for all future believers: that they may all be one, as the Father and the Son are one, so that the world may believe. The petition for unity — that they may be one, even as you are in me and I in you, so that the world may know you have loved them as you have loved me — has been central to ecumenical discussion for centuries. The prayer ends: I made known to them your name, and I will continue to make it known, that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them.

John 18

The Arrest and Trial Before Pilate

Chapter 18 covers the arrest, the hearings before Annas and Caiaphas, Peter's three denials, and the first half of the Roman trial before Pilate. The arrest in the garden (verses 1–12) is distinctive in John: when the soldiers identify themselves as seeking Jesus of Nazareth, he says I am he and they draw back and fall to the ground. He asks them to let his disciples go; Peter cuts off the ear of the servant of the high priest; Jesus tells him to put away the sword. The hearings before Annas and Caiaphas are narrated briefly (verses 13–27), intercut with Peter's three denials, each one timed by John with care. The Roman trial (verses 28–40) is the most developed Pilate scene in any gospel. Pilate goes back and forth between Jesus inside and the crowd outside. He interrogates Jesus about kingship; Jesus says his kingdom is not of this world; his servants would have fought for him otherwise; everyone who is of the truth listens to his voice. Pilate says what is truth? — and then goes back out to the crowd and says he finds no guilt in him. The crowd demands Barabbas instead.

John 19

The Crucifixion and Burial

Chapter 19 covers the final stages of the trial and the crucifixion. Pilate has Jesus scourged and the soldiers crown him with thorns and robe him in purple — behold the man. The chief priests press for crucifixion; Pilate brings Jesus out again and sits on the judgment seat at the place called The Pavement; he says behold your king; they say we have no king but Caesar. Pilate hands him over. The crucifixion is narrated with Johannine restraint: Jesus carries his own cross to Golgotha, the Place of the Skull. He is crucified between two others. The inscription — Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews — is written in Hebrew, Latin, and Greek; when the chief priests ask Pilate to change it, he says what I have written I have written. The soldiers divide his garments, and his tunic — woven in one piece — they leave intact and cast lots for it, fulfilling Psalm 22. Mary the mother of Jesus, her sister, Mary the wife of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene stand at the foot of the cross. Jesus gives his mother into the care of the Beloved Disciple. He says I thirst; they give him sour wine on hyssop; he says it is finished, bows his head, and hands over the Spirit. A soldier pierces his side with a spear and blood and water flow out — John stresses the eyewitness testimony. Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus take and bury the body in a new tomb.

John 20

The Empty Tomb and Thomas

Chapter 20 narrates the resurrection in two stages across two distinct scenes. The first stage (verses 1–18) belongs almost entirely to Mary Magdalene. She comes to the tomb while it is still dark, sees the stone removed, runs to tell Peter and the Beloved Disciple. The two disciples run to the tomb; the Beloved Disciple outruns Peter; both see the linen cloths; the Beloved Disciple sees and believes. They go home. Mary stands weeping at the tomb; looks in and sees two angels; turns and sees a man she takes for the gardener; he asks why she is weeping; she asks where the body is. He says her name — Mary. She says rabbouni. He tells her not to cling to him but to go and tell his brothers. She goes and announces: I have seen the Lord. The evening of that day Jesus appears to the disciples gathered behind locked doors; he shows them his hands and side; he breathes on them and says receive the Holy Spirit. The second stage (verses 24–31) is Thomas. He was absent; he refuses to believe without touching the wounds. Eight days later Jesus appears through locked doors and addresses Thomas: put your finger here and see my hands. Thomas says my Lord and my God — the gospel's highest Christological confession. The chapter closes with the purpose statement of the entire gospel.

John 21

Breakfast on the Shore and Peter Restored

Chapter 21 is often discussed as a possible appendix, since chapter 20 closes with a purpose statement that reads like an ending. But whatever its compositional history, the chapter functions as an essential coda, resolving two narrative threads: Peter's future and the fate of the Beloved Disciple. The scene opens at the Sea of Tiberias: seven disciples decide to go fishing; they catch nothing all night; at dawn a figure on the shore tells them to cast on the right side, and they cannot haul the net in for the weight of fish. The Beloved Disciple says it is the Lord; Peter jumps into the water and swims ashore. Jesus has bread and fish on a fire of coals; he says come and have breakfast; no one dares ask who he is, because they know it is the Lord. He takes the bread and gives it to them, and the fish likewise. The rehabilitation of Peter comes after the meal: three times Jesus asks if Peter loves him; three times Peter says yes; three times Jesus assigns him a pastoral commission — feed my lambs, tend my sheep, feed my sheep. A saying about Peter's death follows. The chapter closes with the witness of the Beloved Disciple: this is the disciple who is bearing witness to these things, and we know that his testimony is true.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

In the Beginning Was the Word

The first eighteen verses of John are one of the most carefully constructed passages in the New Testament, and one of the most consequential. The gospel does not begin in Galilee or Bethlehem; it begins where Genesis 1 begins, with the same opening words.

The Seven Signs

John never calls the miracles miracles. He calls them signs, and the change in vocabulary is theologically loaded. A sign in John is not primarily an act of power; it is an act that points to something beyond itself.

The I Am Sayings

Seven times in this gospel and only here, Jesus says I am — egō eimi in Greek — followed by a metaphor rich in the Hebrew Bible. The phrase itself carries the weight of the divine name revealed at the burning bush.

The Farewell Discourse

After the foot-washing in chapter 13, the gospel slows almost to a standstill. Jesus speaks to his disciples for four chapters — a farewell meditation unique in the New Testament.

Witness and Testimony

John is structured like a legal proceeding. Witness language runs through the entire gospel — the Baptist witnesses, the works witness, the Father witnesses, the Beloved Disciple witnesses. John ends with a claim of eyewitness authority.

Key figures

The 5 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Jesus
The Word Made Flesh

The Johannine Jesus is the most theologically explicit of the four gospels' portraits. He speaks of himself in long discourses rather than parables; he uses the I am formula seven times; he claims pre-existence (before Abraham was, I am) and equality with the Father (I and the Father are one) in language that is unmistakable. He performs seven signs, each carefully framed. He weeps at Lazarus's tomb. He washes his disciples' feet. He prays for them at length on the night of his arrest. He is in command throughout the passion in a way the synoptic Jesus is not — Pilate does not condemn him so much as fail to free him — and Jesus dies saying it is finished, having handed over the Spirit.

The Beloved Disciple
Witness and Implied Author

The unnamed figure introduced at the last supper as the disciple whom Jesus loved. He reclines next to Jesus at the meal, stands with Mary at the foot of the cross, runs with Peter to the empty tomb, recognizes the risen Jesus on the beach before Peter does. The gospel ends with a direct claim: this is the disciple who is bearing witness to these things, and we know that his testimony is true. Tradition identifies him as John the apostle, the son of Zebedee; modern scholarship is divided. Whoever he is, John insists on his presence at the central scenes and on his testimony as the gospel's authoritative source.

Mary Magdalene
First Witness to the Resurrection

John's account of the resurrection morning belongs almost entirely to her. She comes to the tomb while it is still dark, sees the stone removed, runs to tell Peter and the Beloved Disciple, returns weeping, sees two angels in white, turns and sees a man she takes for the gardener. He says her name — Mary — and she says Rabboni. He tells her to go and tell his brothers he is ascending to the Father. She goes and tells the disciples I have seen the Lord. The early church called her, on the strength of this chapter, the apostle to the apostles.

Pilate
Roman Prefect

The Roman prefect of Judea from 26 to 36 CE. The historical Pilate was, by the testimony of Josephus and Philo, a brutal administrator. The Johannine Pilate is more layered. He interrogates Jesus, asks if he is a king, hears the answer about bearing witness to the truth, and replies with the line that has echoed for two thousand years: what is truth? He tries repeatedly to release Jesus, presents him in the purple robe and crown of thorns — behold the man — and finally hands him over. He writes the inscription Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews in three languages and refuses to change it: what I have written I have written.

Thomas
The Doubting Disciple

One of the Twelve, called Didymus (the twin). He appears three times in John. In chapter 11 he says let us also go, that we may die with him — a line of grim, courageous loyalty. In chapter 14 his question — Lord, we do not know where you are going; how can we know the way? — prompts the I am the way saying. In chapter 20 he is absent when the risen Jesus first appears, refuses to believe without touching the wounds, and eight days later addresses Jesus with the gospel's highest Christological confession: my Lord and my God.
