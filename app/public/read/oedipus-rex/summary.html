Oedipus Rex

429 BCE · by Sophocles

The book in brief

Oedipus Rex is the play in which Oedipus, king of Thebes, discovers that he is the murderer of his father Laius and the husband of his mother Iocasta — exactly what an oracle predicted of him at his birth. The play opens with a plague on Thebes. Oedipus has sent his brother-in-law Creon to Delphi to ask the gods what is causing it. The oracle answers: the murderer of the previous king is still living in the city. Oedipus pledges to find the killer, declaring sweeping curses on whoever it turns out to be. The rest of the play is the investigation. Each witness brings him closer to the truth; each piece of evidence narrows the circle. By the end of a single afternoon, Oedipus has identified the murderer, his queen has hanged herself, and he has put out his own eyes with the brooches from her gown.

Sophocles wrote Oedipus Rex around 429 BCE, when he was about sixty-six. It is in eleven scenes, structured as a relentless tightening of the noose around the protagonist. The plague that opens the play was probably a reference to the actual plague that had hit Athens in 430 BCE and killed Pericles. The myth was already well-known to the audience; Sophocles is not telling them what happened, he is showing them how it gets discovered.

It is the most influential single play in Western dramatic literature. Aristotle, in the Poetics, used it as the model of tragic structure — the irony of a man pursuing the truth of a crime that turns out to be his own, the recognition arriving late, the action turning on a single discovery. Twenty-four hundred years later it is still the standard example of dramatic irony in any course on drama, and the figure of Oedipus the unwitting parricide has been absorbed into psychology, philosophy, and ordinary speech in ways no later text has quite matched.

Why it still matters

The Model of Tragic Structure

Aristotle held up Oedipus Rex as the example of how tragedy ought to work — a great man brought down by a single error, the recognition arriving late, the action turning on a discovery, the reversal complete. He uses it more often than any other play in the Poetics. Most subsequent tragedy in the Western tradition is patterned on what Sophocles did here: a single afternoon, a tightening investigation, a recognition scene at the climax, a fall that the audience has been able to see coming from the first line. Hamlet, Phèdre, Othello, Death of a Salesman, Long Day's Journey into Night — none of them is fully comprehensible without this play in the background. It is the form working at its purest.

The Detective Plot

Oedipus is the original detective — a man investigating a murder that turns out to lead, witness by witness, back to himself. The structure is more familiar than its source: the missing piece that turns the case, the inconvenient witness who should not be questioned, the moment the investigator realizes he should have stopped. Every story in which a hero pursues a truth that destroys him is in conversation with this play. Borges argued that all detective fiction is a pale rewriting of it. The Coen brothers' Inside Llewyn Davis and David Fincher's Zodiac both explicitly invoke its architecture. Reading the original is reading the form before it knew it was a form.

Guilt Without Choice

The play asks what a man is guilty of when his worst acts were predicted before his birth and committed before he knew what he was doing. Oedipus killed his father at a crossroads, not knowing who he was. He married his mother, not knowing who she was. He has, in any modern sense of the word, no intention. And yet the city is poisoned and he, by his own oath, has cursed himself. Sophocles refuses to let him off. He is guilty of nothing he chose to do and ruined by all of it. The questions the play raises about fate, consent, and responsibility have not been settled by twenty-four hundred years of philosophy. Bernard Williams returned to them in his last book.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The virtues that destroy him.

Oedipus is not brought down by weakness. He is too smart, too honest, too relentless. Every quality that made him a good king — and a good detective — becomes the instrument of his destruction. Track what his virtues cost him.

Tragic irony and the audience's knowledge.

Sophocles performed this for an audience who already knew the story. Every hopeful line lands differently when you know where it leads. Notice which moments the audience understands and Oedipus does not — and what Sophocles does with that gap.

Fate versus self-determination.

The oracle told Oedipus's parents what he would do. Oedipus's parents acted to prevent it — and caused it. Oedipus acted to escape it — and fulfilled it. Ask whether anyone in this play could have chosen differently, or whether choice itself is the mechanism the gods use.

Pollution and the city.

The Greek concept of miasma — ritual pollution that spreads from a guilty person outward into the land itself — is not metaphor here. It is the operating premise. The city is sick because something unclean is at its centre. Ask what this logic demands of a king.

Key figures

Oedipus
King of Thebes

Brilliant, fearless, and impatient. He arrived in Thebes years earlier as a stranger, solved the riddle of the Sphinx that no one else could solve, and was given the throne and the widowed queen as reward. He is genuinely a good king. The plague is killing his city and he has no patience for delay. Every step of the investigation he insists on, every witness he summons, every threat he makes against the unknown killer turns the noose another notch. He is the detective and the criminal.

Iocasta
His Queen

Wife of Oedipus and, before him, of Laius. She is pragmatic and shrewd, and she begins to understand the truth several scenes before Oedipus does. Her response is to dismiss prophecy as a fraud — she tells how the oracle predicted Laius would be killed by his own son and how it didn't happen, since Laius was killed by robbers at a crossroads. The detail destroys her. She begs Oedipus to stop asking questions. When he refuses, she goes inside and hangs herself.

Creon
The Queen's Brother

Oedipus's brother-in-law and second-in-command in the city. He brings back Apollo's oracle from Delphi at the start and bears the brunt of Oedipus's suspicion in the middle of the play, when Oedipus convinces himself that Creon and Tiresias are conspiring against him. He defends himself with a dignified speech about the costs and benefits of being king, and is eventually pardoned. By the play's last scene he is the one in charge, leading the blinded Oedipus indoors with restraint that is its own kind of dignity.

Tiresias
The Blind Prophet

The aged seer of Thebes, who already knows the answer when Oedipus summons him. He refuses to speak. Oedipus, enraged, accuses him of complicity in the murder. Only then does Tiresias name Oedipus as the killer of Laius — the truth, delivered in anger, which no one in the room believes including the man it is about. He is the play's first warning that the answer is already inside the city. Sophocles uses his blindness as a deliberate counterpoint to Oedipus's clear-eyed pride.

Priest of Zeus
Voice of the City

He opens the play at the altar steps in front of the palace, leading the suppliants of Thebes — children, elders, priests — who have come to beg Oedipus to do something about the plague. His speech sets the entire investigation in motion. He addresses Oedipus as the man who saved the city once before, from the Sphinx, and asks him to save it again. Oedipus, who has already sent Creon to Delphi, takes up the charge with full confidence. The Priest does not appear again.

Chorus of Theban Elders
The City's Conscience

A group of older citizens of Thebes who comment, grieve, and interpret between scenes. They sing a celebrated ode in defense of the prophecies after Iocasta has dismissed them, and another after Oedipus has demanded the truth from the herdsman. They resist the truth longer than anyone except Oedipus himself, because accepting it means accepting what their king has unknowingly done. Their final lines, as Oedipus is led away blind, are the play's last words — a warning against calling any man happy until he is dead.
