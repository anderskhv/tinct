Beowulf

1000 CE · by Anonymous

The book in brief

Beowulf is the story of a Geatish warrior who comes from southern Sweden to the aid of the Danish king Hrothgar, whose great mead-hall Heorot has been emptied every night for twelve years by a monster called Grendel. Beowulf fights Grendel bare-handed and tears off his arm. Grendel's mother comes for revenge; Beowulf descends to her lair under a black mere, kills her with a giant sword he finds in her cave, and returns home laden with gifts. Fifty years pass. A fugitive steals a single cup from a sleeping dragon, and the dragon burns the Geatish countryside. Beowulf, now an old king, goes to face it. His thanes flee. Only one young warrior, Wiglaf, stays. Together they kill the dragon. Beowulf dies of his wounds.

The poem is roughly 3,200 lines of Old English alliterative verse, composed between the 8th and 11th centuries by an unknown poet who had absorbed both pagan heroic tradition and Christian theology. It survives in a single manuscript in the British Library — the Nowell Codex, which nearly burned in the Cotton Library fire of 1731. The edges of some pages are still scorched. The poet sets the action in a half-remembered Scandinavian past two or three centuries before the writing, in a world the audience knew was already gone.

Beowulf is the founding poem of English literature. For most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries it was treated mainly as historical evidence — a quarry for facts about Anglo-Saxon kingship and burial practice. J. R. R. Tolkien's 1936 lecture, The Monsters and the Critics, was the turning point. Tolkien insisted the poem be read as a poem: a meditation on heroism, fate, and what a Christian poet could and could not say about the pagan men he was honoring. It tells a story about a hero defeating monsters and reminds the reader that even the greatest hall will fall, even the strongest king will die, even the most lasting fame will be forgotten.

Why it still matters

The Beginning of English Literature

Beowulf is the earliest substantial poem in the language now called English — though the Old English of the manuscript is barely intelligible without translation; even the alphabet looks foreign. Reading it is reading the soil from which a thousand years of English literature grew. The alliterative line that drives the poem comes back in Langland's Piers Plowman, in the medieval Pearl, in some of Hopkins, in parts of Auden and Heaney. The story of a hero who fights a monster in a hall and a king who falls to a dragon shows up, traceably, in Tolkien's The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings. Seamus Heaney's 1999 translation of Beowulf was a Whitbread Book of the Year. The poem is a thousand years old and a New York Times bestseller within living memory.

A Hero Who Will Lose

Beowulf wins his first two fights — against Grendel and Grendel's mother — and is celebrated in the hall, given gold, sent home to his king. The poem could end there. Instead it skips fifty years and shows Beowulf as an old king himself, facing a dragon that he knows he will not survive. The whole second half of the poem is the slow approach to a death that has been visible from a long way off. The poet argues that heroism is not what a man does at thirty, when he is the strongest man alive and can tear an arm off a monster. Heroism is what he does at sixty, when defeat is certain, his thanes have run, and the hall behind him will fall whether he fights or not.

Pagan and Christian, Both at Once

The poet is Christian. The warriors he writes about lived before Christianity reached northern Europe, and he knows it. He cannot make them Christian without lying about who they were. He cannot endorse their world without contradicting his own faith. The poem holds both at once. He shows Hrothgar's people falling back into idol-worship under the pressure of Grendel's attacks and condemns it. He shows Beowulf burying gold with the dead, an act his own theology calls vain, and lets it stand. The double vision gives the poem its strange depth — a Christian poet honoring the men his religion cannot save, mourning a world he believes was already lost before he ever wrote a word about it.

Reading angles

Four perspectives to bring with you. Pick one as you start, or skip and let the book lead.

The heroic code in action.

Beowulf operates by a precise set of rules: boast before battle, fight with your own hands, give your treasures away, never flinch. Every speech is a declaration of what he owes and what he is owed. This is ethics as honor, and it runs entirely on reputation.

Pagan world, Christian poem.

Beowulf’s world is full of fate, glory, and doom — yet the poet is Christian, and keeps interrupting with references to Cain, God’s creation, and Providence. The two frameworks sit uneasily together. Watching where they collide is one of the poem’s deepest pleasures.

Three monsters, one arc.

Grendel is brute force in the dark. Grendel’s Mother is grief turned to vengeance. The dragon is ancient, patient, and provoked by a single theft. The three fights are not repetitions — they chart Beowulf’s whole life: young strength, maturity, and the death that no hero escapes.

The outsider defines the tribe.

Grendel is exiled from the hall, excluded from the feast, cursed to wander the marshes alone. The poem defines what is human — warmth, community, song, gift-exchange — by showing what Grendel cannot be. He is not evil so much as irreparably outside.

Key figures

Beowulf
Hero of the Geats

Nephew of King Hygelac of the Geats and the strongest man alive. He sails to Denmark with fourteen companions when he hears of Hrothgar's troubles, not because anyone has asked him but because his strength obligates him to use it. He rules the Geats for fifty years after Hygelac's death. He dies killing the dragon, asking with his last breath to see the treasure he has won — and the treasure is buried with him uncounted, useless to anyone.

Grendel
Monster of the Mere

Descended from Cain, exiled by God, cursed to wander the moors and marshes alone. He cannot enter the hall because he cannot bear the sound of human joy — the harp, the laughter, the songs of creation. For twelve years he comes to Heorot at night, takes thirty thanes at a time, and eats them. He has no language. He has no use for the gold piled in the hall. When Beowulf grips his arm, the grip is the first thing in his life he cannot break.

Grendel's Mother
Avenger of the Deep

Older than her son, more dangerous, more deliberate. After Beowulf displays Grendel's torn-off arm in the hall, she comes the next night, takes Hrothgar's closest counselor, and drags the body back to her lair beneath a black mere where the water burns at night. Beowulf descends through the water in his armor and fights her on her own ground, in a hall under the lake. The fight is harder than the first. He kills her with an ancient giant-forged sword that he finds hanging on her wall.

Hrothgar
King of the Danes

The aged Danish king who built Heorot, the greatest mead-hall in the world, when he was at the height of his power. He has watched the hall be emptied every night for twelve years. When Beowulf delivers him from Grendel and from Grendel's mother, Hrothgar gives the long speech sometimes called his sermon — a warning to the young hero about pride, the suddenness with which a king's fortune can turn, and what waits for every man who lives long enough.

Wiglaf
The One Who Stayed

A young Geatish thane, distantly related to Beowulf, who has never been in serious battle. When the dragon's fire drives the rest of Beowulf's chosen warband back into the woods, Wiglaf alone refuses to flee. He charges through the flames to stand beside his lord, takes the second blow that lets Beowulf land the killing one, and holds Beowulf's head as the old king dies. He becomes the poem's image of what loyalty actually requires when loyalty is tested rather than recited.

The Dragon
Guardian of the Hoard

Three hundred years old, coiled over the buried treasure of a dead people in a stone barrow. The treasure was placed there by the last survivor of the people who first owned it, with a long lament for the warriors who would never use it. The dragon found the hoard and slept on it. When a fugitive stumbles into the barrow and steals a single cup, the dragon wakes, finds the loss, and burns the Geatish countryside to ash. Beowulf decides to face it alone.
