1 Kings a guided tour

c. 6th c. BCE · Hebrew Bible · Former Prophets

A dying king is cold, a beautiful girl is brought to warm him, and while David lies in bed his sons are already fighting over the throne. The book that begins with a deathbed succession ends with Elijah hearing God in a sound of thin silence.

The book in brief

1 Kings continues the Deuteronomistic History — the long narrative arc from Joshua through 2 Kings, compiled in something close to its present form during the Babylonian exile. It covers roughly a century and a half: the final days of David, the reign of Solomon, the catastrophic schism that divides the kingdom into Israel and Judah the day after Solomon is buried, and the prophetic career of Elijah in the court of Ahab and Jezebel.

The book divides cleanly into three movements. Chapters 1–11 are Solomon's reign — the wisest king in the world, builder of the temple, author of three thousand proverbs, and the man whose seven hundred wives turn his heart to foreign gods before he dies. Chapters 12–16 are the schism and the rapid succession of kings in the divided kingdoms, narrated in a tight evaluative formula. Chapters 17–22 are the Elijah cycle, one of the most influential prophetic narratives in the Hebrew Bible.

Kings 11 of 22
1 Kings 1

David's deathbed and Solomon's anointing

The chapter opens with David aged, unable to keep warm despite blankets; his servants find a young woman named Abishag to lie beside him. Into this scene of royal decline, Adonijah — David's oldest surviving son — calls a feast at En-rogel and declares himself king. Nathan the prophet alerts Bathsheba: the two of them approach David, and through their combined pressure, David orders Solomon anointed at the Gihon spring immediately. Solomon rides David's mule. The priest Zadok anoints him, the trumpet is blown, the people shout 'God save King Solomon,' and the earth rings. Word reaches Adonijah's feast mid-celebration. His guests scatter. Adonijah, afraid, grabs the horns of the altar for sanctuary. Solomon grants him his life — for now.

1 Kings 2

David's last words; Solomon clears the field

David's deathbed charge to Solomon is double: keep the law of Moses, and also settle the accounts from the past — Joab who shed innocent blood, Shimei who cursed the king during Absalom's revolt, and Barzillai's sons who are to be honoured. David dies after a forty-year reign. Adonijah immediately makes a request through Bathsheba: he wants Abishag, David's last attendant, for a wife. Solomon sees the request as a veiled claim to the throne and has Adonijah executed. Abiathar the priest is exiled to his estate at Anathoth, fulfilling the earlier judgment against the house of Eli. Joab, hearing this, takes sanctuary at the altar; Benaiah kills him at the altar itself. Then Shimei is placed under house arrest in Jerusalem — a careful legal trap. Three years later he leaves Jerusalem to retrieve escaped slaves and Solomon has him killed. The throne is established.

1 Kings 3

The dream at Gibeon; wisdom demonstrated

Solomon makes a marriage alliance with Pharaoh of Egypt and brings his daughter to Jerusalem while the temple is still unbuilt. He sacrifices at Gibeon, the great high place. The Lord appears to him in a dream and says: ask what I shall give thee. Solomon's answer is a model of the petition of good government: he does not ask for riches, honour, long life, or the death of his enemies, but for an understanding heart to judge the people and to discern between good and bad. The Lord grants all of it — wisdom and the things he did not ask for besides. The dream's truth is immediately tested: two prostitutes come before the king with a living child and a dead one, each claiming the living child. Solomon calls for a sword and proposes to divide the child. The true mother cries out. The child is given to her. All Israel hears the judgment and fears the king, knowing that the wisdom of God is in him.

1 Kings 4

Solomon's administration and its scale

The chapter catalogs Solomon's government. Twelve district officers are named, each responsible for provisioning the royal household one month in twelve. The daily provisions are staggering: thirty measures of fine flour, sixty of meal, ten fat oxen, twenty pasture oxen, a hundred sheep, plus deer, gazelles, roebucks, and fattened fowl. Solomon's horses number forty thousand stalls — the text notes this detail with particular care, since Deuteronomy had warned kings against multiplying horses. The peace is described with the famous phrase: Judah and Israel dwell safely, every man under his vine and under his fig tree, from Dan to Beersheba. Solomon's wisdom is then quantified: three thousand proverbs, a thousand and five songs, botanical and zoological knowledge from cedars to hyssop and beasts to fish. Men come from all kings of the earth to hear his wisdom.

1 Kings 5

The Lebanon timber deal and the forced labour

Solomon sends word to Hiram king of Tyre, who had been a friend of David, explaining his intention to build the house of the Lord that David could not build because of surrounding wars. He requests cedar and fir trees from Lebanon, offering wages to Hiram's woodcutters. Hiram responds warmly: he will supply all the cedar and fir Solomon desires, floated in rafts down the coast to wherever Solomon directs. In return Solomon sends Hiram twenty thousand measures of wheat and twenty measures of pure oil annually. The timber negotiations are diplomatic and profitable for both parties. But the chapter also records the labour mechanism: Solomon conscripts thirty thousand men from all Israel, sending them to Lebanon in rotating shifts of ten thousand a month, three months in Lebanon and two at home, under the supervision of Adoniram. Plus seventy thousand burden-bearers and eighty thousand stonecutters in the hill country, with three thousand three hundred overseers.

1 Kings 6

Building the temple: dimensions, wood, and gold

The chapter opens with the precise date: the four hundred and eightieth year after the Exodus, the fourth year of Solomon's reign, the second month. The temple's dimensions are given in cubits — sixty long, twenty wide, thirty high — with an outer porch, side chambers built against its walls, windows of narrow lights, and a flight of stairs. The interior is lined with cedar from floor to ceiling, with carved gourds and open flowers; there is no stone visible inside. The inner sanctuary, the debir or Holy of Holies, is a perfect cube of twenty cubits, lined with gold. Within it stand two cherubim of olive wood, each ten cubits high, their inner wings touching at the centre of the room and their outer wings touching the walls. Every surface of the inner and outer house is carved and overlaid with gold. A word of the Lord comes to Solomon in the middle of this construction: if you walk in my statutes and keep my commandments, I will dwell among the children of Israel. The temple is finished in the eleventh year of Solomon's reign.

1 Kings 7

Solomon's palace and the temple's bronze furnishings

Solomon builds his own palace — the House of the Forest of Lebanon, the Hall of Pillars, the Hall of the Throne — over thirteen years, a detail the narrator places immediately after the temple's seven-year construction, inviting comparison. Then the temple's bronze furnishings: Hiram a craftsman from Tyre, son of a widow of the tribe of Naphtali and a man of brass, is brought to Jerusalem. He casts two bronze pillars, each eighteen cubits high, with elaborate capitals of lily work and networks of chain. The pillars are set at the porch of the temple and named Jachin and Boaz. Hiram also casts the molten sea — a basin of cast bronze ten cubits in diameter, five cubits high, thirty cubits in circumference, resting on twelve cast oxen in four groups of three facing outward. The sea holds two thousand baths of water. Ten lavers of bronze are set on wheeled stands, along with pots, shovels, and basins, all of polished bronze.

1 Kings 8

The ark brought to the temple; Solomon's dedicatory prayer

Solomon assembles all Israel at Jerusalem and has the priests bring the ark of the covenant — containing only the two stone tablets Moses placed in it at Horeb — to the Holy of Holies beneath the cherubim's wings. As the priests withdraw, the cloud of the Lord fills the house so that the priests cannot minister. Solomon then speaks to the assembled congregation and begins the dedicatory prayer. The prayer is a careful, theologically nuanced petition: Solomon acknowledges that the heavens cannot contain the Lord, much less this house; asks that the Lord hear the prayers directed toward this place; and presents seven distinct cases in which prayer might be needed — oath-taking, defeat in war, drought, famine, the prayer of a foreigner, battle, captivity. The prayer is followed by a blessing of the congregation, burnt offerings, and a fourteen-day feast before the people depart.

1 Kings 9

God's second appearance; treaty with Hiram; the labour record

After the dedication, the Lord appears to Solomon a second time, as he had appeared at Gibeon. The word is conditional: if Solomon walks before the Lord as David his father walked, the throne will be established; but if he turns away and serves other gods, the Lord will cut off Israel from the land and cast out this house, and it will become a byword among all peoples. The warning is stated immediately after the dedication — the building is barely finished before the condition on which it stands is articulated. Then the chapter turns to Solomon's other dealings: the treaty with Hiram of Tyre, in which Solomon gives Hiram twenty cities in Galilee in exchange for the timber and gold supplied for the temple — and Hiram is displeased with the cities. Solomon's levy of forced labour is enumerated again: all the surviving Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites are reduced to bond service; the children of Israel are officers, warriors, and rulers, not servants. Pharaoh's daughter is settled in the house Solomon built for her.

1 Kings 10

The Queen of Sheba; the golden throne; the international summit

The Queen of Sheba, hearing of Solomon's fame concerning the name of the Lord, comes to test him with hard questions. She arrives with a great train of camels, spices, gold, and precious stones. Solomon answers all her questions; there is nothing hidden from him that he does not tell her. When she has seen his wisdom, his house, his table, his servants, his cupbearers, his stairway to the temple — there is no more spirit in her. She gives him gifts and pronounces a benediction on his servants, happy to hear such wisdom. Solomon's gold: six hundred and sixty-six talents annually, plus revenues from merchants and traders. He makes two hundred targets of beaten gold, three hundred shields, a great throne of ivory overlaid with gold. Kings of the earth seek his wisdom and bring him yearly tribute. Horses and chariots accumulate: fourteen hundred chariots, twelve thousand horsemen. Deuteronomy had forbidden this. The chapter ends at the height of Solomon's glory.

1 Kings 11

The foreign wives; the heart turned; Ahijah tears the cloak

The narrative delivers its verdict on Solomon: he loved many foreign women, from the nations the Lord had said the children of Israel shall not mix with, for they will surely turn away your heart. He had seven hundred wives of royal birth and three hundred concubines. In his old age his wives turn his heart after other gods — Ashtoreth the goddess of the Sidonians, Milcom the abomination of the Ammonites — and he builds high places for Chemosh and Molech on the mount before Jerusalem. The Lord is angry and raises adversaries: Hadad the Edomite, Rezon of Syria. Then Ahijah the prophet meets Jeroboam, an Ephraimite officer in charge of the forced labour of the house of Joseph, outside Jerusalem. He takes the new garment he is wearing and tears it into twelve pieces, giving Jeroboam ten. The Lord will give ten tribes to Jeroboam. Jeroboam flees to Egypt; Solomon dies after a forty-year reign. His son Rehoboam succeeds him.

1 Kings 12

Rehoboam's folly; the kingdom divides

Rehoboam goes to Shechem where all Israel is assembled to make him king. Jeroboam returns from Egypt and leads the people's petition: your father made our yoke heavy — lighten it, and we will serve you. Rehoboam asks the old men who had served Solomon; they advise him to speak good words to them. He then asks the young men he has grown up with; they advise him to say his little finger shall be thicker than his father's loins, and where his father chastised them with whips, he will chastise them with scorpions. Rehoboam follows the young men's counsel. The northern tribes depart with the cry of Jeroboam's revolt — 'What portion have we in David? To your tents, O Israel!' — and when Rehoboam sends Adoram over the labour levy, they stone him. Rehoboam barely escapes to Jerusalem in his chariot. Jeroboam rules the northern ten tribes. Rehoboam plans a war of reconquest; the prophet Shemaiah forbids it as the Lord's act. Jeroboam sets up two golden calves at Bethel and Dan.

1 Kings 13

The man of God from Judah; the lying prophet

A man of God comes from Judah to Bethel while Jeroboam is standing at the altar to burn incense. He cries out against the altar by a word of the Lord: a son shall be born to the house of David named Josiah, who will burn human bones upon it. He gives a sign: the altar will be rent and the ashes poured out. Jeroboam points at him and says 'Seize him'; the king's hand withers and the altar splits. The king asks the man of God to restore his hand and offers him a meal. The man of God declines: even if given half the king's house he would not eat or drink in this place, for so the Lord commanded him. He returns by a different way. An old prophet from Bethel hears the account, chases after him, and lies to him: an angel told me to bring you back. The man of God returns and eats. On the road home a lion kills him. The old prophet buries him and mourns. The sign against the altar is recorded. Jeroboam does not return from his evil way.

1 Kings 14

Ahijah's oracle; the deaths of Jeroboam's son and Rehoboam

Jeroboam's son Abijah falls ill. Jeroboam sends his wife disguised to the prophet Ahijah at Shiloh, who had originally told him he would be king. The Lord tells Ahijah she is coming. Though Ahijah is old and his eyes dim, he greets her at the door as Jeroboam's wife and gives the oracle: because Jeroboam has done evil above all who were before him, making other gods and images and provoking the Lord, the Lord will bring evil on the house of Jeroboam, cutting off every male of his line. Dogs will eat those who die in the city; birds will eat those in the field. The child will die when she returns home, and he alone of Jeroboam's house will be buried, for in him was found something good toward the Lord. It happens as Ahijah said. Then the chapter turns to Rehoboam of Judah: he permits high places and pillars and Asherah poles and sodomites in the land. Egypt's Shishak comes and takes the treasures of the temple and the palace, and Rehoboam makes shields of bronze to replace the gold ones Solomon made. Both kings die. Rehoboam's son Abijam succeeds him.

1 Kings 15

Abijam and Asa of Judah; Nadab and Baasha of Israel

Abijam reigns in Judah for three years and walks in the sins of his father; his heart is not perfect with the Lord, but the Lord keeps the lamp of David burning in Jerusalem for David's sake. His son Asa reigns for forty-one years and does that which is right in the eyes of the Lord, like David. Asa removes the male cult prostitutes, puts away the idols his fathers had made. He deposes his mother Maacah from the position of queen mother because she made a disgusting image for Asherah; he cuts it down and burns it at the brook Kidron. The high places, however, are not removed — Asa's heart is perfect with the Lord all his days, but the high places remain, a qualification that will follow many Judahite kings. He makes a treaty with Ben-hadad of Syria against Baasha of Israel, sending temple treasures as the inducement. Meanwhile in Israel: Nadab son of Jeroboam reigns two years and is killed by Baasha, who kills all the house of Jeroboam — fulfilling Ahijah's oracle.

1 Kings 16

Baasha to Omri; Ahab's accession; Hiel rebuilds Jericho

The prophet Jehu speaks a word against Baasha: his house will fall as Jeroboam's fell, for he killed Jeroboam's house but walked in Jeroboam's sins. Baasha's son Elah reigns two years in Tirzah and is killed by Zimri, a commander of half the chariots, while drunk in the house of Arza. Zimri kills all the house of Baasha, fulfilling Jehu's oracle. Zimri reigns seven days: the people make Omri, commander of the army, king instead, and Omri besieges Tirzah. Zimri goes into the citadel, burns the king's palace around himself, and dies. A civil war between the followers of Tibni and the followers of Omri is resolved when Tibni dies. Omri reigns twelve years, buys the hill of Samaria from Shemer for two talents of silver, builds the city on it, and names it Samaria after Shemer. He does evil above all before him. His son Ahab succeeds and does evil above Omri, marries Jezebel daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, serves Baal, builds a temple of Baal in Samaria, and makes an Asherah. Hiel of Bethel rebuilds Jericho, losing his eldest and youngest sons as Joshua had said.

1 Kings 17

Elijah appears; the drought; the widow of Zarephath

Elijah the Tishbite of Gilead appears before Ahab and announces there will be no dew or rain except by his word. The Lord then directs him to the brook Cherith east of the Jordan, where ravens bring him bread and flesh morning and evening and he drinks from the brook. The brook dries up in the drought. The Lord sends him to Zarephath, a city in Sidon — Jezebel's home territory — to a widow he has commanded to sustain him. He finds her gathering sticks for a last meal from the last handful of meal and the last drop of oil before she and her son die. He asks her to make him a small cake first, and promises that the barrel of meal will not waste and the cruse of oil will not fail until the Lord sends rain. She does it; the miracle is ongoing through the drought. Then her son dies. She brings the dead child to Elijah in reproach. He takes the child to his upper room, lies upon him three times, and prays. The Lord hears; the child revives. The widow says: 'Now I know that thou art a man of God, and that the word of the Lord in thy mouth is truth.'

1 Kings 18

Mount Carmel: the test by fire

The Lord tells Elijah to show himself to Ahab, for rain is coming. Elijah meets Obadiah, the royal steward who has been hiding a hundred prophets of the Lord in two caves during Jezebel's persecution, and instructs him to arrange a meeting with Ahab. Ahab meets him: 'Art thou he that troubleth Israel?' Elijah answers: 'I have not troubled Israel; thou hast.' He calls all Israel and all the prophets of Baal and Asherah to Carmel. The trial: each side prepares a bull on wood but sets no fire. The god that answers by fire is God. The Baal prophets cry from morning to noon, cut themselves with lancets; nothing answers. Elijah repairs the altar of the Lord with twelve stones, digs a trench, lays the wood and the bullock, pours so much water over it that the trench fills. He prays briefly. Fire falls and consumes the burnt sacrifice, the wood, the stones, the dust, the water. The people fall on their faces. Elijah kills all the prophets of Baal at the brook Kishon. He prays for rain seven times, sends his servant to look toward the sea; a cloud the size of a man's hand appears; by the time Ahab's chariot reaches Jezreel, the rain has fallen.

1 Kings 19

Flight from Jezebel; Horeb; the still small voice

Jezebel sends Elijah a message: tomorrow your life will be as the life of one of the prophets you killed. Elijah runs to Beersheba, leaves his servant, goes a day into the wilderness, sits under a juniper tree, and asks to die — 'It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life, for I am not better than my fathers.' He sleeps. An angel touches him and feeds him. He sleeps again; the angel feeds him again for the journey. He walks forty days and forty nights to Horeb the mount of God and lodges in a cave. The Lord asks: what are you doing here, Elijah? He answers in the language of prophetic exhaustion. He is told to stand at the entrance of the cave. A great wind rends the rocks; God is not in the wind. An earthquake; God is not in the earthquake. A fire; God is not in the fire. After the fire, qol demamah daqqah — a sound of thin silence. Elijah wraps his face and stands at the cave's entrance. The voice asks again: what are you doing here? He gives the same speech. He is told to go anoint Hazael, Jehu, and Elisha. Seven thousand remain in Israel who have not bowed to Baal. He finds Elisha plowing and throws his mantle on him.

1 Kings 20

The Syrian wars; Ahab spares Ben-hadad

Ben-hadad of Syria with thirty-two allied kings besieges Samaria and sends messengers demanding Ahab's silver, gold, wives, and children. Ahab accepts the first demand but not the second, which extends to searching his house and his servants' houses. Ben-hadad threatens to destroy Samaria. A prophet comes to Ahab promising victory so that he will know the Lord is God. With a small force of district officers, Ahab routs the Syrians in the first battle. A second campaign in the spring: the Syrians come to Aphek in the plain, believing the Israelite God is a god of hills, not of valleys. Another prophetic promise. Israel defeats a hundred thousand Syrian footmen in a day, twenty-seven thousand more die when the wall of Aphek falls. Ben-hadad hides in an inner chamber. His servants dress in sackcloth and surrender. Ahab makes a treaty with Ben-hadad and releases him. A prophet disguised as a wounded soldier then condemns Ahab: because you have let go out of your hand a man whom I appointed to utter destruction, your life shall go for his life.

1 Kings 21

The vineyard of Naboth; Elijah's indictment

Naboth the Jezreelite has a vineyard beside Ahab's palace at Jezreel. Ahab wants it for a kitchen garden and offers to buy it or exchange it for a better vineyard. Naboth refuses: the Lord forbids him to give his ancestral inheritance. Ahab returns home and lies on his bed, turning away his face, refusing to eat. Jezebel asks why he is sulking, hears the reason, and says: arise, eat bread — I will give you the vineyard. She writes letters in Ahab's name with his seal to the elders of Jezreel, ordering a fast, seating Naboth at the head of the people, and procuring two false witnesses to accuse him of blaspheming God and the king. Naboth is stoned. Jezebel tells Ahab: arise, take possession. As Ahab goes down to take possession, Elijah meets him. The indictment is in two sentences: hast thou killed and also taken possession? In the place where the dogs licked the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick thy blood, even thine. Ahab tears his clothes, fasts, walks softly. The Lord postpones the punishment to the next generation for Ahab's humbling of himself.

1 Kings 22

The battle of Ramoth-gilead; Ahab's death

Jehoshaphat of Judah visits Ahab in Samaria. Ahab wants to recapture Ramoth-gilead and asks Jehoshaphat to join him. Jehoshaphat asks for a word of the Lord. Four hundred prophets of Israel say: go up, the Lord will deliver it. Jehoshaphat asks for a prophet of the Lord. Micaiah son of Imlah is sent for, predicted to prophesy evil. He initially echoes the four hundred, then — pressed — gives the true oracle: he saw Israel scattered on the hills like sheep without a shepherd, and the Lord on his throne with a lying spirit entering all four hundred prophets' mouths, to entice Ahab to go up to Ramoth-gilead and fall. Micaiah is imprisoned. Ahab and Jehoshaphat go. Ahab disguises himself; Jehoshaphat is in his robes. Syrian charioteers chase Jehoshaphat, realise he is not the king of Israel, turn back. A man draws a bow at random and strikes Ahab between the joints of his armour. He is propped up in his chariot through the day and dies at evening. The chariot is washed in the pool of Samaria; dogs lick the blood. The oracle is fulfilled. His son Ahaziah succeeds him.

Key themes

5 threads that hold the book together. Full analysis →

Solomon's wisdom and the cost of the temple

The Solomon of chapters 3 to 10 is the wisest king in the world. He asks for an understanding heart at Gibeon and is given that and everything else he did not ask for. By chapter 11 the foreign wives have turned his heart, and the prophet Ahijah tears a new cloak into twelve pieces.

The schism and the formula

When the northern tribes secede under Jeroboam in chapter 12, the narrative gear changes. From this point the historian works in a tightly disciplined formula, evaluating each king by a single criterion and compressing two centuries of history into a sequence of brief, almost identical paragraphs.

Elijah on Carmel

Chapter 17 introduces Elijah the Tishbite without preamble — no genealogy, no court of origin. He simply appears in Ahab's throne room and announces a drought. Two chapters later he stands alone on Carmel against four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal and calls down fire.

The still small voice

Immediately after Carmel, Jezebel sends a death threat and Elijah runs. He sits under a juniper tree and asks to die. He travels forty days to Horeb. There he hears God not in wind, not in earthquake, not in fire — but in a sound of thin silence.

The vineyard of Naboth

Ahab the king wants his neighbour's vineyard for a kitchen garden. Naboth refuses — the land is his fathers' inheritance. Ahab comes home heavy and displeased and lies on his bed and turns his face away. Jezebel takes care of it. Elijah walks into the vineyard with an indictment.

Key figures

The 6 who matter most. More in the full character guide.

Solomon
King of united Israel

David's son by Bathsheba. Anointed at Gihon while Adonijah is still feasting. Asks for an understanding heart at Gibeon. Builds the temple over seven years. Speaks three thousand proverbs. Marries seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines; by chapter 11 his heart is turned. Dies after a forty-year reign leaving a kingdom that splits the next day.

Elijah
Prophet of the North

Appears in chapter 17 without genealogy — Elijah the Tishbite, of the inhabitants of Gilead. Announces a three-year drought to Ahab. Confronts the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel and calls down fire. Runs from Jezebel's death threat, hears God in a sound of thin silence at Horeb. Confronts Ahab in Naboth's vineyard. The model of the prophet who works outside the apparatus of the state.

Ahab
King of Israel

Son of Omri, king in Samaria for twenty-two years. Marries Jezebel of Sidon, builds the temple of Baal in Samaria. Sulks when Naboth refuses to sell his vineyard. Dies disguised in his chariot at Ramoth-gilead — struck by an arrow shot at random; the dogs lick his blood at the pool of Samaria, as Elijah had said.

Jezebel
Queen of Israel

Daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians. Brings her own prophets to Samaria and persecutes the prophets of the Lord. Writes the letters that procure Naboth's death, sealing them with the king's seal. The political will Ahab lacks. Her daughter Athaliah marries into the house of Judah; the Davidic line will nearly end in her purges in 2 Kings 11.

Jeroboam
First king of the northern kingdom

Solomon's official over the labour gangs. Ahijah the prophet tears a cloak into twelve pieces and gives him ten. After Solomon's death, Rehoboam's folly drives the northern tribes to him. He sets up golden calves at Bethel and Dan — "these be thy gods, O Israel" — and becomes the template against whom every subsequent northern king is measured: "in the sin of Jeroboam who made Israel to sin."

The Queen of Sheba
Foreign monarch

Comes to Jerusalem with a great train and camels bearing spices and gold and precious stones to test Solomon with hard questions. He answers all of them. When she has seen his wisdom, his table, his palace, his ascent to the temple — there was no more spirit in her. The chapter is the high point of Solomon's international prestige, and the moment after which the narrative will begin to count the cost.
