# Let the classics dye you through

The warmest, most accurate naming story for Tinct is that **"tinct" means *dyed***. It comes from the Latin *tinctus*, the past participle of *tingere*, "to dip, soak, dye". The best-known writer to use the image is Marcus Aurelius, whose *Meditations* is already in the Tinct library: "**the soul is dyed by the thoughts**" (George Long, 1862). In the first English translation (Casaubon, 1634) the same line contains the brand word itself: the soul "**doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies, and imaginations**". Montaigne, also in the library, applies the image directly to learning. Knowledge should not just "tincture" the mind, he says, but "**give it a thorough and perfect dye**". These sources answer the founder's worry that the name sounds cold. The clinical sense (pharmacy tinctures, CBD drops) is a narrow modern meaning. The older meanings are colour, dyed cloth, and the alchemist's drop that turns metal to gold, and all of them are warm and about transformation. A short "Why Tinct?" story built on dye and the *Meditations*, with a light touch of alchemy through Cleopatra's "with his tinct gilded thee", fits the product: you dip into a great book, it soaks in, and you come out a different colour.

## The word's root is a story about immersion, not medicine

The Latin verb *tingo* first meant "to wet, moisten, bathe". From there it came to mean "to soak in colour, to dye". It also had a figurative sense, being "imbued with" something, and in late Latin it could mean "to baptize" ([Lewis & Short](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=tingo)). The modern reconstruction traces it to an ancient root *\*teng-*, "to make wet" ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/teng-)). In Middle English, "tincture" already covered **dye, colour, medicinal salve, and the alchemical power to turn base metal into gold**, all at once ([Wiktionary, citing the Middle English Dictionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tincture)). The sequence of meanings, wet, then dip, then dye, then imbue, works as a ready-made brand story.

The same word family has a second detail useful to a reading app. *Tint*, *tinge* and *taint* all come from *tinctus*. So do the German *Tinte* and the Spanish *tinta*, both meaning **ink** ([Wiktionary: tint](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tint)). Heraldry still calls a coat of arms' colours its "tinctures" ([Dictionary.com](https://dictionary.com/browse/tincture)), which fits the library's "Houses" taxonomy. There is also practical evidence for the founder's worry. Search engines fall back from "Tinct" to "Tint", and the only exact-name app found is a small paid colour-puzzle game from Spain ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.piqture.tinct&hl=en)). A spoken line such as "Tinct, as in tincture: dyed" teaches the spelling and the story together. No trademark search has been done.

## Three stories rank above the rest

The research turned up about a dozen usable passages. Three combine warmth, verified wording, a link to reading or transformation, and a home in the Tinct library.

| Rank | Story | Warmth | Accuracy | Fit to reading/transformation | In library? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.16: the soul is dyed by its thoughts | High: practical, hopeful | Verified word for word in two public-domain translations | Strong, though Marcus means *thoughts*, not books | Yes |
| 2 | Montaigne, "Of Pedantry": learning must give "a thorough and perfect dye" | High, if a little demanding | Verified in Cotton's translation | Strongest: it is literally about learning | Yes |
| 3 | Shakespeare, *Antony and Cleopatra* 1.5: "that great medicine hath / With his tinct gilded thee" | Highest: loving and playful | Verified in Gutenberg and Folger | Good: contact with greatness gilds you | Yes |

**Marcus Aurelius wins because it is the only quote that is both famous and contains the brand word.** Long's 1862 version reads: "Such as are thy habitual thoughts, such also will be the character of thy mind; for the soul is dyed by the thoughts. Dye it then with a continuous series of such thoughts" ([Gutenberg #15877](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)). Casaubon's 1634 version, the first in English, reads: "For the soul doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies, and imaginations. **Dye it therefore and thoroughly soak it**" ([Gutenberg #2680](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt)). One honest caveat applies. Marcus is writing about habitual thoughts, not reading. The step from "what you read becomes what you think" to "you become what you read" is Tinct's own claim and should be worded that way.

**Montaigne is the best fit for the thesis.** Here is Cotton's translation: "We are not to tie learning to the soul, but to work and incorporate them together: **not to tincture it only, but to give it a thorough and perfect dye**" ([Gutenberg #3600](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3600/pg3600.txt)). It describes what Tinct offers: the original text, a modern English version and a companion, so the book soaks in instead of washing off. The rest of the sentence is sterner ("it were without question better to let it alone"), so quote only the first half.

**Cleopatra's line brings in the alchemy without the fraud.** Alexas arrives from Antony, and Cleopatra says that Antony, "that great medicine" (the alchemists' elixir), has "with his tinct gilded thee" ([Folger, A&C 1.5](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/antony-and-cleopatra/read/1/5/)). Cambridge's Slow Shakespeare commentary reads it the same way: Antony as the philosopher's stone, gilding whatever he touches ([Cambridge English Faculty](https://www.english.cam.ac.uk/research/slow-shakespeare/?p=129)). Spending time near greatness gilds you, which is a warm way to describe reading the classics.

Several runners-up are worth keeping for secondary copy. George Herbert's "The Elixir" (1633) is the most beautiful use of the word: "Nothing can be so mean, / Which with his tincture (for thy sake) / Will not grow bright and clean" ([CCEL](https://www.ccel.org/h/herbert/temple/Elixir.html)). It is devotional and not in the library. Seneca's Letter 71 compares philosophy to wool that must be "soaked and steeped… many times" until it has "not merely coloured but thoroughly permeated the soul" ([Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_71)). That is a good description of rereading, but Seneca is not on the stated library list. Milton's *Paradise Lost* Book VII, which is in the library, describes lesser stars that "By tincture or reflection… augment / Their small peculiar" by drawing on the sun ([Gutenberg #26](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26/pg26.txt)). That is a fitting image for a modern edition and an AI companion that draw their light from the original.

## Copy that is ready to use, built only from verified wording

**Recommended one-liner:** *Tinct comes from the Latin* tinctus, *"dyed". As Marcus Aurelius put it, "the soul is dyed by the thoughts."*

**About-page version (5 sentences):**

> *Tinct* is an old English word for colour, from the Latin *tinctus*: dipped, soaked, dyed. In the first English translation of Marcus Aurelius (1634), the soul "doth as it were receive its tincture from the fancies, and imaginations"; in George Long's plainer words, "the soul is dyed by the thoughts." Montaigne asked the same of learning: "not to tincture it only, but to give it a thorough and perfect dye." The old alchemists used the word for the drop that turned base metal to gold, which is why Shakespeare's Cleopatra says Antony, "that great medicine," has "with his tinct gilded" the man who brings his message. Tinct is a place to steep in the great books, original and modern side by side, until their colour stays with you.

Every quoted phrase above was checked word for word against a public-domain primary text (Gutenberg #2680, #15877, #3600, #100 and Folger). The surrounding sentences are Tinct's own framing. Each quote can link straight to its passage: *Meditations* Book 5 §16 (§XV in Casaubon's numbering), Montaigne Book I, "Of Pedantry" (I.24 in Cotton, I.25 in modern numbering), and *Antony and Cleopatra* 1.5.

Shakespeare sources are [Gutenberg #100](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/100/pg100.txt) and Folger. "Grain" was the medieval name for kermes, the prized scarlet dye, so cloth "dyed in grain" would not wash out. That is where the word "ingrained" comes from ([Wikipedia: Kermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermes_(dye))).

## Stories and quotes to keep off the About page

**Hamlet 3.4 is the most famous use of "tinct", and it is about guilt.** Gertrude sees "black and grained spots" on her soul. *Julius Caesar* 2.2 is about dipping cloths in Caesar's blood on the morning of his murder. "Heaven's own tinct" in *Cymbeline* is spoken by a man creeping into a sleeping woman's bedroom. The *Winter's Tale* line comes as Paulina announces a queen's death. Sonnet 111's "dyer's hand" is rueful and sits beside "my name receives a brand", which is the wrong word for a brand story. Marcus Aurelius 6.30, "not dyed with this dye", is a warning about being corrupted. Donne's "poysonous tincture of Originall sinne" is dark too ([Gutenberg #48688](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48688/pg48688.txt)).

**Alchemy carries a fraud connotation, so use it as a single image and never as a promise.** Ben Jonson's *The Alchemist* (1610) is the richest source of "tincture" and "projection" lines, but the whole play satirises con men ([Gutenberg #4081](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4081/pg4081.txt)). Period writers catalogued fake projectors' tricks, such as crucibles with hidden gold ([Wikipedia: Projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_(alchemy))). Avoid "turn lead into gold" claims. Avoid the van Helmont and Helvetius transmutation accounts too, which survive only in secondary translations. George Ripley's 1471 dyer stanza is accurate and lovely ([Alchemy Web Site](https://alchemywebsite.com/tcbcompound.html)), but its Middle English spelling is too hard for an About page.

**Several popular quotes or claims have unverified wording.** The well-known line "Your soul takes on the color of your thoughts" is Gregory Hays's copyrighted 2002 translation and is attested only on quote sites. "The soul becomes dyed with the colour of its thoughts" was found in no checked translation. Use Long or Casaubon instead. Etymonline's dates (tincture c. 1400, tinct c. 1600) come only from search summaries. They also conflict with Spenser's printed "tinct" of 1579, so do not print a "first used in" year. The Voltaire line behind Kindle's naming, Paracelsus's *Tincture of the Philosophers*, any Newton "tincture" quote, Herbert's manuscript stanzas and the English wording of Tertullian's *De Baptismo* (where *tinguere* means "to baptize") were all left unverified. The baptism angle is also more religious than a secular brand needs. Finally, two tempting word links should not be presented as fact. "Tinct" and "think" come from *different* ancient roots that happen to look alike ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/teng-)). "Dunk" is only *possibly* a distant cousin ([Wiktionary: dunk](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/dunk)).

## Conclusion

The cold reading of the name comes from one modern sense of the word. The other meanings make it one of the better literary brand names available. Kindle's naming worked because a concrete image ("reading lights you up") had a checkable literary pedigree ([How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/870829/why-is-it-called-an-amazon-kindle-ereader/)). Tinct has the same structure: the image is "reading dyes you". Its pedigree runs through three books users can open in the app with one tap, so the naming story doubles as a route into the library. The practical next step is a "Why Tinct?" page that links each quote to its passage in the reader, plus a spoken "as in tincture" to fix the spelling. Run a formal trademark search before investing heavily in the name.
