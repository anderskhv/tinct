# Build knowledge ramps, not reading levels

Adults find classics like *Ulysses*, Plato and Kant hard mainly because the books assume knowledge and reading conventions the reader lacks, not because the sentences are hard. The best-supported practices follow from that. First, build the specific background a book assumes. Second, frame each book and chapter before reading, not after. Third, make the reader do the thinking through recall, self-explanation and interpretive discussion, instead of handing them summaries. Fourth, let readers stretch into harder books with support rather than holding them at a "matched" level. Standard readability formulas cannot see this kind of difficulty. Lexile puts *Ulysses* (about 1050L) within edition-to-edition noise of Chopin's *The Awakening* (960–1030L), so the gap Tinct's team senses is real but invisible to the usual tools. A multi-dimensional rubric can capture it: structure, knowledge demands, levels of meaning, language and argument density, scored per chapter, with large language models (LLMs) helping at scale. The evidence base has one major weakness. Almost every controlled study involves schoolchildren, undergraduates or second-language learners reading short passages for a test. **No controlled study was found of adults reading book-length literary or philosophical classics by choice**, so every recommendation below is an extrapolation, and this report says where. For Tinct's reading paths, that points to a prerequisite graph of books, layered with difficulty and support, and a reader model built from what readers tell Tinct and what they have finished. Any model that infers level from behaviour is a new category of personal data. It needs Anders's approval and an update to `app/public/privacy.html` before it ships.

## Knowledge, not general reading skill, gates comprehension

Background knowledge has the strongest and most consistent evidence of any factor reviewed. In the classic "baseball study", **poor readers who knew baseball out-recalled good readers who did not**, and low-knowledge students did poorly whatever their reading ability ([Wikipedia: Baseball Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_Study)). That study involved only 64 seventh and eighth graders and is often over-cited ([Wexler](https://nataliewexler.substack.com/p/clearing-up-misconceptions-about-a44)). A much larger study of **3,534 high-school students** found a knowledge threshold at about **59% correct** on a topic test. Below it, knowledge barely predicted comprehension (β = 0.18); above it, the relationship was strong (β = 0.81). Specific key terms predicted which side of the threshold a student fell on ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/knowledge-reading-comprehension.html); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797619862276)). Over years, a knowledge-rich curriculum produced reading gains worth about **16 percentile points by grade 6** in a lottery-based RCT of Core Knowledge charter schools (ITT 0.241, TOT 0.473) ([Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/ednext_XIV_2_grissmer_et_al.pdf)). All three studies are **K-12**, and the last is a multi-year curriculum effect that says nothing about a short primer. Willingham explains why the mechanism should still carry over to adults: writers leave out what they assume readers already know, so comprehension depends "far more" on topic knowledge than on practised comprehension skill ([AFT](https://www.aft.org/ae/spring2006); [Keys to Literacy](https://keystoliteracy.com/blog/background-knowledge-and-reading-comprehension/)). Classics assume an unusually large amount: Homer, scripture, liturgy, earlier philosophy.

The adult evidence points the same way but is lab-scale. In Bransford and Johnson's experiment, adults given a deliberately vague passage understood and recalled it better **only when told the topic before reading**; context supplied afterwards did not help. A 52-adult replication found the same direction of effect ([Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125955129.pdf)). Purpose works like a lens. Undergraduates who read a story "as a burglar" or "as a home buyer" encoded and later retrieved information relevant to that perspective ([ERIC ED142974](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED142974.pdf)). Relevance instructions improved learning without adding reading time ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-general-relevance-instructions-on-shallow-and-deepe/)). For Tinct, two caveats follow. A reading angle raises recall of angle-relevant material and can lower attention to everything else. And in adult second-language readers, **relevance effects were larger for easy texts than for hard ones** ([Reading in a Foreign Language](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstream/10125/67412/1/34_1_10125-67412.pdf)), so on Kant or *Ulysses* a goal alone is not enough without knowledge support.

Structural previews help modestly. Advance organizers have small average effects with wide variation, "generally positive" in an updated meta-analysis ([Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)). Text-structure instruction in grades 4–6 showed positive immediate effects but **no advantage at delayed post-test** ([Utrecht](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/408174)). Some of the evidence here is from adults. In Bonnie Meyer's studies, about nine hours of structure-strategy training **raised recall of main ideas in both younger and older adults** ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/effects-of-structure-strategy-training-and-signaling-on-recall-of/)). Older adults also recalled more when key ideas sat higher in a text's structure ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/using-text-structure-to-improve-older-adults-recall-of-important-/)). This supports chapter-level orientation for argumentative works like Plato and Kant. It has not been tested on experimental narrative like *Ulysses*, and because the effects fade, the orientation has to recur chapter by chapter rather than appear once at onboarding.

## A strong why helps, but early success sustains reading

Motivation and comprehension are reliably but modestly linked. A meta-analysis of 132 articles found **r = .22** between reading motivation and achievement. Longitudinally, **earlier achievement predicted later motivation more strongly than motivation predicted achievement** ([UT Austin](https://education.utexas.edu/news/2020/04/08/review-of-education-research-features-article-by-toste-and-peng)). Interventions that raise motivation improved comprehension by **d = 0.27**, and those that triggered interest worked best. But their effect on intrinsic motivation **fell from d = 0.40 immediately to a non-significant d = 0.19 at delayed testing** ([van der Sande et al. 2023](https://pure.eur.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/85465579/Effectiveness_of_Interventions_that_Foster_Reading_Motivation.pdf)). Interest in the specific text (situational interest) appears to relate to learning slightly more than general topic interest, about r ≈ .33 against .27. Those figures come only from a secondary citation of Schiefele's review ([Trames 2010](https://kirj.ee/public/trames_pdf/2010/issue_1/trames-2010-1-54-70.pdf)). Everything in this paragraph comes from **schoolchildren**, apart from the Schiefele figures, which mix lab studies of students and undergraduates. In the one adult study that compared the two directly, **structure training improved recall and interest training showed no comparable benefit** ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/effects-of-structure-strategy-training-and-signaling-on-recall-of/)).

So a "strong why" is worth having, but it decays, and the more durable motivator is the experience of understanding. A vivid, text-specific hook before reading fits the interest evidence. Persistence, though, is better protected by making the first chapters comprehensible than by a stronger pitch. Expertise research adds a reason to ramp gradually: **pleasure rises with literary expertise**. PhD readers enjoyed difficult poems more than novices did, and older students more than younger ones ([OnFiction/Peskin](https://www.onfiction.ca/2008/11/research-bulletin-by-joan-peskin.html)).

There is essentially **no peer-reviewed research on adults abandoning hard books**. The available signals are anecdotal. Ellenberg's "Hawking Index", which he called "not remotely scientific", places the average top Kindle highlight in Piketty's *Capital* at 2.4% of the way through and in *Infinite Jest* at 6.4% ([Wikipedia: Hawking Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawking_Index)). A 2013 Goodreads survey found about **28% of abandoners quit before page 100** ([IOL](https://iol.co.za/entertainment/celebrity-news/2013-07-12-the-books-we-just-cant-finish/)). Weak as it is, this evidence agrees with the motivation research: the opening of a hard book is where readers are lost, and that is where support should be concentrated.

## Lexile cannot see the gap between Chopin and Joyce

Traditional readability formulas such as Flesch-Kincaid, Lexile and ATOS rest on two proxies: sentence length and word familiarity ([T.E.R.A., ASU](https://soletlab.asu.edu/t-e-r-a/)). They rank school texts reasonably well, but they badly misrank literature. ***The Grapes of Wrath* scores 680L**, and by Lexile alone *Jane Eyre*, *Crime and Punishment* and *A Farewell to Arms* would fall in grades 2–5 ([WOW Literacy](https://wowlit.org/blog/2018/05/14/limitations-of-lexile-levels-for-global-literature); [The New Republic](https://newrepublic.com/article/115393/common-core-standards-make-mockery-novels-complexity)). The team's question can be answered directly. Library catalogues list ***Ulysses* at 1050L** ([Marmot catalog](https://bemis2.marmot.org/Record/.b20369104)) and ***The Awakening* at 960L–1030L depending on edition** ([Wake County catalog](https://catalog.wake.gov/Record/39911)). The gap between the two books is smaller than the gap between editions of one of them. Lexile has no measure for what makes Joyce hard: a style that changes every episode, stream of consciousness, dense allusion and heavy knowledge demands.

The gap can be measured, but only along other dimensions. Coh-Metrix research identifies five factors that explain most variation in text difficulty: **narrativity, syntactic simplicity, word concreteness, referential cohesion and deep cohesion** ([T.E.R.A.](https://soletlab.asu.edu/t-e-r-a/)). The Common Core model adds qualitative judgement on four continua: **levels of meaning, structure, language conventionality and knowledge demands**. Complex structure means shifts in chronology and point of view, and knowledge demands rise with allusion to other texts ([Rhode Island DOE](https://ride.ri.gov:443/Portals/0/Uploads/Documents/Common-Core/Considering-Text-Complexity-Qs-to-Guide-Discussion.pdf)). Its authors called those qualitative dimensions tentative and said better measures were "urgently needed" ([Hiebert](https://textproject.org/wp-content/uploads/powerpoints/Hiebert_Scholastic-Book-Summit-2012-10-22_Keynote-Inside-Text-Complexity.pdf)). Machine methods help. In the CLEAR corpus, about 4,700 excerpts were ranked by teachers' pairwise judgements, and traditional formulas reached only **r ≈ .50–.58** with those rankings ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10027808)). Zero-shot GPT-4 Turbo estimates reached **r = .76** ([arXiv 2410.14028](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14028)). Both results come from **excerpts of 140–200 words**, not whole novels. No study was found that applies Coh-Metrix or an LLM to *Ulysses*.

| Dimension | *The Awakening* | *Ulysses* | Can a formula see it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lexile (measured, catalogue) | 960–1030L | 1050L | Yes, and it shows almost no gap |
| Structure (chronology, point of view) | Linear, third person | Style and viewpoint shift by episode | No |
| Knowledge demands / allusion | Moderate (1890s Creole society) | Very high (Homer, *Hamlet*, *Faust*, liturgy, Dublin) | No |
| Language conventionality | Plain, some period diction | Parody, neologism, multilingual play | Partly |
| Levels of meaning | Layered | Highly layered and ironic | No |

Only the Lexile row is measured. The other rows are qualitative judgements built on the Common Core dimensions, not published scores. On a 1–4 scale for each dimension, they would likely put a **2–3 point gap on structure and knowledge demands**, which is where the difficulty lies.

Readiness research also undermines the idea of a precise "matched" level. Betts's 1946 instructional level (95–98% word accuracy, 75–89% comprehension) was never empirically validated. Shanahan reviews studies, all of **schoolchildren**, in which **harder text with support produced equal or greater gains**. He also notes that assessment error is wider than the bands placement tries to detect ([Shanahan](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/upload/publications/308/pdf/Teaching-Reading-With-Complex-Text.pdf); [Fordham](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/leveled-reading-making-literacy-myth)). Harder text without support does not help ([Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/new-evidence-teaching-reading-frustration-levels)). The one computable lexical threshold comes from **adult second-language readers**. Comprehension rises roughly linearly with the share of words known, and **98% is a reasonable target for unassisted reading**, with about 95% workable when help is available ([Schmitt et al. 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf); [Hu & Nation](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ887877.pdf)). Making reading harder for its own sake does not help: a meta-analysis of hard-to-read fonts found **no learning benefit** and lower judgements of learning ([Springer 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11409-023-09366-7)). The defensible rule is stretch with support, not matching.

## Generating beats consuming: what readers should do while reading

The landmark review of learning techniques rates **practice testing and distributed practice as high utility**, and self-explanation and elaborative interrogation as moderate. It rates **highlighting, rereading and summarisation as low**, and says highlighting may even hurt inference ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)). With prose passages, undergraduates who read once and then tested themselves three times recalled **61% after a week, against 40% for those who read four times**. Yet the rereaders felt more confident ([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2006-roediger.pdf)). Self-explanation has a meta-analytic effect of **g = 0.55**, and prompting learners to explain beat giving them explanations ([SFU Summit](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/34322)). Readers are poor judges of their own understanding: **judgement–performance correlations average about r = .27 in adults** ([Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/3/30-1)). Screen readers are additionally overconfident ([Education Week on Clinton 2019](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/screen-reading-worse-for-comprehension-leads-to-overconfidence-new-meta-analysis-concludes/2019/04)). Prompts need to be frequent. When elaboration prompts in longer texts came only every one to two pages, their effects were diluted or reversed ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.gwern.net/docs/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-dunlosky.pdf)).

Formal comprehension-strategy instruction pays off less than its headline numbers suggest, and less for skilled adults. In whole-class studies, effects on standardised tests were only **d = 0.19**, against **0.79** on the strategies themselves ([Rotterdam UAS/Okkinga 2018](https://www.rotterdamuas.com/research/projects-and-publications/pub/effectiveness-of-reading-strategy-interventio/4c38634a-4354-4885-9093-6405172b969d/)). Adult basic-education studies show g = 0.17 ([KU CRL](https://kucrl.ku.edu/sites/kucrl/files/files/RER%20Manuscript%203-28-23.pdf)). Willingham argues benefits plateau after 5–10 hours ([ASCD](https://ascd.org/el/articles/beyond-comprehension)), though Shanahan says the evidence on dosage is weak ([Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/how-much-comprehension-strategy-instruction)). All of this concerns **children or struggling readers** studying expository text. Experienced readers already predict, question and monitor spontaneously, as think-aloud studies of skilled reading show ([Hiroshima Univ.](https://ir.lib.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/00033991?locale=en)). Their bottleneck with an unfamiliar classic is far more likely to be knowledge than strategy. Adler's inspectional-then-analytical method is consistent with the evidence on previews and expert readers but **has never been tested as a package** ([Wikipedia: How to Read a Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Read_a_Book)).

Medium matters little for novels. Paper beats screens by **g = −0.21 overall**. The gap is **about zero for narrative texts** and shrinks to g = −0.09 when reading is self-paced ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)). Philosophy plausibly carries the informational-text penalty. Rereading spaced a week apart beat a single reading on delayed tests among college students ([PMC review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8554015/)), which favours "what happened last time?" recall when a reader returns over an automatic recap.

## Discussion, audio and AI work as complements, not substitutes

Structured discussion improves comprehension. A 42-study meta-analysis, mostly of **grades 4–6**, found "substantial improvements in text comprehension" ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/examining-the-effects-of-classroom-discussion-on-students-compreh/)). **Critical-analytic** discussion, which questions the text and weighs perspectives, did more for high-level comprehension than fact retrieval or personal response. Quality Talk raised written argumentation by **d = .35**, and small groups of **3–4 outperformed groups of 5–7** ([Murphy et al. 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED590416.pdf)). The Great Books Foundation's shared inquiry has **no studies meeting What Works Clearinghouse standards** ([WWC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED521414.pdf)). For adults the evidence is qualitative. Long's ethnography shows book clubs give reading social purpose and a place in members' identity, but it measures neither comprehension nor completion ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/978-0-226-49262-9)).

Audio does not hurt understanding, and it does not measurably improve it. Across 46 studies, listening and reading gave similar comprehension (**g = 0.07, non-significant**). Reading came out ahead when **self-paced (g = 0.13)** and on **inferential** comprehension, which is what hard prose demands. Reading while listening added only a **trivial g = .18**, confined to experimenter-paced studies ([Clinton-Lisell, ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf)). Adults randomised to audiobook, e-text or both showed **no differences** immediately or two weeks later ([SAGE Open](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/2158244016669550)). Synced audio is best positioned for engagement, access and prosody, and as an easy way back into dense passages. Its pause, slow-down and re-read controls matter because they restore self-pacing. The hypothesis that narration helps more for verse and drama is untested.

AI support is promising only with guardrails. Classic intelligent tutors raise scores by a **median 0.66 SD** ([IDA/Kulik & Fletcher](https://www.ida.org/research-and-publications/publications/all/e/ef/effectiveness-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems-a-meta-analytic-review)). The iSTART self-explanation tutor chiefly improves **bridging inferences** in college students ([Kurby et al.](https://www.gvsu.edu/cms4/asset/92386C7F-BF75-96ED-67D9FAA81701D122/kurbyjecr12.pdf)). With LLMs the pattern turns on how the tool is used. Among about 1,000 high-school maths students, unguarded GPT access lifted practice scores 48% but left students **17% worse on exams** once access was removed. A hint-giving tutor version avoided that harm ([SSRN/Bastani et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)). A tutor that revealed one step at a time helped Harvard physics students learn **more than twice as much** as in an active-learning class ([Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/?p=103689)). Students who used an LLM as a substitute covered more ground but understood less, those who used it as a complement understood more, and **the LLM widened the gap between low- and high-knowledge learners** ([arXiv 2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047v2)). None of this involves literature or adults reading for pleasure. The lesson that carries over is that the companion should ask the reader what they think before it explains, and should give help in steps. Spoiler research, all with **undergraduates reading short stories**, is mixed. One study found spoilers raised enjoyment ([UArk](https://news.uark.edu/articles/38085/fulbright-college-research-team-explores-how-spoilers-affect-reading-enjoyment)). Another found they lowered suspense, appreciation and immersion ([OnFiction](https://www.onfiction.ca/2015/08/research-bulletin-do-spoilers-spoil.html)). Chat answers and recaps should therefore stop at the reader's current position by default. Study-guide evidence for adults does not exist. The concern about summaries replacing reading comes only from high-school newspaper surveys ([The Oracle](https://theoracle.glenbrook225.org/opinions/2011/12/16/sparknotes-to-be-used-as-aid-not-ultimate-source/)).

## Experts read by convention, and curricula sequence by dependency

What separates expert literary readers is what they do with what they notice, not decoding. In Peskin's think-aloud study, English PhD students used period and genre knowledge to direct their attention and **treated oppositions as sites of meaning**. A novice noticed the same oppositions and discarded them as "jumbl[ing] things" ([OnFiction/Peskin](https://www.onfiction.ca/2008/11/research-bulletin-by-joan-peskin.html)). Sensitivity to striking style turned out to be **independent of literary competence** ([Miall & Kuiken 1994](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Miall_Kuiken_1994.htm)). So novices see the strangeness but do not know it is deliberate. Zeitz found literary experts' advantage **did not transfer to a scientific text** ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/54h4s99w)): the expertise is knowledge, not general ability. Teaching Rabinowitz's "rules of notice" increased students' interpretive inferences ([UIC thesis](https://indigo.uic.edu/articles/thesis/The_Role_of_Prior_Knowledge_in_Interpretive_Inferences_and_Reasoning/10854842)). In philosophy, a four-person study found experts read an article as a move in a debate, modelling who argues what against whom, while novices read to absorb information ([Geisler](https://innovationtest1.colostate.edu/books/textual_dynamics/chapter7.pdf)). Accumulated literary reading predicts readiness. Among 203 adults aged 18–30, **only literary-fiction exposure** predicted comprehension ([UCA](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/handle/123456789/18358?locale=en)), and the Author Recognition Test is the standard way to measure exposure ([Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.662940/pdf)). These samples are students and young adults, and the evidence is correlational.

No major program orders the classics by difficulty. St. John's College runs from the Greeks to the moderns and calls its order "primarily a matter of convenience and intelligibility" ([SJC reading list](https://www.sjc.edu/reading-list); catalogue wording via a [forum reproduction](https://www.askandyaboutclothes.com/threads/the-classics.48231)). Columbia's Literature Humanities runs "from Homer to Virginia Woolf" ([Columbia](https://doc.sis.columbia.edu/subj/HUMA/C1001-20233-043/)). Bauer's lists for adult self-learners are chronological within each genre ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780393050943)). Adler advises reading an author's influences first ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Read_a_Book)). Chronology works as a **dependency order**: later books answer and allude to earlier ones. University courses pair Kant with Hume ([MIT OCW mirror](https://ocw.tau.edu.ng/courses/linguistics-and-philosophy/24-805-topics-in-theory-of-knowledge-a-priori-knowledge-spring-2005/readings)). Seasoned *Ulysses* readers recommend episode guides and expect to reread ([UlyssesGuide](https://www.ulyssesguide.com/how-to-read)), and some find *Hamlet* and *Faust* more useful preparation than the *Odyssey* ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/topic/show/17529138-how-to-read-ulysses-advice-and-opinions?tab=book)). **No study compares chronological, thematic and difficulty-ordered sequences.** Readers of the Great Books ten-year plan report that difficulty, not the schedule, is what derails them ([Great Conversation group](https://links.billerickson.net/?p=280)). This is the gap reading paths could fill.

## What this means for Tinct's reading paths

The table separates what the evidence supports from what Tinct would be extrapolating.

| Recommendation | Strongest evidence | Population | Strength for adult classics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build prerequisite knowledge before hard books | Knowledge threshold, baseball study, Core Knowledge RCT | K-12 | Strong mechanism, untested transfer |
| Frame before reading (angle, structure preview) | Bransford & Johnson, Anderson & Pichert, Meyer | Adults and undergraduates (lab) | Moderate |
| Stretch with support rather than match levels | Shanahan's review of frustration-level studies | K-12 | Moderate; no adult data |
| Recall and self-explanation prompts over summaries | Roediger & Karpicke, Bisra, Dunlosky | Undergraduates, expository texts | Moderate |
| Critical-analytic discussion in chat | Murphy et al. 2009 and 2017 | Grades 4–6 | Weak to moderate |
| AI that asks before telling and gives help in steps | Bastani, Kestin, Lehmann | High school and university, STEM | Moderate for the principle, none for literature |
| Synced audio as an engagement aid, not a comprehension aid | Clinton-Lisell, Rogowsky | Mixed, including adults | Moderate |
| Concentrate support in the opening chapters | Hawking Index, Goodreads survey | Anecdotal | Weak |

A reading path should be built as a **prerequisite graph with a difficulty layer on top**, not a ladder of reading levels. A Chopin-to-Joyce path would go *The Awakening* → *Dubliners* → *A Portrait of the Artist* → *Odyssey* and *Hamlet* → *Ulysses*. A Kant path would go Plato's *Apology*/*Euthyphro* → Descartes → Hume → Kant. Each edge carries a reason the reader can see ("Scylla and Charybdis argues about *Hamlet*"). Human guides own the edges and the reasons. AI drafts candidate edges, primers and difficulty scores for guides to accept, edit or reject. Every path node needs three things. Before reading, a short text-specific hook and a structural orientation for each chapter, given before the chapter, not after. A glossary of the book's key terms, matching the finding that particular topic words predict crossing the knowledge threshold. And explicit "rules of notice" at the moments where they matter ("Joyce changes style here on purpose; what does the new style do?"). Support is front-loaded in the first 5–15% of each book, and **chapter-one completion is the leading metric**. Chapter recaps work better as retrieval practice: the reader recalls first and the recap follows as feedback. They should never stand in for reading, and never run past the reader's position. The companion's default is critical-analytic: it asks what the reader thinks, gives help in steps from hint to passage pointer to explanation, and prompts bridging inferences across chapters. Modern-English editions lower the language dimension only, so difficulty has to be recorded **per edition**. Readers should never be blocked from a book. A hard jump gets a warning, a prerequisite primer and more scaffolding.

### Proposed book-difficulty model

Score each book per edition and per chapter on six dimensions from 1 to 5, then aggregate to the book. Two dimensions are computed. **Lexical/language** is the vocabulary-coverage share beyond a high-frequency band, with archaism flagged; below about 95% estimated coverage, Tinct recommends the modern edition or glossing. **Syntax** covers sentence length, embedding and words before the main verb. Four dimensions are rated with an LLM rubric. **Structure** covers narrativity, chronology shifts, point-of-view and style shifts. **Knowledge demands** covers allusion density and assumed tradition. **Levels of meaning** covers irony, ambiguity and unreliable narration. **Argument density** covers abstraction and how deeply claims nest, mainly for philosophy. Length and stamina are recorded separately. Place books on one scale through **pairwise LLM comparisons combined with a Bradley-Terry model**, the method CLEAR used, then validate a sample with a small panel of human guides. Scores apply to excerpts and chapters first because that is all the LLM validation covers. Outputs are a difficulty profile, not a single number, plus the prerequisite edges. Under this model *The Awakening* and *Ulysses* would differ little on lexical and syntax scores and sharply on structure, knowledge demands and meaning. Those values are a prediction to test, not a measured result.

### Proposed reader-level model

Reader level should be a stage plus a knowledge map, not a Lexile-style score. The stages follow the expertise findings. A **story reader** follows plot and characters. A **theme reader** builds an interpretive layer above the plot. A **convention reader** treats strangeness as deliberate and uses rules of notice. A **conversation reader** reads intertextually and as part of a debate, as Geisler's experts did. A path step is "comfortable" when a book's profile sits at most one band above the reader's demonstrated band on every dimension. It is a "stretch" at two bands, which brings full scaffolding and a primer. It is "steep" above that, which brings a warning and a suggested detour, never a block. The knowledge map records which prerequisite edges the reader has satisfied.

Version one should draw only on what readers tell Tinct and what they have finished. That means an optional self-placement ("which of these authors have you read?", modelled on the Author Recognition Test), the books completed on the platform, and optional one- or two-question chapter checks that readers choose to answer. Proxy signals of difficulty, such as time per page, lookup and chat frequency, rereading and abandonment, could later calibrate reader ability and chapter difficulty together with a Rasch/IRT model. **Privacy gate:** inferring a reader's level from behaviour collects new personal data. That covers modelling from reading telemetry and stored self-placement and check answers, and even repurposing existing reading-position data into an ability estimate. Under Tinct's privacy commitments, it needs **Anders's approval and an update to `app/public/privacy.html`** in the same change before anything ships. The book-difficulty model touches no personal data and can be built now.

## Conclusion

The evidence changes the question Tinct should ask. "Is this reader at the right level for this book?" assumes a precision that reading assessment does not have and a single dimension that literary difficulty does not have. The better question is which knowledge and conventions a book assumes that this reader has not yet met, and how they can be supplied just in time. That makes reading paths closer to a Great Books seminar than to graded readers: an ordered set of dependencies with a guide in the margin. An AI companion fits the guide role only if it is held back from doing the reader's thinking.

Tinct also has an opening the literature lacks. Nobody has published a quantified difficulty ladder for adult classics, and nobody has measured whether ramps raise completion of hard books. A per-chapter difficulty profile, pairwise calibration and an A/B comparison of path versus no-path completion on a book like *Ulysses* would produce the first real evidence on the question this report could only answer by extrapolation. Any part of that work that touches readers' own data goes through the privacy gate first.
